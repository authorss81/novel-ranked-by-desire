# PART ONE — THE CLOSE AUDIT

## 1 · THE VOLUME PROMISE

> *Lin Hao must answer for the three lines that have left that building in other people's mouths, or the only service this office has ever offered becomes the one thing in Ostara anybody can buy.*

**Did it land? Yes, and it landed by the planned mechanism. The chapter is 964.**

The two halves are separate and only the first was ever a threat.

**The first half — a sentence leaving that room in strangers' mouths — is answered in the volume's first ten chapters and the answer is a body.** At **951** the cost of the four lines is set up exactly: a man in a passage with a coin in his hand says the street, the door and the hours **and one more true thing nobody told him**, and Lin Hao stands four seconds away and works out that there is no longer any way to tell which part of it is his. At **952** the counterman explains the mechanism, and the second half of the volume's promise is already visible in a kindness: *the light goes off that street at about ten past four and you cannot read a thing.* At **954** a man in a yard says his price before he names the door, has never been up those four flights, and has lied to nobody. At **958** the counterman says the ground was dug by the man who gave it away for nine years and that he would go on doing it.

**The threat is answered at 964, by the last link and not by a counter-speech.** A woman who has paid that morning stands at the bottom of a third court and asks the man standing there to confirm the address. He confirms it, and then tells her to her face what she has just bought, and she does not go up. The price dies there, because the person who ends it is a man who cannot be led anywhere, does not charge, and gives her the truth about her own transaction instead of only the door.

**The cost is stated on the page by a person, not by a narrator, and it is stated at 960 before the thing is done once.** Wenna Raut makes him say it: *about four hundred yards of distance gone, a door nobody can knock on without walking past a man, every carrier's back visible, about four people a day who will learn his face before they learn anything else,* and it comes out of his three days off. He then finds out, at **965**, that he has been spending those days on the one part that was never load-bearing — four flights work perfectly well without him — and there is nowhere for the anger to go.

**The second half — the service was not bought — and the honest reading of it.** The service was not bought, and **the reason it was not bought is stated in the mouth of the man in the street at 964 and paid for in the mouth of a woman at 970.** At **970**, standing in the court with the ink still wet, he tells them a man who wanted a coin may have the same thing for nothing, on purpose; and when the standing woman asks what happens if that man comes tomorrow, the answer is *nothing*, because his own sentence says he will not stop anybody and there is no second use of the one. She says: *you have not got a first use left either.*

**Is that a defeat? No, and it is not written as one.** Four feet of pavement says three lines faster than four flights carry one person up them, and the volume proves it at **991** with about four people doing it inside nine feet of him. The man in the street is not stopped and is not diminished and is right about the walk. The volume says so at **963** (the free woman at the far end of the line who cannot afford him), at **970** (*no person here steals from him*), and at **991**. **What is lost is not the service and it is not the price; it is him.** The answer to the volume's promise is: the thing that can be sold is not the address, it is his face at the bottom of a stair, and he gave that away too.

## 2 · THE VOLUME'S OWN QUESTION

> *Three lines have stood on a door for a year and they have gone out of that room in about nine strangers' mouths a fortnight, and every rule that office has is about paper, and not one of them covers a person. He can take the card down in about four seconds and not one of those mouths closes. What does a man do with a sentence he has said and cannot unsay, in a city where the only service he has ever offered is the one thing people can sell?*

**The answer is a mouth, and the mouth stops. The chapter that decides it is 1000.**

The six chapters the question names, read in order:

- **951.** He takes the card down in four seconds and it changes nothing; instead he says the three lines out loud twice, the second time from his own second step, and the clerk enters not one word of it. The instrument is named here: Wenna Raut says there is nothing whatever on this table that anybody can put in front of anybody. **First mouth.**
- **960.** He puts his body where the card was. **The instrument becomes a body** and stays one for forty chapters.
- **970.** He gives the mouth away. *A man who wanted a coin may have the same thing for nothing.* **The instrument becomes anybody's.**
- **980.** He puts his name on a stranger's paper, because a thing that has been outside is a thing other people have seen. **The instrument leaves the building.**
- **990.** He is not one of the answerers any more. **The instrument becomes three mouths and not his.**
- **1000.** He says them one last time, says what it costs, says he is not going to say them again. **The mouth stops.**

**It is not an instrument, not a refusal, not a practice, not a piece of card. It is a mouth, and it stops.** And it is worth being precise about why that is an answer rather than a shrug: the volume spent fifty chapters establishing that every other available answer fails on the page.

A **practice** fails at **986**, where he finds four of the twelve can be pointed at a sheet somewhere in the building and eight cannot be pointed at anything, and he cannot find out which eight. A **piece of card** fails at **996**, where he goes up his own four flights to read his own card and cannot read the end of the second line, and the clerk tells him he has been a copy of a card at the bottom of a stair and a copy does not wear out. A **refusal** fails at **969**, where he refuses a woman out loud with the door open and puts nothing in the book and cannot prove in about four years that he ever said it. An **instrument** — a second card, a board, a lamp over the arch, a person on a rota at the bottom of that stair after ten past four — is refused out loud at **995** and upstairs with the door open, and the refusal is a list of people.

So the only thing left is the one thing that leaves nothing behind and cannot be called back, and the last thing he can do with it is **stop**. The volume's own closing sentence says it: *a mouth cannot be corrected, cannot be counted, cannot be closed and cannot be called back, and the answer is a thing said out loud that stops.*

## 3 · THE FIVE IRREVERSIBLE DECISIONS

Identified from the pages, not from the outline. **All five are his. One per batch.**

**960 — HIS, A PRACTICE AND NOT INK.** From the twenty-fourth of March he stands at the foot of that stair from a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven.
*Closes:* four hundred yards of distance, and the one thing he has spent nine years arranging — that every road to that door arrives at a person who cannot be described afterwards.
*Costs, said out loud with the door open before it is done once:* about four hundred yards gone, a door nobody can knock on without walking past a man, every carrier's back visible, about four people a day who will learn his face first, and it comes out of his three days off.
*Stated by a person?* **Yes** — Wenna Raut makes him say it: *then say what it costs, because you have said the part that sounds like a decision four times this month and not once the cost.*

**970 — HIS, IN THE HOUSE'S OWN FORM, ON THE PRACTICE SHEET AND NOT THE STANDING SHEET.** From the twenty-third of May anybody may stand at the foot of that stair and say the three lines out loud, no person may be charged, and he will not stop anybody.
*Closes:* being the only person in this city who can say those three lines true.
*Costs, said out loud before the ink is dry, and in his own words at the bottom of his own stair:* he tells a man who wanted a coin that he may have the same thing for nothing, on purpose, where anybody standing there can hear him say it; people will be standing there who are not him and not one of them will say it the way he says it; the wrong versions keep going after every mouth that says them is dead and nobody will ever know how many; the only copy is a line on a sheet nobody has ever been shown. **And it takes two things from him while he is making it** — the office cannot keep the rule, so the sentence he has just written is one he may say **once**, and he must answer the woman who came first with *nothing happens*, because his own sentence says he will not stop anybody.
*Stated by a person?* **Yes**, twice, in his own mouth and in Wenna Raut's: *a thing said once in a court belongs to the court* · *you have not got a first use left either.* **The standing sheet keeps six lines and the sixth of the second of December of the ninth year is still the last** (970:7, 970:33).

**980 — HIS, AND THE INK IS ON SOMEBODY ELSE'S PAPER.** He writes his name on the tailboard slip of a hired cart, and the slip goes into a stranger's pocket and out of that building.
*Closes:* the idea that the record of that office stays in the room where the office is.
*Costs, said out loud in a street in a mouth and not in ink:* nine days with no register at all; about nine answers a day refused or given out of a person's memory; and one copy of nine years of other people's answers on a street in a cart under a man who does not know what is in it and has not been told. It cannot be un-been, because it has been outside.
*Stated by a person?* **Yes** — the woman at the doorway four hundred yards off, told four hundred yards and a wide door, who says her sister has been trying to find that door for a month. **The office's own paper is untouched:** the standing sheet six, the practice sheet one, the cart slip is not a seventh line and not a practice and not a list.

**990 — HIS, AND IT IS IN A MOUTH, AND THERE IS NO INK IN THAT MOVEMENT AT ALL.** From this morning the clerk and the two carriers answer, in their own words, in that room, and he will stand at the bottom of the stair and come up if anybody asks him.
*Closes:* being the last person in this city whose answer to a stranger is worth anything.
*Costs, said before it is done once:* about nine answers a day given by people who have not been in that room for years; in about nine days one of them will say something to a stranger he would not have said, and it will be better. **What it costs and cannot be called back:** the whole of nine years of argument about the words used is now three people's argument and not his.
*Stated by a person?* **Yes, three times.** The clerk's: it is not a practice and there is no form for it. His own: he has carried a strap for two years and has never read a row to anybody in his life and did not know that until four minutes ago. **And the carrier with the bad back, from the second landing with no volume in it at all: *anybody of the three of us can be wrong.*** He says the three lines at twenty to five to an empty court, two strangers come up the arch saying them wrong, and he does not stop them and does not correct them.

**1000 — HIS, IN A MOUTH, AND A MOUTH IS THE ONLY INSTRUMENT HE CANNOT TAKE BACK.** He says the three lines one last time at the bottom of his own stair in the dark at about nine minutes to five, then says the cost out loud, then says he is not going to say them again, in the ordinary voice, giving no order to anybody, because he has no standing to give one and has had none since the twenty-fourth of March.
*Closes:* the only thing he built in the winter of year twelve, which was a voice that replaced a number.
*Costs, said out loud at the bottom of his own stair:* about four people a fortnight who cannot read that card lose the last road to that door and this office will not build them one, because building them one is a list and a list of who cannot read is a list of people; the wrong copies keep going and are going out of that court at that moment out of his own mouth, four in the afternoon on about half of them and a version with the second court in it and a version with three flights in it, none of which anybody invented; and he will never know how many, because nothing in this city can count them and he is the last man in it who would try.
*Stated by a person?* **Yes.** Every word of the cost is in his own speech, and the woman at the back with the shawl over her head hands his last sentence to the man beside her in the third person — *He is not going to say them again* — and a court that has just been told the sentences stop takes one of them and gives it to somebody else.

**THE MOVEMENT FROM A PRACTICE TO A MOUTH IS THE VOLUME'S WHOLE SHAPE.** 960 is a body on a step. 970 is a line on a practice sheet. 980 is a name on a stranger's slip. 990 is a sentence in a room. 1000 is a sentence in a street in the dark that stops. **The instrument gets less durable at every step and the cost gets larger, and by the last one there is nothing left to give away but the saying.**

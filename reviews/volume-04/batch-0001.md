## Gate A found nine real errors, and two of them would have been caught by any reader with a calendar

**The mechanical gates returned clean on a batch that contained nine wrong intervals.** They are listed
in full in `state/open-threads.md`; the four that mattered:

1. **Runa Sato's hearing printed on a Tuesday, in a volume whose calendar puts the twelfth on a Thursday,
   and the fifteenth printed as a Thursday when it is not.** Chapter 158. Caught by subtracting the day map.
2. **A nine-day span that is seven.** Chapter 151: the tile went up on the twenty-second and the bench cloth
   went on in the first. 22 → 29 is seven, 29 → 1 is three, 22 → 1 is ten, and the chapter said nine and
   then said it twice. It now prints the working.
3. **A nine-day span that is one day.** Chapter 154: Wren Kallo dates the corridor question the third of
   this month and then says she has had it for nine days, in a chapter set on the fourth.
4. **A clock running backwards inside one scene.** Chapter 157: the chart is timed at twenty past midnight
   and the caretaker who cuts the light comes at midnight.

**This is now the fourth batch in three volumes in which Gate A has found things every mechanical gate in
this repository returned clean on, and the lesson is the same one the close record has been carrying since
item 30: a gate that has never been wrong is not evidence.** The batch's own paragraph-gate line is the
first Volume 4 line of that kind that is true when written, and it was re-derived by command after the last
edit rather than before it.

## What a reviewer should attack first, in this author's own judgement

1. **Whether Volume 4's engine should be a composition return at all.** It is traced to Chapter 122 and it
   is the first document in four volumes about somebody other than him. It is also the first legal
   mechanism in this book built entirely out of ordinary domestic paperwork, and a reader who finds that
   slow will find it slow in the first ten pages rather than in a chapter. Chapter 153 exists to make the
   mechanism frightening in a woman's mouth before the reader ever sees the form, and if that does not work
   the engine is in trouble.
2. **Whether the turn at 155 is too neat.** Four months of a moral question become a blank space in a
   printed margin in one morning. The defence is that the Bureau really is this dull and really has been
   returning the same line since December, so the shock is available to any reader who has ever had a form
   returned. The risk is that it reads as a joke, and the chapter's own defence against that is the woman
   at the rail on band three.
3. **Whether Chapter 160 gives away too much in one chapter.** Six copies, a pencil sentence on a wall, a
   refusal in a doorway, a ledger rise and a woman's whole cost, in 2,495 words. **If anything is cut it
   should be the ledger rise at the top**, which is the one beat in it there to move a count.
4. **Whether the six answers at 158–159 are too neat to have been written by six women who never discussed
   them.** They are deliberately unalike — a filing, a joke that is true, two words, a price — and Beatrix
   names the reason on the page. **That paragraph is the last thing anyone should cut**, because if it goes
   the chapter stops working.

## The title bank, and the finding it exists to answer

All ten titles are names, and a reader who has not read any of them would learn nothing about the chapter
from any of them. **The Volume 3 batch titles are the worked example of what is being rejected** — nine of
those ten described what happened and one was a clock — and the failure lived in `outline/volume-03.md`
§11, which is where the bank is made. **The fix was made there and Volume 3 was not retitled.**

**Ten of the fifty bank entries are bare personal names or a bare place name and carry a declared
exception**, because forcing four words onto a name produces a description, which is the defect being
fixed. The manuscript's own precedent is *Ola Bright*, *Lantern Row*, *Cordwain* and *Signal*.

## Volume 1 — Rank Four

### Ch 1 — The Second Basin
Lin Hao's Wednesday plate comes back **3,412 grains with no interval** — a figure with no rate behind it, so no band, so no publication on Thursday. He takes it to the counter clerk, Faber, instead of walking out, and Faber calls it *unsettled*, wraps the plate in oilcloth, and puts him on the Thursday review list, which is how he ends up in a forty-name queue that goes up to the forty-first office. Behind the last counter he finally reads a door he has walked past for four years: **CLASSIFICATION WING — INTAKE AND SUB-LEVEL ACCESS.** Continuity: the counter shuts at eleven and an unpublished plate reads as nothing at all; six washrooms on the second floor and no window anywhere on it; his two-year blank in the record predates this posting. Open: what is on a Thursday list and who reads it.

### Ch 2 — No Interval
He reads the public band table and understands for the first time that **six is the first rank with a verb in it** — sealed filings open — so the want stops being a boy's arithmetic about a door and becomes a number that tells him what to do next. At the Correction Office petition rail he finds Petition 4118: Ding D. Duarte, rank two, auditor, refused, *no instrument of record*, act not scored under any available heading. A messenger gives him the floor phrase — *taken for a reading* — and a counter clerk, whom he deliberately lets see him ask, tells him they keep you until the arithmetic closes and to take a book. At the end of the corridor a rank-two auditor in grey is leaning against the wall where she has plainly been for some time, and she says his name. Continuity: Faber is settled and untouchable; **eleven unsettled readings a year citywide, nine audited by Ines** — the one figure in the batch, quoted identically in Ch 1, 7 and 8. Open: how a woman forty-one offices up has his name.

### Ch 3 — Nineteen Grains
On the Ferrier tram his hand is on a stranger's wrist before he decides to put it there. Something cold and heavy runs out of him into **Halvard Rees**, a wire-splicer from the yards, and a four-year-old pylon injury goes quiet. **In the same instant the cold crosses back** — three seconds of it, up through the wrist and into the old place, visible from the outside in the man's shoulders and breath — and Halvard attributes it to a draught off the junction, as any sane man would. In the concourse under the Reading his wrapped plate reads 3,393 — nineteen grains lower — and the notation arrives.
[Ledger. Three lines.]
Continuity: the draw is relief, not strength; Halvard is finished, not well. **He does not thank him** — he refuses to, out loud, in front of Lin Hao, and warns him against doing it twice. That refusal is the first turned line, made in nine seconds, by accident. Two of the three lines belong to people he has never met. Open: what the other two are.

### Ch 4 — Cold Minutes
Two days of touching nobody cost him the ordinary ability to stand in a corridor with people in it. The Thursday review is nineteen minutes and nine questions with a tired auditor; nothing is wrong with him that is anybody's business, and of the forty names on the list nine go down. On the landing outside, two auditors discuss a woman in a Vestry lift who has been logging cold spells for four months — **the third one is different, because a pattern gets an address.** On the Friday his figure has risen by 69 while he spent nothing: accrual is proximity, not contact. Exhausted and sleepless he takes the Vestry lift and the operator **Nadia Ferrous** catches his wrist as the car lurches; a cold minute goes into her hand and she laughs, and says *that's the third one this week*.
[Ledger. Nine lines. One turned.]
He makes a rule he admits is cowardice, and it lasts seven days. Continuity: **the grey at his left temple starts here**, after two days of not sleeping, and stays. It does not exist before this chapter.

### Ch 5 — The Junior's Return
**Tobias Renn**, junior filing clerk, rank two, four years in the filing room, tells him at length and with pleasure that the draw-down opens Monday the nineteenth, that ninety-one names get re-read and nine go down, that the *files* are what matter, and — reading from the card — that Lin Hao's second line reads **COMMON ORIGIN. SOURCE UNPLACED.** Tobias is a non-feeder: Lin Hao takes hold of the same requisition slip at the same moment, nothing moves, nothing is given, and he is grey at the temple before he reaches the second floor. Supply room three is closed pending audit, the key is with Colm Deveraux, and **Emrys Vaz**, seventeen, carries stock up from the yard at six. His want, for the first time, has a face on it that is not his own. Continuity: **Tobias's file goes in Chapter 23 on his own arithmetic.** Bernt Halse on the fourth floor gets one paragraph here and has no idea what is coming.

### Ch 6 — Supply Room Three
Withdrawn from the rota, Colm Deveraux tells him there is no form that opens a store in a draw-down week and refuses to be moved. **At ten past ten on the Thursday night** Lin Hao puts a hand on his chest to shove a door — and the wrong thing goes in: not relief but **account**. Colm's Exchange figure jumps a thousand and one grains, his rate does not move, and he goes up a back stair and locks his office and will not see him again. **Forty minutes later**, at eleven, the Vestry supper run comes up; he takes four trays off the rail and **Ola Bright** catches his arm on the step, and a cold minute goes out of him, and she shakes her hand out and laughs about it before she has let go. Stock moves at six. Four sheets of penciled diary are found under a saucer on the landing rail — eleven entries from the fourth of June, one word, *again* underlined twice.
[Ledger. Fourteen lines. Two turned.]

### Ch 7 — Lantern Row
He goes to Lantern Row because he cannot think. **Ines Duarte**, rank two, thirty-four, two rooms she cannot afford, four hundred files, will not take her coat off. His file is four pages and a bone-coloured card: four years of employment, a mop, a two-year gap where he does not appear at all, and at the bottom of the gap a cross-reference — **LIN WEI. R.6. SEE ALSO — READING OFFICE TRANSFERS, CLASS.** The want moves from nine to six. The bigger fact is the one she cannot stop thinking about: in three weeks not one instrument has touched that file, and somebody has gone to the trouble of keeping it clean for years. She asks *who helped you*. He does not answer; he tells her what Petition 4118 says about her sister instead. She lets him, and does not leave. Continuity: he has one felt want on the page — the ninety-one — and it is what drives him into the room.

### Ch 8 — Afterward, Ines
He tells her nineteen grains. She assembles the whole thing in under a minute and gives him the rule: rank six petitions a sealed filing. She tells him about Ding and the long stair and the 300 grains she has been carrying for four years. He says *I've got some of that* and tells her the cost before she decides. She takes her coat off and hangs it where anyone coming in will see it, and says **yes, do it, now, before I think about it.** The relief goes into her; she shakes from the heels up and says her sister's name into a pillow.

Then the scene, written at length — explicit, unhurried, and entirely hers, including an instruction she gives him *mid-scene* that the giving and the wanting are two different transactions and she is not going to let him confuse them. Afterwards she says the sentence he has believed without checking for three years: you have never once in your life been the thing that happened to somebody. Then, with her head on his chest, she feels the ledger — nine of the fourteen — reads off nine names she can identify, finds that not one of the nine is on the Thursday list, and writes a tenth in pencil on a clean page. The name is **Ola Bright**: not on his file, not on hers, not in any list in the city.

### Ch 9 — A Cold Minute, Recorded
The four pages go on the table and Ines reads them in the order the cold happened. The argument: he will go alone, she will not let him, and she is right — she is the only person who can find where a person with no reading sleeps. The count is fourteen; he has deliberately touched three; **eleven lines belong to people he has never touched.** At four in the morning she says *somebody has to* has just been proved twice — once by her sister and once by a woman on a bench with a pencil — and refuses to be sent back to her room. They come down into the Vestry arcade at ten to five into a shelter hall of thirty people who have been waiting since six the previous evening, and on the end of the front bench is a woman in a woollen hat with a school exercise book open on her knees.

### Ch 10 — No One Keeps the Diary
Ola Bright is delighted, not frightened, and hands over forty-one pages. Nadia has been telling the whole arcade, including a man from the Church of the Quiet Number. Eleven episodes, never four in a week. Ines explains that the diary is the only record the cold leaves and that the last tenth of the distance to interesting is a room with no window. **Bernt Halse**, 51, fourth-floor stock clerk, went down at ten past ten on the Thursday **in the morning** — the same clock time, twelve hours earlier, as Lin Hao putting a hand on Colm Deveraux, which is the sentence he cannot stop trying to join up — and has not come up; his sister **Ditta** has been on the Vestry benches since six on Friday, twice sleeping there. **Hester Quill** at the Classification Wing's public counter explains, kindly, that nobody is taken for a reading for anything they did, that the person stays until the book closes, and that her job is to make sure the name was on a form. He asks Tessa Okonkwo whether a body can be found in the records that does not match the body in front of her; she says yes, she has a box, and she does not count them. On the clinic rota board: `MON WED FRI — KITCHEN RUN — O. BRIGHT`. He does not see it. Across the road, a rank-five clerk in grey has stood on the step of the Church of the Quiet Number every morning for three years filing the same petition — *count the ones nobody counted* — and asks Ines, the fourth auditor in three years, to stand on the step and read it.

### Ch 11 — Ola Bright
He has the ruled sheet and the pencils for copying her book in his coat and does not use them. She is
delighted to be asked to *work*; he asks to read all forty-one pages sitting down, tells her one true
thing — the third one is different — and watches her write it down. Nadia crosses the corridor twice
loudly. The arcade is the widest room in the Vestry and it is full of doors nobody records. Ola's rota,
flat, flatmate Mord and her refusal to have it explained all come out between entries. He leaves the
book with her, which costs him the copy, and takes the Beatrix Halm name instead — eleven times, four
spelled Bright. Ines tells him admitting you is something he is unexpectedly good at.

### Ch 12 — The Ferrier Line
He pays for a ticket with a plate that has opened a staff gate for four years and stands in the public
queue for twenty minutes. The 980 shortfall is passengers, not staff: strangers helping each other to
the door is the densest thing in Ostara. He gets on with a good day and gets off heavy, has to sit in the
shelter for eleven minutes. Ines rides out with him and audits the doors instead of the people, and makes
him say the thing he wants out loud before she will show him anything. One mis-spelling is a date, and the
date is a roster day when Beatrix drives the arcade car to the yards.

### Ch 13 — Nine Hundred and Eighty
Rank five is 8,000 and he is at 4,012, so 3,988 of his is in no ledger he can see. Tobias Renn explains
it with pleasure. Two porters and a bag of brass fittings go past and one of them attests it in front of
him: forty-one grains, a stamp, and a crowd. He stands in the marked square alone and learns from a clerk
of twenty-nine years that a man may attest his own hands, and that the only part of the machine still
requiring a person is somebody asking somebody else. Two new tram lines take him to 16/3. Ines works out
the nineteen turns at the yards and makes him promise to write to her with exactly what he asked for and
exactly what he got.

### Ch 14 — Two Witnesses
The Board is repainting and Dereck Hain's tile goes to white four rows above his own, and he finds that
wanting a tile and wanting to be looked at are not the same thing. Four refusals: Nadia in a second,
Ines because an auditor attesting a drawing account ends her seal and she says so out loud, Ola because
the one person genuinely willing is the one person the city cannot record. He reads Beatrix's depot
roster from just inside the door, washes the pencil off his hand, walks out through a gate that is not
guarded, and finds his heart going. Nadia gives him a roll for a day that has not happened yet. Ola shows
him four years of somebody else's pencil work and the word headache underlined until the paper went soft.

### Ch 15 — The Interchange at Four
He stands where two streams cross and practises being looked at, and nobody returns it. He cannot stage
anything. The nineteen comes round too fast, he moves before deciding, and for half a second it is not
about the Exchange at all. The clerk stamps 400 grains in front of two hundred people, eleven plates go
up, and she witnesses it herself: permanent, and it does not come off for being sorry. He cannot find the
woman with the basket afterwards. Three feeders feel it at once — Beatrix Halm at the brake with thirty
aboard, Nadia Ferrous at the terminus, Halvard Rees at the Cordwain bench — each named, housed, working,
and none of them thanks him. Sixteen lines, three turned, and the count has not moved.

### Ch 16 — What a Band Costs
The Exchange is marble and a permissions board priced in waiting time. A woman with grey hair carries a
slip she has kept since last winter and is offered March, and takes it. 3,588 still to run, 400 at a
time, nine evenings, twenty-seven cold minutes, one of them a person who keeps a book. Emrys Vaz's rota
line is deleted on the twenty-second and he is on the list at the bottom under a heading for staff who
hold no plate. He takes the day off and spends it on the arcade wall doing nothing — and the figure goes
up anyway. **Stopping is not available as a category.** Ines tells him the difference between him and his
brother is a document, and goes down the stairs without her file. He says out loud that he would do it
again. Tobias has worked it out.

### Ch 17 — The Cab at the End of the Shift
Beatrix states her terms before hello: a use, not a man. The price is agreed out loud before anything
starts. The scene is the scene — cab and roof in the rain, unhurried, and afterwards the argument about
it, which is the actual price: she felt the interchange in the brake and put a man through a wall last
winter for less, and she has a hearing she cannot afford and a column with three ticks in it. She gives
him all four years, including a name she has seen on a rota in his own building. And the triangulation:
nine turned lines is a legible shape, and the dot is not a street, it is a house.

### Ch 18 — Signal
Four hours of laying her counts against his on his own table with a ruler across them, and the arc
appears: yards to free clinics, four times a day, with the shifts. He walks it and counts eleven doors in
two hundred yards. His poverty is a power stat. Ines puts her coat on his table, which she has never
done, and tells him what happens to the one person who is asked for an address, and what happens when
somebody with a seal asks where his figure comes from. Beatrix brings the week's plate herself: two
hundred, not four hundred, because the Exchange prices the deed and not the man, and says the ninth one
is her in September. Nineteen lines, three turned, all located.

### Ch 19 — Field Order
The ninety-one are on a board by the second-floor door, his thirty-eighth down, Emrys Vaz last under a
heading for staff without a plate. The clerk reads his plate twice and calls Vole, who reads it a third
time. Every plate in the branch carries two figures — the man's and the floor's — and the floor's is
released in slices only to somebody with a witnessed deed. **His 3,529 of doors and trays and washrooms
was held for four years and releases automatically.** 4,612 + 3,529 = 8,141. White tile, rank five. He is
grinning in a corridor for ninety seconds and then goes out to the rail and sees his own tile is still
black, because the Board publishes once a week. The same plate carries a Field observation request.
Tobias tells him at the turn of the stair, to the wall, that eighty-two of the ninety-one settle, nine do not, Lin Hao is number four on Tobias's sheet and Tobias did not put him there, and that he has about six weeks before it matters.

### Ch 20 — A Woman With a File
Rhee's office has a door that closes and a chair with a broken caster. He wants Lin Hao employed, not
punished — plate reissued in the branch name, four times a week for two hours in that room, on the record,
credited to the Branch and not to him, and he says out loud that the day Lin Hao wants out is the day a
man in an office asks him a question. He neither takes nor refuses. He asks for the audit; Rhee grants
fourteen days because nine would bring him back on the ninth. On the stair, Runa Sato says the order is
not Rhee's, is not Tobias's, was written before the twentieth, and that she has read four other files this
month of people who fit the same shape.

### Ch 21 — Ninety-One Names
Runa's observation room is a store cupboard she furnished herself, and she states what observation costs the observer: every word she writes is a thing she did, and a Field operative who reports a supply is the instrument that took him. She asks what Rhee offered and he tells her all of it, term for term, which is the lever he spends. Then he gives her the other thing out loud: the account is **geography, not intention** — one arc between the yards and the free clinics, four times a day with the shifts — and it moves even when he stands still. She writes `Runa Sato, Field. Observation only. No requisition recommended at this time` and signs it, and tells him it is worth exactly a week. Rhee is in the concourse at ten in the morning in a coat four hours early and closes the offer without heat: a man who has put what I offered on a public record cannot then sit in my private room twice a week on the record. **The last friendly door in his working life closes with a courtesy and he did it himself.** Ends on the formal deferred schedule: ninety-one names, a time against each, his at 11.20, and the small print — *attendance compulsory; those not attending are deemed unsettled.*

### Ch 22 — The Draw-down
The Branch cleans itself at seven and nobody comes in early. The clipboard copy of the ninety-one left on the second-floor rail gives up two names he already knows the shape of: **Emrys Vaz** and **Ottoline Barret**, forty-second down, eleven years holding the fourth-floor stair door. Nineteen lines, nineteen people in four years, ninety-one names in one building — the two lists meet, and the draw-down has landed on the invisible. He stops riding the Ferrier line the long way: the figure keeps moving and the faces stop coming. **Spending on somebody listed marks them**, because relief is invisible and a cold minute is not. Then Lantern Row, and the debt paid: what he asked Beatrix for, what he was given, and — the uncomfortable part — that he paid for it with himself, in a cab, at her insistence, before anything started. Ines's real anger is not the book. It is that she could have asked first and could not have moved a page of it, and he could. She will not take the rota name for a month and signs a paper saying why. Ends on the half-landing with Tobias Renn holding out a folder and asking, on the record, **what he is** — and Lin Hao saying "I don't know," because every true sentence available contains the word he cannot afford.

### Ch 23 — Two Kinds of Clean
The witness book has a left-hand column for automatic releases and a narrow column for attestations, and the narrow column is no longer empty: the 27th's release of the 3,529 carries *Attestation entered to satisfy reconciliation. T. RENN.* A true release with a false attestation, entered on a junior clerk's own initiative to protect the Branch's reconciliation on a draw-down week. A retraction before four o'clock is a correction and nobody writes it up. He has seven hours and forty-nine minutes and takes it nowhere, because every route — Vole, Ines, Rhee — ends in a document with LIN HAO on it. Tobias works it out, states all four lines against himself in the front of his own file, offers Lin Hao the witness line, and is refused. The cost is named: off the filing register, not suspended; twenty-nine months of correct filings re-opened by hand; then the boxes in an annexe. Ends on the empty chair and a name written out for the first time — **EMRYS VAZ**, the third tick in Beatrix's column, the boy he promised in a cab not to spend.

### Ch 24 — What the Clinic Files
The Vestry keeps paper because a headcount file cannot hold a temperature at four in the morning and nobody funds asking. Tessa shows him intake charts that are all correct and a rack of ten clipboards in the cold store that are not, one of them a woman on a cart at arrival who walked out carrying her coat four hours later with two identical entries in one hand. He has been walking forty-one minutes instead of riding eleven, and the count stopped when he did. He tells her about Colm Deveraux's 1,001 and asks to **aim it**: put the figure in an account on purpose, on a consenting patient. She refuses the ward and allows the cold store, after hours, because it is the one room where nothing belongs to anybody else. Consent is hers to obtain and she has not asked yet. Ends with permission and a time: **9.40, and Wenna Ost**, a laundress refused a hearing four years running who wants it written down if nothing happens.

### Ch 25 — The Wrong Direction
Three people in a cold store at ten past ten: Wenna Ost, who consented; Teodor Mace, who agreed at eight to an intake test because he cannot sleep; and Tessa with a clipboard. He has been awake two nights, has walked eighty-two minutes that day, and his hands are not steady. There is no stranger here and no deed, which is why the first deliberate act of his life has nothing to react to. He takes a wrist and aims, and four hundred grains go sideways. Teodor Mace's plate reads 22,640 with an unchanged rate and an unchanged chest, and Wenna Ost's reads what it always has read, because he has drained the woman who agreed to it and given the man who did not. Records stop matching: 2,640 was a man's figure in May, on a clipboard on Tessa's rack, dated the second of May. **The account was running before he knew what it was**, and he ends on the wrong chart.
[Ledger. Nineteen lines. Four turned.]
He keeps back from her, for the first time, that the count has not moved since the 29th.

### Ch 26 — Cold Store
Tessa takes the unreconcilable error out of Teodor Mace and into her own frame, where nobody checks it, and tells him this cold is not one to four seconds and is not in the hand. That is her gift and her inventory, eleven years of it, grey-black stippling from wrist to mid-forearm. She turns: nine of his colds since June on other people, unexplained, called being tired. She also tells him there is somebody else in this city doing what he did in May, and that she has a date. Then the scene — no file, no note, adults, awake, unrestrained, and the first of them who will not let him treat it as a transaction; she makes him ask. Afterwards she takes her position out loud: she is going to be in it, not as one of his but as the person who holds it, and she gives him the count — nineteen, four turned, two going into a room on Thursday, one of them a boy who carries stock up from a yard at six — and tells him a man who cleans washrooms cannot do what comes next in a corridor on the second floor. **He has to do it where they can see him.**

### Ch 27 — The Thursday Line
He finds his own white tile in the third row from the top and nothing happens: two thousand people look at nine hundred names and none of them look at his, because a man going from four to five is ordinary in a city where people move every week. That is the frightening part — the machine has read him in public and has not made a fuss. On the branch floor the queue runs from seven and nobody has been told anything; that is the design. Four names unsettle: **Ottoline Barret, Emrys Vaz, Tobias Renn** and a fourth at eleven o'clock. Emrys was handed a plate that morning at the north gate for the first time in his life and it reads 441. Tobias's reading fails two hours after he has destroyed his own career. Slips print at four, a man collects them at half past four, and he gets ninety seconds a person. Runa tells him the standing and the cost: a referral is not a decision, it is a thing that has already happened. Dread, with a use in it: **one sentence**.

### Ch 28 — Public Record
"Lin Hao. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains. Interval — unsettled." He walks out of the line instead of back down it and tells ninety-one colleagues, a clerk, a Field operative and a floor of witnesses that the unsettled reading is his, is on that board in that hand every Thursday, that anybody may come and look at the plate, and that he will stand in the Exchange box at nine every Thursday and read it out loud until it settles. Irreversible. Emrys follows him with his eyes and understands all of it. Ottoline Barret says one sentence — eleven years on that door, a different man held it before — and goes. A man of sixty says he has counted the words: eleven thousand a day. Runa puts the notepad away and tells him it is going in, word for word, filed on the 6th, and that he has not yet found out what a document about him does to the next four years. **Rhee closes the offer permanently through two inches of door** and buys a fortnight, and says it is the last one he will put his name to. The new board prints `UNSETTLED AT READING — CARRIED AND DECLARED`; five names struck through. The slips print at four. There were none.

### Ch 29 — Between Floors
He sits on the Vestry bench doing nothing for two hours, which is new, because there is nowhere else to go. Ola puts the forty-one thousand of the quarter on him. At twenty past nine Nadia Ferrous stops her own car between the arcade and the clinic and makes him get in. She has told sixty-one people by four o'clock and the whole Vestry by the weekend, and she knows exactly what she is, and she came to say one thing badly and says it: everything she feels is a floor, she has never once gone up, and on Thursday morning she felt him go up from the arcade with her hand on the brake. She spends **four seconds** on it, in a lift, in lift operator's boots, in the voice she uses for announcements, and she is not quiet once. Afterwards she carries the car **up one floor** — as far as she goes, at a price — so that it cannot be a thing that happened to her, and asks for her choosing of every part of it to be on the record. Then the doors open on the arcade and she tells nine people at the top of her lungs. She has read the notice: the re-read is quarterly, and a man who says a thing in a corridor is worth nothing, and a man who *keeps* saying it is worth something.

### Ch 30 — A Fifth Figure
Seven days later the Branch has opinions about him and no one has said any of them out loud; forty people watched him wait two hours, a dozen changed their good morning, and a woman from the second column leaves tea outside the washroom. The ninety-one are off the wall in a single line — *no further action this quarter* — and Emrys Vaz has the yard at six again. His tile is white with a black five, four figures under the name, and a **fifth figure that is not a rank**: 8,141, his exact grains, printed on a public tile for the first time in the Board's history, because a declared unsettled reading cannot be banded and the machine fell back on the raw number. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains, and nineteen people inside it. Beside the Board, a Classification Wing sheet: accounts under continuing observation, register opened **nine years ago, thirty-one entries, entries are accounts and not persons**, listed by plate number with no names at all. Runa has never seen it. The oldest entry predates the printed column — opened by hand, in pencil — and is the only one of the thirty-one still running. Five years of nobody's book before anyone's book.
[Ledger. Nineteen lines. Five turned. The count has not moved since the 29th.]

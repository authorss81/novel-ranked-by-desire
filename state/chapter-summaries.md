## THE TEN CHAPTERS

**941 · 28 Oct · 4,227 · The Door Used By Everybody.** A woman comes up those four flights at ten past ten with a man a pace behind her who has never been up that stair and has, and both of those were visible inside about four seconds. The way up cost her something and she did not hide it. She asks about her husband, who was there in the spring and would not come. **He is in the room. That is the only thing about the morning that went right, and it is also the whole of the problem: the practice from the eighteenth of August gives an answer about a person only with that person in the room, and it says nothing whatever about the person having asked.** He says the thing the office has said all year — we send nobody to a house, we keep no list, neither can be written down — and then says that somebody has done it for them, at a cost of two landings and forty minutes and her own morning, **and it worked.** Then he asks the man. **HE DOES NOT ANSWER HER QUESTION. HE ASKS THE MAN IN THE ORDINARY VOICE WHETHER HE WANTS THE ANSWER, THE CLERK'S PEN AN INCH OFF THE PAGE, AND THE MAN SAYS NO AND THEN SAYS WHY: twice to that office, once at two in the morning in the eighth month to a shut door because a piece of paper sent him, never angry, embarrassed, which is worse.** The clerk enters the worst available sentence and says so out loud before she writes it. **Ending: the only thing the no-list rule produced this morning was a doorway in which to refuse, and about four people had stopped on the second landing to hear a man use it.**

**942 · 5 Nov · 4,234 · The Dark Comes Early. CROSSING.** Thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven. Five mornings, a white edge on every tread, about nine people a morning coming up more slowly than in the spring. **THE BACK BOARD OF THAT BOOK HAS BEGUN TO HINGE OPEN**, with a tick about every four seconds and a line of daylight between the two halves of the board, and the only proper repair is a binder, which is four hundred yards in a cart in daylight with somebody else's hands on it. He says the word binder on the second morning and does not do it. **THE LIGHT GOES OFF THAT STREET AT ABOUT TEN PAST FOUR AND THE CARD RUNS TO HALF PAST FIVE**, so about four people a day are standing outside a door they cannot read. He takes hours off nothing, adds nothing, and puts no lamp in that passage. **INSTEAD HE READS THE THREE LINES OUT LOUD FROM HIS OWN SECOND STEP TO THE LAST FOUR PEOPLE OF THE DAY, FROM MEMORY AND NOT OFF THE CARD**, because a man reading three lines off a card at his own door is an official thing at a door and that office has no official thing at a door. **THE PRICE, SAID WITH THE DOOR OPEN: a man who says his own address in the dark at his own door is a man anybody can find in the dark, and in August he handed that job to a man who is paid nothing so that it would not be his.** The clerk names what he has replaced a number with. Ending: he stands at the bottom of a yard with a ticking book under his arm and a question for a woman at the top of a walled road, and he has had it since the third of September and has not found a day.

**943 · 12 Nov · 4,241 · The Intake Desk. THE SCENE.** The Ninth Terrace intake hall at half past one on a working morning, about nine hundred a week, shutting at four, the queue at the wall because the light is off the notices. **THE AXIS IS THE WINTER LIGHT AND NOBODY NAMES IT: after the light has gone off a wall nothing on it is read until morning, because a carried lamp puts the shadow of whoever is holding it on the words**, and that has happened twice at that hall in twenty years, both times on purpose. She will not let him make her a hardship: **she has had the same bench in the same hall for twenty years and has ended about nine hundred people a week standing on this side of the counter instead of in the street, and that is a better life than most of the people on that wall have.** He walks the length of the hall and describes how somebody finds him, in a minute, in nine ways, eight of them carrying paper. **Then she says do it for me, and he cannot do it in one sentence, and it takes him four tries, and on the fourth he hears himself say *ask somebody who knows*, and she says there is your answer and you did not need me for it.** She gives him the one thing that would make a card reach her — a person who knows where she is and can knock on that counter — **and it is the one thing this office has refused for nine years.** The light comes off the wall, comes down onto the counter, and goes off the floor. **She says she has wanted this since the fifth of August, says she has about nine minutes and not four hours for a speech, and tells him not to describe it.** She initiates, is quick, is enjoyed and says so plainly, tells him at every stage what it is doing, and says afterwards that the lamp on the counter is still sitting there and neither of them touched it. **Then she asks him for the one thing he has given away about nine times a fortnight all year and has never given to her: she has never seen the card, only read it in other mouths, and she wants the man who wrote it to say it to her in daylight in a public hall, and he does, and about nine people heard it and not one of them had a pen out.** Scene span lines 79–128, 866 words.

**944 · 19 Nov · 4,248 · Not On The Roll.** The same hall, the same working afternoon, and she has been at that counter twenty years and has never once been asked about herself by any of about nine hundred people a week. He comes up with a question and the mechanism is the chapter. **EVERY ROAD THAT CARD IS ON ENDS AT SOMEBODY WHO IS LOOKING FOR A PLATE OR A FLOOR OR A NAME, AND THERE IS NOTHING ON HER FOR ANY OF THEM TO LOOK FOR.** She tells him what would fix it — a person — and says it at half past one on a working morning in a hall with about nine people in it, because that is when there is anybody to hear, and **that is not the same thing as asking him to send anybody, and she knows the difference and has had twenty years to confuse the two.** He asks the clerk the same question at half past four and she answers before he has finished it: a card is a thing a person finds once and the Terrace is a thing four hundred people a week walk up a stone road to stand in front of, and putting a card on the front of that is a worse version of both. **AND THE PRICE OUT LOUD IS ABOUT FOUR TIMES A WEEK FOR THE REST OF HIS LIFE WITHOUT ONCE BEING IN A POSITION TO HAND A STRANGER A NAME.** He gets the book down at half past five and works out the two costs in the last thirty yards, and the second one is that **he had spent a year making an address and every single thing he had refused this year was on purpose, and the two halves of that sentence had never been in the same paragraph before, and when they were, the second one looked like the first one.**

**945 · 26 Nov · 4,255 · In Her Head Only. THE BATCH MIDPOINT.** The third time up that road in a fortnight and the gate is open and he does not like that about it. **She tells him before he asks that the book is not going to open in front of him today, and puts both hands on the counter two feet apart, and he understands the hands before she explains them.** The page is not going to move. **She is not going to refuse him the way she refused him three years ago, because the first refusal was a performance and this one is not, and the reason is about what a precedent is and not about him: in about four years there would be a page in a man's house with a name on it that came off a public post, and the next person up that road wanting to be legible would be told there is a way of doing it.** Then the worse answer and the better answer. **THERE IS NO PAGE ANYBODY CAN PRODUCE, THERE IS NO SECOND COPY ANYWHERE IN THIS CITY, EVERY NAME IN THAT BOOK IS IN HER HEAD AND NOWHERE ELSE, AND SHE HAS BEEN WRONG ABOUT ABOUT FOUR OF THEM IN TWENTY YEARS AND HAS NEVER HAD A WAY OF FINDING OUT, BECAUSE A THING YOU REMEMBER CANNOT BE CHECKED AGAINST ANYTHING.** She compares his office to it in one paragraph: one copy, in one place, that cannot be produced, and about nine copies in the city that are all wrong. **He has the question in his mouth and puts it back down in about four seconds, and does not ask her to look, and she tells him exactly why he did not and does not let him argue.** He tells the whole of it up four flights and she enters four words of it. Ending: at ten past three he stands four inches off his own card in daylight with his own shadow across the middle of it, cannot read it, and has never once gone and looked at it.

**946 · 3 Dec · 4,262 · Soft At The Corners. CROSSING.** Thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven. A quarter to one, with nothing in his hands, for the first time in about a year, to look at the card. **The first line is the only one with nothing wrong with it anywhere, because the street is the line he wrote first and steadied on before the two long ones.** **He cannot read it two feet away in daylight: the fourth and fifth words of the second line come out of the card as one word where the ink has gone out of the fibres.** He then goes and turns the lamp off in that room and reads it in the dark with his hand on it, straight through, every word, and gets the third line about half wrong without touching it. **THAT IS WHAT A MONTH OF FOUR NIGHTS A WEEK HAS DONE TO HIM: the ones he has learned are the ones that are in his mouth and not the ones that are on that card.** The carrier with the bad back notices the ticking. **Three nails out, the card off, the pale ring round each hole about a quarter of an inch wider than the head, both top corners of the second line soft, the street sound — and the same card goes back on the same three nails about ten minutes later with nothing written on it and nothing added and no name on it, and the holes are bigger when the nails go back in.** He does not re-nail it in different holes, because four holes and three nails is about four years of a stranger counting which was the first. He says out loud with the door open that it is coming down in the spring or whenever it comes down, and neither of them writes it anywhere.

**947 · 10 Dec · 4,269 · The Worn Card Read Back.** A man with a piece of paper that is half an hour different at one end comes up at twenty past eleven after standing at the bottom of that stair for a quarter of an hour, and asks him to read the three lines. **He had told himself in the corridor that he would refuse. He read it, and in the middle of the second line he stopped dead, because the card had gone soft under his eyes and two words that had been separate for a year were standing about the depth of a coat button apart.** And then the man said the whole of it back to him — not the first line, the second and the third, from the door, in the voice of a man at a counter who hands people things all day — **and got every word of it, including the half of the second line where the corners are gone.** **EVERY RULE IN THAT OFFICE IS ABOUT PAPER: A WRITTEN ANSWER, A COPY, A TRANSCRIPT, A SLIP, A FORM, A WALL. NOT ONE OF THEM COVERS A MOUTH, AND THE SECOND ONE GOES BY SOMEBODY'S MOUTH AND NOT BY ANYBODY'S HAND.** He says the reason out loud anyway so that the reason travels with the address, and then says the part he has never said to a stranger: he could stop reading it to strangers and about four people a week would go and stand at the bottom of that stair on the wrong morning in the dark, **and he cannot stop the mouth and will not stand at the top of four flights pretending that refusing to write things down is the same as refusing to say them.** Two people behind him do not go down, and one writes half of it on the back of her hand and gets the half wrong. **Ending: he tells the clerk that somebody is going to come up those four flights with a paper saying that office is shut at the turn of the year, that it is going to happen in the next nine days, and that he cannot stop it and will not correct it when it does.**

**948 · 17 Dec · 4,276 · The Board Bare With Paste.** Half past three, an hour earlier than September, because the light goes off that kerb at about four in December. **THE RAIL IS BARE.** Under it, along the top batten, a strip of board the length of a forearm where the wood was covered in paste and the paper is not, tacky to hard, grey at the edges, lifting at one corner. Somebody took that sheet down; it was not the wind, because the wind takes the top corner first and does not leave the paste behind in a rectangle. The two ruled squares are empty. **Her sheet under the lower batten has not moved and is not moved.** **THE BOY WITH THE PASTE BRUSH IS THERE AND NEITHER OF THEM SAYS ANYTHING: four things go unasked, three of them since the ninth of March, because a question from a man in a white plate at that kerb is the first thing anybody has ever asked anybody there.** He goes at the curl of paste with the flat of the brush handle, puts it in his coat pocket, looks along the batten for about four seconds, and goes down the hill with the brush. The stone is four inches deep and about sixty words in seven hands and he goes along it without stopping. **THEN THE THING HE CAME DOWN WITH: he stands at that board with the intention of putting the three lines on a piece of paper and gumming it to that rail, and works out standing there that it would take about nine minutes and be the second copy in this city that anybody had ever made and that he would have made it himself, and he does not do it. THE REASON IS NOT A RULE. THE REASON IS THAT A RAIL IS FOR THINGS PEOPLE TAKE DOWN.** He says it out loud to the clerk afterwards, with the door open, and calls it a bad reason he used anyway. **Ending: a board that has gone quiet, a person who stood at it for about nine seconds and went on, and a man who is glad about it and was angry about the same board in September.**

**949 · 24 Dec · 4,283 · What Is Still Owed.** The last working day of the year that is not the thirtieth, about four hours of useful light, about nine people up those four flights, one row with a date and an hour and nothing under the hour that has been there since the twenty-sixth of August. **The last asking of the year comes at about twenty past four and it is whether the door is there after the turn, and there are about nine minutes of the card's hours left.** He answers in three parts: the card does not say anything about a turn of the year and he is not putting a fourth line on it about one; he is not going to promise a thing he has not decided; and a man who has promised says four words at a quarter to ten and a man who has not says four words at about twenty past four. **THEN THE MAN TELLS HIM, HAVING WALKED UP FOUR FLIGHTS TO TELL HIM AND NOT TO ASK: a woman on his ground floor has been telling about nine people since about the ninth that the office is closed over the turn, off a man who read it at a counter on the second.** He does not ask to see the paper and says why. It has been going round eleven days that the office knew about it would have cost the same and nine of those were free, and he agrees. **He gives him the hours, out loud, at the top of his own stair, on the last ordinary morning of the year, and adds nothing, takes nothing off, and says he will want his name off it in about four years and will not be in a position to do anything about that either.** The drawer is looked at for about four seconds and named once — **sixty-two months and three days — and is not an event**, and he does not put a hand on it. He goes out onto the landing at twenty to five to look at a piece of stiff grey card about the length of a forearm with three lines on it and not one name, and finds a street that is sound and two soft corners and three straight nails in holes that are wider than they were in November. **Ending: it would still be there on the thirtieth, and on the morning after that if not one person did anything about it.**

**950 · 30 Dec · 4,289 · Not One Name. QUARTER-END, THE FORTY-EIGHTH SHEET, THE VOLUME'S IRREVERSIBLE DECISION, IN INK.** The six lines at the foot of that standing sheet are read out first, because that is the order they are read in and it is not changed because it is a quarter-end. Then the head. Then he says the next part is said in a passage off Cordwain and not in that room, and it is said at the counter under the window by the man who is paid nothing there, **who does the nine-word service first, to a woman with no card in her hands, who writes half of it on the back of her hand and gets the half wrong.** **THREE NAILS OUT AT ABOUT ELEVEN.** The winter card comes off, he holds it up in the light going down that stairwell, and the street along the top is sound and both top corners of the second line are soft with the fourth and fifth words standing about the depth of a coat button apart, and the three nails are straight and have been straight the whole year. It goes into the inside of that coat on the right. **THEN HE WRITES.** There has been a piece of stiff grey card in a drawer in the bin room off that branch since the autumn and he has carried it up four flights every morning since the twenty-ninth of October. **He writes the three lines in ink with his own hand in about four minutes, and does not write a name, and does not write a fourth line, and does not write an hour about the turn of the year, and does not write anything about the second of December of the ninth year or about a division return with a bracket at the end of a line.** And the hand did not shake. **The card goes up on the same three nails because there are three nails in that stair and they have been the same three since the first year, and it stands about a sixteenth of an inch off the door, which it did before.** **THE COST, SAID WITH THE DOOR OPEN: he is not going to have a number for how many wrong versions exist, because nothing in this city can count them including him; the card comes down in the spring if he takes it down; and there is nothing in the next twelve months that stops a stranger walking up that stair.** The head is then printed at the counter with the new sheet face up on the rail and about nine strangers in the passage: **thirty-two sheets and thirty-one quarters, differing by one, four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand seven hundred and ninety, and two thousand seven hundred and ninety is thirty-one times ninety, and it is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them and it is not money and it is not people.** The counterman writes it in his own small hand and puts the sheet where a person can stand and read it. At four he takes the forty-seventh off that rail and puts the forty-eighth on the same two nails, and the young filing clerk with no window writes the date and the hour and nothing else, **because a caption under a figure is a sentence the office cannot take back.** **THEN THE WOMAN WHO ASKED ON THE TWENTY-FOURTH IS STANDING AT THE BOTTOM OF FOUR FLIGHTS**, and he comes down four steps and tells her that the card does not say anything about a turn of the year and has not changed what it says and that it is there and is going to be there after the turn, and she says how do I know that, and the answer is that she does not, that there is nothing to check it against, that he is not going to make one, that there is no register of who has read it and no page anywhere that says what that card says, and that in about four years a stranger will come up that stair and ask why a room with nothing at the foot of its page for nine years has a door anybody can walk up to. **"That I decided it on the last working day of the twelfth year," he said, "and that not one person alive is going to be able to tell him when."** He goes up the four flights and says the hours out loud to four people on that stair, because that is what he does now, and gets the book down at half past five by his own hands. **THE LAST SENTENCE OF THE VOLUME IS NOT A WINDOW AND NOT A LOOK BACK: it is that the card came down off three nails at about eleven and another card with the same three lines and not one name went up on the same three nails at about a quarter past eleven, and nothing entered the office's hand at the foot of that standing sheet in ten chapters.**

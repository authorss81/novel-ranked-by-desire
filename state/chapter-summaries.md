| **811** | the ninety-fourth form | **THE MONTH'S FORM GOES UP AT THE KERB ON THE SEVENTH WITH BOTH PRINTED BOXES EMPTY, AND A BOY SQUARES IT WITH THE TOE OF HIS SHOE AND IS NOT ASKED ANYTHING.** Her sheet with her own pencil line is still face down under the lower batten where she put it on the sixteenth of December. **AND THE PRACTICE OF 810, SIX DAYS OLD, FAILS ON AN ORDINARY MORNING FOR AN ORDINARY REASON: on the fourth of April an answer went down four flights in a runner's hand, because the officer who carries was out on a district, and the carrier's book has two columns in it and no line for a runner, so both stand empty in exactly the way they stand empty on a morning when nothing went out.** He goes to the counters and looks at the runner's printed book, which has a place for the hour and no place for the hand, and asks for nothing. **THE ROW ENTERED AT TWENTY PAST FOUR SAYS THE ANSWER WAS CARRIED BY HAND, DELIVERED INTO THE ASKER'S HAND AT HALF PAST ELEVEN, AND THAT A CARRIER WROTE THE HAND AND THE ORDER IN HER OWN BOOK. THREE OF THOSE FOUR THINGS ARE TRUE AND THE FOURTH IS NOT, AND HE CANNOT FIND ONE WORD IN IT TO TAKE BACK.** **THE CHANGE: this office has decided that it goes on saying things about itself that it cannot check, and that neither he nor the clerk will write down that it is doing that — and he says it out loud in the room and the clerk enters the part of it that is true and nothing else. There is also a short account of what is left of the slips: four copies made by ear in four hands in this city, two thousand originals going out four times a year, and nothing anywhere that can be asked about any of the four.** |
| **812** | a question about a neighbour | **THE FIRST NEIGHBOUR QUESTION IN WRITING COMES OFF THE MAN IN THE CAP'S OWN PENCIL PAPER IN HIS OWN HAND, WITH HIS OWN DATE AND HIS OWN HOUR ON IT, BECAUSE ON HIS FIRST THREE VISITS HE SAID THAT IF IT WAS WRITTEN DOWN HE WOULD FIND OUT.** He still will not name the paper and still will not say whose. The clerk reads it twice before entering any of it, reads it back to him standing up, and enters it in his words with a date and an hour, and a row may be added to and not taken from. **About four people in a corridor about four feet wide heard a stranger's question about another stranger entered word for word twice, because a clerk reads everything back and the stair is the only thing she has.** He walks it down four hundred yards to a district office; she reads it twice with two fingers on the line and says it is properly put. **AND THE HARD PART, WHICH IS NOT AN ENEMY: the same question was properly put this month in four offices in this city and properly refused in three and properly entered in one, and his office is the only one that put it in a book, and that is not a fault.** She will not be cited, allows only that she read it, and gives him the one honest refusal and takes the dishonest one away — a row saying *he was refused*, never one saying *we will not answer you*. **END: the first properly put question about another person this office has ever had, which it cannot answer, cannot refuse in any form it can enter, and may not make the book look as though it had never been asked.** |
| **813** | the desk that takes one | **SCENE, WREN KALLO, EIGHTY AFTER 733. THE AXIS IS THE QUEUE AND NOBODY NAMES IT.** A desk takes questions in the order people stand and answers them in the order they were asked, and a question answered out of order is a favour; there is no column in a printed book for a favour, because a favour has no order and an hour is the order. **THE ROOM IS THE RETURN WELL UNDER THE SECOND COUNTER OF THE COUNTERS HALL — a flap, a wire cage on castors, a deal shelf, a lamp on a bracket hook, about three and a half feet of headroom, and a grating in the counter top with about nine pairs of boots above it. 733's ROOM OVER THE COUNTERS, 803's BOOKING ROOM AND 808'S CORRIDOR ARE NOT RESTAGED.** She unties a bundle that came in tied in three places and shows him what opening one in the middle means: the string never goes back on in the order it came in. She names the cost in a room — the man at the front learns he is not the front and that something behind him was worth more, and the one it goes to learns only that it worked, because the reason is that she has a face he can see. **AND SHE OFFERS HIM THE FAVOUR PLAINLY, IN ABOUT FOUR SECONDS, WITH A QUEUE OF ABOUT NINE AT HER RAIL BEHIND HER AND A WOMAN IN IT WHO HAS ASKED HER TWICE THIS MORNING WHETHER THIS OFFICE HOLDS ANYTHING ABOUT HER HUSBAND. HE CANNOT HAVE IT: HE HAS NO STANDING, NO PERMISSION AND NO FIGURE, AND A FAVOUR IS THE CHEAPEST THING IN THIS CITY.** The scene is on the boards under a counter in three and a half feet; he stops in it and she waits without being asked and goes on after him, which nobody in that building has done in eleven years. **END: about four people in a month have asked her at that counter whether this office holds anything about their neighbour and will not say which office they meant, and she has sent four of them away from a counter they had no business at — because his office is up four flights with a door onto a yard and hers is on the ground floor. Those two queues are the same queue and nothing in this city joins them.** |
| **814** | she files it under the day | **A QUESTION IS FILED UNDER THE DAY IT WAS ASKED AND AN ANSWER UNDER THE DAY IT IS GIVEN, AND NEITHER DAY MAY BE MOVED TO MEET THE OTHER, BECAUSE A DAY IS THE ONLY THING THE OFFICE HAS EVER BEEN ABLE TO STAND ON.** The question of the fourteenth stands with nothing under it for a fortnight while an answer dated the twenty-fourth sits in a drawer under the counter. **THE JOIN HAS TO BE A WORD, AND A WORD THAT SAYS *THE ANSWER TO THE QUESTION OF THE FOURTEENTH IS HERE* IS THE FIRST HEADING THAT BOUND REGISTER WOULD EVER CARRY, AND A HEADING IS A THING A PERSON CAN BE ASKED TO STOP.** She refuses to originate it in about nine words and the reason is not modesty: every page of that book is somebody's mouth with a date against it, and a clerk who originates one has nothing to stand behind afterwards, and a thing you may do once is a thing you may do again. The two rows are a fortnight apart and nothing on the page joins them, and the comfort is that about two people can open that book. **THE CHANGE: the answer to the fourteenth cannot be sent, because it says a day on which a paper came through that door and that is a fact about whoever carried it. He picks it off the wood and carries it out of the room and into the inside of his coat on the left, beside Perla Quist's copy and the empty sheet out of his own drawer, and says so out loud. Three things about somebody else, and not one of them can be produced.** A man who carries answers in a coat is a man who cannot be asked where an answer is, and a clerk cannot ask him either. |
| **815** | five mornings of paper | **FIVE ANSWERS IN FIVE FOLDS DOWN FOUR FLIGHTS ON ONE PAIR OF LEGS, NINE MINUTES EACH WAY AND TEN ON THE FIFTH, AND FOUR OF THE FIVE WERE ABOUT A PAPER THE ASKER HAD BROUGHT UP THEMSELVES, WHICH IS THE EASY SHAPE.** The fifth was not. **AND IT IS REFUSED AT A DOOR BECAUSE HE MADE A MISTAKE IN THE DOORWAY: tired of carrying a thing he could not say, he said the first line of it out loud — in daylight, in the street, to a woman who had come out of her own house to ask about it — about four seconds before she asked for it.** She took the fold, read all four lines standing up with her back to the street, handed it back, and said not one word about it being wrong, and said the answer was about her husband and he had not thought it was. He stands there four seconds without putting his hand out for it. He carries it back up four flights, fourteen minutes, and has to stop on the second landing. **THE ROW STAYS AND NO REFUSAL IS ENTERED, AND THE CLERK SAYS OUT LOUD WHY: a page carrying a delivery refused at a door goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters.** He tells her the true version himself — that he gave four lines about a man he has never met in a street doorway in daylight because he was tired — and it is not entered, and she says it is the first true thing anybody has said at that table about a delivery in nine years and she would not have wanted it in. **END: the worst thing in his coat is the four lines of the fourteenth, and next to it is a woman's paper from the fifth of September of the ninth year.** |
| **816** | the clerk reads back | **THE OFFER MADE IN A ROOM ON THE NINTH OF MARCH STANDS FOR SIXTY-ONE DAYS AND IS TAKEN ON THE NINTH OF THIS MONTH BY A MAN WHO BROUGHT A PAPER UP HIMSELF IN THE AUTUMN AND WANTS TO HEAR IT — WHICH IS THE ONE SAFE READING, BECAUSE AN ANSWER ABOUT A PAPER A PERSON BROUGHT IS ABOUT THAT PERSON AND NOBODY ELSE.** She says the price before she does it: about four people in that corridor, and the other side of a door is not a thing she can do anything about. She reads all four lines aloud in the voice she reads everything in, unhurried, and enters the reading as a row with its own date and the words he used. **AND THE DIFFERENCE FROM FEBRUARY IS NAMED OUT LOUD AND IT IS NOT IN HER FAVOUR: in February she refused to read an answer into that corridor on her own judgement and nobody had asked her to; this morning a man stood at the wood and asked. The difference is a request, and a request is a thing a person can be got to change their mind about and a decision is a thing they cannot — so in about four years there is a row with his words against hers.** She says it is the right thing to do, it is safe, and it will be the reason there is no rule, because a rule cannot say *only ever about the asker's own paper* in a book with no heading in it. **END: of the four who heard it, the fourth lives four doors down from the man it was about, went down and wrote all four lines out on pencil paper in his own hand in about two minutes, and has not been near that building since and does not know what it was about.** |
| **817** | a door with two names on it | **TWO PEOPLE WHO HAVE NEVER MET ASK ABOUT ONE PAPER, IN THE SAME RUN OF ROWS WITH THE SAME SHAPE OF SENTENCE, AND THE PAPER BELONGS TO A THIRD PERSON WHO IS NOT IN THAT BUILDING AND HAS NEVER BEEN IN IT.** **THE OBVIOUS THING IS WRITTEN OUT LOUD AND NOT DONE: five words and a date under the two printed words that say *carried by*, with no name and no figure on them, and it would be the best sentence that page has carried since the thirtieth of March. NO FIFTH LINE GOES IN AND NO NAME GOES IN — those two printed words are a works department's routing line and about two thousand counters hold the page, and there is a woman's name in the fourth line of it and she is paid once for it and will not be paid twice.** Both are refused with the door open, forty seconds apart, in exactly the same words, and the man who asked is told that the office may not ask anybody anything they have not been asked. **END: about nine people in that corridor heard the word refused twice about the same paper, and one of them said to the woman beside her, in the ordinary voice people use for a thing they have just worked out: *so there is one.*** A rule about what an answer may be carried in does nothing whatever about what a refusal sounds like from four feet, and he had not thought about it for one second in four months. |
| **818** | nobody is listed | **PERLA QUIST REFUSES A HEADING FOR THE ANSWERS OUT LOUD, BEFORE HE GETS THE SENTENCE OUT, AND SAYS SHE HAS KNOWN SINCE ABOUT FOUR MINUTES AFTER THE SECOND OF DECEMBER.** She says it on the landing with the hand-truck, not in the back room, and he does not go near the third table. **THE REASON SHE HAS NEVER GIVEN IN SIX YEARS: a heading is a thing that can be asked to stop. It is not a danger and it is not an offence, and the person who will ask this office to stop using one will be right to ask. With a heading there are two hundred and thirty answers in nine sheets and somebody can put two of them together to make a street; without one, there is nothing.** What she keeps instead is days and not people, because a day has no name on it unless somebody puts one there, and joining two entries up is how you get a street — and she has been asked six times by men with standing to add a column, and every one of the six was a man doing her a kindness. **AND THEN THE THING THAT COSTS HER: about nine people have asked her in that lane and on that stair in four weeks whether that office holds anything about their neighbour, and she has said nothing six times.** He asks her nothing about them, and says why out loud: nothing in this city lets him ask a woman who keeps an index what people say to her on a step, and if he did then in about four years there would be a question in that lane about her. **END: he did not come up four flights to ask her for a heading. He came because a refusal got out of a room on the nineteenth of this month and nothing in this city will hold that for him, and she is the only one left with the standing to refuse a thing in writing and be right about it, and she has it because nobody has ever paid her.** |
| **819** | three kitchens ask at once | **ONE STREET ABOUT NINE FEET WIDE WITH ELEVEN DOORS ON THE WEST SIDE AND NINE ON THE EAST, ABOUT FOUR HUNDRED YARDS FROM THE TOP OF THE HILL TO A YARD GATE, ABOUT NINE PEOPLE IN IT IN THE LAST HOUR OF THE LIGHT AND THREE DOORS OPEN BECAUSE IT WAS THE LAST COOL EVENING IN THAT MONTH.** Three of them have asked questions in that building. The answer is about a man who brought a paper through that front door in the autumn and lives at the top of that street, and the woman who asked for it lives four doors from the bottom of it, and there is no way of arriving at her door without walking the length of the street under about nine windows. He walks the whole length of it first, in daylight, counting twice, and the thing he does not know is the light: **about half past four in the third month of the year that street is lit end to end and not one door on it is out of shadow until about ten to five, and a man walking down it is not standing in one person's shadow, he is standing in a window.** The rule of 810 is a working-day rule and there is no officer and no woman who carries things for a living on that street at ten at night, so he will not do it, and the clerk enters the practice as it stands. He walks it down at half past four at the pace of a man out for no reason, eleven minutes for four hundred yards, with his hands empty, and the woman at the door reads it with her back to the room and says thank you and asks him nothing. **END: at the bottom of it a woman comes out of a door two feet behind him and says across nine feet of street to another woman at a rail that is the man from the office off a third court, and that he has been going in and out of that lane all week with a paper in his coat. Neither of them says one word to him. He has been identified in a street by a person who is under no obligation to identify anybody.** |
| **820** | the answer goes with both present | **THE MOVEMENT'S IRREVERSIBLE DECISION, AND IT IS HIS, AND IT IS NOT INK.** The woman refused in a doorway on the third of May and the man refused on the nineteenth came up the four flights together on the tenth of this month, side by side, having stood four feet apart at the bottom of that stair in the yard on the morning of the nineteenth and gone out through the gate in opposite directions, and having arranged it between them in twenty-one days without saying one word about this office to any of it, and neither of them knew why the other was there. They ask for the answer to be read in a room they are both in, and then the woman says the thing neither of them came to say: *ask him to come.* **There is no instruction in this city that lets this office ask anybody anything they have not asked, a clerk cannot originate the request, a runner cannot be sent to a house with it, and so nothing at the foot of any page can start it.** *From this morning, two things. An answer that is about a paper the person asking brought up themselves goes by hand to that person, in a fold, and the flap is gummed shut and the date and the hour are on the outside and nothing else is. An answer that is about any other person is not carried anywhere: it is read out in that room once, in the voice this office reads everything in, and the person it is about is in the room when it is read and the person who asked is in the room when it is read, and if the person it is about will not come then there is no answer and the row stands as it is.* He says it to the two of them and not to the clerk, and asks whether they will both have it, and the man says the man will say no, because a woman he lives four doors from will come to his door and say somebody has been asking after him, and he would rather not know who for the rest of his life. **WHAT IT CLOSES: answering one person about another alone, for ever. WHAT IT COSTS: the rule is kept by nobody who can be made to keep it, and the first person who has to keep it is a man who lives four doors from a woman who stood in a corridor for an hour and a half waiting to be told something about herself.** The clerk enters a refusal against both rows and says the third row is not going to be in that book at all, because a row is a mouth with a date on it and nobody is going to say anything. The thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for seventy days and comes down at four on the thirtieth of June, which is 823 and 824 and not this chapter, because a sheet goes up on a quarter-end and the tenth of June is not one. **AND MARGUERITE VALE PRICES IT AT A GUILD HOUSE ON CORDWAIN, AND SHE IS THE ONLY PERSON IN THIS VOLUME WHO PRICES ANYTHING AND SHE IS RIGHT: about nine questions a month, four of them at one morning each, five of them about a person who is not standing there at two mornings each, and about five mornings a month is a quarter of a day that nobody will feel — and more than half of the five will not come, because the ones who can come are the ones who have a morning, and they are not the ones asking, and it falls hardest on the people who can least afford it.** END: there was no answer on the tenth of this month and there is none on any morning after it, and the thing that has to happen first is a woman standing at a door at half past four on a working afternoon saying a man's name out loud. |

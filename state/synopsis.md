## Back cover

Ostara scores every citizen each Thursday and nails the figures to a board anyone can read. Lin Hao is rank four and a washroom attendant at the Bureau of Merit, worth less than the equipment he cleans. His brother told a public hall the number was wrong and was gone by morning. Then Lin Hao's reading came back with a figure and no rate, and behind the missing rate sits an account fed by every unrecorded good deed in his reach, growing and answerable to nobody. He wants rank nine. Rank nine opens his brother's file. Every woman worth having wants to know where that account came from, and he is not hiding well.

## Synopsis

Power came out of a broken sky over Ostara twenty years ago and the city answered it with arithmetic. Every registered citizen is scored by the Bureau of Merit every Thursday, and the figure goes up on a board anyone can stand and read. The bands decide what a person may open, own, be compelled to answer for, and quietly eat.

Lin Hao is twenty-five and cleans washrooms at the Fourth Branch. He has been scanned on his way to work every morning for four years, which makes him somebody the city measures on the way past. What he wants is rank nine, and rank nine opens the sealed file of a brother who stood in a public hall with his band on the wall behind him, said the number was wrong, and was gone by morning.

Then his own reading comes back with a figure and no rate, which is a machine declining to write down something it could not place. What it will not place is an account of merit given quietly, in doorways and on landings and in the ordinary business of being decent, by people nobody ever wrote down, and it settles in him instead of scoring.

The power gives back only what was given to it. Every draw is paid for by the people who fed it, briefly, in cold they did not choose, and some of them notice. So the reader watches a man who hoards every advantage turn hoarded merit into public standing, standing into a household, and a household into cover. Nine women are sworn into that household in a public room, and under the law of the city every one of them is a person a requisition can be served on. All of them can leave. Some of them do, and one comes back on her own terms.

The opposition is never a villain. It is procedure, and a measurement wing, and a corrections office that runs public hearings on purpose, and a Council of nine people who have held a city still for twenty years because they were countable, and who are right about why. The antagonist of the whole book is the metric: a city measuring what it can measure, publishing it, and being right.

The final movement is not the climb. It is a room off a third court, a book, a card on a door, and three lines of address that he refuses to copy, correct, charge for or keep a roll of: and what happens when the arrangement starts saying itself in voices that all sound like his and that cannot be checked by anybody.

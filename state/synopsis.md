## Back cover

Ostara scores every citizen each Thursday and paints the figures on a board anyone can read. Lin Hao is rank four, a washroom attendant at the Bureau of Merit, worth less than the equipment he cleans. His brother stood in a public hall, said the number was wrong, and was gone by morning. Then Lin Hao's reading came back with a figure and no rate behind it, and under the missing rate sits an account fed by every unrecorded good deed in his reach, growing and answering to nobody. He wants rank nine, because nine opens any file in the city. Nine women are sworn to him before he gets there.

## Synopsis

Twenty years ago the Veil broke over Ostara, power came out of it, and the city answered with arithmetic. Every registered citizen is scored weekly by the Bureau of Merit, the figure goes up on a board anyone can read, and the rank decides what you may open, own, and be compelled to answer for.

Lin Hao is twenty-five and cleans washrooms at the Fourth Branch, scanned at the gate every morning for four years, which makes him somebody the city measures as it goes past. He wants rank nine, because nine opens the sealed file of a brother who said in public that the number was wrong and was gone by morning.

His reading comes back with a figure and no interval, and puts him on a review list and a road to a room under the building. What it cannot place is merit given quietly, in doorways and on landings, by people nobody wrote down. It settles in him instead of scoring.

The power gives back only what was given to it. Every draw is paid for by the people who fed it, briefly, in cold they did not choose, and feeling it twice makes a reportable pattern. He turns unrecorded merit into attested public deeds, and standing into a household of nine sworn women. All of them can leave. One does, and she is back in the room long before the book ends.

The opposition is never a villain. It is a counter and a carbon copy, a Classification Wing that wants to name what he is, the Correction Office, which runs the hearings and publishes the refusals, and the Concil of the Nine, who held a city still for twenty years because they were countable and are right about why. The antagonist of the book is the metric.

And the account is not his. It is an address his brother made, filling itself from strangers ever since, and the man the city does not audit is the only one low enough to be given it.

The wanting is fast and the book is explicit. What the reader watches is a hoarder becoming a public figure, a household that can be requisitioned, and a hearing in which the most powerful woman in the city argues sincerely that he should be somewhere quiet. The last movement is not the climb. It is a room off a third court, a book, a card on a door with no name on it, and the discovery that the one service nobody can buy has begun saying itself in voices that all sound like his and that nothing in Ostara can check.

# The Cough That Is His Now

It was two days. The chest went on the fifteenth of July at about a quarter past twelve in the afternoon in the middle of a square with about nine hundred people in it, and this was the seventeenth, and thirty taken from seventeen leaves thirteen, and thirteen is the last number that anybody in that building uses for anything.

He came in at about seven in the evening from the north end of the ranks wall, where he had done six and a half hours instead of eleven, and he was two days into a thing that does not go, and he came up the stairs at Ferrier Street at the speed of a man who has learned what the stairs are for.

## Six And A Half Hours

He had done six and a half hours at the north end of a wall on the seventeenth of July and got through about two hundred and forty hands, and he had stood up four times, and twice he had got all the way to the rail at the low end of the wall before he had to sit down on it.

There was a woman at the fruit stall on the south side who had started keeping the place at seven for people who could not stand for an hour, and on the seventeenth there were sixty people waiting at half past six instead of forty, and at nine o'clock there were about two hundred at the wall, and by half past five there were still people coming up the square to find out whether it had finished.

He did not stop for lunch. He had not stopped for lunch on any of the seven days and there was nowhere at the north end of that wall to stop for lunch.

At about eleven a man in the queue had asked him whether it was supposed to hurt, and he had said no, and the man had said, "It is hurting you," and had gone off up the square, and that was the only time in seven days that anybody had put it into words and he had not corrected him.

## What Eight Women Did With A Cough

It went about half past seven in a kitchen with eight women in it, and it went for about eleven seconds, and it was the first time it had happened in front of somebody who was not a colleague.

Nadia Ferrous was at the table with a bowl of something and a cloth and was the only one of the eight who stopped moving.

She did not ask. That is the thing about that kitchen, and it is a thing she has had four years to be good at, and everybody in it learned it off her.

Nadia Ferrous cannot go anywhere. She has not been able to leave a building and get down a floor since she was young enough to be told about it, and she has been the loudest woman on that side of the city for longer than anybody in that kitchen has been together, and she hears a cough in a room of nine people before a door shuts in a corridor forty feet away.

She looked at him for about two seconds and then she said, "Is that the step or is that you."

"It is the step."

"Right," said Nadia Ferrous, and went back to the bowl.

Then, about four seconds later, she said, "There is a man on the fruit side who has been sitting in the same place since the tenth of July and he has not spoken to anybody and one of the girls at the top of the building says he is waiting for somebody to come out of the water office and has been since the twelfth."

"Thank you."

"I did not ask," said Nadia Ferrous.

## The Kitchen Table At Half Past Seven

Eight women and a man at a table in a building he had named four times in a sealed finding nine days before.

They knew about the chair. Isolde Marchetti had sung the shape of it at the table on the night of the ninth, in nine seconds, flat and local, and a room of nine people had agreed with all of it before the second bar, and he had said nothing at the time because nothing in this city stops a thing like that and because a woman who can make a room agree has never once in four years agreed with a room about anything that was not true.

They had all believed him on the ninth of July and they believed him still.

Runa Sato priced a basket of fish out loud at about ten past eight, and gave the price, and named the man it had come through, and did not look up.

"There is a woman at a counter at Cordwain who says the Bureau has come off the wall," she said, in the middle of that, "and two men have organised something about it in a lane, and I priced both of them on the fourteenth and one of them is worth about nine grains a week."

Nine grains. The floor of every rank in this city.

"Runa."

"I am telling you what a thing costs," said Runa Sato. "That is the conversation in this kitchen and you are in it whether or not you have anything to say about what you have been doing since the eleventh of July."

## Beatrix Halm Came At Nine

She came in at about nine with her coat off and her notebook out and a cup of tea she did not drink, and she put the notebook on the table and turned it round and pushed it along the wood, and nobody in that kitchen ever says anything about the notebook.

"Ninety-one," said Beatrix Halm. "On the eleventh, from the north end, at about three in the afternoon, and there were about ninety on the wall by the time the light went, and I counted them twice."

"Who put them up."

"That is what I have come about," she said. "I have counted four of those walls in this city for nine years and there has not been a mark on any of them that was not made by an office, and on the eleventh of July there were ninety-one marks in somebody else's chalk with nothing behind them, and by the thirteenth there were marks on the wall at the Vestry end and on a wall off Lamplit Row."

She put her finger on the line.

"That is not a crowd," said Beatrix Halm. "A crowd does not carry chalk between four walls."

She looked up.

"And there is something else and I have had it since the fourteenth and I have not written it down." She touched her own notebook. "On the wall at Cordwain there are seven marks and they are all the same seven people and they are all in the same week, and I know what a week of the same seven people looks like because I have been writing them down for four years and it is the exact shape of my own round."

The kitchen went on being a kitchen.

"I am not asking you about it," she said. "I am telling you I have it, because if I ever use it I want you to have been told first."

## Two Flights Of Stairs And A Glass Of Water

Tessa Okonkwo had been at the far end of that table since seven and had said about four sentences all evening, and at about a quarter to ten she got up, filled a glass at the sink, put it down next to his plate, and sat down again.

She did not ask him to drink it. She did not ask him anything at all.

Then at about ten past ten, when it went again and went on for about nine seconds and he had both hands on the table, she said, "That is a chest," and stopped.

Everybody at that table stopped.

"No," said Tessa Okonkwo, and turned a page of nothing. "I am not going to do that in this kitchen and I am not going to do it on the sixteenth."

"I want you all to hear me say that I know, and that I am not going to be the woman who explains it to eight people at a table at ten o'clock at night. If it happens again I am going to take him to a room with a door that shuts and I am going to put him on my own table and I am going to count."

"Tessa."

"Not tonight," said Tessa Okonkwo.

## What He Was Letting It Be

He did not correct one of them.

That is the whole of what he did on the evening of the seventeenth of July. He did it with a glass of water next to his plate that he drank about half of.

He did it in a kitchen with eight women in it, four of whom had known something about it since the fifteenth and one of whom had known since the thirteenth of July and had written it on a sheet of paper in a room below a stage with a figure on it that she would not give to him.

Every single one of them said something else instead.

A man came in at about ten and found out that there was a woman at a counter in Cordwain who had a grey on the side of her head and would not go to a clinic about it.

A carter off a lane had been given a hand at the north end of a wall and had been so much better that he had taken his mother's coal in and had not been the same since. There was a mark on a wall at Lamplit Row with nothing behind it. There was a man in a warm lift who had felt something on the twelfth and had told everybody he knew and had been laughed at.

Every one of those was true. He had said them. He checked each of them against himself while it was being said and every one of them came out right, and he sat there and let eight women not ask him a single question about the thing that was going to kill him in about nine years or in about thirty, whichever of those turned out to be the longer.

He was not protecting them.

He had worked that out somewhere around the second cough and had not told anyone and it was true: there was no version of the sentence that was not a woman in that kitchen saying *you have decided* and a man at that table either agreeing or lying, and he had sworn on the ninth of May never to make a room need anything and he was not going to start on his own kitchen.

So he let it be that.

## The Thing He Has

He went up at about half past ten and his chest was not better and it was not worse.

There is a difference between a thing a man is doing and a thing a man has, and on the eleventh of July he was doing it, and on the fifteenth at a quarter past twelve in a square with nine hundred people in it he crossed over into having it, and there is no instrument in this city that crosses back, and nobody who has crossed back exists.

It was slower now. That was the arithmetic and there was nothing else in it: he had spent seven days at the north end of a wall and he had moved about four thousand people through a chest that had nothing left in it, and a woman with a pencil had told him on the fifteenth in front of four hundred people that the account was exactly as full as it had been on the eleventh, and she was right.

He went back the next morning at seven.

He did not get through as many. About two hundred and forty hands on the seventeenth, and he stood up four times, and he was still there at half past five, and he went home with a piece of brass in his coat and a flat book in the inside pocket with a line in it in his own hand that said he had done a thing to a woman who counts for a living at a counter in a hall with no door on it.

Three streets off, a woman was sitting in a room with no window under a stage with her hand over one side of her head and going through forty weeks of a figure.

There were three weeks in there she could not find in her head and the grey had not gone. She had written his times down and would not give them to him, and she was going to be at the third window of that building on the ninth with a figure she had got off the paper and not off her memory.

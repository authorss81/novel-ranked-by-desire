# Three Hours And No Use To Anyone

The hearing room at the Correction Office held about three hundred and forty people at ten in the morning on the twenty-seventh of July, and at nine names had been put in the day's book at the door, and one of the nine had been put in on the twenty-first of July in a yard behind a wall in Cordwain.

She was on the book under her own name. She had been on it under her own name for six days and she had told nobody else she had put it there, and the seven other names on that page belonged to a witness from the Vestry, a man who kept the Bellhouse returns, a woman off a landing at the Exchange, and four others.

Nine names in four days for a hearing anybody may attend is what the Board's own figures say a city of this size produces when the thing being discussed has been true for thirty years.

The chair asked whether anybody wished to be heard.

## She Stood Up Without Being Called

Agnete Lindqvist stood up at about ten past ten and did not wait to be asked twice, which is not a thing anybody does in a Correction Office hearing, and the officer said, "You are on the book."

"I am on the book," she said, "and I am going to begin by saying the thing that is true about this room and about the next three hours, and I would rather say it in the first minute than have it discovered later."

Nobody in that room had asked her for anything.

"In this room nothing can be compelled," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I am holding it and I have held it for eleven years and I am going to hold it until the chair tells me to stop. It means that none of you can be made to say a word of what you are about to hear,"

"and that nothing any of you says while I am standing up can be used against you. Not in a finding. Not in a minute. Not in anybody's office in this city, now or in four years. It is a free statement and a free statement binds nobody, including the person who makes it."

She turned round and looked at about sixty people who had come to hear about an account.

"If any of you is here because you have been told that what you say in here can be used, then that is not what this room is and you have been told wrongly by somebody, and I would be obliged if you would tell them."

## The First Twenty Minutes

She did not begin with the reservoir and she did not begin with the requisition and she did not begin with herself.

"The fourteenth of February. A man who keeps the returns of a building off a third court came to me and told me that a figure he had certified had been printed against a certificate he had signed and that he had signed the certificate from memory with his own name on it and there was nothing behind it. I have got his name and I am going to say it. Hollis Prenn."

Somebody at the back said, "She is saying the names."

"I am saying the names," said Agnete Lindqvist, "because that is the entire use of this room. A thing said to a chaplain is true and goes nowhere. A thing said to a chaplain and written down is a document. I am about to make eleven of them documents by saying them in a place where they cannot be used against anybody, and I want the people who have come to watch to understand that that is a thing that is being done to them deliberately and not by accident."

"Second. The third of October of a year. A woman who sells at a stall on the south side of the Reading told me that her son scored nine and that she had signed the composition for a set of premises herself, and I did not understand what she meant for about forty minutes and then I did."

"Third. The second of December. A keeper of a counting room two floors under a sales floor told me that her fourth block for a quarter would be filled out of her own head on the last working day of the quarter because there was not a certificate for it, and she told me she was going to do it, and she did it."

That was the shape of the three hours. It was not a speech and it did not have a shape that anybody could have called a speech, because it was about sixty names and about a hundred and forty particulars, and it kept stopping.

People in the fourth row kept putting questions and she kept answering them in order and then going back to where she had been, which she was allowed to do and which almost no one else in the history of hearings has ever been allowed to do.

## What It Was Actually About

By about half past eleven she had been going for eighty minutes and the room had stopped being an audience.

It was about the city. That was the whole of it and it took three hours and there was no rhetoric in any of it and there was not one sentence in it that anybody could take out of that room and put in front of anybody else.

A man who had carried his mother up three floors at two in the morning and had told nobody, and whose name she gave, and which was in a ledger in a coat somewhere in this city on a turned line.

A woman who had told her that the score she was carrying was wrong by a number she would not say out loud to anybody with a stamp on them.

A boy of nineteen who had told her that he had felt something in the street in March and had been laughed at by four people and had stopped saying anything to anybody about anything.

An officer of a division who had told her, in eleven words in a corridor, that a category of thing in this city has neither a witness nor a stamp and no board in the world has ever had a place for it, and that this is why the office that counts it has no door.

Nobody in that room could use one word of it. Not the man who carried his mother, who was in the eleventh row and did not know she was going to say it. Not the woman with the stall. Not the boy from the tram, who was not there, and who would be told in a month by a man who did not have to.

## About Two O'Clock She Was Running Out Of Room

At about twenty to one she got to the end of what she had and stopped, which is not a thing anybody does at a hearing, and the officer looked at the clock, and she said, "There is more and I am going to keep going and the chair may stop me at any hour."

The chair said, "You may."

"So I will keep going," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I will tell you the last of it now and it is the only part of this morning that is about the matter you are all here about, and I have left it until last because it is the part I most wanted to be able to stand in."

The room went completely still, and about nine thousand people were walking under a wall four streets away and none of them was in this building.

"There is a thing in this city that thirty years of people have been putting into the same place without writing down one grain of it," she said. "There is no list of who put it there."

"There is no instrument, no witness, no deed and no floor under anybody who received it. It has been filling for thirty years and it has never been drawn on and it has never been paid, and there are people in this room who know every single thing about that except who it belongs to."

"I have not said whose it is. I have said what I know and I have said it in a room where it cannot be used against the people who told me the parts of it,"

"and I am going to go on sitting in this chair when the fourth day comes, and on the fourth day I am going to put a claim in on behalf of eleven hundred people out of eleven thousand, and every word of it is going to be built out of the three hours you have just sat through."

She sat down at about one o'clock, which is two hours and fifty minutes and not three hours, and the officer wrote the figure down and she corrected it to two hours and fifty and the officer entered the correction without asking why.

## The Thank That Was Refused

A man in the third row stood up at about ten past one when the room began to move and started to say something about a city that ought to have been told this thirty years ago, and got as far as the word *grateful*.

"No," said Lin Hao.

The room turned round. About three hundred and forty people who had been in a chair for three hours looking at a woman.

"Not him," he said, to the man. "Not like that. She is not going to be thanked for it and she told me four days earlier that if somebody thanked her she would correct them, and she is right and I am not going to let it be done in this room."

The man said, "You are not the man who ought to be stopping that."

"I am the man in this room who is going to be standing up on the thirtieth of July arguing against her," said Lin Hao, "and I have been thinking about it since about eleven o'clock this morning and I would like it to be clean from both ends."

Agnete Lindqvist turned round in her chair.

"Correct him again if he does it twice," she said, to the officer, and about eleven people in that room laughed, which was the first noise in three hours and which she did not appear to have expected or to have been counting.

## The Woman At The Back Of The Room

He found her afterwards on the second landing, where Beatrix Halm was sitting on the step of a stair with her notebook open on her knee and both hands flat on the page.

"You were in there for three hours."

"I was in there for two hours and fifty minutes and it was two hours and fifty minutes because a woman corrected it herself at the end." She had the notebook turned round. "It is in order. It is the first thing in four years that has gone in order that I did not choose the order of."

"How much of it could you feel."

"All of it," said Beatrix Halm. "A room with three hundred people in it going up in a curve from about eleven, and a hole in it about a hand wide that opened and shut twice and did not go deep, and about half past twelve it got shallower, and at about one o'clock it closed and she sat down. And a woman at the front who has been holding something open for about forty years and let it shut at one o'clock on purpose."

## The Corridor, And What He Worked Out In It

He worked it out on the first landing with his back against a wall at about ten past one in the afternoon and it took him about two minutes.

For three years he had been a man who had found out what was against him and had gone at it. A file that could not be produced, a clause that could not be struck, a woman who kept her hands in her lap, a finding that named a set of premises four times and eight people under a roof.

This was different. This was the first thing that had ever come at him from a side he could not walk round, and it was not coming at him: it was sitting in a chair, correcting the length of its own testimony, and every single word of it was true.

There was a thing in this city that thirty years of strangers had been putting into the same place with no instrument and no witness. She had said it in about fifty words in a room where it could not be used against anybody, and she had said it last, and she had told the room in advance what she was going to build out of three hours of people's private lives, and she had sat down.

He had had the whole of the twenty-seventh and the whole of the twenty-eighth to dismantle sixty names and a hundred and forty particulars and he had spent one hour of a day and a half on it and the only thing he had come up with was that a man who could not hold a thing had cost him a division and a woman who could had cost him a case.

She had told him four days earlier that in her room he would not be able to refuse. He had thought that meant he would have to sit and be examined.

It meant she was going to build something in public, out of true things, that he would have to answer standing up on the thirtieth of July, in front of three hundred people, with nothing in his hands that he had not walked in with.

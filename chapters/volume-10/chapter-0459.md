# The Thing Spent In His Chest

He had been on the step at the north end of the ranks wall for four days and on the fifteenth of July he was faster than he had been on the eleventh, and the reason was not courage.

A woman selling fruit on the south side had started keeping the place at seven in the morning for people who could not stand for an hour.

There was a queue by half past six. There were forty people waiting at nine o'clock who would not have waited at nine o'clock on the eleventh, and on the twelfth, and on the thirteenth, and on the fourteenth, and on the fifteenth, because a man who does that for four days in a city of nine thousand is not a rumour by the fifth morning.

He moved down the line faster on the fifteenth than he had moved down it on any of the other four days.

## The Woman Who Came And Sat Down

She brought a chair and a board and a pencil and a sheet of paper and she set them up on the second step at about seven in the morning and she told him her name and he told her what she already knew, and then she sat down in the chair facing him and started counting.

"Counting what."

"You," said Ada. "I am a counter. I have a chair and a sheet and I have nowhere else to be until six and you have told me what you are doing, and if I am going to hand somebody a figure on the ninth then I would rather the figure were about you."

She wrote the hour at the top of the sheet and the date and nothing else.

Then she said, "That is two seconds."

"Since when."

"Since your last four," said Ada. "They were two and a half and three in the morning and it has gone down all day and if it goes under a second then either you have got better at it or something is wrong, and I do not know which and I intend to find out."

## Nine O'Clock

It went under a second at about twenty past ten in the morning.

He got warm on the eleventh, a little, by about eleven. On the twelfth and the thirteenth and the fourteenth he got warm at about ten and was warm for about a quarter of an hour and then it went off him and he thought nothing of it, because he had been doing this for four years and he knew exactly what it costs and what it does not cost.

He was hot on the fifteenth by about a quarter past nine, in July, at the north end of a wall in a square with no sun on that side until two o'clock.

And he was not going slower.

## What Ada Said About It

She came down off the step and stood in the space between the queue and the step and said it to him at the only volume a city like this has, once, in front of about four hundred people, in the ordinary voice of a woman making an observation at a counter.

"You have gone past the person in front of you."

"I have."

"That one took about half of what the first one took on the eleventh and she was heavier than him and she did not need any of it." Ada had the sheet in her hand and she did not wave it about. "And there are forty people behind her and you are not waiting for any of them to finish."

"I am not."

"I am going to write down what I am looking at," said Ada, "and I want you to hear the sentence in the right order, which is: a person in front of you does not get what they are owed, they get what is left over, and what is left over is coming out of you, and you have known that since I told you on the seventh and you have been going faster ever since."

Somebody in the queue said "move along" and the queue moved along.

## The First One

He coughed at about half past eleven in the morning.

It was not a cough that started anywhere. It came out of the middle of him with nothing in front of it, and about four hundred people heard it and about nine hundred people were in the square by then, and no one in that square knew what it was.

Ada wrote down the hour.

He got up off the step and stood in the sun for about a minute with his hands at his sides and then he sat back down on it and put out his hand to the next person in the line, who was a man of about sixty with a bag of vegetables, and the man's hand was cold and he took it and got two seconds of it and gave the man back about a quarter of what the man had come for.

Then he coughed again.

## What He Told Her At The Second One

"You are going to go back to two and a half seconds," said Ada, from the chair, "and I would like to know that you have decided that on purpose."

"I have decided it."

"Say it to me as though I am the wall."

He said it to the wall.

"That is the third time somebody has said the true thing to me about this in four years," he said, "and two of them said it to me in a room and one of them is on a chair with a sheet of paper at seven in the morning, and I want you to understand that you have not said anything they did not say and I am going to do it anyway."

[Spending past the frame. It will not empty faster than a body can be finished.]

Ada read the wall. She read it the way she read a wall, which is that she read it and did not move her mouth.

"Then that is the rule," she said, "and you have known it since the fourteenth of March and you are on a step in July."

## Quarter Past Twelve

The third cough was at about a quarter past twelve and it went on for longer than the other two and the man in front of him had to hold him by the arm.

That one was different in the way that a thing is different when it is not going to stop. He got his breath in at the top and about a third of it went nowhere. He got it in again and about a third of it went nowhere, and the third time he got all of it and it felt like something being handed to him in pieces by somebody who was tired.

He sat down on the step and put his hands on his knees.

There was no pain. He had been hurt in this city and he knew what pain was like and it was not pain, and that was the thing he understood last and understood completely.

## The Word For It

Ada put the sheet on her knee and came down and sat on the step beside him with about a foot between them, and neither of them said anything for about two minutes, and the queue went on being a queue and about two hundred people who had not got to him yet went and stood by the wall and waited.

"That is not an injury," said Ada.

"No."

"You are not hurt."

"No."

"Then what is it."

"Something has been spent," he said.

She did not write that down. She looked at the sheet for a while with the pencil not moving, and then she said, "Say that in a different way, because I am a counter and if you say it in a different way I can put a figure on it."

"Your figure is the number of seconds between my hands and it was two seconds at ten o'clock this morning and it is not a number any more."

"That is the first figure I have been given by anybody in eleven years," said Ada, "and it is a bad one, and I am going to take it."

## The Thing He Had Miscalculated

"You have not emptied anything."

"I know."

"You have moved it," said Ada. "That is all you have done in five days and you have moved it out of about four thousand people and into a man on a step, and the account is exactly as full as it was on the eleventh of July, because a person can only be finished once and you went past four thousand people who had not finished, and what came out of them did not go anywhere. It came up your arm."

"I know."

"And you knew at about twenty past ten this morning, because I watched you work it out, and then you went faster." She put the sheet down on the step. "You have done the arithmetic wrong for five days and the error is in you, and there is not one rule in this city that can stop you, and I have looked, and I have said that out loud in a room with the door shut and I am saying it here."

## Twenty Past Two

He stood up at about twenty past two in the afternoon.

Getting up took two attempts. He got his hands off his knees and he got most of the way and then he stopped for about four seconds in the middle of it with his hand on the rail of the wall, and about nine hundred people in that square watched a man try to stand up, and none of them made a sound.

He got up.

"Go on then," said Ada, from the chair.

"You are telling me to go on."

"I am telling you what I see," said Ada, "which is a man who is going to go on, and I have been a counter for eleven years and a queue does not get told by me not to hand somebody their turn."

He went back to the step and put out his hand to the next person in the line, who was about nineteen and had come at seven in the morning and had been in the queue for five hours.

He took that hand and the cold went up through the boy's wrist and forearm and into a chest that had nothing left in it that afternoon. The boy's shoulders came down about an inch and he said thank you and went off up the square crying and did not know why.

## Half Past Five

He came off the step at about half past five and he stayed on it, and Ada came and got her chair and folded the board and did not fold the sheet, and there were about a hundred and forty people still standing at the north end of that wall who had been there since before noon and who did not leave.

"You spent for ten hours and eleven minutes," said Ada. "I have the figures. I counted from seven and I counted every one of them and I am not going to give them to you and I am not going to give them to anybody who asks."

"Give them to me."

"No."

"Ada."

"You will spend them," she said. "You will spend them on yourself the way you spent everything else you have ever had, and that is what the eight uses in that phrase you swore to in May are for, and I am not the instrument for that. Write the times down yourself tonight and put them in a book, and then you will have to read them, and I will not."

He coughed on the step and it went on for about six seconds.

## What He Bought And What It Closed

"You knew what it would cost," she said.

"I knew it would cost this."

"You knew it would cost a chest," said Ada, "because you have been down this road before, once, when you were a year or two into it, and you told me on the seventh what happens when you go past a person. You have not got another chest and you knew that on the eleventh, and you have been faster every day since the eleventh, and I want it written somewhere that the man knew."

She shouldered the board and picked up the chair with the other hand.

"There is one thing that has come out of five days on that step and I am going to say it because I am a counter and I say what the paper says and not what the person wants. You have not drained anything. You cannot drain it with a body. You have moved about four thousand people's share of it into yourself and you have spent part of what was already spent on you in April, and the account is where it was on the eleventh of July."

"That is all of it."

"And that is the whole of it, and you are going to be able to keep doing it until a chest stops, and then you are going to find out what is on the other side of that, and I do not know what is on the other side of that, and I have been counting for eleven years and I have never once had to count that."

She got to the bottom step and stopped.

"The cough is yours now," said Ada. "It is not the cold. The cold goes in a second and a half and it always has. That is going to be with you for the rest of whatever you have got, and you are going to be doing sums about it for the rest of that as well, and I will not be here for it and neither will anybody else in that square."

She went off up the square with a chair and a board under her arm, and there were about a hundred and forty people standing at the north end of the ranks wall in the light of a July afternoon, and about ninety strokes in somebody else's chalk were down the side of it with nothing behind them, and none of it was on a card.

Lin Hao sat on the step at the foot of the wall with his hands on his knees and the brass in his coat and could not get all of the air in.

He had gone and got up again.

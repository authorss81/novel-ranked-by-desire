# Four Hundred That Are Not His

The sheet came back from Ines Duarte on the fourth of January with a corner of it folded and Beatrix Halm's four hundred lines in her own hand on three pages and a line at the bottom that Ines had written and signed and dated, which said that a round writes a line when there is something on it, and that this one carried nothing except four hundred times.

"There is no figure on it anywhere and I have not made one," said Ines Duarte, "and I have held it in my head since the first of January and it is four hundred times and no two of them are the same and not one of them is a number."

"What is it then."

"It is proof that four hundred people stood still in a street for about four seconds in three months," said Ines Duarte, "and nothing else in this city is on paper about that, and it is not evidence of anything at all, because it is a woman's handwriting and a clock, and in about four years it will be a plate in a hall with a case round it and a person who will not know what it was for."

He kept it in the coat with the white plate.

## Two Hundred And Eighty, And Then

"The second of October was two hundred and twenty-two," said Beatrix Halm. "I have been counting on the wall every morning since the eighth, when it was two hundred and twenty-eight, and I have got the thirtieth of November at two hundred and eighty, the twelfth of December at two hundred and ninety-two, and the twenty-eighth of December at three hundred and eight."

"And today."

"One hundred and thirty at the end of June, and a hundred and ninety-four days after it, and it is the fourteenth of January," said Beatrix Halm. "That is three hundred and twenty-four, and it has gone up one a day since the eighth of October without a single person administering it."

"Nobody is administering it."

"Nobody is administering it and it has not been decided since the first of October, and the address has nothing on it to decide it with."

"You told four hundred people on the first of October that this is what a floor gone out from under a thing looks like. Here it is. Here you are. You are on the same side of it as about nine thousand people."

## The Fourteenth Of January

There is a public counter at Cordwain that opens at eight and closes at four and on the afternoon of the fourteenth of January a woman came in at about eleven with her hands empty and stood at the front of it.

The clerk was twenty-six and had been at that counter for about two years and had been refused about eleven thousand things and had never once had to be told why by anybody.

"I want to enter something," she said.

"I have got a sheet."

"It has not got a box for it."

"Most of them have not," said the clerk, "and I have got four boxes and two of them are for things that happen inside a building."

"It is a deed. It is a thing I did. No instrument on it and no witness, and there never was going to be."

"Then it cannot be entered," said the clerk, "and it is not going to be entered by me and it is not going to be entered anywhere in this city, and that is the same sentence I have said about four hundred times since the first of October and I am sorry and I am not going to soften it."

The woman did not go away.

"I read your wall," she said. "On the bottom of it. Two words and a dash and two words. I have read it every morning since October and it is the only thing I have got of anything and I have come down here on the strength of it."

## The Line At The Foot Of The Wall

He was nine feet back in the lane with a plate in his coat, because he had slept in the day and had come down the hill at eleven and had not meant to be at a counter in his life again.

The woman turned round and looked at him and then looked at the plate inside the coat where she could see the edge of it.

"You have got a plate."

"I have got a plate."

"Are you the one," she said. "Are you the one that emptied it."

"Yes."

The lane went on being a lane with trams and about nine people going through it and nobody stopped.

## What He Said

He came and stood at the counter, which is not what an auditor does and which nobody in the lane knows anything about, and the clerk said, "You are not at this counter."

"I am not," said Lin Hao. "I am going to say the same sentence you said and I am going to say it once and you are going to hear whether I say it the way you say it, and then you are going to carry on with your sheet."

Then he said it.

"It cannot be entered. Not here and not anywhere in this city."

"Nothing will put it on a form and nothing will print it in a column, and not one book at one counter is for a thing a person did, and there is not going to be one in your lifetime or mine. I have spent four months saying that sentence in about nine different rooms and I have got better at it than you are, and I am not going to do that to you. I am going to say it plainly."

"It is the same sentence."

"It is the same sentence and I heard you say it before I said it," said Lin Hao. "You did not have to be told by anybody. That is not a complaint and it is not praise. That is what a city is on the two days after it has decided something about a wall."

## The Second Line

"That line at the bottom," said the woman.

"What about it."

"It says unrecorded, carried by," she said. "That is me. That is exactly me. I did a thing in a doorway in a February and a woman I do not know was helped and neither of us will ever know about each other and it has been in me for eleven months. That line says it is carried by somebody."

"It says it is not carried by anybody."

"It says carried by."

"It says unrecorded and it says carried by and it does not say by whom," said Lin Hao, "and in about four hundred houses in this city a person who reads that line the way you have just read it, and every one of them will come down here in about four years and ask the same question, and I am telling you now what the answer is so that you can be angry about it before you find it out later."

"Go on."

"It is true," said Lin Hao. "That is the whole trouble with it. It is the truest thing on that wall, and it is the least useful thing on that wall, and it is about you, and it cannot be produced at a counter."

## Nine Seconds Over A Page

The woman stood there and then said: "Then what is the point of it."

"I am going to do one thing," he said, "and I am going to be honest with you about what it is, because it is about nine seconds long and I want you to know what I am doing before I do it."

"Go ahead."

He took the sheet out of his coat and put it on the counter and opened it and turned it round so that she could see the writing and not the paper.

"Four hundred lines," he said. "Three months. A time beside every one of them, in this hand, and no figure on any of it, because the woman who wrote it does not write figures."

"Is one of them me."

"I do not know," said Lin Hao. "I am going to look."

He stood at a public counter in Cordwain on the afternoon of the fourteenth of January and read three pages of four hundred clock times in a small level hand.

The times were in the morning and in the afternoon and at about two in the morning. Nine of them fell between ten past four and twenty past four in the afternoon on the second of October, four fell at about a quarter to eleven in the morning, one fell at ten past one, and one fell at nine in the evening.

"There," said Beatrix Halm's handwriting. "Four hundred and a page and a time."

"What have you found."

"Nothing," said Lin Hao. "I have found out that you stood somewhere for about four seconds in three months and that a woman who cannot hear a room was standing near you, and that is all that is on it, and I cannot find your line in it, and I have just spent about ninety seconds proving to myself that I cannot."

He shut it.

## The Question She Asked

"Then you do not know what it was for," said the woman.

"That is correct."

"Not even whether it worked."

"That is correct as well, and I am not going to say one word more about it, and here is exactly why." He put his hand flat on the counter. "There are two things that could be true about the four days of the first of October, and each of them is true in a different part of this city, and not one figure anywhere in Ostara can tell you which part you were in."

"Which two things."

"That it was justice," said Lin Hao, "and that it was a man showing off in front of nine thousand people."

"Nothing in this city separates those two, and I did it, and I am not going to stand in a lane and award you one of them because you walked down here this morning."

The woman picked her hands up off the counter.

"You are the first person in four months who has said that out loud to me," she said. "I want you to know that. Everybody else has told me what happened."

The clerk turned a sheet over.

And the woman went back up the lane with her hands empty and nothing entered, on the fourteenth of January, and he stood at a counter off a lane in Cordwain with four hundred times in a page and three hundred and twenty-four days on a date in his head and not one way of telling her what any of it had been for, and the second line stayed on the wall two streets away where it had been since the eighth of October, true, and about her, and useless.

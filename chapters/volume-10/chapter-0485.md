# The Card

The card came down at half past four in the afternoon on the thirtieth of September and Beatrix Halm was at the north end of the ranks wall with her notebook open on the stone.

She has read that wall alone in the dark before six every morning for nine years and there has never been anybody else under it at that hour but a boy with a brush, and she had been there since half past four.

He came up the hill at about a quarter to five and she did not turn round, because she had known since the beginning of the month which afternoon it would be, and she had been standing in that square since half past four.

"Two hundred and twenty," he said, behind her.

"Write it down and do not say it out loud," said Beatrix Halm. "A card is never read out loud. That is not a superstition, it is that the moment two people have said a figure out loud at this wall it is a thing that happened at this wall instead of a thing that was painted on it, and I have nine years of not doing that."

He wrote it in the margin of the notebook, small, and shut it, and stood at her shoulder.

## The Boy

The boy came at half past four with the paste brush and the list of numbers and a piece of chalk in his ear, and he put the top figure on first and then the column, and he worked down the wall and across and said not one word, and had not asked anything on any of the five cards before this one, and not one office in this city would have told him to and not one would have told him not to.

Nobody has ever told him. He is given a wall and a brush and a list and he is not told whose premises he is reading, and it is the same wall every quarter and he has painted the line at the top of it thirty or forty times and he has never once been told what the line means.

When he had finished the column he stood back about four feet to look at it and then he went along the wall to the far end and started on a plate that was not his business.

He was sixteen under a floor and did not know it, and the card said so in paint, in two figures, in a boy's hand, sixteen points below the line he had laid down first.

## The Two Figures, In Ink

It is the office's own hand on the office's own paper, and it carries 8,148 in figures along the head of it, and 8,132 in the column underneath, against premises 34-6-118.

Beatrix Halm read both of them before the paint was dry and wrote them down in her book in the small level hand she used for walls, and then she wrote the time against them, and then she sat down on the stone with her back against the rail and did not say one word for about two minutes.

He crouched down beside her.

She turned the notebook round and pushed it at him, and he read it, and neither of them said any of it out loud.

## What She Worked Out Without Saying

"It rises by exactly one at each card," said Beatrix Halm, in a voice with nothing in it at all. "Every quarter. It has risen by exactly one since before I started and it will go on rising by one until nobody in this city has a hand that can lay a line down."

"And the address."

"The address has gone up one as well," she said. "Which it has not done since the thirtieth of December, and I have got nine years of that column and I can tell you it has not moved once in nine years except when eight people went up fourteen on one day at the end of December, and this is not that. This is one grain, on a card, at half past four in the afternoon, from a boy who does not know."

"Sixteen under."

She wrote *sixteen under* in the margin in full words, and put a line under it, and turned the book back round so that he could see it, and neither of them said any part of it out loud.

"It is the same distance from the floor as ninety days ago," said Beatrix Halm. "The floor has gone up one and the plate has gone up one, and the net of it is that nothing has happened, and that is what ninety days of nothing looks like when somebody writes it in paint." She shut the book and put it under her arm. "You have not moved in ninety days and the wall has moved twice."

## The Woman Who Filled The Block

Hanneke Ruis came down off the second stair at about ten past five with a lamp in one hand and a book under her arm, and she came to the wall and looked at it and did not touch it.

"Seven times," she said.

"Seven times," said Beatrix Halm. "You wrote eight."

"I wrote eight," said Hanneke Ruis. "The certificate of that composition has not existed since the fourteenth of March, and I filled that block out of my own head at about half past four this afternoon in my own hand with my name at the foot of it."

"And I wrote eight, and it is the first figure I have put in that column in four years that was correct, and I would like the two of you to understand what I have just done and not to make a speech about it."

"How many people sleep under that roof," said Lin Hao.

"Eight," said Hanneke Ruis. "And the certificate that says seven is gone, and a name at the foot of the block says eight."

"In about four years the chain will come off that book and the old plate will be scraped off its backing and a new one cut from whatever is on it, with nothing added. It goes into a case in a hall where nobody stops walking."

"And after that nobody in this city will have any way of telling how many people slept at that address."

She put the lamp down on the stone.

"So I would like the two of you to be clear about the price of the seventh block," said Hanneke Ruis, "which is that I have made this address true and I have made it true on my word, and nothing whatever stands behind my word, and in four years that is either going to be the most correct figure in the book or a keeper's handwriting with nothing behind it, and one of the two it is, and not one living person will be able to tell you which."

## What The Boy Knows

They did not go back and ask him anything.

He was at the far end of the wall with the paste brush in his hand and a plate that was not anybody's business, and Beatrix Halm looked at him for about four seconds and then looked away, on purpose, the way she has looked away from about nine hundred people in nine years.

"He has asked nothing in five quarters," she said. "He will not ask this one either. He has got a wall and a brush and a list of numbers and about forty cards behind him and not one of them has ever had a name on it, and in about four years somebody in an office is going to find that out and write a report about it, and by then he will be at a different wall."

## What She Said At The Rail

They walked up the hill at about twenty past five and at the corner where the lane comes out she stopped and put her hand flat on the stone of the wall.

"Two things," said Beatrix Halm, "and I am not going to say them out loud and you are not going to ask me to."

"Go on."

"The first one is that the wall goes up tomorrow morning at half past four and there is not one figure in this city that will go on that card for this address except what it goes up by in the ordinary way, and you know what that is, because you have known what that is since the fifteenth of April."

He did not answer.

"The second one is that the first band in this city has no floor at all," said Beatrix Halm. "There is nothing to be out of band against. You have been sixteen under for ninety days and out of band by thirteen, and the card has said so in paint twice."

"And tomorrow afternoon you are going to be the first band, and when that happens there is no line at the top of the wall for you to be under, and the card will say so as well, in paint, in a boy's hand, and he will not know that he has said it."

She took her hand off the wall.

"Go and do the thing you have been getting ready for since the twelfth of July," said Beatrix Halm, "and do it standing up, and do not put your name on anything you can carry in your own hand."

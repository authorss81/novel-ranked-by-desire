# The Condition On The Table

The chair of the Concil of the Nine has a room two floors above the room at the end of the corridor and a woman had gone up the outside stair ahead of him on the morning of the third of July and had not waited, and the door was open when he got to the landing because she does not shut a door for a member.

There was one table, two chairs, a window with the blind half down, and a sheet of paper face up on the table with nine lines on it in one hand.

"Sit down," said Anouk Ferreira. "I am not going to stand up and I am not going to have this in the room at the end of the corridor, and you will work out why in about four minutes and then you will thank me for it, and you are not going to, and that is fine."

## The Fifth Line

She put her finger on the paper.

"The standing sheet. Nine lines and the room stands at five and I write it at the end of every quarter, and I have written this one at four in the afternoon."

Her finger did not move. "The fourth line is a man who was nobody for two months and a rank for three. The fifth line is yours. Your standing entered in a keeping book on the fifteenth of April at ten past nine and not entered out on any day, and there is no instrument in this city that can take it out of that book."

"I know what it is."

"You know what it is and nobody else in this building has ever said it to you, and I have had it in front of my eye for ninety days, and it is the reason you are sitting in a chair." She took her hand off the paper. "I want you to know that first, because everything else I am going to say is ordinary and this is not, and I am not a woman who gives things away for free."

## Four Things And A Condition

She said it in order, once, and she did not write anything down while she said it, and there was nothing on the table but the standing sheet.

"A standing at the Terrace, entered in the return at the first of the quarter, with your name on it in my hand and mine under it."

"A chair, when one turns, and a chair of the Council does not turn very often and I have not said that this one is going to turn and I am not going to tell you which one."

"A name that outlives you. There is a wall in this city with nine ranks on it and the ninth of them has been empty in living memory and the name of a man on it is read every morning by a woman with a notebook and she will read it long after you are not here to be troubled by it."

"The Ninth Seal, yours to use, for the good of the city, and I will put my own hand to the finding that says so and I do not say that lightly and I have not said it to anybody else in four years."

"That is four things and they are everything you came into this city wanting at fifteen, and I have had them priced for you by two people who do not like me and one of them came back with the same number."

"On one condition."

## The Condition

"The reservoir is classified once, cleanly, by Alma Quayle."

She said the name and she did not look at the window.

"Not administered. Not registered. Not read at a valuation. Classified, in a room with a form on it, with a finding, with a holder, and the holder is Alma Quayle, and after that it is yours to spend in whatever direction you liked and there is not one person in this city who could ask you twice."

"That woman has been recused since December."

"On her own signature, on the twelfth of December, in her own hand, and it has been in a drawer in this building for seven months and she has sat in the chair that counts for nothing at the end of that table for every day since, hearing everything and sealing nothing and taking no part in any of it."

"And you have had seven months to think of a way round it and you have not found one."

"I have found one," said Anouk Ferreira. "It is one sentence and it is eleven words long and you are the only man alive who is entitled to say it, and there is no sheet for it and nothing is required and nobody has to be asked and nobody will thank you for it."

## What He Did Not Say

He had the sentence in him. It had been in him since the thirteenth of May, when he had said a different sentence out loud in front of a clerk and been right to, and a recusal is lifted by the subject and by nobody else and the subject was a man standing in the room on the twenty-first of June being told by a woman who prices things that he was a doorway.

He did not say it.

He did not say it because the sentence would put Alma Quayle back into a room where she counted for something on the first of October, and because in about four seconds he understood why the whole offer had been built the way it was, and it was not built round the recusal.

It was built round the consignment.

"Tell me the rest of it," he said.

## Why A Classification Ends His Standing

"Classification is a holder," said Anouk Ferreira. "That is the entire mechanism and it has been the entire mechanism since before I came into this room. An account with a holder is an account with an owner, and an owner is a person who can be administered against, and a person who can be administered against is a person inside this system instead of a hole in the side of it."

She turned the standing sheet a quarter of an inch.

"You put the whole of your standing into a keeping book at a public counter at ten minutes to nine on the morning of the fifteenth of April, in your own hand, in ninety seconds, with no instrument and no witness and no copy, and that is the only reason there is not one paper in this city that can be used against you on the question of what you are."

"And the moment Alma Quayle puts a classification on that account, the account has an owner."

"The account has an owner, and the owner is a man whose standing is a consignment in a book in this building, and the consignment is the instrument, and the classification says the instrument answers to a holder."

She put both hands flat on the table. "You would be the first man in Ostara with a lawful account and no standing to defend it, and there are people who have wanted you administered against since the seventeenth of March and every one of them is waiting for exactly that, and I am not one of them and I have read their files."

She let that sit.

"That is why it is Alma Quayle and not a clerk and not a keeper and not me," she said. "She has spent thirty years arranging to be the person who can finally say what a thing is. If she says it about that account then what she says is true, and she has never said a false thing in this building in my time."

## She Is Right

"Now the part where I am right," said Ferreira, "because you are doing it now and I would rather do it first than have it done to me."

"You have been spending it since the twenty-third of April."

He did not say anything.

"You have done it four doors at a time for ten weeks on a round nobody is paid to walk, and there is no instrument in this city that can call that what it is either."

She said it without heat. "And on the eleventh of July you are going to do it at the head of the Reading under a wall with a black tile at the top of it, and nine thousand people are going to watch a man take strangers by the hand all day, and there is not one office in Ostara that can put a stop to it, and I am not going to send anybody to stand near you."

"A man draining his own account has nothing left to requisition, and this room would rather have an empty account than a classified one. I said that on the third of July and I am not going to take it back in October."

"You have known since the twenty-third of April."

"I have known since the twenty-second, and a woman in a division of audit worked it out in ten days after that and filed something on it, and I know about the something."

## Thirty Years Of Strangers

"There is one more thing and then you can go and not come back to this room until I call you."

"Tell me."

"It has been filling for thirty years and it has never been drawn on and it has not been paid and it cannot be closed, because the only thing that closes one is payment, and the books that would take it met at seven on the morning of the sixteenth of November and have not moved since."

"Who put it in."

"Strangers," said Anouk Ferreira. "People who did something in a doorway where there was no witness and no instrument and nobody looking."

"It came in a grain and a half at a time for thirty years from people who have never met each other and have never met you, and every grain of it is unrecorded merit, and it is addressed to the only person in Ostara that this city does not audit. You have known that since the fourteenth of March and I have read the file that says so."

"It is not a destiny. It is a job description. You are a man nobody looks at, and that is the whole reason it has an address, and in four years a stranger with a folder is going to write that sentence in a report and be astonished by it."

"And whose is it," he said.

"That is not the question and you know it is not the question, and you asked it in the wrong order because you have been asking questions in the wrong order for four years."

## The Thing She Did Not Say

She stood up then, which meant the meeting was over, and she did not offer him her hand and she did not thank him for coming.

"Take it in writing if you want it in writing," she said. "I will have it drafted this afternoon and it will be on this table on the eighth of July and you may have it and keep it and show it to whoever you like."

"Why would I want that."

"Because everything else in your life is on paper and you have spent four years learning what paper does," said Anouk Ferreira.

"And because you have a clause of your own that puts you in a corridor for the rest of your life over a document. The only reason that clause does not make you miserable is that there has not been a document about you worth making you miserable, and there has not been one since the nineteenth of June because you said four words in a corridor and got that out of the way."

She picked the standing sheet up off the table.

"So: no paper, and no copy, and I do not repeat myself, and if you want to price it you will have to take it to somebody who prices things and you will have to say out loud where you got it, and I would like to see that."

## The Stair

He went down the outside stair with nothing in his hands at about twenty past eleven in the morning, and he had been in that room eleven minutes.

Four things and one condition, and the condition was eleven words long, and he was the only man in Ostara who could say them, and he had come into this city at fifteen wanting precisely the four things, and she had worked that out from a file rather than from a conversation.

There was no third door. There was the sentence and there was the account, and she had put them in the same room and not once pretended they were the same size.

At the bottom of the stair he stopped, because a clerk of that room was coming up it with a bundle under her arm and she had not expected him to be there.

"One hundred and thirty-three days since the twentieth of February," she said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and three."

"And you have been in a chair on the second floor for eleven minutes," said the clerk, "and I can tell you what came out of that room by the look on a man, and I have looked at a great many of them." She shifted the bundle. "And I am not going to ask you, because you are not going to tell me, and if you did tell me I could not put it anywhere."

That was true, and it was the first thing anybody in that building had said to him in four years that was a kindness with a door shut on purpose behind it.

He went out into the court and across it and down a street with a piece of brass in his coat and eleven words he had not said sitting in him like a swallowed stone, and there was no version of the next four days in which he was going to say them.

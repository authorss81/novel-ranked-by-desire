# A Grey That Stays Where It Came

The counting room at the back of the Bellhouse is below the stage and it has no window and two tables and a rail and a lamp with a green shade, and from about two in the afternoon the stage above it is empty and the room belongs to the counters, and there are four of them and one of them has been at the third window of that building for nine months.

On the afternoon of the thirteenth of July he came in at about four o'clock because a wall is only good for so long.

Nine thousand people had worked out what he was by the second day and had started coming to the north end at seven in the morning instead of nine, and by the thirteenth there was a woman on the south side selling fruit who kept the place for people who could not stand for an hour, and none of that was a queue any more. It was a thing with hours.

So he spent in rooms. That was the difference on the thirteenth and he did not notice he had made it until the middle of the afternoon.

## The Third Of The Three

Ada was counting when he came in.

She had the four columns on the table and a time beside the third one and a line under the week, and she had a second sheet under that one with the weekly figure for the address of a house at Ferrier Street in her own hand since the twenty-eighth of September, and she was going along it with a pencil and she did not look up, because a counter does not look up.

He put his hand flat on the table beside hers and she said, without turning round, "You are not on the round."

"I am going to be on it in about four minutes."

"You are going to be on it in about four minutes and I have got to the twenty-ninth of the week and you are going to make me start again," said Ada.

He took her hand.

It was a hand she had kept behind a counter for nine months and in and out of cold water at every wash-up, and it did not feel like anything for a second, and then it felt like a hand that had been in a bucket.

Then the cold came up the inside of her arm and stopped about four inches below the shoulder and sat there, and Ada put her pencil down, which she has never done in the middle of a count in eleven years.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not faint and she did not collapse and she did not put a hand on the table.

She sat where she was with her hand turned over in his, and she looked up at him, and then she said, "That is different."

"Is it."

"That is not where it goes," said Ada. "It has never gone past my wrist before. I have had two of them and the other two were in the hand and they were over in about a second and a half and I thought I had been cold."

She let go of his hand and picked the pencil up again and put the point on the twenty-ninth of the week.

Then she stopped.

## The Grey

The other woman at the far table said it first, and she said it in the ordinary way a woman says a thing about somebody else's face.

"Ada."

"What."

"You have got something on the side of your head."

Ada put her hand up over her ear and then took it away, because there is no glass in that room and there has never been a mirror in that building.

"What is it," she said.

"It is grey," said the other woman. "On one side. Above the ear. About the width of a hand."

"That is the light."

"That is not the light."

He was standing four feet away with his hands at his sides.

There is one grey in this city and it is his, and it has been at the same place for as long as anybody in Ostara has been alive, and it is the only mark on him that has ever moved, and it moved once and stopped.

Nothing about a cold minute in this city has ever left anything on anybody. It lasts a second and a half and a person thinks they were cold, and in eleven years not one person has come into that room with a mark, because there has never been a mark to come in with.

## She Went On Counting

She counted to the end of the week.

"There," she said, and turned the sheet round and pushed it at him. "That is the week and that is the figure and that is what I am required to hand up, and I have got forty weeks of this address in a stack behind me and I am going to go back through them, because that is what you do when a number does not feel right."

She went back through them.

She had forty weeks. She has counted that address every week since the twenty-eighth of September at the third window of the Bellhouse because an officer of counter-forensics asked her to in nine words in a lane off Cordwain on a working afternoon, and she had never once told the woman whose house it was.

She got back four weeks and stopped.

"Sit down," said the other woman.

"Sit down yourself," said Ada.

She put her finger on a line and then took it off it and put it on the one above.

"That is the fourth," she said. "And that is the third. And there is a second."

"There is a second."

"There are three weeks on this stack that I counted with my own hand at this table and I cannot find them in my head," said Ada, "and I have been counting for eleven years and I can tell you what I had for lunch on the ninth of May, and a counter does not lose three weeks."

"You have been tired," said the other woman.

"I have not been tired," said Ada. "I am the best counter in this building and I have said so to two chairmen and I have been right both times. And I am not going to stand here and be told I was tired by a woman who works on the third window and cannot count to four without a column."

## The Work It Out Faster

It took her about four minutes and he did not say one word in it, and the rule on the wall of a different room in a different building, about the one reading being permitted to stop and the subject not being permitted to, was not in this room and was not in her head, and she worked it out anyway because counting is her whole job and a counter who cannot find three weeks does not get sad about it, she gets a rule.

"You have been doing that at the wall since the eleventh," she said.

"Yes."

"And at four doors on a round, before that."

"Yes."

"And in a queue in April," said Ada, "and my office of counter-forensics was lent a clerk who worked a post off a third court that day and I know that because she was in my office in the afternoon and she had a cold hand and could not hold a pen, and I did not think anything of it at the time."

She turned round on the chair.

"That is what it is. You put it into people and they get cold, and it comes out of them after, and if there is enough of it in one person then something stays."

"There is not enough in one person."

"There is enough in me," said Ada. "I have had two since October and the third one is about four minutes old and it went past my wrist, and I have got a grey on the side of my head that was not there at four o'clock, and I am the one person in this building who has ever written down the time of anything that happened to somebody else, and I am going to write this one down as well."

## The Line On A Round

He took the book out of the inside pocket of his coat then, the flat one with the pencil elastic round it, and he did not have to look far for the page.

Eleven of the lines in that book were turned, which was one more than there had been on the thirtieth of June. The first of the eleven was himself and has been since early in January. The next one after that is a man who works a lane off Ferrier Street and put an old woman up three floors in the small hours of the ninth of April because carrying her was quicker than fetching anybody to help.

He wrote a line. Then he put the pencil down and turned the book round on the table and pushed it the way people push things at counters.

She read it. She read it twice, the way she reads everything, and then she took her own pencil and put a figure in the margin against it, and the figure was not a plate figure and was not anything the Board has ever printed.

"How many times," said Ada.

"You do not need a figure for that."

"I need a figure for everything," she said. "I have never in eleven years written a thing down without one, and I am not starting on the one week I have got something on my head for. How many times have you done it."

He told her.

She wrote it down and put the pencil in the pencil hole in the table, which every counter at the Bellhouse has had since the building was put up, and then she said, "Right," and went back to the twenty-ninth of the week and counted to the end of it.

## Six O'Clock

The lamp came on at about six o'clock because it is July and there is not enough light in that room even in June, and the three of them packed the sheets into a stack and Ada signed the top one in the ordinary way.

"Does it go," said the other woman. "Ada. Does it go away."

"No," said Ada.

"You do not know that."

"I have been counting things for eleven years and I have never once guessed," said Ada, "and I am telling you it does not go, and I would like you both to get off my chair so that I can lock this room, because it is my room and I lock it at six."

He got off the chair.

At the stair he said, "I will come back."

"No," said Ada. "You will come back, and I will be here, and I will lock the room at six either way, and you will put your hand on this table about four times a day until the middle of September because you have told me what you are doing and nobody has told you that it is wrong yet."

She came out behind him and stopped on the second step.

"And the strokes on the wall," she said. "That was you, that was not you, and I do not know which, and it is on about ninety marks in somebody else's chalk with nothing behind it, and there are three weeks missing out of forty and I am going to have to hand somebody a figure on the ninth that I cannot get."

"You can get it. It is on the paper."

"It is on the paper," said Ada, "and I am going to write the paper's figure and I am not going to write what I remember, because in eleven years the paper has never once been wrong and I have twice."

Then she went back in and shut the door of the counting room at the back of the Bellhouse, and there was no sound of anybody in it after that, and she was sitting in it at half past six with her hand over the side of her head and a pencil in the other one, writing down the times of things that happened to a man.

# Thirty Years Of One Handwriting

The counting room was two floors under a sales floor and it had four tables in it and a lamp on a stand and a strap on the fifth book, and Hanneke Ruis was in it at about half past four in the afternoon of the thirtieth of October with the fifth book open in front of her and a sheet of foolscap beside it that she had not written on.

She had not been asked to have the lamp on and she had it on because the light from the sales floor above does not reach a table on the far side of a room at four in the afternoon in the last week of a month.

"Sit down if you are going to stand there," she said. "The chair is a chair, and I am not going to pretend I did not get that from a woman at the Ninth Terrace who has been saying it since the twelfth of December."

"It is four in the afternoon."

"Then it is no different than that either," said Hanneke Ruis.

## One Hundred And Twenty

"The books that would take a thing like that do not sit again until seven on the morning of the sixteenth of November," she said. "You were told that on the fifth of October by a man standing in a lane and you have been carrying the sentence for twenty-five days and it has not improved."

"It has not."

"It is correct and it is going to remain correct, and I am going to tell you why in about four minutes and then you are going to go away and let me do the thirtieth of December."

She turned the fifth book a quarter of an inch so that he could see the page and did not offer him the book.

"Four times a year. The thirtieth of March, the thirtieth of June, the thirtieth of September, the thirtieth of December. Thirty years. Four and thirty is one hundred and twenty."

"That is a count of handwritings."

"It is a count of handwritings," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is not a figure against anything, it is not a balance, it cannot be added up, and there is nowhere in this city to put it. It is the number of times one woman has written six words in a real book in her own hand under her own name, and one hundred and twenty is the whole of what there is to say about it."

"Six words."

"*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*" She put her finger on the line. "Six, with the marks. Four times a year since before I took these keys, and every one of them in this hand, and I know it is this hand, because I have been a keeper for nineteen years and I have never seen that hand until I started looking at it in the summer."

## The Office That Certifies It

"And the certificate behind each of them."

"Is mine," said Hanneke Ruis. "All one hundred and twenty of them. Written in my own division, in my own hand, under my own name, certifying the composition of an account that no instrument in this city can classify, close, or hold, and not one of those certificates has ever been asked for by anybody in thirty years."

She squared the sheet of foolscap to the edge of the table.

"You want the shape of that in one piece and you are going to get it in one piece and I am not going to enjoy it. For thirty years I have been the creditor and the debtor and the clerk and the certifier of one thing in this city. Not one person has ever checked me and not one person has ever needed to, and the reason not one of them needed to is that there was nothing in the book that anybody wanted."

She put her thumb on the page.

"That was a defect. I have written it up twice since the first of July and both times I wrote it up as a defect, because a keeper who certifies her own figure is the exact thing this floor exists to stop, and I am the one who does it, and I have been doing it since the fourteenth of March and in four hundred years of that book before that."

## What Is Left Standing

"Since the first of October it is not a defect."

"No," said Hanneke Ruis. "Since the first of October it is the only thing in this city standing up."

She looked up, which she does perhaps four times in a year.

"Think about what happened to that account. Thirty years of it, carried forward, never drawn on, never paid, and on the first of October of this year it went out into a whole city in the open in four days, and the books that would have taken it sit once in the year and not twice, on the sixteenth of November, and nothing anybody does in a lane between now and then is going to be in the room when they do."

"And when they sit on the sixteenth of November there is not going to be one entry in any book in Ostara that says that account is empty," said Hanneke Ruis. "There is not going to be one. There is a line of handwriting four times a year in a chained book in a room two floors under a sales floor, and it says *carried forward*, and that line is the only thing in this city that stands between that account and a description of a thing that never existed."

"And the man who emptied it."

"The man who emptied it is the only person in this city who could tell you what went out of it, and he has no figure, and he has said in public that he cannot tell you and neither can anybody else."

She turned the fifth book back square again.

"So on the thirtieth of December a woman two floors under a sales floor is going to write, in her own hand, under her own name, that the account is carried forward and unclosed and has no holder."

"And that is going to be true, and it is going to be the truth about a thing that four hundred people in a yard and nine thousand people in a square have already decided means something else."

## The Woman Who Is Not Asked

Sun Meiling was at the far table with her coat still on and a docket folder under her arm where it has sat since the first of January, and she had come down at about four and had not sat down.

She is the officer of the composition and the only person in this city who could certify a household draw, and she is not on the roll of the house at Ferrier Street and cannot be sworn to it, and about two months ago a woman in this room said out loud that not one of them was going to ask her a single question.

Not one of them asked her one.

She read the page over Hanneke Ruis's shoulder for about a minute and a half and did not touch it and did not say anything, and Hanneke Ruis did not turn round, and he stood on the other side of the room and did not go over, and it was one of the three things that happened in that room that day and only one of them was in words.

At about twenty past four she put her folder under her arm and went up, and Hanneke Ruis said, to the middle of her own table, "She comes down twice a quarter and she has never once been asked a thing, and I have now been not asking her for about nine years, and I do not know whose rule it is and I am not going to find out."

## The Eighth Block

"The thirtieth of December."

"Half past four in the afternoon, as always, and the certificate behind it will not exist, and the seventh block that I filled on the thirtieth of September said eight and was right."

"Eight."

"Eight people sleep under that roof and the certificate has not existed since the fourteenth of March, and the plate at the end of the chain will have eight in it and the plate behind the eight will have nothing at all in it, and I have been asked twice by two clerks whether I meant eight and I have told both of them that I meant eight."

She put the foolscap into a drawer and shut it.

"You will want me to tell you what happens to that plate in about four years and I am going to tell you, and then this conversation is finished."

## The Plate That Gets Scraped

"About four years from now the chain comes off the fifth book. That is the ordinary cycle, it is not a decision, and it is nobody's fault. The old plate comes off its backing and it is scraped, and a new plate is cut from whatever was on the old one with nothing added, and it goes into a case in a hall where the walking never stops."

"So the eight goes forward and the nothing behind it does not go forward at all."

"The eight goes forward," said Hanneke Ruis. "Because a new plate is cut from the old one and not from the certificate behind it."

"The eight will be in that hall in four years in cut metal, and not one document anywhere in this city will say where the eight came from. A stranger with a folder will read it and see a correct figure about a house in this city, and he will have no way of telling that it was a woman's memory on a night in September."

She took her hand off the drawer.

"That is the price of the seventh block and I paid it on the thirtieth of September and I did not know I was paying it, and I have had two months to decide whether I would do it again, and I would like it understood that I did it again on the thirtieth of December, out loud, in the same hand, with my name at the foot of it."

## The Only Thing Standing

"So the rule is a defect," he said, "and you have written it up twice."

"It is a defect," said Hanneke Ruis. "It has been a defect since before I came, and I am going to keep writing it up, and on the thirtieth of December it is going to be the only provision in this city by which anybody can say what anything is, and I am not going to be sentimental about that and neither are you."

She turned the lamp off and then turned it on again because the room needed it.

"Thirty years, one hundred and twenty lines, six words each, in one hand," she said. "And in four years it will be a number in metal in a hall and no words at all, and the woman will be dead or retired or gone to the Vestry and not one living person will be able to stand in front of that plate and say where it came from."

That is the whole of what thirty years of one handwriting is worth on the thirtieth of October: a keeper in a room two floors under a sales floor, doing the same six words for the hundred and twenty-first time on the thirtieth of December, in a lamp, alone, and not asking a woman in a coat anything at all.

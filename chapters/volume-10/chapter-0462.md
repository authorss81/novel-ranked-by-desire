# Eleven Thousand In A Vestry

The Vestry chapel of the Church of the Quiet Number is behind a yard off Cordwain and it holds two hundred and it was built for a congregation that has spent eleven years being told what it is, and on the morning of the twenty-first of July there were nine people in it and a table with a book on it.

A Church is not a person. The Correction Office had sent for a human being on the nineteenth and had been sent, by the twenty-first, to a yard behind a wall in Cordwain.

She was standing at the head of the long table when he came in and she did not come out from behind it.

"Sit down," said Agnete Lindqvist. "There is no other chair in this room and I am not giving you the one behind me."

## The Book On The Table

It was a bound book, about two inches thick, ruled in two columns, and it was open at a page with a number written at the top of it in her own hand, and above the number, on the fly-leaf, there were two lines.

The first line said the name of the Church.

The second line said her name.

Not the household. Not the nine sworn women of a standing household recognised at a terrace in April and entered against a figure on a roll. Not the name of a man. Her name, alone, in the second box of a fly-leaf, and under it a line ruled for a witness that had one name on it, and the one name was hers as well.

"You have put your own name on it," he said.

"I have put my own name on it," said Agnete Lindqvist, "because I am the only person in this city who can sign this book and be speaking for herself at the same time."

## One In Ten, By Lot, In The Order Of The Roll

"The congregation is eleven thousand," she said. "It has been between ten and a half and eleven thousand for nine years and it is on a roll that is kept by two wardens and it has not been re-ordered since the fourteenth of February."

"How many are coming."

"Eleven hundred," said Agnete Lindqvist. "One in ten, taken by lot, in the order they stand on the roll. Not chosen. Chosen is what a board does and I have not asked this board to choose anything in twenty years. So it is the roll and the lot and the number comes out of the arithmetic."

"That is a very large room to want."

"It is a very large thing to be in a room about," she said. "Two hundred is what this room holds and I have asked the Correction Office about the other room and I am told there is one that holds nine hundred, and I have also been told, by a woman at a counter in a court, that there is no hearing room in this city that can hold nine hundred people and that I should raise it as a difficulty rather than as a wish."

## The Board Of Nine

Nine chairs at the side of the chapel with nine names on a sheet of paper, and none of the nine had been in the room.

"They do not choose her," said the woman who had come to the door with him, a warden with a Church key on a string and grey at the edge of her hair. "They have not chosen her since she took it. She has never once asked that board for anything and I have been in this yard for nineteen years and I can tell you that as an observation and not as a complaint."

"What has she asked it for."

"She asked it on the twentieth of July for eleven hundred and a room and two days' notice of a matter it had never been asked about before," said the warden. "It gave them to her in about nine minutes and she thanked all nine of them individually, which none of us expected, and then she went upstairs and did not come down for two hours."

## What Eleven Thousand People Think Is Being Discussed

"You have told them what the matter is."

"I have told them what the notice says, which is a matter carried on the thirtieth of June under five seals, and I have told them that the subject is a man and that the matter concerns something the city has been putting into the same place for thirty years without writing any of it down."

"Who wrote that."

"I did, on the evening of the nineteenth, in about ninety words, and the warden had it read at a meeting the same night."

"The congregation was told that the notice names no person and that a Church that turns up with a name it has made up is a Church that will not be listened to." She closed the book and squared it. "They have had two days with that. It is not a position yet. It is the truth about a notice, and it is what I have."

"And when it is a position."

"Then it will be an argument," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and it will be made by me and not by a warden, and it will be about a word."

## The Word

She said it once and she did not say it twice, and he did not ask her to, and it went into the air of a chapel that holds two hundred and had nine people in it.

"That is the whole of our position and I am giving it to you now, four days before it is made in a room, because I would rather you had it from a woman sitting down than from a record you read afterwards."

"The correct disposition is distribution and not return."

"The correct disposition is that it be given out," said Agnete Lindqvist. "To people who are below a floor and have no instrument and no witness and no deed."

"That is what this Church has been doing for thirty years with nothing but a room and a roll, and we have never once pretended that a person who came in one week and not the next was owed anything, because there is no list of who is owed, and there has not been one since before I took this, and there will not be one in October either."

She put her hands flat on the book.

"You will tell me I am talking about an account with a number in it and I am not," she said. "There is no number. That is the entire difference between this and every scheme that has ever been proposed for returning things to people in this city, and it is the difference between giving and owing."

## What He Said, And What It Cost Him

He had come into that yard with one instrument and it was the only one he had and it was four years old, and he had come to find out what the Church wanted, and he had got what he came for, and there was no version of the next ten minutes in which a man with a brass in his coat could make any part of it worse for himself by arguing.

"You are asking me to hand a thing I have carried for four years to a board of nine people who have not chosen the woman who is asking."

"I am asking you to hand it to eleven hundred people out of eleven thousand, taken by lot, in the order of a roll, and to the two hundred who cannot get a seat in a room that holds two hundred."

"And you are asking me to agree that it was never mine."

"I am asking you to say out loud, once, in a room, whether it was ever yours," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and to say it in words anybody can repeat, and I have not been asked to be thanked for it and I will not be thanked for it either, and if somebody thanks me I will correct them."

## The Yard Afterwards

He was let out into the yard behind the chapel and the warden walked with him to the gate because it is the rule of that yard, and they went in silence for about half of the way up it.

"You have not asked me a question in twenty minutes," said the warden. "People come into this yard and ask me what the Church thinks and then they go up to the Vestry and hear it and then they go home and put it in a letter. You have come twice in six days and both times you have gone out looking like a man who has been given something."

"I have been given an argument," he said. "That is not the same and it is worse."

"No," said the warden. "It is better."

"An argument you can go home and write a letter about is not worth the paper it is printed on. An argument that has to be answered in a room on the thirtieth of July is the only kind that is, and we have had eleven hundred people agree to put their names in a book this week and not one of them asked to be thanked for it, which is the first time in nineteen years that has happened in this yard and I have not told the board."

At the gate she stopped and turned round.

"There is one thing you will not have thought about and it is the thing I would think about," she said. "One in ten by lot in the order of the roll means the two hundredth name on that roll is a woman who came to a meeting in the third week of March and has not been to one since."

"She is coming. She does not know what it is about and nobody has told her, because the book has no column for that and the rule is the rule."

"Then somebody has to tell her."

"Somebody has to tell her, and she is not going to be reached by a notice on a wall," said the warden, "and if that building only holds nine hundred then two hundred of our eleven thousand will be standing in a yard outside it, and both of those things were true before you arrived this morning and you did not make either of them."

She looked at him and went back in and left the gate on the latch, which is how that yard is left.

## One Hundred And Fifty-One

He walked back up the yard at about half past one in the afternoon with the sun on the left side of the yard wall and one hundred and fifty-one days in his head, because ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty, and one hundred and thirty with twenty-one is one hundred and fifty-one.

He had come into that yard with a question and he went out of it with the answer, and the answer was that the Church of the Quiet Number was not hostile and had never been hostile and had a position with a better disposition attached to it, and he had not yet in his life had to argue against a set of people he respected.

That was new. For four years every opposition he had met had been a person who wanted something off him, and he had been able to work out in about four minutes what a thing was worth and then let it go, and he had built the whole of the last four years on that.

And there was a woman behind a wall in Cordwain who had gone to a room she had not left in eleven years and had asked a board that had never chosen her for a thing she had never asked it for, and there was nothing in his coat he could have priced her at.

At the corner of Cordwain he stopped and worked out the number of days to the end of the month, because thirty taken from twenty-one leaves nine, and then he worked out what a clerk was, and that a clerk could not be asked to hurry and could not refuse an entry, and that the days of that hearing had not been set and would be set by somebody who could not be argued with.

Nine days, and then somebody else's hand on the calendar.

# The Family That Lost Its File

The Ninth Terrace intake hall had been ordinary for eighteen days by the twenty-fourth of October, and on the morning of the twenty-fourth there was one person in the corridor at about a quarter past eleven with a folded paper in his hand who had not come about a cold minute.

"Queue or desk," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

"Desk."

"Then take the chair, because you are going to be at that counter for about a quarter of an hour and there is nowhere in this hall to put a person like that is standing."

## What He Had Come About

He was a carter and he had come on foot from Lamplit Row and he had a piece of paper with a number on it that had been copied out of an index at another desk four days before, and he put it on the counter edge and turned it round.

"That is the file," he said. "It is our file. It has been ours since my father and it is in the hall off Lamplit Row and the index says it is there and it is not there."

"How long have you looked."

"Nine days," said the carter. "We looked at eleven days. We went on two of them. Somebody in a division told us that files are moved when there is a great deal of interest in them, and then he stopped talking."

He had a wife and two children at the end of the row and he had walked down here instead of asking at four counters nearer home, and he had walked down here because somebody had told him that the woman at this desk was the one who never gave an answer that was not true.

"You have been told that about me."

"People say it."

"It is not true," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "It is true that I will not give you an answer that is not true. Those are different and the difference has cost this desk about two hundred people since the first of October, most of them because they wanted the other one."

## The Index Is Right

She took the piece of paper and read it and put it down and squared it to the edge of the counter with one finger, the way she does with the day's sheet.

"The index says a file of that number exists and it was last opened at a valuation four years ago," she said. "The index is not wrong. The index has never once been wrong in nineteen years of this desk and I have looked at about nine hundred of them wrong."

"Then it is in the hall."

"It is in the index," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "Where the case is, is a different thing, and I cannot go and look, because a person at this counter who goes and looks is off this counter, and there are nine people in that corridor and the ninth of them has been outside since a quarter to nine."

She looked at him for about four seconds.

"I want to say one thing and I am going to say it exactly and you are not going to like it."

"Go on."

"I am not going to tell you when it went, and I am not going to guess at it," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "A guess from this desk in this quarter gets repeated in Cordwain by eleven o'clock tomorrow, and I have watched a thing that was wrong travel around a district for nine years and kill a man's trade twice."

## Nine Days In A Hall

She had a piece of paper of her own, ruled off, and she did not offer it to him.

"I have been at this desk for twenty years. Twice in that time a family has come to me about a file that was in the index and not on the shelf," she said. "Both times it was on the wrong shelf, because both times it had been taken down and looked at and put back, and the shelf it went back to is not the shelf it came off."

"Is that what happened to ours."

"I do not know what happened to yours," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I have told you the two things I have seen and I have told you I am not going to turn them into an answer for you at eleven o'clock in the morning, and that is the difference between this desk and every other one you have been to in nine days and it is not going to be any use to you."

He looked at the paper on the counter for a while.

"Eleven days," he said. "We were at it on two of them. In the week after the first."

"Lots of people were in halls that week."

"Lots of people were."

Then he said the thing that the whole nine days had been built around, and he said it in the flat voice of a man who has said it to his wife several times.

"They broke the wall," he said. "They changed everything and my family is not on it."

## What The Wall Actually Says

"There is a line at the bottom of that wall now," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I have read it, because I read everything that is on a wall in this city, and it says *unrecorded, carried by*."

"Then that is my wife."

"It is not anybody's wife," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "It is not a name and it is not a figure and it is not in any book in this building, and if I were to send you to a desk that could produce it in a hand you could hold, I would be sending you for about four years."

She put her hand flat on the counter.

"But I am going to tell you the true thing about it, and it is the only true thing I have, and it will not help you. Your whole life has been a set of tiles in a case in a hall and a figure off a wall. That is not a small life. That is the life of about four hundred households in this city, and it is measured, and it is kept, and it can be looked at by anybody who walks past on a working afternoon."

"And on the eighth of October there is a sentence at the bottom of it that nobody can read," she said, "and it is about people like you, and it is true, and it cannot be produced at a counter."

## What He Cannot Produce

He put the paper back in his pocket and then took it out again, because a man who has walked nine days does not put a thing away at that point.

"Can you tell me what my premises is."

"No."

"Can you tell me who certifies it."

"I could not tell you that either," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I am not going to find out for you, because finding out is not a thing this desk does, and because if I found out I would be the second person in this city to have gone looking and the first one told me to stop."

He put the paper away then.

"My wife is going to be sick," he said. "She has been not sick since the fourth of October and she is going to be sick about eleven times before this is finished, and I want you to understand that I am not saying that to get anything out of you. I am saying it because you are the first one in nine days who has told me anything true and I have got nobody to tell."

"Then say it upstairs," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "There is a clinic at the Vestry gate and it is open and it is not mine and I do not know anybody in it."

## The Count On The First Of January

The girl is on the roll of a school off Cordwain and the place is held against the composition of the premises, and the composition is a figure on a tile in a case in a hall off Lamplit Row.

"How many is the girl."

"That is not my business and it is not on the paper you have."

"Then I am not going to say it," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I am going to say the next thing instead, and it is worse, and you may as well have it now rather than on the last day of the month."

He listened.

"A roll has to be made," she said. "And a roll is made out of what can be produced. And on the first of January somebody in a building off a third court is going to ask for the certificate of the composition of your premises, and that certificate is made four times a year by one person, and your premises has not been certified since the fourteenth of March."

"Then who makes it."

"I have run this desk for twenty years and I do not know," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I have never known. It is made two floors under a sales floor by a woman whose name I have never had in a book, and I am not going to start finding out at a public counter at a quarter past eleven on a morning in October."

He sat there for a while with his hands on his knees.

"You have not asked me one question about how it happened."

"No."

"You have not said that anybody might have taken it out of the hall."

"There might have been," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I have not got one fact about that and I will not give you a shape to carry round Cordwain that I cannot stand behind on the thirtieth of December."

## What Happens To A File That Is Fine

He went out at about ten to twelve and she let him get to the end of the corridor before she said the last of it, because she had said everything else at the desk and this was not a desk thing.

"Mr Orek."

He turned round.

"Your file is not lost," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I want you to hear that from one person and then think about it on the walk home, because it is the worst version and it is the true one."

"What version is that."

"The index is right. The case is on a shelf in a hall in this city. It has been there for the whole of nine days and it will be there in four years, and about half the people who lose a file in Ostara lose it because they want it to be gone and the other half lose it because they would rather it were gone than be wrong about it."

She put her hands flat on the desk.

"You cannot find it and neither can I, and in about four years a stranger with a folder will come to that desk and read that index and find a shelf, and he will have what you have lost, and he will not know what it was for, and he will put it back."

They stood there for about four seconds.

"Then it is not gone," said the carter.

"It is not gone," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "It is just that in this city the difference between the two is worth about two floors of a hall and a great many people's whole lives, and nobody has ever been able to put a figure on the difference and that is not going to start now."

She went back to the day's sheet.

The queue was four deep by one o'clock and every one of them in it had come about something else.

There were about nine hundred files in the halls off Lamplit Row and Cordwain and the Vestry in this city at that moment, sitting on their correct shelves. At least four of them had been opened in the week after the first of October by somebody who was told to look inside, and put back afterwards by a different person, and not one of the four had been missed.

# Two Days Before The Numbers

Beatrix Halm read the last line of the ranks wall at about a quarter past six on the morning of the ninth of October and there were about eleven people under it who had come on purpose, which had never once happened at that hour in nine years.

By eight there were about ninety.

"They have come to look at it," she said. "Nine years I have come to look at it. Nobody else has ever come to look at it. That is what your two words have done and it took one morning."

## What The Foot Of The Wall Does To A Column

The column that a boy paints down the wall with the paste brush is the standing of named premises at their composition, and it runs for about forty feet and it is read by whoever wants to read it, and in Cordwain it is read every morning by people checking their own number.

What happened on the ninth of October is that about forty people in Cordwain stood at the foot of that column and could not find the end of the wall.

The column finishes where the stone finishes. That has been true for as long as the wall has been painted and every person in this city knows where the bottom of it is, because the bottom of it is where you stop looking.

And then, on the morning of the ninth, there was two words and a dash and two more words at the bottom of the stone under the last tile, in the office's own hand, at the height of a name on a plate.

"So it goes under it," said a woman in Cordwain, to Beatrix Halm, at about half past seven, at the north end. "That is a new line. That means the card is out of date and the figures on it are out of date, because there is a thing under the figures now that was not under them in July."

"It is not under them."

"Then what is it."

"It is the bottom of the wall," said Beatrix Halm, "and it has been the bottom of the wall since before I started, and you are looking for a reading of it that nobody in this city is able to give you, and I have been standing at this stone since half past four and I have not got one."

## A District Doing Its Own Arithmetic

There is a difference between a city that cannot read a thing and a city that has decided what the thing means, and Cordwain spent the ninth of October somewhere in the middle of the two.

At about nine they worked out, about ninety people together in about forty minutes, that if the sentence is a new line then the floor has changed, and if the floor has changed then every figure on that wall is wrong, and if every figure on that wall is wrong then nine hundred households in Cordwain have been checking a wrong number every morning for thirty years.

That is not a true conclusion and it was worked out in about forty minutes by people with no instrument at all, and Runa Sato stood in a lane off Cordwain for two hours on the ninth and listened to every version of it, and did not correct one.

"You could have corrected one," said Lin Hao.

"I could have corrected one in about eleven seconds," said Runa Sato. "That is not what I am for. What I am for is finding out what a district believes when it has been left alone with a thing for a morning."

"And what does it believe."

"This district believes the wall is lying. About a third of them think it has been lying for thirty years and the other two-thirds think it began on the eighth," said Runa Sato. "Both of those are available to them now because of two words and a dash, and I did not put it there."

## The Counter At The Third Court

Ines Duarte had been auditing a branch office off a third court for eight days and she had a ledger open on the desk in front of her and about nine people in the corridor who had not come to be audited.

A man of about sixty came in at about eleven and asked her whether his premises was still on the wall.

"Your premises is on the wall every morning at six and it has not moved and it is not going to," said Ines Duarte. "It is painted by a boy who is given a list. Nothing you did in October went onto that column and nothing you did in July went onto it either."

"Then read me the foot of it."

"Two words and a dash and two words, and I have not got an answer for them and neither has the man who painted them," said Ines Duarte. "That is the truth and it is the whole of the truth and I am not going to send you to another desk to hear the same sentence."

That was the ninth time in eight days that she had said some version of that sentence to a person at that desk, and she had not softened it once, and there is a woman in that branch who can hold a record in her head and knows when it has been touched, and she has never in eleven years been so tired of being right early.

## Four O'Clock

At about four in the afternoon of the ninth of October a clerk of the Correction Office put a notice up at the north end of the ranks wall on the other side of the stone from the new line, and read it out to about four hundred people.

It carried a number, a date, and not one word.

The number was a room. The date was the tenth of October, at about eleven in the morning. Not one word of it said what the matter was, and the only way to take a notice off that wall is to go and ask the office that put it up, and about two hundred people did, and the answer they got was the same answer, and it was given in the same voice by four different clerks.

A petition has been laid. The room sits at eleven on the tenth. A said thing in a room that cannot carry it is a minute of a sitting and goes out at four with the standing sheet on it.

"And what is the petition about," said the woman with the basket from Cordwain, who had come down the hill to find out and who got the answer from a clerk on the second attempt.

"It is about the Bureau," said the clerk. "That is all I am allowed to say and I have said all of it."

## What The Vestry Says About It

Agnete Lindqvist came down the hill at about two in the afternoon on the ninth with about forty of her own behind her and stood under the wall and read the last line with her head on one side.

"That is our four words with the hand cut off," she said.

She stood there for about two minutes, and her own forty stood behind her, and she did not say anything else for the first of them.

"For twenty years I have said that it cannot be given back, because there is no list of what it is made of. Those two words and a dash and two words say the same thing in the office's own hand at six in the morning, and they say it about a pot nobody owns, and they were typed into a box by a woman who was given a sheet."

"And we are claiming it," said a man behind her, who had walked down from Cordwain.

"You are not claiming anything," said Agnete Lindqvist. "If the Church of the Quiet Number claims a line of text on a wall in the morning then about nine hundred people in Cordwain will say we have got a wall, and the Church has not got a wall and has never wanted one, and a thing that wants a wall is the thing I have been standing nine feet off for a week."

"And if it is true."

"Then it was true on the first of October as well and it was true on the twenty-ninth of July and it was true every day of the four days," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is what a true thing is. It is not new because it is on stone."

She turned round to her own forty.

"Go back up the hill and be extremely dull about this," she said. "We do not gain from that line and we are not going to claim it and I would like every person on this pavement to go to work today and to be the least interesting thing in Cordwain until the end of the week."

They went up the hill. It is the first instruction that chaplaincy of twenty years has ever given to her own for anything, and she gave it in front of a wall, and about four hundred people in the district heard it.

## Two Days

The counters in Cordwain shut at four and on the tenth they had almost nothing in front of them at any hour of the day. A landlord off Lamplit Row, who has quoted the standing of a premises at that column to a tenant every quarter for nine years, could not find out what the number was on the tenth and wrote *as per the Board* for the first time in his life and did not like the sound of it and left it.

A bakery kept a slate. Two laundries kept slates. A woman who ran a rooming house off Cordwain could not answer a question about a deposit, and the deposit was not large, and she did not know what to do with a person who asked her.

That is the thing about a district that has lived its whole life on a wall. It has never in thirty years had to decide what it thought.

On the ninth of October it had to, and about two hundred people in Cordwain decided that the Bureau had broken. About four hundred decided that the Bureau had been broken for thirty years and had been writing its own figures up.

He stood in the lane at about half past six on the evening of the ninth and listened to a city that could not read a wall decide what it had always thought about the man who put the figures on it.

## It Is Not A Victory

"It has gone quiet," said Beatrix Halm.

"Yes."

"That is not us winning," said Beatrix Halm. "Be careful about that, because I have known you four years and I have watched you stand under that wall and take a number off a man who was not ready to give it to you, and this is what the opposite of that looks like."

"What is it."

"It is the sound a thing makes," said Beatrix Halm, "when nobody has decided about it yet. That is all this is."

"What have you got."

"A wall in a book. A woman in Cordwain who thinks it has been lying for thirty years. A man with a slate who thinks it is in competition with him." She shut the notebook. "And two days of that."

"And the two words stay."

"The two words stay until somebody scrapes them off or paints over them, and there is no schedule for that and no office that has it on a form, and it will be the last line on that wall for as long as either of us is alive."

Two days, she said. The ninth and the tenth.

And the room off a third court would sit at eleven on the morning of the second of them, and could carry nothing at all, and the whole of the city was going to find out that fact at about eleven o'clock on the tenth from a corridor full of people who had come to shout.

# The Household Decides For Itself

He told them about his chest at about six o'clock in the evening on the twenty-sixth of August, before anything else, and he stood up to do it, and nobody had asked him to, and it cost him something he was unable to name in the room.

"One hundred and eighty-six days," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with twenty-six is one hundred and eighty-six. And that is the last number in this house for a month."

"Lin Hao—"

"On the fifteenth of July I spent about two thousand people's worth of cold in about four hours in a square with about nine hundred people in it, on purpose, faster than a frame can take, and something in my chest is spent. It is not pain."

"It is a thing that has been used up and will not mend, and a cough has come with it that belongs to me until I am dead, and I have never once expected to be given it back."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"I have not told one person in this house that in four months. Not one. Not because I was protecting anybody, and I want that said before anybody starts being kind at me about it: I did it because every possible version of the sentence put a woman at that table saying *you have decided*, and I did not want to hear it said, and that was cowardice and not manners."

Tessa Okonkwo said, "That is a chest," and then stopped, and then said, "No. Not tonight."

"Not tonight," he agreed. "I have told you and it is out of me and I am not going to do anything with it."

## Why He Was Not Going To Be The Chair

"I am not chairing this," he said, "and I am going to say why before anybody can put me in it, because if I sit at that end of the table I am doing what I have done for four years and I have had enough of it."

"Not one of us is chairing this."

"There is a chair by definition," said Ines Duarte. "There are nine people and somebody decides the order and somebody keeps the tally, and you are the only man at the table and you have spent four years being the only man at the table, and nine women are not going to run a vote without somebody in it."

"I am not going to be it," he said. "A man who cannot take a cold minute has got no business asking nine women to take eleven, and any vote I ran would be a vote I could see the shape of before anybody opened their hands, and I have been the shape of a room for four years and I have had enough of it."

"Sit at the side, then," said Runa Sato. "You. By the window. And do not touch the tally."

## The Motion

Ines Duarte put it in the ordinary form, which is the form a thing takes when it has to be carried into a building.

"State it so that a stranger could carry it," she said, "and not one of them may add to it and not one of them may take from it. State what goes in front of the room at the end of a corridor on the first of October, and who says it."

Beatrix Halm said it, because it had to be said by a woman and not by a man.

"That on the fourteenth of August, in a kitchen at this address, three women drew against an account that has been filling for thirty years, deliberately, at a cost to themselves, in a room with nine other people in it. That the household of nine persons keeping this address was told on the second of August and has been working since the sixth."

"And that the household of this address has decided what it will say about it, and has decided it on the twenty-sixth of August, and will say it in a room anybody may attend, and will not be represented by the man who brought it to the door."

"They cannot be represented by you," said Lin Hao.

"They cannot be represented by you," said Beatrix Halm, "and that is not an insult and it is a rule you wrote yourself in about eleven seconds on the twenty-first of June. A person keeping an address so named may be produced by a minute about that address only in the presence of the subject, and the subject may not be absent from the making of it. You are the doorway."

"You are not in the room and you are not out of it and I am sorry and there is no version of it where that is otherwise."

## What Ines Ruled On It

"Ines," said Runa Sato.

"I have been two hours on it," said Ines Duarte. "Here is what can and what cannot be carried, and when I have finished I am sitting down and I rule on nothing else tonight."

"A minute of that room cannot carry a household's resolution. No box exists for it. It carries four parts and a heading, and the heading is entered first and read back before the body, and a heading that is wrong about itself is a record a stranger in four years will read as a lie."

"So we cannot put this in a minute, and I have not found any instrument of any kind in this city that will hold a decision of nine people who are not sitting in a room that can carry it."

"Then nothing goes," said Nadia Ferrous.

"Something goes," said Ines Duarte. "A person goes."

"There is a claim on the table and an answer on the table and neither of them has one word of the fourteenth of August in it, and on the first of October a room of nine seals is going to decide the disposition of a thing that three women in a kitchen have already touched."

"And if not one person stands up in that room and says so then in about four years there will be a plate on a wall with a number on it and no line anywhere in the whole of Ostara that says three women went cold in a kitchen on the afternoon of the fourteenth."

"A person goes," said Runa Sato. "That is the worst possible answer and it is the only one."

"It is the only one in this city," said Ines Duarte. "I have been looking for four hours and it is the only one there is and I am sorry."

## The Hands

Runa Sato did not count them out loud. She kept the tally on the back of a docket with a pencil and she did not call a price on anything, and she has not called a price on anything in this house since the fourth of August and she never will again.

Nine women.

Seven hands.

Agnete Lindqvist did not put hers up and said so before anybody asked her.

"You have my hand," she said. "I have been up since the twenty-ninth of July and I am not going to insult either of you by pretending otherwise."

"Say it."

"I will not put the Church's name beside a return," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I said on the twenty-ninth that it cannot be given back and I have not changed my mind and I did not change it on the twentieth of August when I liked all of you very much in a yard. Eleven hundred of my people came to a hearing about this."

"They are going to come to a square in October because I told them to and I have not told them to yet and I am going to have to decide about that by the end of the month."

"They will be told the Church says give it out," said Isolde Marchetti.

"They will be told the Church says give it out," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I will stand in that square and say it, and it will be true, and it will be a serious argument, and it will not be the same argument as the one nine women made in a kitchen off Ferrier Street, and I have thought very hard about that for a week and I could not make them the same, and I would rather lose than pretend."

Marguerite Vale said, "Eight."

"Eight," said Ines Duarte, "and one against, and the one against has said why out loud, which is more than the eight did."

## He Voted

"The ninth hand is mine," said Lin Hao, "and I am going to use it, because I have been in this house four years as a man who decided things in it and I am not going to do that again tonight."

Not one of them said anything.

"No," said Lin Hao. "I vote no. And I am going to tell you why in four sentences and then I am going to go and stand by the window and I am not going to argue with anybody afterwards."

"That is not—"

"You have nine names on that sheet and eight of them are willing," he said. "Every one of those names is answerable at a counter in this city for four years and six of the nine of you hold a post a division can serve a paper at, and I have known that since the twentieth of April and I have never once done anything about it, and on the first of October that is what this is."

"It is eight women walking into a room that can put a paper on an address."

"That is an argument for it," said Beatrix Halm.

"That is an argument against it," he said. "And there is a second thing and it is not an argument at all. I wrote thirty-eight words in a minute in June that put me in a corridor outside the door of every matter about this address, for ever, and I wrote it in nine minutes to keep six of you from having to be fetched through a man."

"On the first of October a matter about this address is going to be decided, and I am going to be standing in a corridor, and there is not one thing I can do about it, and I built that myself in a kitchen at a table with a pencil and no price and I would do it again."

He went and stood by the window.

"Right," said Runa Sato, and made a mark on the back of a docket with a pencil, and did not say what it was.

## Eight Names And No Addressee

Marguerite Vale read the thing out at about half past nine, which was her right and not a favour, and which she did in the shape of a call because it is the only form in her that knows how to do it.

"What moved. Eight names and one date. When: the twenty-sixth of August, at about nine in the evening, in a kitchen. At what price: every person who signed it is answerable at a counter for four years, and nothing in this city will make an instrument of any of it, and I am not going to write that down in a book a keeper carries into a room, because a keeper carries books and does not carry opinions."

"Nothing on it says who it is for," said Petra Ilyin.

"Nothing any of us has got says who it is for," said Ines Duarte.

"The claim has none and the answer has none and the heading has none, and the sheet on the table has my tally on the back of it and my name is not on the front of it and I am not going to put it there, because if my name is on the front of it then a rank-two auditor is on the front of it, and in four years there will be a document in which an auditor of this city is on the front of it."

It was not tidy and not one of them made it tidy.

There were eight women and one man in a kitchen and there were two pieces of paper on that table, one of them with eight names on it and a date and no address, and one of them with a paragraph written in a guild enforcer's own hand in a yard, on a sheet off a docket roll, that not one person in that house was in a position to correct, and neither of them had anywhere to go for five weeks.

## After The Washing Up

Nadia Ferrous came up the stairs at about a quarter to eleven, which is what she does, and stood in the doorway while the table was being cleared.

"You said the twenty-sixth," she said.

"I did."

"After the washing up. In the kitchen."

"After the washing up," said Lin Hao, "in the kitchen, and I have been here since six o'clock and I have not said one word about it in front of those eight women because it is not theirs to hear."

"You are a fool," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I am, and you have known it for four years," he said, "and I have been standing outside that room for four years and I have got a cough and nine women who have just decided the largest thing anybody in this city has decided in thirty years without me, and you are standing in a doorway, so say the thing you came down for and say it before the plate goes in the cupboard."

She said it.

And then she said, "And you have got until the first of October to work out how to say it in front of nine women who have just watched you vote no on them," and went down the stairs backwards, calling out from about halfway that she was not going to be carrying anything up at eleven o'clock at night in August and anybody who needed a plate could come and get it.

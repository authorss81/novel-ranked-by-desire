# Nine Women, Dispersed And Intact

The kitchen off Ferrier Street had a table in it that had been in it for four years and nine chairs that had not all been in it at once in eleven months, and on the evening of the seventeenth of October there were nine women sitting at it and a man standing at the end of it with his coat on.

He had been in that kitchen about four hundred times. He had never once stood at the end of it.

"Nobody answer anybody," said Marguerite Vale, taking the chair at the head, which is a broker's chair and not the host's and never has been. "Each of us says one thing. We do not answer anybody. And I go last."

"You go last," said Agnete Lindqvist, from the doorway, in her coat.

"I go last."

## The Rule Of The Table

It is not a new rule and it has never been written down, and Beatrix Halm, who was on the step with her boots still on, said that it came from about the third of August when nine women had been in that room for six hours and three of them had said the same thing four times each.

"It is not a punishment and it is not for you," she said. "It is because the last of us to speak on the sixth of August made a speech and six of us answered it inside four minutes, and by a quarter past ten the whole of what any of us had come in with."

"So one thing," said Isolde Marchetti, from the back of the stove, with a cloth in her hand. "And do not answer."

"And do not answer," said Isolde Marchetti. "And whoever goes last, goes last."

Marguerite Vale put the last of the washing up on the table between them, which is a thing she does when she wants a room to stop moving.

"I am going to go last," she said. "I have said so twice and I am going to stop saying it. My one thing is about the Vant and it is about a book and it will keep until about two in the morning."

## One Thing Each

Marguerite Vale went first, which was not expected, because she is a broker and a broker waits.

"The entry closed," she said. "The one in the real book since the sixteenth of September, the one that has been called at four valuations and never answered. I closed it in my own hand at about eleven on the morning of the second of October, in front of Solveig Rask, because the thing is gone, and a book that carries nothing is worse than a book that is empty."

She put her hands flat on the table.

"That is my one thing and I am not answering anybody."

Runa Sato was second and she did not sit down.

"I have not been in this room since the twenty-sixth of August," she said, "and that is not a complaint, that is a round. I am out at six and in at dark and there are nine divisions in this city that have not seen me and there are two counters in Cordwain that have seen me every day for eleven days."

She looked at Agnete Lindqvist in the doorway.

"You are going to say that I should be here and I am not going to answer you, so save it, and do not save it either, because I have a round at four."

Ines Duarte was third and she was at the sink with her sleeves turned up, because she has her own rooms on Lantern Row and her own plate and has done for four years, and a woman who does not live in a house does its sink.

"I have been auditing a branch office off a third court since the first of October," she said, "and it is a corridor and a door and a great many people saying that somebody has wronged them, and it is the best work I have had in four years and I am going to be there until the end of January."

She turned the tap off.

"I do not live here. I have not lived here since the twenty-fourth of December of the year before last and I signed for this house in person at the Terrace in April and I did not argue about it, and both of those things are true and neither of them is going to be resolved tonight."

Beatrix Halm was fourth and did not come in off the step.

"I was under that wall at half past four on the eighth," she said, "and there were eleven people there who had come on purpose, which has never once happened at that hour in nine years. And I wrote the two words down once and tore the page out and gave it to him, and he has it in his coat."

She looked at the floor of the kitchen.

"Tomorrow I will be under that wall at half past four and there will be no one else, and that is the trade and I knew the trade."

Nadia Ferrous was fifth and she came up the stair for it, which she does not do, and she was out of breath at the top of it in a way that everybody let go of.

"I have taken the liftman's nights at a branch office off a third court," she said. "Nights. Eleven to four, four nights, and there is a rota and a man keeps it and my name is on it in his handwriting because he wrote it down and I watched him do it."

She put both hands on the table and laughed at nothing in particular.

"That is my one thing and I am not answering anybody and I would like it observed that I came up that stair and I will not come up it again this month."

Tessa Okonkwo was sixth, and she was late, and she came in with her coat already off.

"I have three months of room where I had four on the twelfth of September," she said, "and the difference went to about two hundred people at a table by the Vestry gate over four days, and I took a thirtieth and not a fortieth because I was the only one there and I am not going to sit here and take credit for it."

She looked at the stippling on her own forearm while she said it, the way a person does with something they have had since before anybody in this room knew them.

"I am a doctor on a night rota at the Vestry from the middle of November," she said. "That is my one thing, I am not going to explain it, and I am not going to be asked about the arithmetic of it by anybody, including me."

Agnete Lindqvist was seventh, from the doorway, in her coat, standing up.

"On the thirtieth of December I am going to call a sitting in a room that nothing can be done in, and I am going to hold it at about eleven hundred, and I am going to say the four words one more time in front of all of them."

Not one of them answered her.

"I stood nine feet off a wall on the first of October for four days because I thought that was what standing apart was," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"It is not. It was the only thing I knew how to do with a thing I could not stop. In December I am going to be in the room with it and I am going to hold it myself, and I am going to be wrong in front of eleven hundred people if I am wrong."

"That is my one thing, and in December I am going to take my coat off in this kitchen."

She put her hand on the back of the last chair in the room, which is a chair at the table, and she did not sit in it.

Petra Ilyin was eighth.

"There is a room over a laundry off Cordwain," she said. "Two rooms, and a landing, and it is mine, and I have been in it a fortnight, and I pay for it out of what I am paid, and not one other person has put anything into it, and I am not going to let anybody else put anything into it."

She put her hands on the table and they were not working, and they had not been working properly since the fourteenth of August, and she left them there.

"I am not going to be able to say no to a person who comes to that door," she said. "That is not going to change because I have moved eleven hundred yards. I have wanted one thing out of eleven years and I did not get it, and I am telling the nine of you so that I am not going to be a woman whose life the next year gets arranged for her. That is my one thing."

Nobody answered her.

## The Landing

He went out of the kitchen and up the first flight because the kitchen only has one door and he could not get past nine people in it, and Petra Ilyin was on the landing in about nine seconds because she had been on the landing already.

She did not ask him anything and she did not say one word about the eighth thing she had said downstairs.

"A fortnight," she said. "Two rooms. It is colder than this house by about four degrees and it is the only place in this city I have ever paid to be in."

She put her hands flat on his chest, and they were not working, and she put them there anyway, and she found the place where the cough lives in about two seconds because she has known where it lives since the second of July.

"That has not gone down," she said. "You have had four days at rank one with a guaranteed subsistence and you have had three floors of washrooms in nine days and it has not gone down at all."

"No."

"Good," said Petra Ilyin. "Do not tell anybody. I am telling you because you are the only one of the nine who cannot be asked at a counter."

Then she took her hands off him, and got hold of him by the back of the neck with two hands that cannot do fine work and pulled his head down, and kissed him with her mouth open and did not stop when he tried to talk.

She was not quick about it. She was thorough in a way that had nothing to do with being in a hurry and everything to do with eleven years of a promise she could not remember being given, and about the middle of it she got a hand behind his ear and held on.

"Say it again that you are not asking me for anything," she said, against his jaw.

"I am not."

"Then do it properly and stop thinking about the rota," said Petra Ilyin.

He did. It was not fast and it was not careful. She took her coat off over the bannister and it went down the stair and neither of them went after it, and when she had finished with his mouth she made him hold still and she put her forehead against his and breathed.

"I am going to say a thing on this landing," she said, "and I am not asking you to solve it and I am not asking you to have an opinion about it, and you are going to stand there and hear it, and then we are going downstairs, and the rule holds, and that is the whole of what I have got tonight."

He stood there.

"There is a maker of that promise," said Petra Ilyin. "Not a face. Not a name. I have had eleven years to go looking for a man in a doorway and there has never been anybody there, and I have finally understood that he is not in this city, and it never was a person, it was a door, and somebody stood in it once on a night and said something to me and went."

She let go of him.

"Now downstairs," she said. "And you have four minutes to wash your face because Agnete is going to know."

## The Yard At The Back

Nadia Ferrous was in the yard at the back with the door propped open and a coat on over what she was wearing, because the yard was four degrees warmer than the kitchen and she had gone up eleven flights that week.

She had a bottle of something and two glasses and she had already had one.

"I got here first," she said. "I always get here first. It is not a superpower, it is a lift."

She handed him the glass.

"I am going to be honest with you about one thing and then I am going to stop being honest with you for about an hour, because that is the arrangement and I have had a lot of practice at it."

"Go on."

"I am not going to be able to do that." She pointed down. "That. Stairs, upward, more than one flight. In January I will be at the bottom of a shaft in a building two streets from where you will be working, and I will be there at eleven at night, and I will be there at four in the morning, and if you want me you will have to come down."

She drank.

"And you have to come down," said Nadia Ferrous. "Four years I have been in this house and I have never once had to ask anybody for anything that I had to go up a stair for, and I did not think about it, and I am thinking about it now at about eleven o'clock at night in a yard, and that is my one thing downstairs was too short for it and I am saying it again because the rule does not have a limit on saying."

She put the bottle down on the step.

"Get down here," she said. "You have got about nine seconds before somebody in that kitchen comes out and finds us standing in the cold like two people at a bus stop."

He got down there.

She was quicker than anybody in that kitchen had a right to expect and completely without ceremony about it. She had a thing she did with her hands that she had learned from watching cars go under a bridge, and she laughed once, out loud, at something else in that kitchen would have found funny.

She kept both her hands on him the entire time in a way that made it obvious she was counting something, and when she counted it she told him the number out loud, because she has never in her life pretended about anything.

"Seven," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is the highest it has ever been and I have been at this four years and it is because you are not working any more and I am not tired, and write that down somewhere."

"You cannot write it down."

"I know that. That is why I said it out loud," she said. "Somebody in this house has to have said it."

## The Sink

Ines Duarte was still at the sink when he came back in, with her sleeves still turned up, washing two plates that had already been washed.

"You have got about four minutes," she said.

"You did not come out."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "I am not going to come out of a room because something is happening in it. That is the whole of what I have learned in nine years of my own life and I am not going to be a woman who abandons a sink."

She put the plates down and turned round and dried her hands on a cloth that has not been the right cloth for that job since the spring.

"I have been thinking about a form," she said.

"I know."

"It will not be drafted. I looked for it for a whole day in August and it does not go anywhere and nothing at any counter in this city will take a thing a person did in a doorway, and I told a man of about fifty the same sentence on the fourth of October and I am telling it to you now because in four days somebody is going to tell you I have found one."

She came to him and put her arms round him, and she did it in the ordinary way, the way a woman with a dishcloth over her shoulder and her sleeves turned up does it to a man in a kitchen, and she was quick and competent and thorough, and did not waste a second of it.

And then she stopped, with her hands still on him, and put her forehead against his collarbone, and stood there.

"Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years of holding other people's paperwork in my head, and I could feel that account from the eighth year of this thing, and I have never once been able to say what it was, and now there is a line of text on a wall that says what it was and I still cannot read it, and I have never in my life resented a record as much as I resented that line."

She went back to the sink.

"I am going to be in this branch office every working morning until the end of January," she said, "and you are going to be cleaning washrooms in it, and neither of us has ever once mentioned that to the other one and I am not going to start tonight."

## The Table

He came back into the kitchen at about eleven and the nine of them were at the table, and there was an hour of that after, which was not an hour he counted and did not try to count, because it was the longest night of his life and every attempt he made afterwards to put a figure on it came out different.

Beatrix Halm got up off the step first and came in and put herself against the end of the table where his coat was and did not ask for anything and got what she wanted by being the coldest person in a warm room, which she has been able to do on a tram since she had the season on the Ferrier line, and which nobody in this house has ever got used to.

She was quick and she was not tender and she is deaf in one ear, so she kept her good side turned to him the whole time and talked straight into it, and about four minutes in she stopped talking altogether and made a noise she plainly had not planned to make, and put her hand flat on the back of his neck and left it there.

Later, on the stair, with her notebook under her arm and her boots in her hand:

"That is the last hour in which I could hear the room from inside it," she said. "Do not tell anybody I said that."

"Isolde sings four bars and the room agrees," said Lin Hao.

"Yes," said Beatrix Halm. "And the room is going to do it tonight, and nine women are going to hear it, and it is going to be the only time in the whole of this house that happens."

Tessa Okonkwo came at him at about half past eleven with the flat of her hand on his sternum and told him to stop, and he stopped.

She put her ear against his chest through his shirt for about eleven seconds and told him that whatever it was, it was a thing she could not take a fraction of, because not one fraction is left in it to take. Then she made him lie down on the kitchen table with his boots on the chair and did not touch him for about two minutes.

Then she did, at length, with one hand fisted in his collar, and she talked to him the entire time in the low clinical voice she uses across a trolley, telling him exactly what she could see happening on his face.

About four minutes in she stopped describing it and started telling him what she wanted instead.

It was about a room in a building two streets away, and about being in it when he was not in it, and about how much of her own week she is going to spend in a branch office after the middle of November. She said that part twice.

Marguerite Vale was at the head of the table the whole night with her hands folded and she did not touch him once, and at about half past twelve she got up, came round, put both hands on the back of his neck and bent his head back, and kissed him for a long time with a total absence of ceremony in a room with eight people in it.

Then she sat down again.

"Sixteen years of books," she said. "That is my one comment and I am not answering anybody about it."

Nadia came back in from the yard at about midnight with her coat over her arm and got hold of him from behind in the passage and did not say a word for about nine minutes, and the stairs are narrow and the passage is narrower and he had the cold from the yard on the back of his neck for the whole of it, and neither of them minded.

Petra Ilyin found him at about one in the morning in the passage with his coat on and put her hands flat on his chest one more time and left them there for about four seconds.

"It has not gone down," she said.

"I know."

"It is going to be like this in a laundry off Cordwain in four weeks' time as well," said Petra Ilyin, "and nothing in this house will take it down, and I am going to stop checking it after tonight and I am telling you so that when I stop you will know it is on purpose."

## Hers, Last

At about two in the morning the kitchen had been cleared and the stove was out and the nine of them were at the table with nothing in front of them, and Isolde Marchetti had not said her one thing.

She had not said it at eight o'clock or at ten or at midnight, and two of the nine had asked her for it and she had said not yet, and it was the rule, and she went last, and she went last at about two in the morning in a cold kitchen because it was the only time the room would be that still.

"I have sung four bars in that room twice," she said, "and both times it was an accusation, and neither time it was true, and one time it was fair."

She stood up.

"So I am going to do it properly. I am going to say one thing and I am not going to sing, because if I sing it then you will all agree with it, and I do not want you to agree with it because I asked you to."

She looked round the table at the eight of them.

"We did not live here for an account," said Isolde Marchetti. "Not one of us. I was on that wall in a square in July because I am a woman who sings four bars at a wall, and Petra was in that kitchen in August because a promise she cannot remember will not let her say no, and Beatrix because she hears things, and Runa because she is the only one who will go and look."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"He was not it. He has never once been it. That covers thirty years of this house, and it is the only sentence I have that is worth saying out loud at two in the morning."

And then she sang four bars, because she could not help it, because a room that has heard her agrees with her, and nine people in a kitchen off Ferrier Street agreed with her all at once and did not one of them answer anybody.

## Afterwards

They washed up, and not one of them said a word about the four bars. The stove went out at about twenty past two and the nine of them were at the table with their coats on and there was a slip of paper in the middle of it that had been there since about eleven and that nobody had picked up.

It was a page off a rota pad. It had three names on it in a keeper's handwriting and a date at the top of it and the date was the thirtieth of December, and the three names were a woman who had taken nights at a lift, a woman with rooms over a laundry, and a woman whose rooms were not free until the eleventh.

There was a fourth line under them and nothing on it.

Not one of them looked at that fourth line for about two minutes, and he stood at the end of it with his coat on and looked at all three women who were on it and at the fourth line, and he worked out inside about four seconds that he was the only person in the room who had anything left that he could use, and that it was a question, and that he was not going to spend it on a piece of paper in a kitchen.

Not one of them answered anybody, because it was the rule.

"Isolde," said Beatrix Halm. "You have a bed in that passage until the eleventh and you have had it since the second, and not one of us has said a word about it."

"Somebody said something about it about nine minutes ago," said Isolde Marchetti.

And the ninth of them put her coat on and went out into a passage and up a stair and down the hill in the cold to a bed in a passage, and it was the last evening on which all nine of them were in one room in this city, and none of them had a reason.

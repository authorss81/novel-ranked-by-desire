# The First Spend In The Open

He got to the north end of the ranks wall at about half past seven in the morning on the eleventh of July and put his coat on the ground and sat down on the step at the foot of it with his back against the stone, and he did not bring anything, and he did not make a speech, and there was nothing on the step but a man sitting on it.

The square does the same thing every morning. It fills up in one direction, out of the Reading, at about ten past eight, and it empties in the other, toward the tram stop, and about nine thousand people go under that wall a day in a city where the fourth ward is the second largest anybody has ever measured.

The first man to come to him was a carter who had come off a night and wanted to sit down for a minute.

## Hands

That is the whole of it and there is no other way to describe what nine thousand people watched a man do at the foot of a wall on the eleventh of July.

He shook hands. That is all it looked like from thirty yards. A man on a step at the north end of the ranks wall, in a coat, taking the hands of people who came past him, in the ordinary way, all day, and saying nothing to any of them, and not charging anything, and not asking for anything.

It looked like a man who has lost his way and does not want to be unkind about it.

Somebody's child was sent to ask him whether he wanted money. He said no to the child. That went round the square by about nine o'clock and it is the reason the queue formed.

## What It Costs A Person

The man in the queue in front of a woman selling fruit on the south side got about two seconds of cold in his right hand at about a quarter past nine and put it down to the weather, and then sat on a low wall for about twenty minutes with his head back and his hands loose, and stood up afterwards and went to work, and he was a different man for the rest of the day and never found out why.

A woman who had been at a tram stop for four hours stood in front of him and had four or five seconds of cold in the chest, which is the place for it in a person who has been standing. She sat down hard on the step beside him without meaning to, and put her face in her hands for about half a minute, and apologised to him, and stood up.

Then she walked away down the square at a considerably better pace than she had come up it.

A boy of about nineteen who had been carrying parcels since half past six could not keep his hands still afterwards and had to put them in his pockets, and stood there in the queue with his hands in his pockets for twenty minutes while the cold came out of his wrists and did not come out of his wrists quickly.

It was not warm. There was nothing about the north end of that wall that was warm. Nine thousand people saw a man taking hands in July and got a chill out of it, and about one in three of them thought they had caught something off somebody else.

## Ola Bright Came And Stood In The Queue

She had been keeping the diary since the year of the Veil and she did it because she was bored, and there is not one entry in it that anybody has ever paid her for, and she came past the step at about eleven o'clock with a parcel under her arm and joined the queue like a person.

"That is the fourth one today," she said, when it was her turn.

"How do you know how many."

"Because I have got a book," said Ola Bright, "and I have got the third one written down and the time on it, and you have been on that step for three and a half hours and a man who is doing that for three and a half hours is not doing it by accident."

She put her hand out. He took it, and she got the cold in the same place everybody gets it, in the hand, and it lasted about a second and a half, and then she stood there and looked at him for a moment with her eyebrows up.

"Right," she said.

"Right."

"That is the fourth one today and the first one I have ever had at a wall."

She went off down the square and she wrote it up that night in a book kept in a flat over a lift shaft, with the place on it and the hour, and she wrote *head of the Reading, north end* in full, and she wrote under it that she had been going to that flat for nine years and had never once been asked by anybody to write any of it down, and that was true and was the whole of her interest in it.

## What Nine Thousand People Made Of It

By about eleven the square had decided what he was and there were three versions going round it and all three of them were wrong.

One was that a man had lost a wager. One was that a man was collecting for something and had put the wrong hat on. The third, which was the one that took hold, was that the Board had come off the wall and was being handed out by hand at the north end, and that this could not last.

That third one was not stupid. It was the closest of the three to the truth, it was said by about forty people before noon, and it was wrong in a way that would take until October to unpick, and by the end of the afternoon there were two men in Cordwain who had organised something about it.

A woman at the fruit stall on the south side told him at about two o'clock that if the Board was coming off the wall then she had been standing next to it for eleven years and would like to know what she is supposed to do, and he had no answer that was any use to her, and she went back to her stall.

## The Second Kind Of Line

There is a rail along the bottom of the ranks wall about four feet off the ground where people lean, and it is a handrail and it is for leaning on, and by about half past two on the afternoon of the eleventh of July there were nine knots in a string on it.

The first one was put there at about eleven o'clock by a man who had been to the step twice and had a bad shoulder and no idea what he was marking.

By four o'clock there was a piece of chalk going round the square and being passed from hand to hand, and a woman on the west side was putting a single stroke on the wall beside the card for every person who went up, and the strokes had no figures against them and no names and nothing whatever behind them, and there were about ninety of them by the time the light went.

Beatrix Halm saw them at about three in the afternoon.

She came past the north end at the end of a shift the way she has come past the north end at the end of a shift for four years, and she stopped.

She stood in front of a wall with about ninety strokes on it beside a card in an office's own hand, and she looked at the top of the wall, where the tiles go up in ranks under a rail and the highest of them is a different colour from all of them.

There is a name on that one in chalk in her hand, put there at ten past six on the morning of the thirtieth of June, and there is nothing under it and there is no figure behind it, and about nine thousand people had gone out under it that day without knowing.

"Ninety-one," she said, to herself, and counted them twice.

Then she wrote the number and the hour in a notebook in the way she writes, quickly, in order, and with the hand that is not the one she holds the pencil in, and she did not stop at the step and she did not say one word to the man sitting on it.

## What The City Said About It At About Five

By about five o'clock there were four people at the north end of that wall who had been saying the same thing to each other for an hour, and one of them said it to him.

"They say the Board has come off."

"Who says that."

"A woman at Cordwain, at a counter, this afternoon, to a woman who came in with a bill." The man had his hands in his pockets and was not enjoying it. "She said it is not the plate, it is the whole wall, and that a clerk in a wing off a third court came out and read the tiles out loud to about forty people and then stopped halfway and would not go on, and she said that is how you know."

"What did she say it was."

"She said the Bureau has come off the wall," said the man, "and I said that is a thing you would hear a hundred times a day from people at that counter, and she said not this once, because the ones who come off it come off it quietly."

## Six In The Evening

He came off that step at about six o'clock in the evening and he had been on it for ten and a half hours and his back hurt and there was a mark on his palm where a man with a cart handle had gripped him.

Nobody has ever written down how much of it went, and he could not have said it if he had been asked, and Ostara has no instrument that could put a number on a thing like that.

A grain of it is not a grain of anything else. It cannot be scored and it cannot be bought and it cannot be drawn, and a man sitting on a step taking hands is not doing anything the Board has ever had a column for.

That is not because the Board is careless. It is because there is no second ledger for it.

There is the ledger in his coat and it is a narrow round about the size of his hand and he did not write one line of it on the eleventh of July, because nothing that happened at the north end of that wall was a deed, and because the round writes a line when there is something on it and there were nine hundred people in that square and not one of them had anything on him.

What it had done was this. It had put about nine hundred cold minutes into strangers over the course of a day, and every one of those people was a minute warmer at the end of it than they had been at the start of it, and about forty of them had noticed.

Nine hundred did not know and would never know and had gone home and slept.

And on four rounds in this city a line is being written that was not there at seven in the morning, because a round writes a line when there is something on it. Three of those four rounds are kept by women who have never met him and one of them is kept by a signal operator on the Ferrier line who writes down the times of things that happen to other people and has done it for four years.

The light went off the tiles at about ten past seven and the black one was the last thing you could see, and the name on it was in chalk in a woman's hand and there was no figure under it. About ninety strokes were down the side of that wall in a different chalk, put there by strangers, and none of it was on a card, and none of it would be read by anybody at a valuation on the thirtieth of September.

He walked back across the square with his hands in his pockets and thought that he had four days of this in him before the first of October and had no idea at all what the fifth day was for.

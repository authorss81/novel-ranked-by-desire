# The Question For The Household

The counting room is two floors under a sales floor and it is cold in August in a way that has nothing to do with the weather, and he came down the stair at about ten in the morning on the fourth of August with a sheet in his hand that he did not put down.

Hanneke Ruis was at the rail with her sleeves pushed up past the elbow.

"One hundred and sixty-four days," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with four is one hundred and sixty-four."

"Two days since you were last in this room and you have come down a cold stair to say a number to a woman who did not ask."

"I have come down a cold stair to ask one question," he said, "and I am going to ask it badly, because I have asked it well to about four people in four years and it has not worked once."

## What He Asked

"In a standing household," he said, "a person may not draw against an account the head of that household is answerable for while any person keeping that address has drawn against it. I have had that since the first of July and I have not been able to find out what a draw is worth, and I am asking you because you are the only person I have ever met who drafts a household out of a book."

Hanneke Ruis put the pen down.

"No," she said.

"That was quicker than I expected."

"No is not the answer. No is me telling you that you have come down the wrong stair," she said. "Sit down if you are going to stand there. What is a draw worth in law. That is your question and it is a bad one, because law in this city is not a set of answers, it is a set of places where a thing gets written down."

"Ask me what a draw is worth on paper and I will tell you nothing in about ninety seconds and it will be the truest nothing you have been given this year."

## The Four Places A Household Figure Can Enter A Book

She turned the sheet round so it faced herself and did not look at it.

"One. A composition certificate, made four times a year, and the fourth block of a quarterly return takes its figure off that certificate and nowhere else. That is the only door in this city that a figure about a house comes through, and it comes through one at a time and a quarter at a time and it comes through with a name at the head of it."

"And the certificate for the quarter that began on the first of July."

"Does not exist," said Hanneke Ruis. "It has not existed since the first of April and there has never been a form for it that anybody in this city could fill, because a certificate of a composition is made out of the composition and the composition is made out of what a house is, and this house has not been certified since the thirtieth of June."

"You filled the last one from memory."

"I filled the last one from memory on the thirtieth of June, in my own hand, with my name at the foot of it, and Solveig Rask witnessed it because a block filled out of somebody's head has to have somebody's name beside it or it is nothing at all," she said.

"And on the thirtieth of September I am going to do it again, and that is the next certification, and it is the sixth time I have written this house out of my own head, and there will be no certificate behind any of the six."

## Why She Would Not Say It In A Sentence

"You are going to ask me to finish it," he said.

"Not tonight," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I will tell you exactly why before you get angry about it." She squared the bundle of clean sheets. "The whole of what you have asked me is now, in my estimation, about four sentences long. I could put it on that sheet and it would be correct and it would take eleven seconds."

"And?"

"And the moment this city can put it in four sentences, a keeper can write it down," said Hanneke Ruis. "And the moment a keeper can write it down there is a column for it."

"And the moment there is a column for it, then a house that has drawn against an account either has a figure against it or has not got one, and in the second case somebody is going to want to know how a house comes to be in that condition."

"And there will be a form, and on the form there will be a line, and in about nine years a stranger will read that form and see a household that drew against an account and be owed something by it, and that stranger will be right to ask."

She put both hands flat on the rail.

"So I am not going to say it in a sentence, and it is not coyness and it is not a keeper's manner. If it is not in a sentence then a house that draws is not a thing this city has decided how to feel about, and that is the only position on this subject that has ever been available to anybody and I have been in this room twenty-nine years."

## The Thing He Cannot Get Round

"What it is worth," he said.

"You have not been listening to the question."

"I have been listening to it for four years," he said. "It is not whether I want to give it away. It is not who it belongs to. I stood in four hundred people on the thirtieth and said it has never been mine and I cannot take it back and that is the shape of me now."

"What I cannot get round is that I cannot empty an account while one of nine people I live with has put a hand in it, and there are three hands in it, and I cannot find out which three without asking, and I asked on the night of the second and one woman answered and I am not going to ask her the other two at that table."

Hanneke Ruis looked at him for about three seconds.

"You are frightened of the answer."

"I am frightened of the answer in front of them."

"Then go and ask them somewhere else," she said. "I have been drafting four times a year for twenty-nine years and I have never once seen a figure go into a book because a man asked a woman properly in a kitchen. It goes in because somebody insists, in the ordinary way, more than once."

## The Only Hand That Touches It

"So there is no figure for it."

"There is no figure for it anywhere in this city, and there is no instrument for it, and there is no place it can be entered, and it is not a service and it is not a composition and it is not a transaction, because a transaction is two books agreeing and this has one book in it and no opposite number." She shut the sheet.

"The only hand that has ever touched one is the hand of the person who drew, on the thing itself, in a room, with other people in the room. That is not law. That is simply what has happened every time it has ever happened and I have kept this book for twenty-nine years and there are four of them and I know the shape of all four."

"Four in thirty years."

"Four that I know of," she said. "One in the fifteenth year, one in the nineteenth, and two in the last four, and I know of them the way a keeper knows of a thing, which is that there was a disagreement somewhere that nobody has ever been able to find and that the guild could not price."

## The Woman At The Rail Is Not The Woman Who Signs It

He came back up the stair at about eleven and found Sun Meiling at the table at Ferrier Street with a docket folder under her arm, doing the thing she has done every morning for about a year, which is to be in a room where papers are served and not to be on the roll.

She said, "You will want a certificate."

"I want somebody to tell me what a draw is worth."

"Then you want the wrong one," said Sun Meiling. "The only person who could certify a figure against this address at any quarter-end is the officer of the composition, and the officer of the composition is me, and I am not on the roll, and I cannot be sworn, and I certify this house four times a year out of my own head with my own name at the foot of it."

She put the folder down.

"And I am not going to be asked about it by anybody, including you, and if you ask me I will answer the way I answered in June, which is that you already know, because you have known since the first of June and you told me so in this kitchen."

"I am not asking."

"Then put the folder back," said Sun Meiling.

Nobody at that table asked her anything else.

## Nadia Ferrous Said It Loudly

He got back at about seven and there were seven of them and Nadia Ferrous was standing at the end of the table with a cloth in her hands, which she had picked up to have something to do with and had forgotten about.

"I did it," she said, before anybody had sat down. "I did it about forty times and I have known for two years what I was doing and I have never once said so out loud in this house and I am saying it now because Petra said hers on the night of the second and I am not going to be the one in this room who waited."

"Nadia—"

"You were going to make a speech," said Nadia Ferrous. "Do not make a speech, you are the worst person in this kitchen at speeches and it is the only thing I have ever thought about in nine years."

He did not make a speech.

"I felt it the first time in the shaft," she said. "I have told that story in this kitchen about nine hundred times and every time I have told it I have told it as a thing the building did to me. A cold minute in a warm lift, on a morning, with the doors shut."

"That is the whole of what I said and it has been the whole of what I said since I was a girl, and about forty people have told me it is the shaft and about four hundred people have heard me tell it."

She put the cloth down on the table.

"And I have been going up that shaft every working day since I was sixteen and I have been cold in it about forty times and every single one of them was somebody who could not climb."

## The Thing She Gave It To

"That is the part I have not said and it is the whole of why I did it and it took me four years to understand it," said Nadia Ferrous.

"They come up that shaft. A woman with two bags and a man with a bad leg and a child who is seven. They stand at the bottom of it and they cannot get up to the counter, and there is no other way up in this building, and I am in it, and they stand there. And I have been able to make them warm for about a second and a half and then I have let them get on."

"They thought they were cold."

"They thought they were cold," said Nadia Ferrous, "and I stood in the car afterwards and I was cold as well and I told myself it was the shaft."

She looked round the table.

"That is why I never went to a clinic about it. A clinic cannot help somebody who cannot climb."

## He Tried To Price It And Could Not

"You told me on the fifth of July," said Lin Hao, "that a woman in the third row at the Board said the Bureau had come off the wall, and that the ones who come off it come off it quietly. You told me that in this kitchen and I did not ask you a single question about it."

"You did not ask me because you were coughing," said Nadia Ferrous.

He put his hand flat on the table.

"Ten of yours," he said. "That is what a draw is worth. A minute and a half, ten times, and about four pounds of a body's time in eleven years of somebody's life, and every one of them given to somebody who was standing at the bottom of a shaft at two floors below the only counter in the building."

Runa Sato said, "I am not doing that."

"Runa—"

"You do not get to price it in this room," said Runa Sato. "You do not get to put a figure on it and hand it round, and I have watched you do that to about four people in four years and I am not one of them and I have told you once already and I am telling you twice."

"Two is fine."

"It is not fine, it is the last time," said Runa Sato, and got up and went out onto the landing and left the door open behind her on purpose.

## Two Of Three

She came in again after about a minute and a half and sat down and did not look at anybody.

"One hundred and sixty-four days," said Lin Hao. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with four is one hundred and sixty-four."

"And it has been four days since I stood in front of nine women in this kitchen and asked them one question, and two of them answered it in four days, and there are three."

"Petra and Nadia," said Beatrix Halm.

"Petra and Nadia," he agreed.

There were nine people at that table and every single one of them looked at the woman at the end by the stove, and Marguerite Vale looked back at all of them and did not say one word.

"She is not going to say it here," said Lin Hao.

"No," said Marguerite Vale. "And neither are you, and that is the correct answer and you arrived at it by yourself, which is the only interesting thing that has happened in this kitchen since the ninth of July."

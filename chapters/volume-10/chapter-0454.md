# The Report That Is True And Useless

The records room at the Ninth Terrace is on the ground floor, past the intake hall and up four steps, and it has a stepped floor so that the back row sits higher than the front, and a bracket lamp over the middle table, and a rule painted on the wall by the door in a hand that is four years older than the paint.

The rule has five lines and every person who walks into that room reads it on the way to a chair.

In order. The date first, and the hour with it where the record carries one. Nothing skipped, not a name, not a figure, not a box with nothing in it. The reader may stop the reading. The subject may attend and may not stop it.

She had booked it at ten past seven in the morning as a closed sitting, which is allowed for a return that has not been filed yet, and a closed sitting is the one thing in this city that an auditor books and cannot defend, because the day's book wants a reason against it and a reason is a sentence in a clerk's hand that stays there.

Her reason was that the return was not yet filed.

In four years somebody will read that book's line and see a rank-two woman who shut a door, and she had spent eleven years being the woman who did not shut a door.

"You booked it shut," he said.

"I booked it shut," said Ines Duarte. "I did the other thing in June and I put it in a corridor with a rail and my name and the hour on a board, and it cost me about an hour of a particular twenty-odd days and it bought me one entry that said an auditor asked first and named herself."

She put her coat over the back of the chair at the near end of the middle table and sat down and squared the chair opposite her.

"This time the return is not filed. If I read it in here before four o'clock then nine hundred people in this building see an auditor's answer before the Council does, and this answer is going to be worthless, and I want it to be worthless in a room, not as a spectacle."

## Her Own Terms, In A Building That Is Not Hers

"I am not impaired and I am not tired and nothing in this room has any hold on me," she said. "I say that first because I said it first in June in a different room and I am going to keep saying it."

"What are you going to tell me the price of."

"About an hour of something I cannot name later, and the shut door, and one sentence I am going to say to you tonight that I am not going to say to you again this year or after it." She did not look up. "That last one is not a price. That one is a decision and you are not going to get it for anything."

"Sit down. You are the subject. The rule says you may attend and may not stop it, and you asked me for that in June and I am holding you to it."

He sat down.

She had nothing in her hands. There was no paper on the table and there was no paper in her coat, because she had held the whole return in her head since the seventh and had not needed a sheet of it since the ninth of June, and a rank-two auditor who reads a return out of her own memory is either a woman who has made a mistake or a woman who intends to be asked afterwards about every word.

## The Reading

"The thirtieth of June. Two o'clock in the afternoon. A room at the end of a corridor standing at five, and the fifth answered a requisition of the twentieth of February, and the sheet was carried, and five seals went down, one of them belonging to the subject, and the subject spoke for twenty seconds."

"That is the order. It is yours before it is mine."

"You will not tell me whether a woman came down that corridor."

"I will not tell you that. I will tell you what was in that room and there was a chair in it that does not count, and there has been a chair in it since the fourth of December, and I am not going to put a woman in it or take one out of it in a return."

"The first of July. Ten past eight in the morning. A member may read the book after the run has gone, and the subject read his own minute in a corridor with the door shut, and it carried a clause under it of thirty-eight words."

"There is a witness line at the foot of it with seven names and a gap in it two fingers wide, and the gap has been there since the eighteenth of April, and that is not mine and is not on my return. You did not ask me about it, so I have not read it out."

"The third of July. About eleven in the morning. Eleven minutes in a room two floors above this one, with a chair at the head of a table, and a standing sheet with nine lines on it, and four things offered and one condition, and the condition was not met."

"You were in the room with the chair of the Council of the Nine for eleven minutes and she does not write anything down and you have been carrying the terms of it in your head since and you have not told one person in this city."

"That is correct and it is on the return and it is not mine to soften."

## The Part She Did Not Read In Order

She went on for a while longer, and the lamp made a small circle and the rest of the room was dark, and at some point in the middle of the eleventh of July she read a line and did not read the hour with it, and the rule on the wall says the hour goes with it, and there was not one person in the building who was in a position to say so.

Then she got to the fifteenth of July and stopped.

She put both hands flat on the table, and she finished, and then she sat there with the report finished and the room completely quiet, and she opened her mouth and said a sentence that is not on the list and has no date on it.

"He is going to keep doing it until something in him stops working, and I am not able to stop him, and there is no rule in this city that reaches a man spending his own account, and I have spent four days looking for one."

"That is out of order."

"Yes."

"That is not in the return."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "It is going to be in the return and it is going to be the second line and I am going to put it second because a first line is a finding and I am not making a finding. It comes out of my mouth now and out of order because I have been holding it since the seventh and it was going to come out between the eleventh and the fifteenth and you would have heard it as an observation about the weather."

She came round the table.

## She Stops Reading In Order

"Say it again to me," he said, "in order."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "That is the one sentence and I said it once and you heard all of it and I am not doing things twice for a man who wants to hear them."

"And if I ask you whether it is true."

"It is true and you know that it is true, and I have watched you not say the answer to that question out loud for eleven days because you are extremely good at not saying it."

She put two fingers between his shirt buttons, took hold of the cloth, and walked him backwards until his hips met the edge of the table. "So there. I have stopped reading. Whoever is reading may stop. That is what the wall says, and I have been inside that rule my whole working life, and today is the first time I have used it."

"And the report."

"The report is going to be filed at four o'clock today in the ordinary way," said Ines Duarte, "and it will say that a man carried a requisition on the thirtieth of June and was offered four things and one condition on the third of July and did not meet the condition, and that since the eleventh of July he has been emptying an account in public at the head of the Reading, and that no rule in this city covers any part of it, and that I have looked."

She got her fingers into his collar.

"And you cannot pay for the part where I do not file it, because I am going to file it. And you cannot pay for the part where I do file it, because what I file is true and it does nothing at all, and neither of us can stop me, and you have known that since the seventh and you came here anyway."

## The Lamp And The Stepped Floor

The lamp was on a bracket and it made a circle about four feet across, and the back row of the stepped floor was outside it and dark, and there was no one on the back row and there had been no one on it since she shut the door at ten past seven.

She took her own coat off the back of the chair and dropped it on the step and put her knees against the front of his and pulled his shirt out of his belt with the flat of her hand.

"Look at me and not at the wall," she said. "You have been looking at a painted rule for eleven minutes and you have read it four times and it is five lines and you know them."

"I know them."

"Half past nine," said Ines Duarte. "The subject of the reading is before me and the return has stopped."

She said the hour the way she said every hour, out loud, before the thing, and it was the last piece of order she had left and she used it like a woman spending the last of a purse.

She had her fingers under his jaw and turned his head and put her mouth against the grey at the side of it, where the etches had been since June and were darker than they had been in June, and he put his hands on the table because she had told him to about six times in four years and he had never once not done it.

She read him the eleventh of July out loud against his throat while she got the buttons of her own shirt open one-handed, which she could do, and which she had told him in June she could do.

"Nine forty-one. The subject's attendance."

"That is not in the return either."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "Nothing from here is in the return. The return stopped when I stopped reading."

## What She Had Done And What It Had Cost Her

She got her back against the edge of the middle table, drew him in against her by the hip, and went on talking with her mouth against his jaw. What she was talking about was nothing, and that was the part he had not expected.

"You understand what this room is going to say about me," she said. "It is going to say that the auditor who spent four hours in June reading a man's own life out loud with four strangers at a rail did not do it again, and that on the fifth of July she shut a door, and that she gave a reason, and the reason is on a book in a clerk's hand for ever."

"Yes."

"And in four years there will be a stranger with a folder who will read that reason and work out what I was frightened of, and he will be right, and I will not be here."

He got a hand into her hair at the back and she took hold of his wrist and moved it, without hurrying, to the place she wanted it.

"Do not do that," she said, and did not take the hand away afterwards.

"You did not have to book it shut."

"I booked it shut because you are about to do something that is going to take weeks."

"If nine hundred people in this building had watched me read it out first, then every one of them would have known by the second of August, and there would not have been a thing left for you to be surprised by when you do it in front of them."

She pulled his head down. "I have taken your surprise off you. That is what the shut door bought, and I want it written somewhere that I know what it bought."

## The Middle Of The Room

She turned him round and set him against the end of the table and planted both palms on the wood either side of him, and the table was old and it did not shift a half-inch, and there was a smell of the lamp and of the dust in the stepped rows.

"One more thing," she said, "and then we are finished with it," and she said, "and after this we do not have it again, and not in October and not at the end of it, and you will not know whether I have decided that or whether I have simply run out."

"Go on."

"You have spent four years making sure that no woman you love is ever standing where an instrument can reach her," said Ines Duarte. "And on the twenty-first of June you bought a sentence that put all nine of us behind a door with you in front of it, and you did it in a notebook, and it cost a pencil, and it was the first thing you have got for nothing in four years, and a woman who prices things told you the price to your face."

"I know what it cost."

"You do not know what it cost and you are about to find out, and I am the only person alive who can tell you what it is going to cost, and here it is."

She leaned in until her mouth was against his ear. "It is going to cost you every one of them, not because you will lose them and not because they will leave."

"It is going to cost you the ability to be in a room with them without one of them looking at you to see whether you are all right. You will have spent that by August, and you will not be able to buy it back with anything in this city."

He had his hands flat on the table.

"Say the date first."

"Say what."

"You always give me the date first and the hour with it," he said, "and you have not given me one since the third of July, and if you are about to say the truest thing anybody has said to me since the fourteenth of March then I want it dated and I want it in order and I want it in your voice, because in four years there is going to be a stranger with a folder and he is not going to have been in this room."

Ines Duarte pulled back far enough to look at him.

"The fifth of July," she said. "About half past nine at night. A room with the door shut on the ground floor of the Ninth Terrace, with a rule on the wall, and a rank-two auditor who booked it shut because she wanted a man surprised in October, and a subject who has been in this city four years and has asked me for a date."

"And then she said it."

"And then she said it," said Ines Duarte, "and she was right, and she has been right all week, and he has known that since the seventh and has done it anyway, in front of witnesses, on purpose, faster than a body can be finished, and there is no rule in this city that reaches a man spending his own account."

She got her hand down between them.

"Now there is a thing on this table that is not in any return in Ostara," she said. "And I am going to be quite sure of the hour, because I am an auditor, and it is half past nine, and the door is shut, and you may not stop this."

## The Back Row Was Empty

She got up off the table and made him sit down on the step at the front of the middle table with his back against the front rail of it, and she stood over him and undressed the rest of it without hurrying, the way a woman undresses when she has decided how long she is going to be there and has no intention of letting the room know about it.

She had been in this city four years and he had never once watched her take her time.

"Nine forty-one," she said, and then gave the true hour, which was a few minutes short of it.

"It is not."

"It is not," she said, "and I have just told a false hour to a man in a room with a painted rule on the wall and I would have been dismissed for it before I finished the sentence, and I am telling you because I want there to be one thing in this room tonight that is not in order and it is going to be that."

Then she went down over him on the floor with her hands flat either side of his head and her hair coming forward over both of them. The lamp was above and behind her and put the light along the underside of her jaw and along the grey at the side of her head, and he got a hand up behind her neck and she let him keep it there, and he noted that she had not told him to take it out.

"There," she said, at some point, not stopping. "That is the whole of what you have been doing since the eleventh of July and it is not a policy and it is not a speech and you are on your back on the floor of a records room in a building with nine hundred people in it and one lamp, and if the day book says a closed sitting then that is the truest thing in it."

She went slowly, and she said the date again when he got his hand on the back of her neck, and she said it against his mouth, and he understood about a third of what she was doing and understood all of it by the end.

Somewhere out in the hall a woman at an intake desk stood up and pushed her chair back, because a rank-two auditor had booked a closed sitting and the rule in this building is that the hall goes quiet for one, and neither of them heard it.

## The Only Hour She Gave Him

The lamp went on burning over the middle table and the back rows stayed dark and the day's book in the corridor said a closed sitting begun at ten past seven and it did not say when it ended, because a closed sitting does not carry an end and she had thought about that on the stair and had decided it on purpose.

She gave the hour out loud first, because she always gave the hour first, and after that she said nothing whatever for a long stretch of it.

The only sounds in that room were the lamp and the boards under two sets of feet and a woman who had lost her twenties behind other people's paperwork for eleven years and had got some of them back in June and was not thinking about that at all.

He came first, with his face in the side of her neck, and she held the back of his head in one hand and did not read anything, and she did not tell him the date afterwards, and he noticed that she had not.

"Twelve o'clock," she said, about four minutes later, with her mouth against his hair. "No. That is a lie and I am not going to lie to you tonight. That is the one thing I have left and you are going to have all of it."

"Then don't."

"I am going to say it anyway." She got up off the edge of the table and stood over him and put both hands on his shoulders. "You are going to spend that account until there is nothing in it and then you are going to spend what is in you, and you know the arithmetic of it better than I do because I have had it in a drawer since the sixth of May and you have had it in your chest since the fourteenth of March."

"And you are going to file the true sentence about it."

"I am going to file the true sentence about it," said Ines Duarte, "and it is going to be the second line of a return that says no rule in this city covers any part of it, and it is going to be read at a counter and put in a pile by a woman who has never heard of you, and that is what it is for. That is all it is for. It is not for stopping you. Nothing in this city is for stopping you."

She came down onto him and said the hour and it was not an hour at all.

She had her thigh between his and her hand flat on the boards beside his hip, and she got him up under her and held him there at the top and said the fifteenth of July into his mouth with her forehead against his, which is not a date any return in this city will carry and which was the only thing she said out loud in that room for about nine minutes.

Then she let him have it, and she watched his face while it went, which she has done four times in four years and has never once told him about, and she was still watching when he finished and she said the hour again, and it was right that time, and neither of them said so.

## What She Took Away

She washed at the basin in the corner of that room with cold water, and then she dried her hands on a square of paper towel and stood with her back against the wall for a moment, looking at the door.

"The return is on the table by the door and it goes at four with the ordinary run and I have not asked you for one word of it and I am not going to send you anything to correct, because you do not get to correct a thing about yourself and you have known that since the seventh."

"And the door."

"The door stays shut and the reason is on a book in a clerk's hand and I will not be able to say anything about that in four years."

She got her coat off the step and put it on and did not fasten it.

"One conversation," she said. "That was it. I said the thing that is true and I said it once and you are not going to get it twice from me and you are not going to get it from anybody else in this city, and in October you may stand in a room with nine seals on it and there will be nobody in that room who has told you the truth about this except the woman you are looking at, and I will not be in it."

"You will be in it."

"I will be in the corridor," said Ines Duarte. "Where the clause puts me. Which is what you bought in a notebook and what I told you the price of in June and what I am telling you again now for free, because you have not listened to one word of it."

She opened the door onto four steps and a hall and an intake desk where a woman with no gift at all in her would be sitting until six.

She did not say goodnight and she did not thank him and she went up the steps and out into the street, and the door shut behind her, and the day's book in the corridor said a closed sitting begun at ten past seven and no end to it, and the lamp was still on over the middle table, and the return was going down a chain at four o'clock in the ordinary way carrying a second line that was true and was going to be put in a pile.

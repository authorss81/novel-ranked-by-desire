# Unrecorded, Carried By

The office that paints the ranks wall came in at about six in the morning on the eighth of October and was finished by about eight, and Beatrix Halm had been standing at the north end of it since half past four.

She had come for the card. There was no card coming, because the card is painted in paint at half past four on the thirtieth of September and it does not move again until the thirtieth of December.

She had come because on the ordinary weekend before this a carter off Lamplit Row had told her there was a man in Cordwain going about saying the wall was being scrubbed, and she goes and looks at walls, and at half past four there was a boy with a paste brush and a ladder at the foot of it.

## Two Hundred And Twenty-Eight

He came up the hill at about ten to six with his coat buttoned to the throat, because it is the eighth of October and there is a wind coming off the ridge that takes the paint off a ladder in about four minutes.

"One hundred and thirty at the end of June," he said. "Thirty of July, thirty of August, thirty of September, and the eighth. That is two hundred and twenty-eight days old."

"I know what day it is," said Beatrix Halm. "I have been here since half past four and you are the fourth person to tell me a number today."

"You wanted the number."

"I wanted the wall," she said. "The number is yours. It has been yours for four years and it has never once been on a wall."

## The Line Under The Last Tile

There is a blank line under the last tile at the foot of the ranks wall. It has been there longer than Beatrix Halm has been reading that wall, which is nine years, and it is painted in the office's own hand along the foot of the stone, and it carries nothing, and nothing has ever been put on it.

At about a quarter past six on the morning of the eighth of October a boy with a paste brush put something on it.

It was not a figure.

It was two words and a dash and two more words, in the office's own hand, in letters the height of a name on a plate, which is the height the office uses when it is painting something it intends a person at forty feet to be able to read.

Beatrix Halm read it twice before the boy had finished the second word and did not say anything to him and did not stop him and did not ask him anything, because he is not asked anything and has not asked anything in six cards now.

## Where The Words Came From

"Painter," she said, to a man of about fifty who had come in behind him with the bucket and had been on that wall longer than she had.

"Painter."

"What does it say."

He read it. He read it the way a man reads a thing he has laid down four hundred times.

"It is on the list," he said.

"Who wrote it."

"It is on the list," said the painter. "The words are on the list and the line is on the list and the list came in with the form at a quarter to six and I do the list."

"Is anybody going to tell you what it means."

"No," said the painter. "Nobody tells me anything."

## The Form, And The Two Boxes

The repainting form comes in with the wall every quarter and every month, and it is a sheet of office paper with two boxes at the foot of it under a line that has been on the form since before the Ferrier line was cut.

One box takes the figure of the thing at the address. One box takes the standing of it.

On the morning of the eighth of October both boxes were blank, and the standing sheet of the first had gone out at four in the afternoon on the fourth with a disposition on it in a clerk's hand and a line under that in another woman's hand, and a man off a third court collected it at about half past six and typed both of them into the two boxes at a quarter to six in the morning because that is what the two boxes take.

She was told the form and the sheet and nothing else. She is a clerk and she may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and what she was given was a sheet with two words on it that are the same two words in the disposition.

She put the words in the box because the box was there and the words were there and she has never in nineteen years been asked what a box is for.

## By Ten Past Seven

There were about nine thousand people under that wall by ten past seven because it is an ordinary morning, and by about ten past eight there were about two hundred of them standing in front of the bottom of it reading the last line.

A woman from Cordwain read it out loud twice and got it wrong both times. A tram man said it was Latin. A man in a delivery coat said the Board had admitted something, and a woman beside him said the Board had admitted that it could not count, and both of them said it in the voice people use when they have decided a thing and would like a second person to have decided it first.

"There is not a figure on it," said Beatrix Halm. "Look at it. It is not a figure. You cannot read it the way you read the rest of that wall and neither can they, and if you cannot read it then you cannot check whether it is true, and that is not the same as it being a lie."

"Then what is it."

"It is two words that somebody typed into a box at a quarter to six this morning because the box was there," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the whole of what I have, and I have been reading that wall since half past four and I could not give you one other thing about it."

## The Man Who Knows And Cannot Say

He stood at about nine feet off it with his hands in his coat and watched two hundred people be unable to read a wall they have read for thirty years.

"It is about us," said a man in the delivery coat, at about a quarter to nine, to him and not to her. "You emptied it. That is what it says. It is carried by nobody, and you know who that is."

"I have never seen that form," said Lin Hao. "I have never seen a wall repainted in my life. I worked at the bottom of this city for four years and I never once had a brush."

"Then somebody did it for you."

"Then somebody typed two words into a box," said Lin Hao, "and a boy painted what was in the box, and none of the three of them will ever know what the box was for, and that is the whole of the reaction this city has made to the largest thing that has ever happened in it."

The delivery coat looked at him for about four seconds and then went to look at the figures higher up the wall, which were the same figures they had been on the previous day.

## What The Slate Said

At about a quarter past nine a man carried a slate up to the north end of the wall and propped it against the rail at the south end of it, the way he has done every morning since the second of October, and chalked two words on it in about four-inch letters.

He said it out loud to about eleven people before he put the chalk down.

"That is mine," he said. "That is not the Board's. I made mine up in three days and I have told that to everybody who stands in this lane, and I have never once said the Board took it off me. That has gone up on their own wall in their own hand and they cannot read it either, so we are the same, and I have waited four days for them to come and say so."

He is right about the facts of it and he waited for a thing that was never coming, and Runa Sato, who came round the corner at a walk and had been out on a round since six in the morning, stood at about four feet and let him get to the end of it.

"They are not going to come," she said. "That wall got itself rewritten at six this morning by a boy who was handed a sheet and does not know what is on it, and by the time anybody in this city finds out what was on that sheet it will be four years old and filed in a building where nobody walks."

"Then I will put it under my figure."

"You put whatever you like under your figure," said Runa Sato. "You have been doing it since the second and none of it has been entered anywhere, and this morning the only thing that has changed is that there is a second line on that wall that nobody wrote. That is not a competition and you should not want it to be one."

## What She Wrote Down

She wrote it in the notebook in the small level hand she used for walls, and she wrote it once, and she did not write what it meant because she has spent nine years refusing to write down a thing she has not got.

Then she tore the page out and gave it to him.

"You keep that," she said. "It is the only copy of that sentence in this city that is not on stone. In about four years the chain comes off a book and a plate is scraped and a new one is cut from whatever was on the old one, and a wall does not last that long, and I am not going to be the only person who ever had it."

He folded it in four and put it in the inside of his coat where a brass used to live.

"There," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the reaction. Two words, a dash, two words, a wall a city cannot read, and a man in a delivery coat telling me it is about him, and he cannot tell me it is not, because he does not know either."

Two hundred people went to work under a wall they could not finish reading, and the office that paints it went off down the hill with the bucket, and the boy stayed at the far end of the stone with a plate that was not his business and had not asked one question.

# A Room That Cannot Be Compelled

Agnete Lindqvist has two rooms on the first floor of a house behind the Vestry chapel and a kitchen with a window that looks at a wall, and the door of the first room is shut every day of the week and there is a bench on the landing outside it for the person who is waiting and cannot be let in.

She opened it at about nine in the morning on the twenty-fourth of July and stood aside and let him through, and the room was about twelve feet by nine and it had a table, two chairs, a lamp, a window, and a jug of water that was not for drinking.

## The Rule, In Her Own Mouth

"I am going to say this once and then I am going to make you use it," she said. "A room I stand in is a room in which nothing can be compelled. Not testimony. Compulsion. I cannot make anybody speak and I cannot make anybody stay and I cannot make anybody answer, and there is no instrument in this city that can override it and there is not one person alive who has ever found a way round it, including the two men who have tried in my time."

"How many people have tried."

"Two, and one of them was me," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is the whole of what I am and it has been the whole of what I am for twenty years, and it is worth precisely nothing to anybody until the day somebody needs a room, and then it is worth the entire city, and that is the arrangement I have lived inside since I was ordained."

"How long has this room been like this."

"Eleven years," she said. "I have not been outside one in eleven years. Before that I went in and out of rooms all day and could not be got out of anything, and I complained about it, and then I learned what it was worth, and then I stopped complaining."

## The Number She Has Never Had To Answer For

He had come up the stairs with one question and it had been sitting in his mouth the whole way.

"How many," he said. "The hearing room at the Correction Office holds nine hundred and Agnete — how many can you hold."

She did not answer it for about four seconds, which from her was a long time.

"Nine," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Nine is what I have held. Nine one morning in this room for twenty minutes and I do not remember one single thing anybody said in it, which is what it is supposed to do to a person, and I have held six and I have held three and I have held nine twice, and the last nine was in February."

"Nine and nine hundred."

"I do not know," she said. "There is no rule for it and there is no figure for it, and there has never been a form anywhere in this city on which I would be asked to put the other one."

"I have never been in a room with a hundred people in it that had anything to do with my gift, and I have told you that in four sentences because you are going into a room on the twenty-seventh and I will not have you finding out at nine hundred."

## Two Days And Not Four

"You said the second day."

"I said the second day." She put her hand flat on the table. "Four days of hearing. On the first day the room is an ordinary room and everything anybody says in it can be used against them for the rest of their life."

"I know that because it has happened in eleven years of this and I know it because a man is going to stand up in that room tomorrow morning and say the truest thing he has ever said and it is going to cost him everything he has."

He did not move.

"And on the second day I hold it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And everything said in that room from about ten until about one can be used against no one, because not a person in it can be made to say a word of it, and a statement nobody was compelled to make is not evidence against the person who made it. It is not even evidence for them. It is only true."

"Three hours."

"I have not decided on three hours. I have decided that I will be standing up at about ten and that I will not stop before the chair stops me." She took her hand off the table. "And I am going to tell you what it costs, and I am going to tell you now, four days early, on purpose, so that you cannot tell me afterwards that you did not know and so that you cannot get yourself ready."

## What It Costs

"Eleven years," she said, "and it has not cost me one day of it that anybody could point at, and I want that understood before I say the rest of it, because the rest of it is not a wound."

"I live in one. I work in one. I eat in one. I have not been asked a question in eleven years that I was not asked in a room where the answer could not be taken out of me, and that has been the best eleven years of my working life and I would not go back for anything anybody has offered me."

"Then what does it cost."

"On the twenty-seventh I am going to be outside one for three hours," said Agnete Lindqvist, "in a building with a rail in it and a hundred and fifty people on the rail by the afternoon, and I am going to be holding it the whole time, and I have never once in eleven years been asked what happens to the inside of me when I am holding it away from where I sleep."

She picked up the jug and put it down again without having poured anything.

"That is the whole of the cost and it is not mine to price and I would not have priced it if I could. If it is nothing then I have wasted three hours of the only life I have had in this city. If it is something then I find out in front of a hundred and fifty people and not in here."

## What She Was Going To Do On The Second Day

"I want the whole of it now," he said, "so that I am not guessing about it tomorrow night."

"There is not a great deal of it and I am telling you the shape rather than the content, because the content is the whole of what I have and I am not going to hand it to a man with a brass in his coat in advance."

"Shape."

"In the morning I stand up about ten and I say who I am and that I am on my own book and not the household's and not a man's, and then I give them the parts of it that other people gave me." She closed her hands flat on the table. "I am not going to argue for anything."

"Argue for something and it is testimony and it can be used against the person who gave you the part you are arguing with. I am not going to be used and I am not going to spend anybody."

"And the parts are true."

"Every one of them is true and not one of them can be used against the person who gave it to me, and that is the entire point of the room and it is why I have been in one for eleven years."

She looked up.

"You are going to hear a great many names over three hours, and you are going to be in the fourth row, and you are not going to be able to do one single thing about any of them, and I have not been able to do one single thing about any of them for eleven years, and it is the best work I have ever done."

## The Thing She Tells Him Last

"Now the other thing, and then you can go, because I have not been out of this room for two days running and I am going to go downstairs and sit on the bench outside my own door."

"Tell me."

"In that room on the twenty-seventh nobody can be compelled," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Not them. You either."

She let it stand there for a moment in a room about twelve feet by nine.

"You have spent four years being a man who cannot be made to do anything," she said, "and in this city that has been the only freedom anybody has ever been able to offer you, and I am going to take a piece of it off you on the twenty-seventh, and I am telling you now, four days early, so that you cannot get used to it first."

"In my room you will not be able to refuse. Not because anybody is putting a question to you. Because the room will not distinguish between a person who is answering and a person who is sitting in a chair with his hands together."

"And I cannot stop it."

"You cannot stop it and you cannot shorten it and you cannot leave it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I cannot shorten it either. That is the trade and I have had it since I was twenty-five."

## What Was True And What He Did About It

He went back down the stairs and out into the yard behind the chapel at about half past ten in the morning with two days and forty-eight hours in front of him, and there was a bench on a landing and a yard with a wall on the other side of it, and he sat on the bench for about two minutes because he could not think of anywhere else to sit.

She was right about the first day. He had come up those stairs on the twenty-fourth knowing that a room was going to be somewhere in the middle of the twenty-seventh and a woman was going to be in it, and he had come up them not knowing that a room which cannot be compelled was going to take the last thing he had that nobody could take.

For four years the one thing nobody had been able to do to him was decline. He had said no to nine women in a kitchen, to a chair with four things on the table, to a standing, to a room at the end of a corridor, to eleven words in a kitchen and eleven words in a corridor, and on the ninth of July he had said yes to four seals and had meant to pay it in somebody else's currency.

It was the only standing he had that the city could not administrate against, and a woman on a landing behind a chapel in Cordwain had just told him that she was going to take it off him for three hours in front of a hundred and fifty people on the morning of the twenty-seventh.

He got up off the bench.

On the way out through the yard he worked out that she had told him four days early and not two, and that she had told him on a working morning in the middle of a working week rather than at the end of it, and that she had done both of those things on purpose.

And he had not been able to do one single thing about either of them, and that on the first of October there would be nine seals in a room at the end of a corridor and he would have to stand up and be asked and he would have to say yes.

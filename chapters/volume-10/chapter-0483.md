# The Fraction Her Body Can Take

She sent for him at about seven in the evening on the twelfth of September with four words on the back of a dispensary docket and he was in the ward of the Vestry clinic at about nine, and the ward was dark along its length with four people asleep in it and one lamp burning over the desk at the far end.

"Come in and shut the door," said Tessa Okonkwo. "The door is the whole of it. It closes and it is the quiet, and if it opens then four people in this room wake up and I have to be a doctor instead of being what I am going to be for about an hour."

"The lamp."

"The lamp is on because I do not do anything in the dark," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That was true in March and it is true tonight and you are not going to be the reason it stops being true."

She had her sleeves turned back and her cuffs undone and the cloth ledger on the desk with the strap round it, and she did not sit down.

## One Question, Asked Once

"There is one thing I am going to ask you and I am going to ask it once, and if you say no I will not ask you again tonight and I will not ask you again in October and you will never know that I asked."

"Ask it."

"Lie down on the table."

He looked at her.

"That is the whole of the request and I have thought about it for six days and I have not found a way to dress it up," said Tessa Okonkwo. "You will not be able to lie down anywhere else in this city this month and I have watched you for four years and I know what you look like at about five in the morning. Lie down on the table."

"Yes."

"Two things before you do, and then I am going to stop talking for a while." She put one hand flat on the edge of the table. "One. This is not me taking something off you to get you into a bed. I would have got you into a bed on the second of July with about nine words and I did not, and I am not going to pretend tonight that I have been waiting nine weeks."

"And two."

"Two is that when it is done I am going to take a fraction off you, and I am going to tell you the fraction out loud, and you are not going to argue with me about the fraction, because I am the only person in this building who can do it and I am the only one in this city who will do it and you have got about twenty days in which to be talked out of something else."

He got his coat off and hung it on the back of the chair she pointed at, which was a chair in eleven years of this ward that had never had a coat on it.

## The Two Things Are Separate

"Not tonight," said Tessa Okonkwo, when he looked at her.

"Not tonight."

"You have been carrying that sentence around since the sixteenth of March like a coin in your pocket." She turned the ledger strap over. "I said it in June about a chest and I meant it then and I do not mean it now and I am going to be honest about the fact that I have changed my mind, because a doctor who changes her mind and does not say so is a doctor who is hiding a treatment."

"What is the treatment."

"It is nine minutes and it is going to happen in about two hours and it is not what you have been imagining, and it is the only reason I have sent for you on a night when the ward is full and I have been on my feet since six."

She looked at him properly for the first time. "You are going to lie down and I am going to do what I have done about four hundred times since the second of December. I am going to take a thirtieth of what is sitting in your chest."

"You will feel considerably better for about a day."

"And I am going to be the one who pays for it, and that is the arrangement, and it has been the arrangement since long before this household had a name." She stopped.

"Since before this household had a name," said Tessa Okonkwo. "You know what I am. Say you know what I am."

"You hold a body past the point it should stop and the damage comes into you."

"That is correct and it is the only sentence anybody in this city needs about me." She unbuttoned one more cuff. "And you have been getting the surplus off strangers at a wall for four years and it has been coming out of your frame, and something in your chest is used up, and there is not one office in Ostara that can give that back and there is not one instrument and there is not one doctor in this building who can do it either."

"Except you."

"Except me, and I have been waiting since the fifteenth of July, and I did not do it then because you had not been told about your chest and I do not touch a man who has not been told what is being taken." She put her hand flat on the table. "You told nine women on the twenty-sixth of August. That is what I have been waiting nine weeks for and you did it at a kitchen table and not to me."

"You could have asked me."

"I could have and I did not and it is mine," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Lie down."

## Slow, And In A Ward

He lay down on the table in his clothes and she stood at the end of it for four seconds looking at him the way she looks at a patient's face on a round, which is to say entirely without comment, and then she wheeled the screen round until it stood between the table and the two beds on her side.

"The screen is not for them," said Tessa Okonkwo. "It is so that whoever walks in has to walk round it, and a person who has walked round something is not surprised by what is behind it."

She unbuttoned the cuffs and then the front of the coat and put the whole of it over the foot of the bed, and she did it in the order of a person taking a coat off at the end of a long day, and not in the order of anybody in this city who has wanted a man.

She was a broad woman with a doctor's hands and she was not small and she did not hurry. Her stomach was not flat and she has never once appeared to have an opinion about it. Her hair had been knotted up out of the way on one side at about six and left that way, which is what happens to a woman who has washed her face once and has been on her feet ever since.

The stippling on her forearms is hers and has been hers since before any of them knew her, and he looked at it once and she saw him look at it and said nothing about it at all.

Then she took the blanket off the foot of the bed and put it round herself and sat down on the edge of the table beside his hip.

"Get your shirt off," she said. "Not after. Now, while I am still dressed enough to be a doctor about it."

He got his shirt off.

"That is better. You have had four women in four rooms this year, and in every one of those rooms a lamp was put where somebody wanted it put. That lamp stays where it is, because I set it at eight o'clock at the right height for working and not for this, and I am not moving it, and if you want it moved you can get up off my table and move it yourself."

She got a hand under him at the hip and turned him over without hurry, and she put a knee up on the trolley to do it, which took the weight off her own back and which she did not mention doing.

"Now come here and do the first thing you want to do, and do it properly, and do not wait for me to tell you what it is. I was on my feet at six this morning and I am not going to spend the next hour giving directions in my own ward."

He did.

She let him four seconds and then put a hand round the back of his neck and turned his head, and she kissed him the way she has watched people do in a ward at about two in the morning, which is badly and for a long time, and she was the one making the noise.

"There," she said. "Now you know what I have been not thinking about, and I have not put a hand on your chest once, and I have about two hours yet."

She had him the length of the table in four seconds flat and she made almost no noise doing it, because she was being careful with the door, and then she sat back on her own heels and looked down at the two of them for about two seconds the way she looks at a plate she has just set down on a trolley.

"I am going to be unkind about something," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and you are going to let me, and it is that you are the best-looking thing I have had on this table in about four years and I have had four hundred people on it."

She got a watch off the desk on its chain and held it up where he could see the face of it.

"Time me."

"I am not doing that."

"You are going to do it, and you are going to say the figure out loud, because four years of standing on a wall have taken the lying out of you and left the counting, and a watch on a chain is the only instrument in this ward that a man cannot argue with." She held it still. "Say it."

He said it.

"In eleven years I have never taken that much out of anybody," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I have held about four hundred of them since the second of December. Go on."

## What She Wants, In A Doctor's Voice

He put his hand where he wanted it and she permitted it to stay, and then she lifted it off him, put it somewhere else, and made him watch her do that instead, and when he objected she said nothing at all and left it where she had put it.

"Slower. Yes. That one."

She was warm and heavy, and she had been on her feet since six, and he could feel the tiredness in the top of her back when she got her weight onto her forearms, and it went out of her about halfway down and he felt exactly where it went.

She got a hand between them and took hold of him and worked him with a slowness that was not kindness and was not hurry, and she watched his face the whole time and told him what she could see happening on it, which is a thing doctors do and which no other woman in this city has ever done to him.

"That is better. You have been somewhere else for about four years and your face is doing something now."

She gave instructions the way she gives them across a trolley, short and unhurried and without a doubt in any of them, and inside about a minute he understood that the level voice was not composure. She was on shift.

"That is a quarter of what I came into this room for, and I have not touched your chest once, and I want you to notice that, because in about two hours I am going to put both hands on it and you are not going to enjoy a minute of it."

She got a hand between them and closed it on him where she could reach him and held him exactly there and went on at a rate that did not vary.

"Here is what I have been not thinking about," said Tessa Okonkwo. "You told nine women about that on the twenty-sixth of August and I was not in the room, and I heard about it from the sister at the dispensary on the twenty-seventh."

"My first reaction was professional and my second one was not, and I have been carrying both of them for sixteen days."

"Which was which."

"My first was: good, he knows, he will stop doing it at a wall with nine hundred people round him." She shifted her grip. "My second was: he will not stop. He will do it in a square, in the open, in October, with his name on it, and he will do it until there is nothing left in him, and he will do it standing up, because he has never once in four years done anything on his knees."

"That is not an accusation."

"It is a forecast, and it is the reason you are on my table," said Tessa Okonkwo. "And I have worked out what happens to a person on the first of October when that thing goes out over four days to about a million people, and I am the only one in that household who can take a piece of it without it going into the floor."

## The Middle Of It

She got him over onto his back after about nine minutes with a hand between his shoulder blades, and it was not gentle and she did not apologise for it, and there was a blanket under him, because a trolley is cold and she had put one there before she started.

"Faster," she said. "And do not stop because I am going to talk about a fever."

He did not stop.

She took him all the way in and then stopped, without moving at all, with her palms spread on his ribs and the weight of her standing on him, and the ward went on being a ward around the two of them for four seconds and neither of them put a hand on anything.

Then she began to move down onto him the whole length of her, slow, the way she does everything, until there was nothing at all between them.

"That," she said, from about four inches off his mouth, "is what it is for. And four years of standing under a wall handing a minute of yourself to whoever walked past has bought you a cough and a chest, and it has bought me one night, one table, and a fraction of about a hundred and forty seconds."

"You have not had a fraction yet."

"I have not," said Tessa Okonkwo. "I am going to, and I am going to do it after, and I have told you the order on purpose and I am not going to be talked out of it."

"Two women in a ward off Cordwain came in on the same day in July," said Tessa Okonkwo, from somewhere above him. "One of them was in service and one of them had a child on her arm, and both of them had been at that wall on the eleventh."

"And neither of them could tell me a single thing that had happened to her, except that she had been cold in the afternoon of the eleventh of July and had not been able to say so."

"It was the eleventh, and I have never once known what day of the week it was, and I am not going to find out."

"It was," she said. "And the point is that I could not enter the sentence. Nothing in this city will take it. I have wanted a form for it since the fourteenth of March and I have looked for one in four buildings, and if I had written one myself it would be a form that one doctor invented on one night because she was upset, and in four years it would be the only piece of evidence in Ostara and it would be worthless."

She got her weight onto her forearms on either side of his ribs and stayed there, with her knees on the blanket and her thighs against the outsides of his, and she was already wet and she did not pretend otherwise about it.

Then she put him inside her with two fingers first, and made him watch her do that as well, because she had decided that in this room a man was going to be shown all of it or none of it and she did not do half measures in a ward.

"This is not affection and I am not going to let either of us call it affection," she said. "Affection is what the eight other women in that house are going to call it, and they are entitled to, and I would like it put down somewhere that I have told them nothing and that you have told them nothing, because I am not theirs to explain and they are not mine."

"It is what it is."

"It is the best hour I have had in this building in about nine weeks, and there is a woman in the next ward who will wake up if I am careless, and I do not care, and I am going to be careful anyway. Because that is what a doctor is and not what a woman is, and I am both, and tonight the doctor is working overtime."

Then she got both hands behind his knees and drew them up over her own shoulders and held them there, which she had done once in this building before with a different man and had never once done on purpose, and he understood that she had decided to and did not know when.

When she came it went on for a long time and she let it, and she got both hands behind his head and kept them there and made a noise into his hair that she plainly had not meant to make, and she did not stop and she did not apologise for it.

It went on longer than he expected and neither of them counted anything at all.

He came after her with both hands flat down her back and the cough went about nine seconds later, and she said three short words about it into her own shoulder and then, for the rest of the night, nothing at all.

## Afterwards, And Then The Other Thing

She sat on the edge of the table with her feet on the floor and her elbows on her knees for about a minute and a half, and he lay where she had left him, and neither of them said anything.

"Now the part I have been waiting six days for," she said. "Sit up. Do not get off the table."

## The Fraction

She got her chair round and sat facing him with her knees against his knees and put both hands flat on his chest, one on each side of the place where the cough starts, and she did not go straight at it.

"Before I do this I am going to say three things, and the first is that you may stop me at any point by saying *Linen*, because that is what we use in this ward for a patient who needs the room back, and I will stop, and you will not owe me anything and I will not be disappointed in a way you can see."

"I will not say Linen."

"I know. That is the second thing." She leaned in and looked at him. "The fraction is a thirtieth. Not a tenth. A tenth is what I did to a rank-five in May and I could not lift my arms for two days and I have not been able to since without thinking about it, and a thirtieth I can do four times in an hour and I will be able to lift my arms at six in the morning."

"And the third thing."

"The third thing is that this is happening because I decided it and not because you asked, and that you are not going to thank me for it. And if gratitude turns up later on its own, I will take your hand off me, and I have the arms for it."

Then she took the breath in and put it out and her hands went from warm to something else.

He felt it go. It was not cold. He had expected cold for four years and it was not cold at all; it was a pulling, somewhere under the place where the cough lives, of the exact kind of thing you feel when somebody lifts a weight off a shelf that has been in a room too long.

It took about a hundred and forty seconds and it felt like getting up out of cold water.

"That is it," said Tessa Okonkwo. "A thirtieth. And that is what one person gives away on the first of October if a million of them are standing still for it, and I am telling you the figure now while you are on my table so that you can hold it, and you are going to need it in three days, and I am the only person alive who is going to have it."

"How much room is left in you."

She took her hands off and looked at them and did not answer for about four seconds.

"Enough for four months at a thirtieth," she said. "And then I stop, and I do not stop because of you and I do not stop because of October. I stop because I have been filling an inventory since the second of December and there is a floor under me in this building and I hit it in April and I have been managing since."

"Managing."

"I have been managing by taking less off people than they need," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Which means four people in this city have walked out of here in eight months slightly better than they should have been and not one of them is alive because of me. That is what managing is and I would like it on the record that I know the word for it."

## What She Had Chosen

"And on the first," he said.

"On the first of October you are going to open that thing in a square in the open with your name on it, and I am going to be in it, and I am not going to be there as your wife or as a woman from the house or as somebody who came to see you do a brave thing."

"What are you going to be there as."

"I am going to be there as the only person in that city who can take a piece of it," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I am going to have a table of mine own at the north end by the Vestry gate from about ten in the morning, and people are going to be brought to it, and I am going to take a thirtieth off anybody who comes and cannot get out of the street."

"You have told nobody."

"I have told Agnete Lindqvist, in the last week of August, in a yard off Cordwain, in four minutes, and she is going to let me put the table there on the Church's ground. She is not going to put the Church's name on it and she is going to stand about nine feet from it for four days saying nothing."

"She has not said yes to your account and she is going to be nine feet from your table anyway, because she is the only person in this city who can hold a room that nothing can be done in, and she would rather that be useful than tidy."

"Have you told the other eight."

"No," said Tessa Okonkwo. "And you are not going to tell them, and here is why, and it is the last thing I am going to say tonight and I am saying it lying on a trolley in a ward with four patients in it, which is not where I say important things."

She pulled her sleeve down over her forearm and did it neatly.

"Three women drew against that pot on purpose on the fourteenth of August and took the cold themselves, and every one of them said out loud that they did it because they wanted to be given something back."

She got up off the trolley. "On the first of October eight of them are going to be standing in a square with their hands on it, and the ninth of us is going to be at a table by the Vestry gate, and not one of the eight is going to be the one that does it. Nine women cannot do it and one woman cannot do it. That is how the arithmetic comes out, and it is not a guess."

"And that is the arithmetic that has been sitting in this city since the fifteenth of July, and nobody has said it out loud."

She said the rest of it looking at the lamp.

"You cannot empty it. I know that. You have told a kitchen full of women that you cannot empty it and you were right and you will be right in October."

"What you can do is stand in a square and be the reason it goes out into a whole city at once. And when it goes out in a whole city at once there are going to be a great many people in it who cannot get out of the street, and I am the only one of the nine who can take a piece of what is going past them."

"And I have chosen that, and I chose it on the twenty-seventh of August in a dispensary with a docket in my hand, and I have not told one of the nine and I am not going to, and you are going to walk out of this room and keep it."

He looked at her.

"That is not fair to them."

"It is not fair to any of them, and I have thought about it for sixteen days and it is the only arrangement available and it is mine," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Now get off my table, because I have to change the linen at four and you are in the way of about nine people, and if you cough on the way down those stairs I will tell you off about it and I will not stop you and we agreed that in August."

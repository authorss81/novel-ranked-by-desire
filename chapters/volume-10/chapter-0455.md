# A Counter In The Auditorate

The box office at the Bellhouse has four windows and three of them sell tickets and the fourth one has been a counting counter since the first of January, and the woman who has kept it for eight months is called Ada and has never once been asked to explain what it is for, because the answer to that question is on no register and the room she sits in is a stair hall.

"You cannot do that here," she said, on the morning of the seventh of July, to a man at the third window who had been standing at it for four minutes with a slip of nothing in his hand. "There is a count at this window and you do not get a turn at it unless you have come in."

"I have come in."

"You have come up the stair," said Ada.

## What A Counter Is

"A counter counts two things," she said, when he did not go away. "What comes in and what goes out. And that is the whole of it and it is two columns and eleven years, and if the two columns do not agree by the end of a day then somebody in this building has walked out of a room they should not have walked out of, and I put a line under it and I carry the line to the woman who sends the sheets."

"And who sends the sheets."

"The Auditorate," said Ada.

She said it the way she said most things, which was once, without weight, to a man at a window.

## The Division With No Building

"That is the first time anybody has asked me for the name of it in eight months and I want to tell you what it is, because it is the thing nobody can find."

She turned the sheet over and squared it against the sill.

"The Auditorate is not a building. There is no door in this city with its number on it and there has not been one since the first of January, and there is not going to be one."

"It is a set of counters and about fourteen people and one rule, and the counters are lent out to wherever there is a flow of people to be counted, which is why I am at a box office and there is a woman on a landing at the Exchange counting the same thing four floors from a keeper's rail."

"Why is that."

"Because the Board cannot see this," said Ada. "The Board reads returns and a return says what a person attests and an attested deed has a witness in it or a stamp on it, and there is a category of thing that has neither, and the Auditorate is the office that counts that category. That is all. That is the entire office. It has had the shape of something since the first of January and no name until this year and no building at all."

He had come up that stair at about eleven in the morning on purpose and had known since the first of July that there was a division holding the fourth part of a carried requisition that kept no list of addresses, and he had stood at the bottom of the stair for about four minutes with his hand on the rail.

He had not gone and stood in front of a door, because there was not one.

"Third window," said Ada. "You have still not come in."

## The Rule She Worked Out Herself

"Two columns," she said. "In and out. And there is a third thing that is not a column and it is the whole of my work and I have never written it in a book because nobody has ever asked me what a column is for."

She turned the sheet round and pushed it at him the way people push things at counters, which is to say without ceremony and right at the gap in the glass.

"A person who comes in and does not go out."

The hall went on being a hall.

"That is not in anything anybody has written," said Ada. "I worked it out at a different counter in eleven years. A return cannot show it, because a return is attested and this is not attested, and there is no instrument in this city that knows the difference between a person who has been somewhere and a person who arrived and stayed and was never let out, and I count them anyway, because somebody has to."

"How many."

"On an ordinary week it is two or three," she said. "In June it was five and I wrote five and I thought nothing of it. In the last six weeks I have not been able to write a week in one figure and I have stopped trying to make it tidy and I have started writing the time beside each one instead, because a counter who cannot make a figure tidy writes the time down, and that is what counters do."

## The Sheets She Had Never Been Told About

"That is the first thing," said Ada. "The second thing is that I have been handing these sheets to a woman for eight months and she has never once said thank you and I did not notice, because I did not know what they were for."

"Wren Kallo."

"She is the officer of counter-forensics and she sends me the sheets and she reads them and she sends them on up a chain that ends in a chair I have never seen." Ada turned the sheet over again. "I asked her in the winter what she did with them and she said she kept them, and I took that at face value, because I have worked behind a counter for eleven years and a woman who keeps a thing is doing her job."

"You asked her in the winter."

"I asked her in the winter," said Ada, "and she answered me in about eleven words and she has not said another one since, and I am a counter and I count what comes in and I have now counted one woman coming into my counter and going out again with a piece of paper and not saying anything about it."

"That is not a secret she is keeping from you on purpose."

"I do not know what it is and it does not matter," said Ada. "What matters is that she has taken six years of sheets off me and the round has got my returns and my returns have a rule on them that she wrote and I did not, and a man who came up that stair four minutes ago asked me what a column is for."

## Isolde Marchetti Came At Half Past Twelve

She came in the way she comes in, which is as though the building has been waiting for her, and she went to the third window because she has gone to the third window since the autumn, and she put her hand flat on the sill in the way of a woman who has been counting a thing for six years.

"Figure for this week," said Isolde Marchetti.

Ada gave her the figure.

Then she said, "Do you want the other one."

The hall was not quiet. It had not been quiet all morning and it was not going to be, and there were two hundred people going through it and three windows selling tickets and one window that was not.

"No," said Isolde Marchetti.

"You have never asked me for it," said Ada, "and I have never offered it to you, and I have offered it to a man in a hat at about eleven o'clock this morning, and he did not know what he was being offered either."

Isolde Marchetti did not move her hand.

"Ada," she said. "How long have you been keeping a third column."

"Nine months," said Ada. "I have been counting them at this window since the first of October and I counted that house of yours weekly for the four weeks before that because somebody asked me to and nobody told me why."

"Who asked you."

"An officer of counter-forensics," said Ada, "in a lane off Cordwain, on the twenty-eighth of September, on a working afternoon, and she asked me in nine words and I said yes, and I have been counting one house a week ever since, and I did not tell you, and you did not tell me you were counting it yourself, and we have been doing the same sum in the same week and neither of us has ever once handed the other a figure."

That was true and it sat in the gap in the glass between them for about four seconds.

## What Isolde Does With It

"You have had a rule on your own counter since the first of October," said Isolde Marchetti, "and you did not write it in a book."

"No."

"And you are going to write it in a book now."

"I am going to write it in a book when somebody gives me a book. I have four columns and a time and a line under the week."

"What I have not got is anybody to hand it to who has said what it is for, and I have had that twice today, from a man in a hat and from you, and the two of you said different things. I am going to write down both of them and I am not going to decide between them until the ninth of July."

Isolde Marchetti looked at her for a while, and the hall went on being a hall, and Ada's hands were flat on the sill on either side of the last figure she had given anybody.

"The ninth," said Isolde Marchetti.

"The ninth," said Ada, "and you may tell me on the eighth or on the ninth or on the tenth and I will write down which."

She went out into the stair hall and did not say anything else, and she has counted her own house every week for six years and has never once used the count, and on the seventh of July she was carrying two thirds of a sum she had kept to herself since the autumn and she had not asked for it once in twenty-nine weeks.

## The Part He Did Not Say

He came back at about four in the afternoon and put his hand on the sill of the third window and did not say anything for about ten seconds.

"There is no door," he said at last.

"There is no door."

"A clerk in that room at the end of a corridor refused to tell me the name of the division holding the fourth part of a sealed finding on the first of July, because if I had it I would have stood in front of it in four minutes and then it would have had a name in a book with my address in it."

"Then she was right," said Ada, "and so is the door, and you have just spent about ten seconds working out that you have found the office instead of the door, and I am going to tell you the same thing I tell every person who comes up that stair: a counter counts and gets its sheets sent on and has never once in eleven years had anything come back the other way."

"Not this week."

"Not this week," said Ada. "That is new and I have not got a word for it and I am not going to invent one before the ninth."

He wrote the day down and the hour and nothing else, and he went out up the stair hall and down into a street in the middle of a city where nine thousand people were going to pass under a wall in four days.

He had learned one thing in a morning that no rule in that building covered, which was that a division which counts what the Board cannot see does not keep a list of addresses and does keep a list of people who come in and do not go out. Somebody in it had been keeping one for eight months and had been asked twice what she was doing it for and had answered herself.

At the head of the stair he stopped, because the two of them had been counting the same house in the same week for twenty-nine weeks and neither had ever handed the other a figure, and one of them was going to hand over a sheet on the ninth, and he had no instrument in this city that could reach either of them.

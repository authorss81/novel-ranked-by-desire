# Five Seals And One Chair

The room at the end of a corridor off a second landing had not sat since the thirtieth of June and could not sit again until the first of October, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth of August it was being prepared by two women who were not going to be in it.

Hanneke Ruis was drafting the quarterly in the counting room two floors under a sales floor with the door propped and one lamp on the table, and Solveig Rask had the real book chained in both arms and was not proposing to unchain it.

"It goes on the table at half past eight on the day and it stays on the table until the room seals," said Solveig Rask. "And there is a chair at that table that is not a member's chair, and I have carried that book up a stair two hundred times and I have never once been allowed to sit down with it."

"It has been Alma Quayle's since the fourth of December," said Hanneke Ruis, "and neither of us has ever asked her about it, and you have not either."

He had been in the corridor outside the room at the end of the corridor for two hours and ten minutes on the strength of a clause of thirty-eight words that he had entered himself, standing up, in nine minutes, on the twenty-first of June, to keep six women from having to be fetched through a man.

There was no business to attend. A quarterly is drafted and it goes on the table and it is sealed or it is not, and every hour a member spends inside that building is an hour nearer the thirtieth of September, and the thirtieth of September is a fixed day.

## The Fifth Item

"I have got one thing to tell you and it is the only thing I know," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I would rather you heard it from me at about nine in the morning than from a plate in a hall in about four years."

"Tell me."

"The list has grown by one since the thirtieth of June." She did not look up from the block. "There was a requisition answered, a composition certified, a quarter closed and a return going out at four. There is a fifth thing on it now and the fifth thing has no form under it."

"What is the fifth thing."

"It is the disposition," said Hanneke Ruis. "A held thing with no person in it, claimed by one body and answered by another, and on the first of October a room is going to be asked what is to be done with it, and the answer goes into the fourth block of a quarterly."

"And a disposition is a figure in the fourth block of a quarterly."

"And a figure in the fourth block is taken off a certificate of the composition of the address," said Hanneke Ruis, "and there has not been a certificate of that composition since the fourteenth of March, and a form cannot be drafted without one, and so I am going to write that block out of my own head on the morning of the thirtieth in my own hand with my name at the foot of it, and it will be the seventh time."

"You have written it six times."

"Six," said Hanneke Ruis. "The seventh is the one where I would like somebody to know in advance that it is happening."

She put the pen down.

"And the disposition is not a figure. I do not know what goes in that block for a thing that has never been held by anybody, and neither does the keeper above me, and there is nobody in this city I can ask, and I have been two days on it."

## The Arithmetic He Was Carrying

"One hundred and eighty-eight days," he said, because he had started saying them and could not stop, and a man who has said a number out loud every morning for a month does not get to decide not to. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with twenty-eight is one hundred and eighty-eight."

"It is two days past the weekly one," said Hanneke Ruis. "The sheet went out on the twenty-sixth and it went to nine persons and one of the nine is a woman in a kitchen off Ferrier Street who counts a house weekly for six years and never once used the count for anything."

"Twenty-two times now."

"Twenty-two," said Hanneke Ruis. "One has gone out every week since the first of April, when a standing of three could not carry it and administration began against the composition instead of against the man, and it goes to that door every week whether anybody opens it or not, and the reason I know the figure is that I drafted it."

She turned a page.

"You have been standing in that corridor for two hours and ten minutes and I have watched you come down that stair four times, and I want to say one thing about it and then I am going back to my block."

"Say it."

"The standing sheet goes out at four this afternoon," said Hanneke Ruis. "It carries a claim in three hundred and ninety words from a chaplain of twenty years, and an answer given in a room by a man who conceded, and four lines on a shelf in a building off a third court."

"And at the head of every line it carries the address, and the heading of the whole thing says *the reservoir, held by no person, claimed by the Church of the Quiet Number, answered by the subject*, and there is not one name in it."

"That is a good heading."

"That is an expensive heading and it is not yours," said Hanneke Ruis. "A heading is entered first and read back before the body, and a clerk who may only enter wrote that in thirty seconds and could not be asked whether she intended it, and in four years a stranger with a folder is going to read *subject* on it and be right about what it means and wrong about who is holding the paper."

She picked the pen up again.

"And on the first of October nine seals are going to sit at that table and read a sheet with your street on the top of every line, and not one of the nine is you, because of a clause you wrote in about eleven seconds in June."

"That is the cheapest and most permanent thing I have ever done."

"Yes," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is. I have thought about that for about a week and I have not worked out a way to tell you that would not make you sound pleased with yourself, so I am not going to."

## The Chair That Does Not Count

She came up at about half past ten and she was carrying a bundle of her own division's paper and she did not speak to him in the stair.

Alma Quayle had been recused on her own signature since the twelfth of December and there is no sheet for lifting one and a recusal is lifted by the subject and by nobody else, and he was the only man alive entitled to say the eleven words that would put her back in a chair that counted, and he had had them in him for forty-nine days and had said them to no one.

Forty-nine: twenty-one days left in July after the ninth, and twenty-eight of August.

She went into that room and sat down at the end of the table in the chair that is not one of the nine, with her bundle on her knees, and she did not take her coat off.

## What She Did

He was in the corridor and the corridor is nine feet long, and at about eleven he went and stood at the end of it with his back to the wall, and at about a quarter past eleven the door of that room opened about two inches and put out a hand.

He did not go.

The hand went back in and the door shut and the corridor was nine feet long again.

Then, at about twenty to twelve, Alma Quayle came out into the corridor and put two fingers flat on the oak of the door frame, and looked at him, and did not say one word, and went back in.

That is the whole of the conversation. It is the whole of it, and it is twice now in five months, and the first time was on the nineteenth of June and a clerk had told him at about two minutes to three that a woman in that chair puts a hand on the door when she wants a man to know she came.

He stood in a nine-foot corridor and did not say the eleven words.

He had carried them since the ninth of July and he had said them to a wall in June in four other words and had been right to, and the sentence that would put her back in a chair that counted was eleven words long and cost him nothing that anybody could price and was the only condition on the only offer he would ever be made.

He did not lift it.

He did not lift it in the corridor, and he did not lift it in September, and he has never once in his life regretted a thing more.

## What Was Left

At about twenty past twelve Solveig Rask came down the second stair with the chained book and stopped on the landing, because they had done this for six weeks and it was the only part of the preparation that either of them had ever bothered to do.

"He is still in the corridor."

"He is where he wrote himself," said Solveig Rask. "Do not tell me about it, Hanneke. He has a chest that is spent and a cough and forty-nine days of a sentence in him and a room that is going to ask him for a thing on the first of October, and every one of us in this building would take the corridor if we had written it."

"What I am going to tell you," said Hanneke Ruis, "is that there is a fifth item on that list and no form under it, and that when a room cannot make a form it makes a shape, and a shape is what a stranger reads in four years when he is trying to work out what happened in Ostara in the autumn."

She went on down.

And at four o'clock in the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of August a clerk in that building went out with the standing sheet.

It carried the address at the head of every line, and a claim, and an answer that conceded, and four lines on a shelf, and at the head of the whole thing four words that said there was a reservoir and no person and a Church and a subject. The fifth item on it had no form under it at all, and a woman's name written out of her own memory at the foot of a block that said seven.

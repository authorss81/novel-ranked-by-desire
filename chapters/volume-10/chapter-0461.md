# The Hearing He Cannot Refuse

He had been on the step at the north end of the ranks wall for eight days and the nineteenth of July was the ninth, and there was a queue at half past six that had been a queue on the eighteenth and a queue on the seventeenth and had not been a queue at all on the eleventh.

Two days, because seventeen taken from nineteen leaves two, and he was two days into something that does not get better.

He got through about a hundred and ninety hands on the nineteenth. He stood up twice instead of four times and the second time was at about half past two in the afternoon and he got it in one attempt, which he wrote down in the flat book in the inside pocket of his coat with the hour against it, because Ada had told him on the fifteenth that if he was going to do the sums then somebody had to write them down.

Then a clerk of the Correction Office came down the square at about ten past ten with a board under her arm and a boy carrying a step-ladder, and she did not go to a door.

## She Read It At The Wall

She set the ladder against the stone below the card and the boy held it and she went up one rung and read a notice to the north end of the Reading, in the ordinary voice, at about the volume a city uses when it has been read things before.

"Correction Office. A public hearing. A matter carried on the thirtieth of June under five seals. Four days. Anybody may attend at any hour. No person may be compelled to testify in it. The days are not yet set and will be set by a clerk and entered. The room is not yet set."

She read it a second time because the crowd behind her had not heard the first.

Then she came down off the ladder and there were about four hundred people standing there, and about four hundred people had heard a matter they could not name, and a woman at the front with a shopping bag said the whole thing out loud to her friend in the flat ordinary voice of somebody repeating something, and got the words right, which is the part that stays with you.

## The Heading Is A Number

The notice had a number at the top of it in the office's own hand and a date under the number and no name anywhere on the page at all.

Not his name. Not the name of the premises. Not the word.

He read it from six feet away over about four hundred heads, because a man sitting on a step at the foot of a wall has a better view of a notice than anybody standing in front of it.

Somebody in that building had written the notice out and had gone to the trouble to leave every name off it, and had gone to a different trouble to put the number at the top where it could not be missed, and the effect of that was a page of paper that said something enormous in a form nobody could act on.

## The Woman Who Asked Him

She got to him at about twenty past ten, when the crowd had gone back to being a crowd and the ladder was down.

"You were the one on the step."

"I was."

"You know what that is about."

He had it. It was the only sentence he had that would have worked at the foot of that wall at twenty past ten in the morning in front of four hundred people and about nine thousand more over the course of a day, and it was eleven words long, and he had said it out loud in a room on the ninth of July to four seals and had not said one word of it since.

He did not say it.

"I know what it is about," he said, "and it is not mine to say in a street."

The woman looked at him for about two seconds longer than the transaction required.

"That is the second useful thing anybody has said to me this month," she said, and went up the square.

## Alma Quayle

He saw her at about eleven and he had not been looking for her, and she was at the rail with her back to the wall and her face to the crowd, and there was about nine hundred people in that square and she was the only one of them who was not looking at the notice.

She was looking at the ladder.

She had been at the end of a table at the end of a corridor every day since the fourth of December, in a chair that goes in no book, hearing everything and sealing nothing, recused on her own signature and unable to be unseated by anybody including him, and she had come to a public square on the nineteenth of July and stood at a rail like a person.

In that square she was nobody at all, and a woman with a shopping bag would have walked straight through her and been right to.

He got up off the step.

She saw him do it, and she did not move, and he understood while he was getting up that if he crossed that square and spoke to her then four hundred people would turn round and work out what the notice was, in about ninety seconds, from the two of them standing in the same place.

He sat back down.

Alma Quayle stood at a rail at the north end of the ranks wall until about half past eleven and read nothing on the notice at all, and then she went off up the square on foot at the speed of a woman with no errand in the city.

## The Office Of The Man Who Called It

He got to the Correction Office at about half past one in the afternoon and Yusuf Adeyemi was at a desk on the ground floor with four files on it and no coat and a room behind him with the door open.

"I read it at the wall," he said. "Your clerk read it at the wall."

"That was not my instruction."

"It was not my instruction either," said Adeyemi, "and I have been in this office for nineteen years and I know what goes out of it on a morning, and I did not stop it, because a notice about a matter carried under five seals belongs in the one place in this city where nine thousand people a day walk under a thing an office has written."

He put the files in a drawer and shut it.

"Now. What do you want."

"I want it withdrawn."

"No."

## The Only Defence

"I am not going to argue with you about whether a requisition is an instrument or a robbery," said Adeyemi. "You have read the file of the finding and you know what your own answer was and it was true. I have read the minute of the thirtieth of June. Five seals went down at about ten to four and one of them was yours and the clerk read thirty-eight words of a clause out loud while she entered it."

"That is not what I am asking about."

"It is exactly what I am answering." He turned the chair round and put his forearms on the back of it. "Here is the whole of my profession and it is one sentence and I have said it in nineteen years of it and it is on the wall of my hearing room in paint that is four years old. The only defence against a requisition is a hearing anybody may attend."

"The finding is carried. It is administered weekly. There is nothing left to defend."

"Against the requisition, no," said Adeyemi. "Against the finding, yes, and you should know the difference better than anybody in this city, because the finding names a set of premises four times and it is administered against the persons keeping the composition of those premises, and there are eight of them, and a man cannot be administered against because he is not keeping it, and a woman can, because a woman is."

He let that sit in the room for a moment.

"I did not go and find you on the twentieth of February. I was handed a piece of paper in a corridor by a woman who runs public hearings and who put a thing in front of me because nobody else would, and I have wanted it ever since, and I am telling you that because I would rather be hated than doubted."

## What He Would Have Said If He Had Been Allowed

"You want me in it."

"I want the room full and I want the record full and I want you in it and I want the account in it, because a thing that is true and private is worth nothing to anybody, including you, and you have had it private for four years and it has cost you a chest and a cough and a woman at a counter, and if you had taken it into a room on the fourteenth of March none of that would have happened to anybody."

"You cannot compel me."

"I cannot," said Adeyemi. "That is the instrument and not the room. Not one person in this city can put a question to you and have it answered, and you may sit in that room for four days and say nothing at all."

"The record will read that the subject attended and did not speak, and that record goes up to a room that stands at five and has not sat since the first of April and does not sit again until the first of October, and if you say nothing then the only account of that matter in the world will be the Council's, and the Council's account is that a man was handed something he does not own."

He said the last part without any weight on it at all, which was how he said everything.

"That is not a threat."

"It is not. It is the balance of the room and I have told you what it is so that you cannot tell anybody afterwards that you did not know." He put the chair back. "You may take the notice down off the wall yourself if you like. It would be the first thing anybody has ever been allowed to do to a notice on that wall and I would enjoy hearing about it."

"It would change nothing, because the days are set by a clerk and not by me and the clerk has not got a form for a matter nobody will name."

## What Changed

He walked back up the street with a hundred and forty-nine days in his coat and a thing in his chest that had been there since the fifteenth.

One hundred and forty-nine, because ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty, and one hundred and thirty with nineteen days of July is one hundred and forty-nine.

There was now a room in this city in which the reservoir was going to be discussed in public, and it was being read out from a ladder at the foot of a wall he had been sitting under for nine days, and he had not asked for it, and there was not one thing in the four years behind him that could take it back.

And he had got through about a hundred and ninety hands that morning, and in twenty minutes he could have said eleven words that would have named the whole of it to four hundred people, and he had not said them, and they were the same eleven words he had undertaken on the ninth of July to say out loud in a room at the end of a corridor, and he had not said those either.

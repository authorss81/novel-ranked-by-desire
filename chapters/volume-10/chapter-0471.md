# Nine Women And A Number

Eight of them were at the table when he came in at about eight on the evening of the second of August, and the ninth came in at nine minutes past, and he had asked for three days to arrange it and had used all three.

Runa Sato had sent for Ines Duarte from Lantern Row at about four in the afternoon with a runner and had not used the word why. Agnete Lindqvist had walked from a house behind the Vestry chapel across a city she had not crossed in eleven years and had not asked anybody to come with her. Marguerite Vale had come from the Vant's smaller rooms in a dark dress with the sleeves pushed back and had not sat down until the last of them was in.

Nobody made him a place. The table at Ferrier Street has had a head at the top of it since the twelfth of May and the head's name is in his own hand on a board on the landing, and he sat down at the side of it like a man who had been given a chair at somebody else's counter.

"Before I start," he said. "The date first, and the hour with it, and nothing skipped, because that is the shape of a thing being entered and I would rather tell you nine of them that way than one of them any other way."

## What A Carried Requisition Does To A Door

"On the thirtieth of June, at about two in the afternoon, a room of nine people carried a sheet against me under five seals, one of them mine, and the finding says the holding is mine and the ground is mine and the premises are not a person keeping them and the people who sleep under that roof are."

He put his hands flat on the table, which he did about four times in four years.

"It has been administered weekly since the first of July. Every week, from the first day of the quarter, down a chain of offices to the division that holds the description of this building, and that division keeps no list of addresses, and since four o'clock on the thirtieth of June it has had one entry in it and the entry is this door."

Nadia Ferrous said, "How many times."

"Five," said Lin Hao. "One on the first of July and then every seventh day after it, so the first, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second and the twenty-ninth, and the next one is on the fifth of August, which is three days from now. And I have not opened one of them and I have not looked at what is in any of them, and I decided that on the first of July and I have held to it every week since."

"You have had it in your hand since the first of July."

"Thirty-two days," he said. "Thirty of July and two of August, and a week is seven days, and four of them go to the first of August and four days into the fifth. There is a difference between that and nine weeks and it is the only difference I have left."

## Four Days In A Building Off A Third Court

"Then on the nineteenth of July a clerk of the Correction Office set a ladder against the ranks wall at the north end of the Reading and read out a notice, and the notice carried a number and a date and not one word, and four hundred people stood there and worked out what it was about on their own."

He went through it. The four days, the twenty-sixth to the twenty-ninth, and the days set by a clerk who may only enter and cannot be asked to hurry. Eleven hundred and forty people in a room that holds nine hundred. Sixty names and a hundred and forty particulars given by a woman in a room where nothing can be compelled, entered in a book on a shelf that anybody in this city may ask to read.

"And on the twenty-ninth a woman with eleven thousand people behind her put four sentences into that record in three hundred and ninety words," he said, "and the first of them is that it was never yours and she is right, and the second is that it cannot be given back because there is no list, and the third is that the correct disposition is that it be given out."

Marguerite Vale said, "By whom."

"That is the fourth sentence and I will come to it."

## The Sentence He Said Out Loud

"On the thirtieth, at ten in the morning, in four hundred people, I said that it had never been mine. I said it out loud and I said it in the front of a room and about three hundred people went out into the yard rather than sit through the rest of it, and I have had two days to find a way of taking it back and there is not one."

He looked round the table.

"I am not going to be able to take it back. That is the thing I have come to say and I have waited until this table to say it and it should have been said in June."

Beatrix Halm had her hand flat on the wood. She said, "You said it has never been yours."

"It has never been yours either. That is what the sentence says. It is not yours, it is not mine, and the four hundred people heard me say so and about two hundred of them went out into the yard and one of them was a woman in the nineteenth row with her sleeves turned back who said she wanted to be in the room where a decision about people like her got made."

"Agnete wanted the answer to be one the room had gone into," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I said that to him in a yard behind that building and I have not stopped saying it to myself since."

## The Heading With No Name On It

"At half past twelve on the thirtieth an officer made an entry and read it back the way a clerk reads back, the heading first, because a record with a heading that is wrong about itself is a record a stranger in four years will read as a lie."

He did not have it in his hand. He said it out loud.

"The reservoir. Held by no person. Claimed by the Church of the Quiet Number, claimed on the twenty-ninth. Answered by the subject on the thirtieth."

"Not your name."

"Not one word of my name anywhere on that page," said Lin Hao. "Not on the heading, not in the body, and the only word about me on the whole of it is the word *subject*, and in this city that is not a name and not an address and not a person anybody can be produced by."

He put his thumb down on the table.

"I have spent four years making sure there was not one paper in this city with my name on it. There is one. A woman entered it in about thirty seconds and she could not be asked whether she meant to and she did not mean anything."

## The Number

"And this is the number, and it is the only one I have, and it means nothing in this kitchen and I am going to say it anyway because I have been carrying it about for nine weeks and I am tired of it."

"One hundred and sixty-two days. Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is a month of thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with two is one hundred and sixty-two."

Runa Sato said, "That is a count of days and not a count of anything."

"That is a count of days and not a count of anything," he agreed, "and in twenty-eight days it will be one hundred and ninety, and on the morning of the first of October nine seals will be behind a door at the end of a corridor, and those two documents will be on the table in front of them, and one of them says it was never mine."

## The Clause He Bought In June

"There is one more and it is the one I came about, and it is mine, and I put it there myself on the twenty-first of June at a pencil's cost in about eleven seconds."

He said it in the words a clerk read out at the making of a minute.

"Where a minute names premises, no person keeping an address so named may be produced by that minute otherwise than in the presence of the subject, and the subject may not be absent from the making of it. Thirty-eight words. It goes under a minute and is read only by whoever opens that minute in four years."

"A doorway," said Runa Sato.

"A doorway," said Lin Hao. "It cannot be moved and it cannot be closed and it cannot be got out of the way, and I wrote it in nine minutes because I wanted six women in this kitchen not to have to be fetched through a man, and it has cost this house more than it has cost me and I did not know that until about a minute ago."

## The Part He Had Not Said

"There is a rule under the account," he said, "and it is not mine and it is not the Council's and it is not the Bureau's. It is a rule of a standing household and it has been in this city longer than any of those."

"In a standing household, a person may not draw against an account that the head of that household is answerable for, while any person keeping that address has drawn against it."

He put his hands flat on the table again.

"That is the whole of it in one sentence and I have had it since the first of July and I have not put it in front of anybody in this building for thirty-two days."

Ines Duarte set down the cup she had not drunk from. "You have been carrying a rule that stops you and you have not read it to us."

"I have been carrying a rule that stops me and I could not read it to you without telling you what the account is, and I did not want to tell you what the account is in a room in which six of you had not agreed to hear it, and that is a reason and it is not a good one."

Nadia Ferrous said, "It is a reason. It is the ordinary reason men have."

"It is the ordinary reason men have," he said, "and I have given it to you anyway because you are owed it."

## He Asked It

He looked at nine women.

"I am going to ask one question and it is the only one I have come with. And I want to say first that a question like this has no instrument behind it."

"There is no instrument behind it at all, there is no register, no box and no sheet, and not one thing in this city would let me compel a single word of the answer, and if you say nothing I am going to take it that nothing was said and I will not come back to it."

The room went quiet in the way a kitchen does when nine people stop moving at the same moment and the clock is the only thing in it.

"Has anybody in this room ever taken anything out of it."

Nobody answered for about eleven seconds.

"Isolde," said Runa Sato, "do not."

"I was not going to," said Isolde Marchetti.

"Then do not," said Runa Sato, "because if you sing one bar of that in this room about nine of us are going to agree to a thing none of us has decided and we will all be certain of it in the morning and none of us will be able to say how we got there."

Isolde Marchetti put both hands flat on the table, which is the posture she takes when she has decided not to.

Then Petra Ilyin said, from the end by the stove, in the ordinary voice of a woman stating a day and an hour:

"I did. I did it for eleven years and I did not know it was yours."

# The Morning It Was Empty

He went up the hill at about ten in the morning on the fifth of October and there were about nine thousand people under the ranks wall because it is an ordinary morning, and about forty of them were standing still, no stool, no slate, no line, and not one of the five hundred people who had come on purpose.

There was a boy with a bucket at the foot of it doing nothing in particular, which is what a boy does at the foot of a wall.

He put his hand on the stone under the card with his right hand, the way he had done at the foot of the same wall on the eleventh of July and for nine weeks afterwards, and he had nothing in him to give, and it took about nine seconds of him standing there working that out.

That is a fact about him and it is not a fact about anything else.

## The Only True Sentence

Beatrix Halm came up the hill at about a quarter past ten with her notebook and she did not open it.

"I was here at about eight," she said. "And at half past seven yesterday I was at the wall at ten past five and there were four hundred people and nine feet of stone and a woman with a table of her own and two borrowed trestles. And this morning the wall is empty."

"Is it over."

"I do not know," said Beatrix Halm. "I have been standing at this wall since half past four yesterday morning and I have written down every hour and I am telling you, as I have told about nine people this week, that I do not know, and it is the only true sentence anybody has got about any of it, and you are not going to get a better one out of me this morning than you would get out of the rail."

"You cannot tell me whether anything has stopped."

"Not one person in this city can tell you whether anything has stopped, and Dagmar Oyelowo at a public counter has told two hundred people the same sentence for three days, and I have written it on my board in my own hand with the hour against it."

"And it does not make it true and it does not make it false," said Beatrix Halm. "It is the only thing any of us have. Go and stand at the rail for a bit and do not ask anybody anything, because everybody in this square has the same face and I have seen it about nine times this week."

## Nine Women And Nine Places

The kitchen off Ferrier Street was empty at about half past ten on the fifth of October and nothing in it to indicate that anybody had ever cooked in it, except the kettle and the table and eight marks on the tablecloth where nine people have sat down for about four years.

Not one of them was there.

Isolde was on the Bellhouse stage at about the second song. Marguerite was at the Vant and the real books were chained on her table and had been since seven.

Runa was out on a round and had been since six in the morning and would not be back until dark. Nadia was at the bottom of the Vestry shaft and had been since about nine. Beatrix was at a wall.

Tessa had her table at the north end by the Vestry gate and had been packing it down since about nine, because the second of October was over and she had not told anybody that in advance.

Ines was at a counter and Petra was at that table with her hands in her lap and Agnete was standing nine feet off a wall.

And three of the nine had begun sleeping somewhere else during the week and had not said why and had not been asked.

He looked at the kitchen for a while.

"I am not going to have this at eight o'clock on the morning of the sixth," he said, to the empty kitchen. "It does not work at eight o'clock in the morning and I am not going to be the man who says something about a kitchen at eight o'clock."

## The Form That Is Not Drafted

Hanneke Ruis was in the counting room two floors under a sales floor on the afternoon of the fifth with the lamp on and the block in front of her and nothing to write in it.

"Nothing has certified that composition since the fourteenth of March," she said. "Not for the quarter that began on the first of July and not for the one before it, and a form cannot be drafted without one, and so the certificate for the quarter that began on the first of October was not drafted this morning and will not be drafted this afternoon."

"So the eighth block."

"The eighth block is on the thirtieth of December at about half past four in the afternoon, out of my own head, in my own hand, with my name at the foot of it, and the certificate behind it will not exist," said Hanneke Ruis. "That is the eighth time. And I wrote eight yesterday and I am not going to change it, and I have been asked twice today by two different clerks whether I meant eight and I have told both of them that I meant eight."

"Will they query it."

"Not one person can query it. Without the certificate, and without a second hand, not one person in this city is able to say that the number of people sleeping at that address on a particular morning ought to have been something else."

She squared the book. "The composition of that premises has been certified six times out of my memory in four years, and four of those were wrong and two of them were right, and not one living person can tell which of them was which, including me."

"And on the thirtieth of December there will be an eight in a keeper's hand and nothing at all behind it."

## The Books In November

He was on the step of the house at about half past four on the afternoon of the fifth of October when Yusuf Adeyemi came up the hill, which he had not done before, and stood in the lane and did not come in.

"You have four days left of not having assigned work," he said. "I am taking you to a branch on the morning of the eleventh at about seven, and I am telling you what it is on the step because it is not a thing you should hear about in a room with a table in it."

"Say it."

"A branch office off a third court, and the hours are mine and not yours, and you will be on a rota that a keeper keeps, and the work is whatever the floor needs."

Adeyemi stood in the lane. "That is what the first band is. It is not a punishment and it is not a comment. It is the whole of what is left under the floor, and you put yourself into it at a public counter at ten minutes to four on the first with a pen in your own hand and a queue of nine people behind you."

"I know what I am."

"You know what you are and you have not worked out what it means," said Yusuf Adeyemi, "and it means that in about nine months you will not decide when you get up. That is all it means. The rest of it is the same as it ever was."

"And I have taken four people into that branch in nineteen years and two of them are still at it, and one of them is a woman who counts things for a living, and I am not going to say her name on your step."

He looked up the hill.

"One more thing and then I will go down it. The books that would take a thing like this did not move at seven on the morning of the sixteenth of November and they have not shifted since, and they open once in the year and not twice. That is forty-one days off: twenty-five days left in October and sixteen days of November."

"And if they have not moved by then."

"Then it cannot be closed," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "And this city will never be able to say that it is not, because a thing that is not closed and not open is the third thing and this city has never had a column for it, and I am telling you on a step on the fifth of October because it is going to be the last piece of furniture in this and I would rather you heard it from a man standing in a lane."

He went down the hill.

## Four O'Clock

The standing sheet went out at four o'clock on the afternoon of the fifth of October with the address at the head of every line.

There was a line under the last one.

There has been a blank line under the last tile at the north end of the ranks wall since before Beatrix Halm started reading it in the dark nine years ago, and it is painted in the office's own hand along the foot of the stone. It carries nothing, and nothing has ever been put on it.

And the office that paints that wall comes in on the eighth of October at about six in the morning and does the whole of it in about two hours.

Ostara does not know what it is for.

Not one person in Ostara was going to know what it was for on the fifth of October at about a quarter past four in the afternoon.

There were about nine thousand people under a wall on an ordinary morning, and about forty of them had been standing still, and a man with a chest that was spent and a first band of standing and a cough that belonged to him until he was dead stood in an empty square at the north end of the ranks wall.

He did not know whether anything had stopped, and would not be told, and had not one person left to ask.

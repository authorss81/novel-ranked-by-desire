# The Cold That Is Not A Minute

On the twentieth of August there were nine women and one man in a building off Ferrier Street who had done the largest thing anybody in Ostara has done in thirty years, and there was not one hour of it that could be put anywhere.

"One hundred and eighty days," said Lin Hao, in the passage, on his way past the stair. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with twenty is one hundred and eighty."

"And that is the last time anybody in this house is going to say a number to me for a while, because I can hear what it sounds like from the bottom of the stairs."

"Say one more," said Runa Sato from the kitchen.

"Noted and refused."

## Six Days Afterwards

Marguerite Vale went back to the Vant on the seventeenth and was in her rooms on the second floor on the eighteenth and the nineteenth, and had a conversation with Solveig Rask on the twentieth that lasted about nine minutes and that nobody was told about.

Petra Ilyin could not get up off the floor on the fourteenth and could get up off it on the fifteenth, slowly, and on the sixteenth she went out at about six in the morning and did not come back until about nine that night, and nobody in that house asked her where she had been.

Nadia Ferrous has never once in her life left a building and got down a floor, and was lighter by the twentieth than she had been on the thirteenth, and told two people about it and not the other seven.

And there was washing up every evening at Ferrier Street from the fifteenth onward, because that is what a house does, and that is the entire machinery of it, and nothing whatever was different about the eighth of the twenty-seventh question asked in that kitchen.

## Where It Would Go

On the nineteenth Ines Duarte spent the whole day finding out where it could go.

She went to a division that holds papers and was told by a clerk that there was no box. She went to a district that keeps a night's book and was told that a night's book is a night's book. She went to the counting room two floors under a sales floor and Hanneke Ruis listened to the whole of it standing at the rail and then said four sentences.

"The fourth block of a quarterly takes a figure off a certificate of the composition," said Hanneke Ruis. "The certificate is made four times a year. The quarter that began on the first of July has no certificate, and the quarter that began on the first of April had no certificate, and the one before that had one and it said seven on the fourteenth of March and has said seven since."

"So the figure cannot go on the return."

"The figure cannot go anywhere," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I am not going to be dramatic about it. I have been keeping these books for twenty-nine years and no column in this city will ever take a thing that three people did in a kitchen, and it never could, and that is not a failure of the record. It is what the record is for."

## The Only Witness Who Cannot Give An Hour

"And the last part of it is Beatrix," said Ines Duarte, on the twentieth, at the table, to eight people.

"Is that the same as the roof."

"It is not the roof and it is worse," said Ines Duarte. "On the roof she could not say when and she would not invent an hour and she told him not to ask. That was the fourteenth of July and there were two days of hearing in front of it. On the fourteenth of August she cannot say when either and not one thing sits in front of it, and she has been asked twice, and she has answered twice."

Beatrix Halm sat at the table with the notebook shut in front of her and her hand flat on the wood.

"I felt nine women at a table on the afternoon of the fourteenth," she said, "and it went through the floor and up through the chairs, and it is the largest single thing I have felt on that line in nine years and I have felt forty thousand people going under a wall in a rainstorm."

"Can you put a minute on it."

"I cannot put a minute on it and I cannot put an hour on it and I have tried for six days, and I am not going to improve it now that it has been asked about three times." She turned the notebook round and did not open it.

"That is the rule, it is the only rule I have, and I did not invent it to look impressive."

"Nine years ago a woman in a cab told me that a report which may be improved afterwards is a report a man can be read out of, and that neither of them would ever know afterwards which of the two they had. That is the whole of what a person owes the next person on a line."

"Beatrix."

"I know what it cost to say that in a room where eight people wanted a minute," said Beatrix Halm. "And I would rather eight people went without than one stranger in four years reads a thing I made nicer for them."

## A Woman At A Counter

"There is one more and it is the whole of what I have," said Beatrix Halm.

"Go on."

"On the eighteenth there was a woman at a counter in Cordwain who came in with a cold hand and I am not talking about the cold minute. She had a cold hand and she was talking about the fourteenth, in a queue, to about four people, and she said that she had been in a room off Ferrier Street in the afternoon and that something had gone through the room and that she had felt it in both arms."

Nadia Ferrous said, "Both arms."

"Both arms," said Beatrix Halm. "And a woman behind her said that she had felt that in about half of Europe when the lift came down at the Vestry, and the two of them disagreed about the hour and agreed about the thing."

"And what did you do."

"I bought a roll off the stall outside and I stood there and let them talk, and I asked one question and I got one answer, and then I went home and wrote it in my own book in my own hand with the time against it and the sentence as it was said, because that is what I do with a thing that is not a line."

"What was the question."

"I asked whether it was the fourteenth of August," said Beatrix Halm. "And the first woman said yes and the second one said the fifteenth, and neither of them had a reason, and both of them meant it."

## The Cold That Is Not A Minute

"You keep going back to it," said Runa Sato.

"You keep going back to it too."

"I have not gone near it," said Runa Sato. "And I am not going to, because here is the thing and it is the only thing I have said in nine years that I have never said out loud, and I have had a division that would have listened to it in April."

She put her forearms on the table.

"A cold minute has never been proved. Not once, in thirty years, not by anybody. Two women in a queue in Cordwain who disagree about the date and agree about the feeling is the entire evidentiary position of the entire city, and that is not a complaint, that is the design of it." She looked up.

"It leaves no mark and no figure and nothing goes on anybody's skin, and if you cannot point at it then it did not happen to you in any room that can be argued in, and that is the whole reason thirty years of people have been able to put their good into a pot that nobody owns and walk away from it feeling fine."

"That is how it works."

"That is how it works," said Runa Sato, "and on the fourteenth of August three women did the one thing not one person in this city has done since the thing started, which is to stand still and wait for it instead of walking away feeling fine, and it has made no difference, because the moment it stops it stops, and a person thinks they were cold, and not one office in Ostara could enter that sentence."

## The Thing That Is Left

"So what have we got," said Isolde Marchetti.

Ines Duarte read it off her fingers, which she did.

"Three names and a date. The fourteenth of August. Three cold minutes, taken deliberately, by people who were not made to and who cannot be produced by anybody and who will all say so if anybody asks them, and nothing to ask them with. A pot that has three women in it. A household of nine. And a room at the end of a corridor that sits on the first of October with two documents on the table in front of it."

"And neither of the two documents has one word of the fourteenth of August in it," said Isolde Marchetti.

"Neither of them has one word of it and neither of them ever could," said Ines Duarte, "because the first of them is a claim entered by the Church of the Quiet Number about who a thing belongs to, and the second is an answer given by a man standing in a room, and the fourteenth of August is three women in a kitchen, and it belongs to neither of those, and it has not got a third door."

## And The Price, Which Was Running

"One more thing and it is not about the fourteenth," said Isolde Marchetti, "and I have had it since about the eighth and I have been waiting for somebody to ask me."

"Ask you."

"Four counters have come off their posting this month," said Isolde Marchetti. "Fourteen lent counters, no door, no building, and four of them have gone. That is what happens when a division in this city is opened, and it is not a punishment, it is a posting rotation and a man in a division cannot stop it and would not try."

"And the four."

"I do not know the four and I have not asked and I am not going to." Isolde Marchetti looked at him. "One of the four was not at her counter on any day of it. I know because I go past it."

"And I am not going to say her name at this table because she is not at this table and because it is not mine and because I know exactly what it cost you to leave her where she was on the twenty-fifth of July and I am not going to make you look at it in a kitchen."

He looked at the tablecloth.

"One hundred and eighty days," he said.

"You promised that was the last one."

"I said it was going to be the last time anybody said a number to me for a while," said Lin Hao, "and I have broken it, and it is the fourth time this month and I am going to let it stand because there is a month of it left and on the first of October a room sits and I would like to walk into it having said one more number than I needed to."

He got up and took his coat off the back of a chair.

"Nothing will take the fourteenth," he said, "and I have spent four years looking for somewhere to put things and the reason I never found it is that everything that has ever been in that pot was put there by somebody who was also not going to find a place for it."

"That is not a plan."

"No," he said. "It is what nine women know at six o'clock in the evening, and it is the fifth of September and the first of October, and nothing in this city turns a fact like that into a document, and if nothing turns one up in five weeks then three women went cold in a kitchen on the afternoon of the fourteenth and not one person alive will ever know it."

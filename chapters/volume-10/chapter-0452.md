# Three Slips Become One Minute

He was in the corridor outside the door of the room at the end of it at about ten past eight on the morning of the first of July, because the first of July was the first working day of a quarter and a clause he had written in a notebook nine days ago said he would be in this corridor, and there was nothing in that clause about how long he had to stay there.

"It is one hundred and thirty-one days since the twentieth of February," he said. "One hundred and thirty at the thirtieth, and one more, which is one hundred and thirty-one."

"One hundred and thirty-one," said the clerk, from inside, "and there is a bench at the end of this corridor and you have never once been offered it and you have sat on it twice."

## What A Member May Do At Eight In The Morning

The door was shut, because the room did not sit until it was called and it was not called, and a member of a room that is not sitting has no business inside it.

A member may read the book after the run has gone. The run goes at four. It had gone at four the previous afternoon, and the bundle had been made up, and the clerk let a member read it at the second table under the window with the door shut and nobody else in the corridor, which she had done for him on the fifteenth of June and had not done for him since.

She brought three things out and laid them down.

They had been three slips in one drawer for nine days under a lock that had never been locked in ninety days, and at about twenty-five past two on the afternoon of yesterday they had become one instrument, and this morning they were three things on a table with a room behind him that had said them out loud.

"The minute of the thirtieth of June," she said. "Five seals. The premises named four times. The finding sealed. The date first and the hour with it, because that is how it is kept. And under the minute, after the last seal and before it went down, your clause."

He read it. Thirty-eight words, entered in her hand, in the space a clause goes in, which is under a minute and not in one.

## The Witness Line

"Turn it over," said the clerk.

Under the minute, at the foot of it, was the witness line, and the witness line was the sheet of the eighteenth of April with seven names in it, and it had been read into a minute twice in May.

Seven names. Two fingers of space between the fifth and the sixth.

The gap is hers and she put it there. That has been on the page since April and it has never been closed and it cannot be closed by anybody, because a clerk may only enter and cannot correct.

A gap in a witness line is not a space. It is a hole where a seat should be, and a room with a hole in it is one short.

The line can be produced against all seven of the others for four years, which means that until some year in this decade it can be produced against them in any matter that touches that address. The only man standing between those seven names and any office in this city is a man with a piece of brass in his coat.

"Four years," he said.

"Four years from the eighteenth of April," said the clerk, "and after that it is paper, and paper can be read by anybody, and the clause you bought does not touch a witness line and never could, and you knew that on the twenty-first of June because I told you that day in this corridor with a woman pricing things standing at my shoulder."

## The Word She Does Not Read

Under the fifth line, in the clerk's own hand, there was one word.

He had read it once before, in a corridor on the evening of the fifteenth of June, and it had been on a slip in a drawer then, and it was on a minute under a seal now, and the same word was under the gap in the same hand, and it is not printed in any book in this repository and it is not going to be printed here.

A member may not ask the clerk of that room a question about a name or about a space.

"Say it," he said.

"No," said the clerk.

"That is twice."

"That is three times, and the first was on the fifteenth of June, and I gave you the same answer I gave you then, and I am not going to be more sympathetic the fourth."

She squared the three things on the table. "That word is mine and it went in on the ninth of May on my own initiative, unasked, because a minute in a division carries an address and a clerk cannot sit on an absence. If I have to say it out loud in this corridor every time a member wants to look at it, then I did not enter it. I published it."

## The First Of The Quarter

"Now," she said. "You have come here at ten past eight on the first morning of a quarter and you have not asked me a question about a name or a space, and that is the first sensible thing anybody has done in this corridor since the fifteenth of April, so I am going to tell you what yesterday did."

"A requisition laid on the twentieth of February has been administered weekly against the composition of those premises since the first of April. It stood at three when it was read on the seventeenth of March and a room of three cannot carry, so it was administered and not carried. Administration without a finding is a thing done to a holding."

"Yesterday it was carried."

"Yesterday it was carried, and there is a finding under five seals, and the finding says the premises are not a person keeping them." She put two fingers on the paper and did not move them. "A finding is administered against the persons keeping the composition. From the first of the month. Weekly. In the ordinary way."

He had known this for about ninety seconds before she said it and it is a different thing knowing it.

## The Division With No Door

"Which division holds the fourth part."

"That is the question you came to ask, and it is the only one you are entitled to ask, and I will answer it properly."

She turned the minute round so the fourth part was uppermost. It was a description made against the head of those premises at about eleven on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October.

"The fourth part is held by a division that does not sit in the Wing and keeps no list of addresses. It has been like that since the first of January. There is no register on which its name appears and there is no door in this building with its number on it."

"How long has it been like that."

"It has had the shape of something since the first of January and it has never been named on anything, and I am a clerk and I know what is held where and I will not tell you which division it is, because you would go and stand in front of its door in about four minutes and then it would have a name in a book with your address in it."

He put both hands flat on the second table.

"It cannot reach a woman," he said. "That is what you told me in June. A division that keeps no list of addresses cannot produce a woman on a list and can only produce the building she is in."

"That is what I told you and it has been true for one hundred and thirty-one days and it was true yesterday morning."

"And this afternoon."

"This afternoon it was handed a sealed finding that names those premises four times, at four o'clock, in the ordinary way, with a clause about the subject under it." She picked up her pen and put it down again without writing anything. "It has an address now. It had a building and it has an address. Those are not the same and a building is a thing you go to and an address is a thing you keep going to."

## The Part Of It That Is His

"And the clause," he said.

"Your clause says that no person keeping that address so named may be produced by that minute otherwise than in the presence of the subject, and that the subject may not be absent from the making of it."

"So every office in this city that touches that address from this afternoon has to come through me."

"Every office that touches that address, and there is no exception in it for a division that keeps no list, because the division did not know it had one until four o'clock yesterday and the clause was entered at the making of the minute and it cannot reach the ninth of May and it cannot reach the twentieth of February, and both of those are in books that are written up."

He stood there for about a minute with his hands on a table in a corridor.

The cheapest thing he had ever bought had become a weekly delivery to a door with six women asleep behind it, and the address on the delivery was his own, and he had put his own name on it, and nothing in this city can take a man's own words out of a book.

"Can it be stopped," he said.

"No."

"Can the finding be struck."

"No."

"Can I lift it."

"You can lift a recusal. A recusal is lifted by the subject and by nobody else and there is no sheet for lifting one, and Alma Quayle's is in a drawer in this building."

"She has been sitting in the chair that counts for nothing since the fourth of December and she will sit in it on the first of October as well."

The clerk shut the bundle. "What you have in front of you is a finding under five seals, one of them yours, and you put that seal on it about an hour and a half after you understood what it said."

## Ten Past Two In The Evening

He walked down the corridor and down the outside stair and across a court and got to Ferrier Street at about twenty past two in the afternoon with the brass in his coat and a piece of paper in his head that he had been carrying since ten past eight.

Eight people sleep under that roof. Six of them are sworn and two of them are not and one of the two is the officer of the composition of the address that the finding names four times, and she is a woman who has certified that address four times a year in her own hand for eleven years and knows exactly what a composition is administered against.

He has told eight women things in a kitchen in this city. He told them about the composition in June and they neither thanked him nor forgave him.

He stood in the street outside number thirty-four with his hand on the gate for about two minutes.

There is no version of telling them.

Telling them puts them in the room he is standing outside. The clause says that no person keeping that address may be produced by any minute otherwise than in his presence.

The only thing his clause ever does is put the eight of them in a corridor with him in it. Every minute that comes down that chain for the next four years comes down it at the rate of one a week, and the corridor is forty feet long, and there is a bench at the end of it that has never been offered to anybody.

If he tells them, he puts them in that corridor on purpose, forever, and they will know it is him.

If he does not tell them, they will be standing in it anyway, on a morning, in front of a woman with a bundle who has never met them, and the reason will be in a book and not in their kitchen.

He opened the gate and went up to the house and had his dinner at about four with eight women in a kitchen and told them about the answer he had given in a room on the afternoon of the thirtieth, which was true, and which is not the thing that happened on the thirtieth.

Nothing about the finding went on that table. Nothing about the fourth part or the division or the weekly word went past his teeth from seven o'clock until half past nine that night, and he did not lie to one of them, and he did not correct one of them, and he has never in his life been able to do either of those two things without it costing somebody something.

# The Man Who Defends The Board

The room was off a third court and it held about ninety people on the morning of the tenth of October and it had a hearing bench in it and eleven chairs and a corridor outside it with about forty more standing in it, and Yusuf Adeyemi had called it and told them in advance that it was not a sitting.

"Everybody has asked me what is happening at eleven this morning," he said, "and I have told all of them the same thing, which is that a room sits when it is called and a room that is called for a thing it cannot carry is still a room."

## What This Room Can Do

"Can you do anything in here."

"No," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "I cannot grant your petition and I cannot refuse it. There is no disposition this room can reach on a matter of this kind until the thirtieth of December, and if it reaches one then, it is seventy-nine days off and you may have stopped caring by the fourteenth."

"Then why are we here."

"Because a thing said in a room is a minute of a sitting, and a minute goes out at four in the afternoon on the standing sheet, and the standing sheet has an address at the head of every line," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "That is all I have got and I have got it on purpose and I did not call this room to do you a favour."

He looked round at about ninety people.

"You will not win anything before lunch. What you can do is make a sentence, out loud, in a room, that a clerk has to write down, and then find out this afternoon whether anybody in this city agrees with it. That is the whole of the instrument. I have been doing it for eleven years and it is worth exactly what it is worth."

## The People Who Want It Struck

About sixty of the ninety had come from Cordwain on foot and had been standing in the corridor before the doors were opened.

The first of them was Solveig Rask, who is a keeper of the Vant's real books and who has been in that trade for twenty-nine years and who has never once in her life spoken in a room like this one.

"No," she said. "That is my petition and I will say it in about nine minutes and you will not like any part of it."

"Go on."

"Thirty years this city has measured what a person did in a doorway at nothing, handed the measurement to an office, and let the office decide who counts. It has been very good at that and it has cost nothing and the cost fell where it always falls, which is on people who were never in a room with a figure on the wall in the first place."

She put a hand flat on the bench.

"A man in this city saw that in a public hall three years ago. He was right. He has been right for three years and every person in this room believes it and it has cost him a wing with no window in it, and I am sorry for it and I am not going to pretend that being sorry is an argument against what happened next."

"Then give them the argument."

"Here it is," said Solveig Rask. "You cannot measure the unrecorded. You have just proved it in public, at a wall, with your own hands, and a thing you cannot measure is not a safe thing. It is an unaccountable thing."

"And I keep books."

"You keep books," said Solveig Rask. "Twenty-nine years of them, and every one of them in the hand of the person who carries the debt, and not one page of any of them that a court can take off you and read. And I will tell a room exactly what an unaccountable thing is, because it is my whole trade."

She turned round and looked at the corridor.

"It is a debt with no creditor. It comes due in about four years, on a morning nobody has chosen, in a building with no door on it."

"And at that desk there will not be one person in this city who can stand up and say what was owed and to whom. You have handed a city a thing that will do that."

"I know that. I did it in the open and my name is on it and that is the best part of what happened and it does not change the morning."

## The Question They Wanted To Ask

That is the case, and it was the best case anybody in that room had heard, and about nine people wanted to argue with it immediately and were stopped by about forty.

Then a man at the back said, "Why did he do it, then."

"Ask him," said Solveig Rask. "He is in the corridor."

"I am not in the corridor," said Lin Hao. "I am in the room, and I have no standing, and there is a clerk at that bench, and a man may speak if the room gives him leave."

"Give him leave."

The room gave him leave, and it took about four seconds, and Yusuf Adeyemi wrote the words *leave given* on a slip and could not improve them and was not asked to hurry.

## What He Said

He stood at the bench, at rank one, with assigned work that began in about twenty-one hours, and he did not have a paper.

"Four things and then I will stop, and then you will do what you came to do."

"One. Look at the wall. You have all been standing under it for two days. On the eighth, at about six in the morning, an office painted two words and a dash and two more words at the bottom of it that it cannot read."

"A form has two boxes at the foot of it, and a clerk typed what the sheet said into the box, because that is what a clerk is for, and she was told nothing."

He looked round.

"That wall has never carried a sentence that the office did not write itself. Not once in thirty years. What it carries is the number the office already holds, and a number you already hold is not a wall, it is a noticeboard, and you cannot argue with a noticeboard. You could argue with that. I do not know what it means and neither does the man who painted it, and that is the first honest thing that building has put up in my lifetime."

"And what does the honest thing do for us," said Solveig Rask.

"Nothing whatsoever. That is the second thing. It does nothing for us at all, and I want to be exact about that, because a man in a delivery coat stood under that wall on the eighth and told me the line was about me, and I could not tell him it was not."

He put his hands on the bench.

"Two. The Board's whole job is to be the thing you can ask. Not to be right. To be there on an ordinary morning with a counter open and a clerk who may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and to have whatever you say written down by somebody who did not choose to write it down."

"Every one of you has wanted, at some point in the last thirty years, to ask this city a question and be given an answer that you could not argue with. That is all the Board has ever been. Most weeks it is worse than that. It is still the only place in Ostara where the answer has to be written down by a person who is not allowed to improve it."

"Three. Strike it," he said, "and tell me what is standing at the north end of the Reading on the morning after this one."

"Stones."

"A slate. Five stools and a slate in a lane, with a figure on it in four-inch letters made up in three days by a man who believes it, and not one word of it wrong and not one word of it checkable by anybody alive."

He looked at the man at the back who had brought it.

"You cannot check a slate. You can check a wall. That is the whole of what I have to say and it took me four years and a coat and a queue at a counter to work out, and I would have worked it out a great deal faster if I had ever once been on the side that wanted to take something away."

## The Thing He Would Not Do

"And the fourth thing," said Solveig Rask. "Say why you did it. That is what this room is for."

"No."

"You emptied an account in a square in front of nine thousand people and reduced yourself to nothing doing it, and you have stood in this city for four days not saying one word about why, and this room has ninety people in it and about half of them came to shout at you personally. Say why."

"Not in this room," said Lin Hao. "And not because it cannot be answered, and not because I am ashamed of it, and I am going to be very careful that the room hears the reason for that, because it is the reason and it is the only reason."

He turned round and looked at the clerk.

"This room cannot carry an answer. Everything said in it goes out at four in the afternoon on a sheet with an address at the head of every line."

"And if I put my reasons for that into that minute, then in about four years a stranger with a folder reads a man who explained himself in a room that could not hear him, and decides that is what happened."

"It is not what happened. I did not have reasons. I had a thing in a square and nine women in it and about four seconds to decide."

"Then what was the fourth thing."

"Ask me on the thirtieth of December," said Lin Hao. "There will be a sitting that can carry an answer and I will give it there, in public, in full, and I will not soften one word of it, and I have told this room the date so that whoever wants to hear it can write it down."

Somebody at the back called out that the thirtieth of December was a long way off. He said that it was seventy-nine days and that he had had the answer since the second of July and that he had been carrying it down a hill in his own hand since then.

## What The Clerk Entered

The clerk entered the minutes of the sitting in the ordinary way, and entered what a member said when a member said it, and entered that leave was given to a member of the public at about eleven minutes past eleven, and the room did not carry anything else.

Then the standing sheet went out at four in the afternoon with the address at the head of every line, and on it, at the bottom, in the hand of a clerk who may only enter and cannot be asked to hurry, was one line.

*The Bureau of Merit and the wall at the head of the Reading were defended in a called sitting by a member of the public at rank one, and the petition to strike them was read and not carried, and neither matter can be reached before the thirtieth of December.*

## What That Costs A Man

He read it at a quarter past four at a public desk, because any person in this city may read that sheet at a public desk on a working afternoon, and he read it twice.

Then he said it out loud to Yusuf Adeyemi in a corridor with nothing in it, which was where he was going to be for ever because of a clause of thirty-eight words he had entered himself on the twenty-first of June.

"You understand what you have done."

"I do it every time," said Lin Hao. "It is the corridor."

"Not the corridor," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "Listen to what is on that sheet and say it back to me. The Bureau of Merit in this city is alive this afternoon because a man with no standing asked a room of ninety people to keep it, and in about seventy-nine days there is a sitting that can strike it, and every person in that corridor will be told by somebody that the man who asked was the man who emptied the pot."

"Yes."

"So it is not doing its own work any more," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "It is doing yours. And you know that, and I have watched your face for about nine seconds and you have known it for about four of them."

Lin Hao stood in a corridor outside a door he is not permitted to stand inside of, and said the thing out loud, once, to one man, and then never to anybody again.

"The Bureau of Merit in this city now survives on a sentence I said in a room that could not carry it," he said. "That is not its merit. It is mine. It is going to be the position of a man who put the city in front of the thing he did, and I would not go back outside that door for anything they could offer me, and there is nothing on offer, and that is closed."

"Good," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "Then you will be able to go to work on the eleventh."

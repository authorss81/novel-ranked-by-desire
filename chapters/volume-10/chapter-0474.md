# The Vant's Entry In Her Own Hand

Her rooms were on the second floor above the Vant's counting house, there was a fire lit in them at nine in the evening on the ninth of August, and there was a bound book on the table in the middle of the room with a ribbon in the spine and nothing else on the cover.

He came up at about nine and she opened the door herself, in a dark dress with the sleeves pushed back and her hair up off her face, and she did not ask him to sit.

"You are one hundred and sixty-nine days into it," said Marguerite Vale, from inside the room, before he had got his coat off. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty at the end of June, and July is thirty, so one hundred and sixty at the end of July, and one hundred and sixty with nine is one hundred and sixty-nine. You did not get that from a clerk and I have not got it from a clerk."

"I did not get it from anybody. It is in my head and it is there every morning."

"Then take it in with you, because I have got about four hours of this and one of them is going to be about a thing I have been carrying since the spring. You have had four days to come and get it and you have not been to the Vant."

"You knew there was something here to come for."

"I knew, and I have spent five years deciding whether to tell you, and I want that on the table before anything else happens tonight, because it is the only thing I have done in this city in nine years that I am not going to put my name under."

## The Entry Since The Sixteenth Of September

"The Vant's real books have carried an entry at your address since the sixteenth of September," she said. "It is initialled and not signed. It is carried at a figure of seven. It has been called at four valuations, the first of December, the first of January, the first of February and the first of March, and it has never been answered and never paid and never produced, because that door cannot be produced from, in or out."

"Solveig has said all of that to me."

"Solveig has said the part of it she is allowed to say," said Marguerite Vale. "She ran the arithmetic on what that entry is actually worth in the winter of a year two years ago, in one night, and got it right, and would not write it on a page and would not say it out loud in a room."

"There has been exactly one other person in this guild who has ever done that work and she is me, and I never wrote it down either, because the moment it is on a page it becomes a figure the guild can price, and the moment the guild can price it, somebody puts a form under it and starts asking who is owed it and by whom." She shut the door behind him.

"So there are two women in this city who know what that pot is worth and neither of us has ever put it in ink. A real book is written in the hand of the person who carries the debt. Not the keeper's hand. Not a clerk's. The hand of whoever is carrying it, at the moment they are carrying it, and a second signature on it would be a second carrier, and a debt can only be carried by one person or the figures do not reconcile."

"And the carrier may not read her own entry out, to a room or a man or a board or the four walls of her own house: if she reads one line of it aloud to another person then the entry is void and the debt goes back on the house it was carried against and the carrier carries it anyway."

"There has been exactly one line in these books in eleven years that I have wanted to read out," said Marguerite Vale, "and it is in here, and I am not going to."

She took her hands off it.

"So you will have it from me in the only other form the guild has, and it is this. When something moves in a real book it is called, and a call is three things. What moved. When. At what price. Not two of them. All three."

"A call with two of the three is a rumour, and nobody in this guild has ever written a rumour down, because a rumour with two thirds is a thing people repeat, and a thing people repeat ends up in an opinion, and an opinion about a figure is how a city loses a person. And you are about eleven seconds into a conversation, and you are going to give me three facts in order before you start."

## Three Facts, In Order

"First. I am not going to use what I have on you or on anybody in that room. A debt I touch binds both parties in writing and in person and every debt I take I carry myself, and I carry more of this city's obligations than any living person, and I have said that sentence out loud in about four rooms in nine years and it has never once been a way of asking anybody for anything."

"Second. Whatever I tell you tonight comes in the only form I am permitted to use and it will carry no more weight than the air in this room, and if you walk out of here with a figure you can produce against me in a division, I will have told you wrong on purpose."

"Third." She did not move. "The night is yours and my morning is empty, and I am not going to handle you gently and you are not going to handle me gently, and you have a chest that is spent and you will not tell me about it, and I have known about it since the fifteenth of July and I have priced it once and I am not going to price it again tonight."

"You will cough at least twice and I am going to tell you off for it and I am not going to stop you."

"You said it in four hundred people on the thirtieth of July," said Marguerite Vale. "I have been keeping a real book for thirty years and I can see what is being spent from the outside of it, and I do not need you to say anything."

## The First Description, Which Is Enough

He looked at her for a moment in the firelight, at the shape of a woman who carries books, at the shoulders and the hands and the sleeves pushed back, and he took that in and he did not say it out loud, and it was enough.

"Three questions," said Marguerite Vale. "Ask."

"The first one."

"I am going to deviate, and I am telling you that I am deviating rather than letting you find out at the end of it. The guild's method is a way of finding out what a figure did, and I am not looking for a figure tonight. I am looking for two people who have both been carrying something and have both been lying about it."

"So it is this. I answer you about me, and then ask you the same question about me, and then answer it about you, and the three answers are not going to be the same shape. Come here."

## What Moved, And When

"What moved," said Marguerite Vale, "is a pot of thirty years' worth of small unrecorded things, and what came out of it for eleven years was the cost of keeping one woman's body above freezing, and the two of those are not the same size and I have known that for eleven years and I have never once been able to put it in a book, because the line would have to carry both halves and the halves are not the same kind of thing."

She said the date, and the hour in it, and the name of the quarter.

"And I did not do it for you and I did not do it for the City. I did it for a woman who could not remember being promised and has never once in eleven years been able to say no to anybody, and I have been the hand that moved and she has been the body that received, and neither of us has ever known what the other one was doing."

"Who promised her."

She looked at him for about two seconds.

"I do not know," said Marguerite Vale. "And she does not know either, and she has been looking for eleven years, and there is not one thing in this city with his face on it. I have looked in the wrong building for eleven years and so has she and neither of us has ever been in the right one, and I am not going to guess at it tonight in a room with a fire in it."

"It is unknown, and it is going to be unknown in about seven weeks when a room at the end of a corridor sits, and that is the whole of what I have, and you may have it in that form or not at all."

## What He Gave

"Your turn. What moved. When. At what price."

"You will not do it in that order," said Marguerite Vale. "You will do it in the order that is comfortable to you, which is price first, because nobody has ever asked you for anything without telling you what it costs. So I am going to make you do it the other way round, and you are going to hate it, and that is the method and not my cruelty."

"Fine," said Lin Hao. "What moved."

And Lin Hao, standing in the best rooms he would ever stand in, in front of a woman who had been keeping the only books in Ostara that knew what that pot was worth, described her body to her, out loud, in the form of a call.

He said the shoulders, and the line of them, and the hair up off her face, and the sleeves pushed back. He said the hands, which are the hands of a woman who has been writing in her own book for thirty years and who has got the guild out of the middle of her palms and who has washed them a thousand times for an office and once for him.

He said the tiredness in one side of her, which is the side she reads on, and the way she holds a room by standing still in it, which she had done for eleven years in a kitchen on Ferrier Street and been the only one at that table who had not needed the argument to go the way she wanted.

And he said the price, which was that she had been carrying this alone since the spring of the year he came up her stair, and had told not one living soul about it, and had told a man she had known for five years and not one woman in a house with eight of them in it.

Marguerite Vale did not move for about four seconds.

"The hands," she said.

"The hands."

"You got the hands wrong."

"How."

"You said I wash them for an office," said Marguerite Vale. "I have not washed them for an office in about nine years and there is a woman on the fourth floor who would tell you that for nothing."

"I wash them because I like the water hot and because a broker who has been writing all day cannot make the ink stop by any other means, and I do not mind it, and it is not a wound and it is not a badge, and it is the only thing about those hands that is true."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be sorry, be accurate. You have spent four years putting people on a wall in chalk in about ninety seconds and you have just spent about ninety seconds on me and you got one line of it wrong out of nine, and I am going to tell you the other eight again in a different order for the rest of my life."

## The Whole Of It

It was not gentle and it was not fast and neither of them tried to make it either of those things.

She took her dress off over her head and did not hurry and laid it on the chair with the sleeves folded once, which is a thing she does, and she stood in front of him in her slip with her hair still up and the fire behind her and did not cover herself.

"Look at me for a count before you come over here," said Marguerite Vale. "I have been in rooms for thirty years where men wanted my books and not my body, and there is one man in this city who has wanted my body for five years and has never once said so out loud in a room, and tonight I am going to take my clothes off in front of you and I am not going to be kind about it."

He looked.

"Good," she said. "Now take your coat off. And when you take your shirt off I am going to put two fingers on the place where your cough starts and count to eleven, and you are going to let me, and if you cough I am going to tell you off about it and I am not going to stop you and that is the arrangement and you agreed to it in a room in June."

She undressed him the way a woman takes a coat off a thing she has been looking at too long, and she got his shirt up over his head and made him lift his arms the way a man lifts them when somebody is doing it for him and not when he is doing it himself, and when her fingers found the place she put them there and stayed.

"Say the number out loud," she said.

"You are not going to count out loud."

"I am going to count out loud because you have been carrying numbers for four years and you do not know how to lie about one," said Marguerite Vale. "Say it."

He said it.

"That is the worst thing I have ever priced," she said. "Now come here."

## The Half Hour Before It Was Anything

She got him sitting on the edge of the bed against the far wall where the firelight came in without glare and got down onto her knees in front of him and took his shoes off herself, one and then the other, and did not make anything of the fact that she did it.

"Tell me about the ten years," he said.

"No," said Marguerite Vale. "Not while you can hear me. I do not talk about the ten years and I have never talked about them to anybody, and if you ask me for it again I am going to put your hand somewhere and you will lose the question entirely."

"You could have said that when I asked."

"I could have and I did not, and that is mine and I am not apologising for it." She got up off the floor and got her slip over her head and stood there naked in front of him with the fire behind her, unhurried, entirely comfortable, a woman who has done this in front of a man she wanted in the year the account was carried at seven and has not been in a hurry since.

"That is the last of my terms and you have had four and you are not getting any more, so come and take what you came up the stair for."

## What She Wanted And Was Not Going To Pretend About

He got his hand to her hip and she let him keep it there for about four seconds and then took it and moved it and made him watch her do it.

"Here," she said. "Not because I am cold. Because I want to see you find it, and because a man who is shown the way finds it faster, and I am thirty years past being patient for a man who is slow."

Her fingers were cold. They were always cold, and he said so, and she said, "They are cold because they have been in a paper bag on a stair at half past seven in the morning for thirty years and I have never once got warm in them and I have stopped minding."

She closed her hand round him, looked at what she had done for a moment, and gave it back the look a broker gives a figure that has come back from a division reconciled.

"You have been spending," she said. "That is what that is. There is a woman at a counter in Cordwain who went grey on one side of her head on the thirteenth of July because of it, and about four thousand strangers have had their minute out of your chest, and you have got nothing left in the tank and you have got the most expensive thing in this city sitting on the end of it."

"I do not think of it that way."

"I do," said Marguerite Vale. "I have been thinking about it since the fifteenth of July and I have never once been able to think about it any other way, and I am not going to pretend to you tonight that I came up here for your sake."

"I came up here because I have been alone in a book for eleven years and I wanted a body in my rooms and I have picked the one I wanted, and it happens to be the one that is being used up, and I am not sorry about that and you may not be either."

## Slow, And On Purpose

He was slow because he was not able to be anything else, and she made him slower, and she did not once let him pretend it was her.

"You are doing it four times a night and you are doing it because you have been told to by about four hundred people and a clerk," she said. "Tonight nobody has told you to do anything."

"Tonight you are in a room on the second floor of the Vant's counting house and the woman on top of you has put a price on every part of herself out loud before she started, and nothing about tonight is in a record anywhere."

She got him back against the wall and got her weight on him and made him watch her, and when his hand went to her hip again she let it and her mouth went to his ear.

"You will not do this at a wall," she said. "You will not do this in a room with nine women in it, and you will not do it in a car on a hill at a quarter past eleven at night, and I know about that and I am not jealous of it and I want it said out loud in this room that I am not jealous of it."

"You have never seen it."

"I know what it cost," said Marguerite Vale. "Now be here."

He was inside her and she took all of it in one slow movement and then sat absolutely still over him with both hands flat on his chest, and neither of them breathed for about four seconds.

"There," she said. "That is what it is for, and you have been giving it away for four years to strangers at a wall and it took you nine days and about two thousand hands and a chest that will not come back, and it has taken you one stair and about eleven seconds to get it back from a woman who was owed it."

She lifted herself a little way off him and then came back down onto him slowly, all of her, until there was nothing between them at all, and set her hands into the sheet on either side of his shoulders and held herself there.

"Like that," she said, from about four inches off his mouth. "I have been thinking about doing it like that since the fifteenth of July and I have not thought about anything else once, and you may have the whole of that as well and I am not being kind, I am being expensive."

She was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire, and her mouth was open when she said it, and he got a hand round the back of her neck and held the back of her head and made her take the weight of herself on him, and she let him for about four seconds and then took it back herself.

"Stop being romantic," said Marguerite Vale. "You have been romantic at me for five years in corridors and I have let you do it because it is cheaper than arguing, and if you start being romantic on this bed then in about four years somebody in a hall will read about it."

She began to move, slow, the whole length, and she got both hands into the hair at the back of his head and kept them there.

"I am going to take this in about nine minutes," she said. "And I am going to tell you what I am taking in my own hand, because that is what tonight is for, and not one other person alive gets it."

## What She Said While It Was Happening

"You have carried a pot alone for four years," said Marguerite Vale, "and every week since the first of July a piece of paper has come to a door you sleep behind and you have not opened it, and on the thirtieth of July you stood in four hundred people and said a sentence you cannot take back and a clerk took your name off it in thirty seconds."

"Yes."

"That is what moved. I am going to say the rest and you are going to let me finish it."

She slowed down and he made a sound and she put a hand over his mouth and waited until he had stopped, and then she took the hand away.

"You can feel it," she said, watching his face. "That is not a figure and it is not an entry and not one office in this city could produce it, and it has been the same thing on this bed and in a stopped lift between two floors and on the roof of a moving car, and you have never once written down what it is."

She got a hand between them and took hold of him where she could reach him and held him exactly where he was, and went on riding him at the pace of somebody counting.

"I am going to be wet for about an hour after this and I am not going to wash and I do not care who knows, because a woman who washes is a woman who thinks somebody is going to be told," said Marguerite Vale.

"That is not a boast. It is a decision about eleven minutes in a room and it is the only decision in this entire business that is mine."

"And not one person is going to be told, because nothing in this city will carry the fact that two people were in this room tonight, and that is not a tragedy and I have written four books in my life and I am telling you that it is not a tragedy."

"You have four thousand strangers in that chest and a woman who counts for a living in it," she said, "and there is a cough in you that is yours for the rest of your life and there is nothing in this city that will give it back to you."

"And you will get up in October in a square full of people and give the rest of it away, and it will not be a gift, because the moment it is out of your hands it is not yours and never was, and you will stand there in the open with your own name on a thing you have never owned, and you will be the only person in the square who is frightened."

She rode down onto him and her whole weight came with it and her mouth was against his jaw.

"That is the whole of what I have for you tonight," said Marguerite Vale. "You can have it now."

She came with her back arched and her hands closed in the hair at the back of his head and her mouth open against his throat, and she did not make a small noise and she did not make no noise, and it went on longer than he expected and longer than she had told him, and about two thirds of the way through it she said a single word that was his name and then said it again.

He came after her with his face against her throat and both fists in the sheet on either side of her, and his chest went about nine seconds later with the cough she had promised him, and she did not stop, and she told him off about it afterwards in four words and did not mention it again that night.

## Afterwards

She lay on her back beside him with one hand flat on his chest, over the place, and let it stay there while her breathing came down.

"Now you have it," said Marguerite Vale, "and you may stop counting."

He did not stop counting and she let him lie there and did not make anything of it, and the fire had burned down about a foot and neither of them went to it.

## What She Gave

"Now my turn," she said, after a while. "In the same form. What moved, when, at what price. And you are going to hear about a body."

"Marguerite—"

"You gave me one and it was ninety seconds long and one line of it in nine was wrong," said Marguerite Vale. "Mine is going to be exact and it is going to be about a different person, and you are not going to like it, and you are not permitted to correct me, and that is the rule and you agreed to it."

She turned her head on the pillow so that he could hear her properly.

"What moved," she said, "is the youngest man I have ever had to enter, and he spent something on the fifteenth of July that a body is not supposed to be able to spend twice, and there is a plate in his coat that says rank nine and a brass in the coat with it and a clause of thirty-eight words in a book in another building, and the whole of those three things is a doorway with a man standing in it."

"When."

"The fifteenth of July, at about a quarter past twelve in the afternoon, in a square with about nine hundred people in it, and he did it on purpose, and there was a woman counting him on the second step with a board and a sheet, and he knew what it would cost and he did it anyway."

"At what price," said Lin Hao.

"At the price of a cough for the rest of your life," said Marguerite Vale, "and one woman on one side of whose head the colour went on the thirteenth of July and will not go."

"And about eleven hundred people at a church who will be told in the next three weeks that he exists, and a division with no door that will be opened in about a month, and a room at the end of a corridor that sits on the first of October with his name nowhere in the heading of either of the two documents on it."

She let that lie for a moment.

"That is your body as I entered it," said Marguerite Vale, "and it is not what you entered for it, and it is not the same body, and neither of us is wrong. You looked at a woman and found a woman. I looked at a man and found a holding that is being consumed by its own owner at a rate I can put a date to."

She sat up and got the sheet back over her shoulders.

"Now you know why I will not let you hold anything, and it is not caution and it is not the guild, and it is that I have spent four years pricing a man who is going to spend himself and I am the only person alive who can tell in what order the parts go."

## What She Would Not Put Her Name Under

She got up and washed at the basin without modesty and gave him the cloth and they dressed by the fire for a while without speaking.

Then she put her hand on the book.

"I am going to say the thing that costs me the room and then you are going to go, because you have got up at five every day this month and I am not going to have you here until two in the morning."

"Say it."

"Somebody has to put a name to a disposition of that account," said Marguerite Vale. "It has to be a person and it has to be a name and it has to be written in a hand, and the four of us who have spoken about it in six days are the only four people on earth who can do it, and three of them are at a table on Ferrier Street and the fourth is me."

"Marguerite—"

"I will not do it," she said.

The room was very quiet.

"Not because I am in the dark. Not because it is a hard question."

"Because a broker who signs a disposition is the broker who made it, and the moment my name is at the foot of that page then everything that comes out of that pot afterwards is administered by the woman who decided who it should go to."

"And in about four years there will be a line on a plate in a case in a hall with my name under it and somebody standing in front of it working out whether I was fair or whether I was right, and there is not one instrument in this city that will let them tell the difference afterwards."

"You would rather it stayed open."

"I would rather it stayed with nine people who have to look at each other," said Marguerite Vale. "That is worse. I want you to hear that it is worse, because I am a broker and I am telling you that the expensive option is the correct one and that is a thing I do not say often enough to make anybody trust it."

"Then who does."

"That is the question you should have asked me in June and not now," she said. "It does not belong to me, it does not belong to you, it does not belong to the Vant, and it does not belong to eleven thousand people behind a wall. Nine women in a kitchen know what three of them have done and the other six know the number. Ask them. You have been asking the wrong room for four years and the reason is that the wrong room always answers."

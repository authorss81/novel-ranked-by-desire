# Put In The Record And Kept

The room at the Correction Office holds nine hundred and on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of July there were about eleven hundred and forty people in it and about ninety of them standing in the aisles because there was nowhere else in the building to put them.

That is the number the Vestry had produced on the twenty-first, one in ten by lot in the order of a roll, and they had all come on the third day because the officer had entered the Church's evidence for the third day and the Vestry's own book had a column headed with the third day on it.

There was no compulsion in that room at about two o'clock in the afternoon and there had been none since about half past twelve.

## The Thing She Could Not Do At That Size

She had stood up at half past nine and told them.

"I held this yesterday for two hours and fifty minutes in front of three hundred and forty people," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have held it nine times in my life and the largest of those was nine, and I told the officer on the twenty-fourth that I did not know what it does at eleven hundred and I am telling this room now instead of finding out in the middle of somebody's sentence."

The room made a noise.

"I can hold it for the morning. At this size I can hold it until about half past twelve and after half past twelve I have nothing, and everything said in this room after half past twelve can be used against the person who said it, including anything I say."

There was no noise from the front and about two hundred people at the back understood at once what had happened to them, which was that the morning was safe and the afternoon was not, and a handful of them left the room and went out into the yard to think about it.

"Which means that this afternoon is the first afternoon of this hearing in which anything anybody says is evidence," she said. "I am sorry for it. I did not choose the size of the room and I could not have chosen it."

## What Was Said About It

The reservoir was discussed in public for the first time in twenty years of Bureau practice and it was discussed for six hours and forty minutes and it was discussed wrong.

A carter who had come in off a night said it was a levy, and said that the Board had been sitting on its own money for three decades and had let a man have it because he was cheap to administer, and about four hundred people agreed with him and it was not the case.

A woman who kept a stall on the south side of the Reading said it was a lottery, and wanted to know whose name was on it, and had wanted to know that for four days.

A man from Cordwain said it was the Veil. He said the city had been measured wrong before the Veil and there was a storehouse of the difference and nobody had ever been given it, and the officer asked him four questions and he answered three of them.

A schoolmistress off Lamplit Row said it belonged to the Church, because eleven thousand people had been putting it in for thirty years and eleven thousand people ought to have it back, and there was about a hundred and twenty people in that room who agreed with her and they were not all in the same line of opinion about it.

And a woman in the fourteenth row, who gave her name as Petra Vahl and who was a keeper of nothing at all, said that it belonged to nobody and that the only honest thing to do with a thing that belonged to nobody was to give it out to people who were under a floor and had no instrument, and that it ought to be done now and by somebody with a book.

That was six answers and not one of them was right and there was not one person in the whole of that room who knew that, including the woman in the fourteenth row, and the answer that was going to be in front of the room on the fourth day was an answer that had been in the fourteenth row of a hearing room since half past twelve on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of July.

## What The Subject May Not Do

He sat in the fourth row for six hours and forty minutes and there were eleven answers he could have corrected and he corrected none of them.

A subject does not instruct the room. Not because anybody wrote it down and not because somebody had thought of it and not because it would be improper: because the moment a man who is the subject of a public hearing begins to tell a room what the facts are, the hearing stops being a hearing and becomes an argument he is having from a seat four rows back.

Every one of the three hundred and forty people who came on the second day and every one of the nine hundred on the third would have gone home having heard a man explain himself.

So he sat in the fourth row with his hands on his knees and said nothing for six hours and forty minutes while three hundred and forty wrong answers went into a book.

At about four o'clock a woman turned round and asked him directly, from the fourth row back, whether the money was his.

He could have said eleven words. He has had them since the sixth of July and he has not said them in public once in three weeks and there were nine hundred people in that room and a clerk at a table with a day's book.

"No," he said.

"Was it ever going to be anybody's."

"I don't know," said Lin Hao. "That is not me being careful. I have worked it out four times since the fourteenth of March and I have got four different answers and all four of them were true when I said them."

## The Sentence That Went In And Stayed

The officer made a short entry at about twenty past four on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of July and read it back before he entered it, in the way a clerk reads back, the date first and the hour with it.

"The twenty-eighth of July. The hearing is in public at the Correction Office. The reservoir referred to in the notice posted at the head of the Reading on the twenty-third of July was discussed. It is held by no person. It has never been paid. It has never been drawn on. There is no list. The record carries the number of the finding of the thirtieth of June and it does not carry the name of the subject, and the subject was attended and did not speak except as recorded."

Then he said the part that mattered and he said it as a clerk and not as an officer.

"The record is kept."

That is the whole of it. A record is not the same thing as a finding and it does not carry a seal and it does not make anybody a holder of anything, and it cannot be ordered, and it cannot be classified, and it cannot be read at a valuation.

It is four lines and an hour and the number of a finding, and it goes into a book on a shelf in that building on the afternoon of the day, and anybody in this city may go to that building and put their name in a day's book and ask to read what the hearing said on the twenty-eighth of July.

It goes up on the standing sheet at the head of every line, and the standing sheet has nine lines on it, and it goes out at four in the afternoon to a room at the end of a corridor that cannot sit again until the first of October.

He had spent four years making sure that there was not one paper in this city with the word on it. There was one now. He had not signed it, he had not asked for it, and he had been in the room for six hours and forty minutes while it was made.

## Nine Thousand People Under A Wall Four Streets Away

He went to the north end of the ranks wall at about half past five on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of July because he went there every morning at seven and this was the nearest thing to an ordinary day he was going to get.

About nine thousand people go under that wall a day in a city where the fourth ward is the second largest anybody has ever measured, and the square was full at about half past five with people coming out of the Reading, and roughly nine hundred of them had been in that building all day and eleven hundred and forty of them had been in it for part of it and none of them knew what the other people had heard.

The word had gone round the north end in five days. It was on the notice on the wall with four days under it, and it was on a piece of paper behind the door of a counter at Cordwain where a woman had written it out in full in a clerk's hand because people kept asking, and it was on the side of the ranks wall in somebody else's chalk in about sixty strokes with nothing behind them, because a square full of people does that.

There was a woman selling fruit on the south side who had kept the place at seven in the morning since the eleventh and who was now, on the twenty-eighth, doing it out of a habit that was no longer for anybody, because he had not been at that step since the nineteenth.

He stood at the north end at about ten past six in the evening, at the hour he had not been there for nine days, with no queue and no chair and about nine thousand people going under a wall above his head, and nothing at all in his hands.

## Six In The Morning

Beatrix Halm had been at the north end at ten to six that morning, before the city was anything, and had written one line in her notebook with the time against it and one word in it and nothing under the word, because there was nothing under it yet and she was not going to invent something.

Then she had gone to a building off a third court on the day before and sat at the back of a hearing room for two hours and fifty minutes, and at about one o'clock she had felt the thing in that room close, and she had not said it to one person and written it in a book and come out onto a landing and been told by a man with his back against a wall what he had worked out.

At ten past six in the evening she came past the north end on the way home from a shift and she stopped, and he was standing there, and neither of them said anything about the nine days.

"Three hundred and forty wrong answers," said Beatrix Halm.

"About that."

"And four lines and an hour and a number and no name on it, kept on a shelf in that building where anybody can ask for it."

"Yes."

She put the notebook away and looked up at the wall for a while, at the tiles going up in ranks under a rail, and at the top of them, where the highest one is a different colour from all of them, and there is a name on it in chalk in her own hand in the hand that is not the one she holds the pencil in, and it has been there since the thirtieth of June with nothing under it.

"Ninety-one strokes on the eleventh," she said, "and about sixty today, and neither lot of them is on a card, and none of it will be read by anybody at a valuation on the thirtieth of September."

"No."

"It is all still not on anything," said Beatrix Halm. "Eleven hundred people in a room, nine thousand people under a wall, and not one grain of it is on a card, and the only thing in the whole of this that is going to still be there in four years is four lines on a shelf in a building off a third court."

"That is what I have been afraid of since the fourteenth of March," he said, "and it took me four years to understand that it was never the wall or the crowd or her, and it was always four lines on a shelf, and that the reason nobody stopped me is that in this city there is not one office that has ever been asked to destroy a record and not one person who has ever been able to pay for one being destroyed."

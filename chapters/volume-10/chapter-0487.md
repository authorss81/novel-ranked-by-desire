# The Second Day

On the morning of the second of October there were people standing still in the streets of Ostara who had been told to, and there were people standing still in the streets of Ostara who had worked it out for themselves, and the two groups did not know about each other and were indistinguishable from about forty feet away.

He went out at about eight in the morning and was back at about half past six and he covered about nine miles of the city on foot and none of it was a round anybody pays for.

"That is the whole of it," said Beatrix Halm, on the step of a tram at about ten past eleven, with her notebook open on her knees. "That is what I have got and it is going to be the same all day, and I am going to write the times down anyway, because a thing that is not a line is all I have ever had to work with."

"What have you got."

"Numbers." She shut it. "And I am not going to say them out loud, and you are not going to ask me, and we had this argument in a kitchen on the twentieth of August and you lost it then and you have lost it since."

## What It Is When A Person Waits For It

He had not understood that, and it took him until about two in the afternoon in a lane off Cordwain.

"Ask that woman what she is doing."

"I have asked her twice and she has told me twice and she is not going to tell me a third time," said Beatrix Halm. "She is standing still because somebody told her to. She does not know what it is for. She was here yesterday at the wall and she was not here at eleven minutes past and somebody told her to be here at eleven minutes past, and she has been here since a quarter to ten."

"And yesterday she went past about nine thousand people in a square and felt nothing at all."

"And yesterday she went past nine thousand people and felt nothing at all," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the whole difference and it took thirty years and a man at a wall to produce it, and it is the only fact anybody has about any of this, and it is not a fact anybody can enter."

## The Card Did Not Move

Nobody was on the wall at the north end of the Reading at about half past two in the afternoon except a boy with a paste brush who was not there either, because the card had come down at half past four yesterday and the next one is in ninety days.

"The card of yesterday afternoon is the only figure this city has produced about any of it," said Beatrix Halm, "and it printed eight against that address at half past four yesterday."

"I was at the wall at ten past five last night and this morning I went and looked at it in daylight and it is the same eight, and it will be the same eight on the first of January. Nothing in this city will move that figure by one grain because of the second of October."

"A woman in a lane off Cordwain stood still for four seconds this morning at about a quarter to eleven."

"And she is not on the card," said Beatrix Halm. "And she is not on the card tomorrow. And there is not a form in four buildings in this city that she could be put on if she wanted one, and if there were, she would not be allowed to write what she felt on it, and if she wrote it anyway it would be one sentence in a stranger's hand in four years with her name on it and nothing to stand behind it."

She put the notebook under her arm.

"Write that down, because you will want it later and you will want it to be somebody else's fault."

## A Household In Nine Places

Nine women, nine streets, and not one of them in a position to be in two places, and the whole of it arranged in about eleven minutes on a step at Ferrier Street at a quarter to eight in the morning by a woman who had not slept.

"Ines is at the counter in Cordwain and she is not doing it as an auditor and she has told nobody that and I have told nobody that," said Beatrix Halm. "Marguerite is at the Vant and has been since seven and the real books are chained on her table. Runa is on a round with a sheet and she is writing down times in her own hand and she is not giving the sheet to anybody."

"Isolde went down off the Bellhouse at about nine with her hair wet and she has been singing four bars about it every hour since and it is not an accusation and it is not a defence."

"Nadia is at the bottom of the Vestry shaft. She is there because she is there. I am not going to say anything else about Nadia and if anybody asks me I will say she was in a lift."

"And Petra."

"Petra is at the table with her hands in her lap," said Beatrix Halm, "and they are not working, and she has said she would rather have it than not have it, and that is the whole of what I have got."

## What A City Does With Something It Cannot Explain

At about four in the afternoon there was a queue at the counter at Cordwain.

It was not a queue for the counter. It was a queue that had formed about two feet in front of the counter and run for about thirty feet back up the lane, and there were about two hundred people in it, and they were standing still.

"That is not a queue for anything," said Beatrix Halm. "That counter is closed at four."

"What is it a queue for."

"It is a queue for the street," said Beatrix Halm, "and there is nothing in this city that has ever formed one of those, and I have been reading walls for nine years and I am telling you that the only time this city has made a line anywhere is at the Exchange of an ordinary weekend when a figure gets out, and that line disperses inside four days."

"It is the second of October."

"It is the second of October," said Beatrix Halm, "and there are about two hundred people standing still in a lane in Cordwain for a reason that none of them can put on anything, and I do not know how long it lasts and I do not know who put it there and neither do you, and I am the only person in this city with a notebook and I am not writing this down in a column."

## What It Is Going To Cost

They went back up the hill at about half past four and at the top of it he stopped in the lane, and the chest was still going at about the rate it had been at eight in the morning and it had not got better in two days, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that can do anything about that either.

"Nine feet of stone," he said.

"Agnete Lindqvist has been standing nine feet off that thing for about thirty hours," said Beatrix Halm, "and four hundred of her own have been standing in a yard on the Church's ground since about nine in the morning with nothing to do and nothing to hear and no list of anything, and she has fed them, because a chaplain who cannot feed four hundred people does not get to keep the yard."

"Four days."

"Four days is the second of them," said Beatrix Halm. "You said four days in that room and I have got it in a book and I have had it since about eleven minutes past five yesterday."

She went up the hill.

And at about a quarter past nine on the night of the second of October, Runa Sato came in off a round and put a sheet on the kitchen table face up and did not sit down.

"There is a man in Cordwain selling it," she said. "A stool and a line and a man taking a grain off anybody who wants to stand in it, and he has been doing it since about six this evening and there are about four hundred people behind his line and he is taking money off every one of them and he is not lying to a single one of them."

"Taking money for a street."

"Taking money for a place to stand still," said Runa Sato. "That is the whole of it and that is all he is doing, and he has invented in one afternoon the only thing anybody in this city has ever wanted from a person who holds a thing."

"And he has invented it on a stool, in a lane, with his own hands, and not one instrument in this city can touch him and there is not one office a person can walk into and say what he is doing."

"And the four hundred behind him."

"The four hundred behind him are not behind him," said Runa Sato. "They are in a lane. They happen to be behind a man with a stool. He put himself in front of them about six hours ago and he has been there ever since, and tomorrow morning there will be a great many more of them, and in nine years I have never once seen this city make a line that a rumour did not make, and a rumour always goes away inside four days."

"Then find out who he is and bring me him."

"I have not got that and I am not going to have it by tomorrow," said Runa Sato, "and I am bringing you the sheet because there is one more thing on it, and it is worse, and I have been standing in a lane for two hours working out whether to bring you a sheet or to bring you nothing."

"How much of it."

"Seventy-one paid him a grain and did not go into the line," said Runa Sato. "Seventy-one, out of about four hundred. They paid and they walked away."

"A grain is about a quarter of a day's subsistence at the bottom band, and he has taken seventy-one of them out of about forty people's worth in one afternoon, out of the right to stand still on a pavement in his own city. He did not have to lie to one of them, and he has done nothing that can be brought before anybody, and that is the second of four."

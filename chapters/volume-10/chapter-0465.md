# The Hour Before On A Roof

The last car up the Ferrier hill on the night of the twenty-fifth of July goes at about eleven and is empty, and Beatrix Halm was on the roof of it with the notebook in the inside pocket of her coat and her hands in her sleeves.

The wind on that hill in July is the kind that finds the gap between two cars, and she had been riding the roof of her own line for nine years because a signal operator who sits inside a car cannot hear a car.

The hearing was at ten in the morning. This was the hour before the night before it, and there was a woman on the roof of a tram and a man on the roof of a tram, and under them the whole of Ostara was going about eleven o'clock at night in the noise of itself.

"I have been up here since eight," she said. "Get on, and do not shout, I will move my head."

He got on and sat down on the ridge above the third car with his back to the wind and let her turn her good ear at him, which is a thing you can see a woman do from four feet away and which makes every conversation she has ever had look like a conversation at the wrong end of a room.

## Two Triangulations

"There are two of them," said Beatrix Halm. "Not two copies. Two."

"Your one and another one."

"My one and a different one, worked from the same marks, by somebody who has never seen my notebook and who did not walk the north end at six in the morning because there is nobody alive who could stand at that wall at six in the morning unless they had been doing it for years." She held the notebook flat against the roof with one hand.

"Mine is finished. It has been finished since the seventeenth and I have not shown it to one person, including the man at the north end of the wall who has spent eight days sitting under it."

"Mine."

"Mine has been in my own pocket since the seventeenth of July, and the second one has been in a room in a building that has had the shape of something since the first of January, since about the eleventh, and it came out four days before I finished mine, and it is not the same work."

## The Shape And Not The Figures

"It does not have your figures in it," he said.

"It has not got a figure in it at all," said Beatrix Halm. "It has got the shape. The wall round the north end of the Reading has had marks on it in somebody else's chalk since the eleventh, and there are marks on three other walls, and a woman on the eleventh was putting one stroke on the wall for every person who went up to that card."

"And there are ninety-one of them by the time the light goes, and about ninety people come past that end every day and not one of them comes past at six in the morning."

"Those are the marks."

"Those are the marks. Somebody has taken the shape of them — the number of walls, the number of people, the spread over four days, the way the weeks of it sit on top of each other — and they have worked the same answer out of the shape that I got out of the round." She turned her face into the wind for a second and back. "And it came out of my own division, Lin Hao. It came out of the desk that takes my returns."

He did not say anything for about half a minute, and the tram went over the crest and dropped into the cutting and came out the other side of it, and under them a street that had been lit at dusk was lit again by nothing at all.

"Who in the desk."

"That is not the question and I know it is not the question and you are going to ask me the other one," she said. "You are going to ask me whether I know who it is."

"Yes."

"I know who it is," said Beatrix Halm. "And I have known since the fourteenth, which is the night I told your kitchen table that a crowd does not carry chalk between four walls, and I have not said one word of it to one person for eleven days because the woman it is has been in my division for two years and she is good at her job and she does not know that she is doing it."

## Wren Kallo Cannot Keep A Secret

"She is Wren Kallo."

"She is Wren Kallo," said Beatrix Halm. "You will get the rest of it inside a day whatever I do, and I would rather you had it from a roof than from a clerk. Wren is the best counter-forensics officer in this city and she has never once been careful, and it is not a failing that anybody has ever had to correct, because she has never had anything to keep."

"She took my sheets for nine months off a window of a box office and she never told a woman at that window what she was doing with them, and when she was asked, she answered in about eleven words and then she said nothing else for nine months."

"That is not careless. That is a person who does not think there is a thing to be careful with."

"It is a person who cannot hold a thing," said Beatrix Halm. "She is the only person I have ever worked beside who cannot hold a thing, and I have watched her for two years and I have never once been able to make her hold one, and I stopped trying about a year ago."

"That is not a complaint. It is the reason I did not know about the second triangulation until the fourteenth of July, when she put a sheet on my desk that had a shape on it that I had used and not written down."

## What He Could Have Done With It

He had it now, all of it, sitting in his chest with the cough, on a moving roof, at about a quarter past eleven at night, a day and a half before a room of three hundred people.

He had a standing of nine and a piece of brass in a coat and a clerk of the Concil of the Nine who would enter anything he brought her and correct nothing, and he had a clause of thirty-eight words in a book that put him in the corridor outside the door of every matter about his address, and there was a hearing in the morning and a finding carried under five seals.

He could have put it in.

Nine hundred words and the shape of a round and the name of a division and a date, entered by a clerk, on a standing sheet, on a morning, in front of a room. The Auditorate would have had to open it.

The Auditorate does not have a door and has never had one and keeps fourteen lent counters, and an opened thing goes to nine desks inside a day, and every counter in it comes off its posting inside a month, and a woman called Wren Kallo who cannot hold a thing would have been the one it was done to, because she is the one who let go of it.

He had been looking for an instrument for four years. This was the best instrument he had ever been handed and it had been handed to him by accident, on a roof, by a woman who was not trying to give it to him.

"I am not going to spend you," he said.

## What He Said About It, And The Rule She Made Him Say It Under

"Say that again and say what it costs."

"I am not going to spend you, and I am not going to put your division in a record, and I am going to go into a room tomorrow morning with nothing in my hands that I did not walk in with, and if Wren Kallo has told four people by the fourth of August then four people will have heard it by the fourth of August and it will be on the Council's desk by the first of October."

"Say it in order," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the rule and you are on a line and I am going to do it to you whether or not you have ever been in a cab."

"Why."

"Because a man on a tram roof at a quarter past eleven at night who says *I am not going to spend you* has made a decision and there is no way on this earth of knowing afterwards what he decided about. In a cab you say what you felt and in the order it came and you are not allowed to go back and improve it, and it has been that way for four years and I have never once wanted to improve one and tonight I want to."

"Fine."

"Fine," said Beatrix Halm. "Say the first one."

"You have been carrying it since the fourteenth of July."

"I have been carrying it since the fourteenth of July."

"The second."

"And I knew on the fourteenth of July that if I carried it, Wren would be found in about four days, because Wren cannot hold a thing and has never been able to."

"The third."

"And I am carrying it anyway."

"The fourth."

"And you are going to hear the rest of it from me and not from a record, and I am telling you the price out loud before I charge it to you, because that is what the rule is for."

## The Rule Of The Cab

"What the line reports is what she felt, in order," said Beatrix Halm, "and she may not go back and improve it afterwards. Not to soften it and not to put it in the right place."

"If it goes out wrong it goes out wrong and it is a wrong report about the city and there are nine hundred and forty other people on this division whose returns go up on the same sheet and I will not be the one who has made this line into something it is not."

"That is not a rule anybody makes you keep."

"It is the only rule I have ever been given that I have never once wanted to break," she said, "and I will tell you why. If I can go back and improve one, then in four years a man has been sitting at a wall reading my times off me, and neither of us has ever known whether he was reading me or reading something I had made better for him. That is the whole of what a person owes the next person on a line."

The tram went over the crest again and the city came up under them on both sides and she put one hand on the rail and did not hold it.

"Now the first one."

"What."

"Tell me what you feel," said Beatrix Halm, "and I will say what I feel, and it is a quarter past eleven and the hearing is in the morning and we have one car left up this hill."

## Out Of Order

She went out of order about nine minutes in and she did not know she had done it.

He had his hand on her knee and she had one hand flat on the roof and her face turned into the good ear, and she said, "That," and then said, "Now that," and then said, "That one is later," and he understood about four seconds after she had said it that the three things had not come in the order they arrived in.

She understood at the same moment, because she took her hand off the roof and put it flat on her own notebook in her pocket and said, out loud, to a tram:

"Out of order."

She did not take it back.

"That is the rule," she said. "I may not go back and improve it. It has gone out of order and I cannot put it back and that is four years of me never once wanting to and it has happened once, tonight, on a roof, and I have not got a figure for it and I am not going to make one."

## The Roof, And The Whole Of It

The wind came round the side of the car and took the words off in pieces and he had to put his mouth against her ear to be heard, and she put her hand on the back of his head to hold him there, and they went up the last of the hill in that position like two people working in a gale.

She got her own coat open and then his, and she was warm underneath it in July and her hands were cold, because they are cold every morning at seven in the cab and they have been cold every morning for nine years, and she put them under his shirt against his ribs and said, "There."

"That is out of order."

"That is out of order," she said, "and you have not been going up this hill for nine years and you do not get to correct it."

He got the coat off her shoulders and she got his open and neither of them managed it the way they would have on a floor, and the coat caught at an elbow and she swore at it in the ordinary way of a woman on a moving tram, and he laughed out loud about four inches from her mouth, and she said, "Now that. Out of order again."

"Out of order."

Her mouth was cold and the inside of it was not. She kissed him with her hand still on the back of his head and made the noise she makes into his throat, because she cannot hear herself make it and has never once tried to, and it comes back at her through the bones of his neck.

"Report."

"I have got it."

"Out of order."

"Out of order."

She pulled back about two inches and put her mouth against his ear instead, which is the only way to say anything at that speed on that line.

"That one is the first one," she said.

"That one came after the one with your hand on the back of my head."

"That is the whole of it," said Beatrix Halm, "and I have been trying to get a proper order out of it since eight o'clock and there is not one, and I am not going to improve it, and if I say it out of order on a line in front of four hundred people tomorrow it will be exactly as wrong as it is now."

The car went over the crest and dropped into the cutting and the whole of the city came up on the right-hand side of them for about two seconds, a wall of lit windows and a chimney and a crane standing over something that was not there any more.

They went up the last of the hill with her against the side of the car and the notebook in her coat pocket pressed against his ribs, and she got it out and dropped it on the floor of the car without looking for it, which is the first time in four years that book has been on the floor of anything.

"That is the one," she said.

"That is the one that came first."

"It did not come first," said Beatrix Halm. "It came last, and I said it first, and I have not got a rule for it and I have got a woman on a roof who cannot hear herself think on a busy evening, and that is very likely the rule."

Her thighs came up round his hips and she got a hand behind his neck and pulled him down onto her, and she lay back on the ridge above the third car with her shoulders off the metal and her back against the rise of it.

"Say it again," she said. "The first one. Out loud. I cannot hear it over the tram and I cannot feel it through a chest."

He said it again.

She said it into his mouth after him, which is not a thing the rule allows and which she did anyway, and then said, "That is an improvement and I am not permitted one, and I am reporting it because I did it."

"Report it."

"It is out of order," said Beatrix Halm, "and I have been in a cab for nine years and the whole of what I am is that I may not go back and improve one, and I have never wanted to until about a quarter past eleven tonight."

He got an arm under her and she got her hips up off the ridge and he got inside her, and the whole of the cold went out of her in about two seconds, which she reported.

"That one," she said. "Cold. Out of order."

"Cold came first."

"Cold came after my mouth."

"Cold came after your mouth and after the noise on the bones of my neck and after the thing I said about being caught at an elbow," he said, "and if you want the whole of it in order then it is not going to happen tonight and it is not going to happen tomorrow either, and you are going into a room full of people who will believe whatever version of you you give them, including the wrong one."

She laughed at that, with her back off the roof of a moving tram, and then the crest caught them.

She said the whole of it out loud after that. She said it into the wind and the wind took the front of it and gave the back of it to the metal of the car, and about a fifth of a second later the roof gave the whole sentence back to her, which is what she has been listening to on that line for nine years and which is the only thing that has ever told her where she was.

"Report," she said, when she could.

"You are shouting."

"Report."

"You are inside me and your back is off the roof and you shouted something about the fifth of a second."

"That is not what I said."

"That is not what I said either," he said, "which is the first time tonight that the two of us have agreed on the order of anything."

"That one is later," said Beatrix Halm, "and I am telling you it is later, and I could not tell you why, and there is a roof under us and it is going over a crest about every ninety seconds and if I could hear myself think I would."

She went on saying things, wrong, in order, and about every fourth one she would put her mouth against his ear and say, "Out of order," out loud, in the ordinary voice of a woman at a window telling another woman a figure, and then carry on.

He held her hips and went with the rhythm she could feel through the bone of him and could not hear, and her fingers went into the hair at the back of his head and held on, and at some point in the middle of it she said his name twice and the second time it was not in order either.

## The End Of It

She came with her mouth open on his wrist and her whole back off the ridge of the car, and the tram went over the crest in the middle of it, and afterwards she said she had not felt the crest at all and had felt the brakes eleven seconds later.

Both of those are true. Only one of them is in order.

He came after her with his face in her hair and his hand closed on hers on the metal, and she held it there and let him, and the whole of the noise of a city went past underneath them for about ninety seconds and neither of them said anything at all, which is the first silence she has had on that hill in nine years and she has never said so and says nothing about it now.

Then she got back off him and sat up and pulled her coat over her knees with both hands and reached down for the notebook on the floor of the car, and picked it up, and did not open it.

He took his time getting his shirt out of the belt of his trousers with the tram going round the bottom of the hill, and she watched his hands and did not offer.

"Right," said Beatrix Halm. "Report."

"That one is yours."

"It is mine and it is in order and you are not to have it," she said, "and I am not going to be told about the chest by a man on a tram roof at one in the morning, and if you cough on me up this hill I am going to put your head out of the window and I have put a head out of a window before."

"I have not coughed."

"You coughed at about twenty past eleven and you did not know you did it, and that is the third time out of order tonight and it is the only one I am not going to report, because I am not writing that down in a book on a tram."

She did not improve anything.

Not one word of it, for the rest of the night, on the rest of the line, going down into the city with the noise coming up through the floor.

## Her Stop

Her stop is four streets from Ferrier Street and it is on the crown of a hill and the car was empty when it stopped and empty when it started again.

He got off with her because there was no other end to it.

"The price," she said, on the pavement, in the wind, turning her good ear. "You asked me to say the price before I charged it."

"Four days. Wren Kallo found on or about the nineteenth, the Auditorate opened or not opened, eleven hundred people at a church with a roll on them know it by the end of the month, and it is on the Council's desk on the first of October in a minute that a clerk will enter because a clerk may only enter."

"That is all of it."

"That is the whole of it and it is not the end of the book and you know that as well as I do."

"I know it," said Beatrix Halm. "And I have been up here since eight and I have not written anything down in nine days, and I am going to write it down tonight in order, and there will be one line out of order in it, and that is the whole of what I am carrying into a room tomorrow morning that I have not already got."

## What She Felt First

"Can I ask you something and you can say no."

"Ask."

She looked up the hill at the line the car had gone up, and then at the four streets between that and Ferrier Street and a building with a door at the end of it that six women were asleep behind.

"What did you feel first," said Beatrix Halm. "In order. The first one, not the one you said first."

He thought about it for about as long as it took a car to clear the junction.

"The roof," he said. "Under us. That it moved and it did not stop moving and we were going over the crest about every ninety seconds and you could feel the whole weight of it go."

"Out of order."

"Out of order."

"That is right," she said, "and it is not what you felt first either, and you have just told me that and heard yourself tell me, and that is three of them tonight."

Then she said the thing she had come up the hill to say and had not said at any point in an hour and ten minutes.

"I felt the room go quiet before this car reached the crest," said Beatrix Halm. "Twice. Once about twenty past ten and once about half past, and both times it was a room with people in it, four streets from here, and both times there were more people in it than there have ever been in it in my life."

"Who was in it."

"Somebody was holding it," she said, "and she has never held it in a room with a hundred people in it in my life, and neither has anybody else, and there are four days of hearing starting at ten in the morning and I have got a place on that room because I have never once been in one and I am going to sit at the back."

"There is no list of anybody."

"There is no list of anybody," said Beatrix Halm, "and I have never once needed one, and I am going to walk into a building in the morning and sit down at the back of a room and you are not able to stop me and neither is anybody else."

# The Fourth Day

By eight o'clock on the morning of the fourth of October the thing had stopped being an event in the streets of Ostara and had become a fact.

And there is a difference between those two things that nobody in this city has ever been able to put on anything. On the first of October about forty people watched a man do nothing at a wall. By the fourth of October there were about three hundred people standing still in a lane in Cordwain who had got there on their own feet and could not have told you where the instruction came from.

He was in the corridor off a second landing from about nine until about eleven because that is where he is now, and the clause of thirty-eight words has not been struck and is not going to be struck, and the room does not sit again until the first of January.

"You have not seen a corridor for four days," said Yusuf Adeyemi.

"I have seen nine feet of a corridor at a time for four days and I have got rather good at it."

"You have got rank one and assigned work in it and you have not taken either of them up yet, and I am going to have to make you take them up by the eleventh, and I am telling you now so that you have four days to be ready to be told by a man who does not enjoy it."

## What Is Left Of A City

There is a thing that happens to a street in the four days after something enormous and it is not the enormous thing. It is that everybody in it is tired and slightly short of sleep and extremely careful with each other, and the trades get slower, and people are unusually polite to people they have never spoken to.

Ostara on the morning of the fourth of October was a city being careful with itself.

At about a quarter past nine he was at the north end of the ranks wall and there were about nine thousand people under it because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it, and there were about thirty of them standing still, and there was a man with a stool and a line at the south end of the wall, and there was another one in a lane off Cordwain.

"There were two stools on the second night," said Beatrix Halm.

"There are five that I have seen since eight o'clock this morning."

"Five," said Beatrix Halm. "I have got them on the board in the last column, which is a column I invented on the second night and which has nothing at all under it, and I have told nobody about the column and I am not going to."

## The Counter Off Cordwain

Ines Duarte was on the counter at Cordwain on the fourth day, in the ordinary way, and at about eleven o'clock a man of about fifty came up with his hat in his hands and no paper at all.

He stood there for about eleven seconds.

"I did a thing in a doorway," he said. "In the spring. I carried a woman down four floors in a February and she died in my arms on the fourth landing and I did not get her name and I do not know who she was and I have had it in my chest since the second of February and somebody in a lane off Bellhouse told me a man gave it back on the first and I want to know if I can put it in."

"No," said Ines Duarte.

That was the whole of what she said and she said it in the ordinary voice of a woman at a counter at about eleven in the morning on the fourth of October, and she did not soften it and she did not explain it, and the man looked at her and then put his hat on and said, "Right," and went away.

He got about four feet into the lane before he stopped and came back.

"Is it because I cannot say her name."

"Because nothing will take it," said Ines Duarte. "Nothing in this city will put it on a form, nothing will print it in a column, and there is no book at any counter for a thing a person did in a doorway, and I have been an auditor for eleven years and I have looked, and I am not going to send you to another desk to hear the same sentence."

"Is that all of it."

"That is all of it and I am sorry and it is not going to change by the sixth," said Ines Duarte, and she turned back to the paper in front of her.

She did not follow him out and she did not watch him go, and she has not mentioned that conversation to anybody since, and that is the whole of how the fourth of October treated the first person in this city who came to a counter with an unrecorded deed and asked whether it could be entered.

It cannot be entered.

## The Yard Off Cordwain

Agnete Lindqvist had held the yard from nine until about two in the afternoon and had let four hundred people stand in it with nothing to do, and had fed them, and had not once said anything about the wall.

"The Church's position is on the record and it is the same position it was on the twenty-ninth of July," she said. "It cannot be given back because there is no list of what it is made of. That is true today and it was true on the first and it will be true in January, and I have said it to four hundred people three days running and I will go on saying it."

"You watched it happen."

"I watched eight women put their hands on a wall and a thousand streets go cold for four seconds and not one person in this city be able to say afterwards whether they had been given something," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And I was right. I have not stopped being right and I have not stopped being the only person in this room who was, and I have no intention of stopping."

"Then what changes."

"About eleven hundred people came to a hearing about this in July because I asked them to in nine minutes, after twenty years of never asking my board for anything," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And eleven thousand know what we said."

"And they did not come to that wall on the first, and they were not asked to, and about four hundred of them came to a yard instead. They stood in a yard for three days being fed off my own board and told a thing they already believed by a woman they had known all their lives."

She stopped.

"That is what changes and it is not enough and it is not going to become enough, and I have known since the twenty-ninth of July that it would not, and I did it anyway, because I am not going to be a woman who was right and said nothing about it."

## The Line Under The Last One

The standing sheet went out at four o'clock in the afternoon of the fourth of October with the address at the head of every line, and the clerk who typed it may only enter and cannot be asked to hurry, and the line under the last one on that sheet was the first line in four months that carried an address and no band and no figure against it.

It said this:

*the reservoir, held by no person, claimed by the Church of the Quiet Number, answered by the subject. Disposition: given away in the open, the first of October, holder none.*

And under that, in the clerk's own hand, because a clerk enters what she is given and cannot improve it:

*amended the same day, the same page, no appeal, the file of the ninth of March, the file bearing the name it bears, removed permanently; the standing sheet and the requisition of the twentieth of February are both there at the head of every line and neither of them can be administered against that address on the morning of the fifth of October.*

"That last part," said Yusuf Adeyemi, reading it over his shoulder at about ten past five. "That is Ferreira's hand and not the clerk's, and she wrote it into a sheet that goes to seven desks, and there is not one instrument in this city that can stop a document going out at four o'clock."

"Did she sign it."

"She signed the amendment on the first," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "And she wrote that on a standing sheet out of her own hand at about ten past five this afternoon, and she did not show it to me, and I found it because I read sheets."

## Five Stools And A Slate

He was up the hill at about half past six when Runa Sato came round the corner of the lane at a walk, and she had been out on a round for about nine hours and she had not been in the house since six in the morning.

"There is a slate," she said.

"Where."

"Against the wall at the south end of the ranks wall, propped on two bricks, with a figure chalked on it in about four-inch letters, and it is a figure of merit and it is not the figure off a card and it is not the figure off any plate in this city, and it is not a price."

"Not a price."

"It is a figure of merit with nothing behind it," said Runa Sato. "That is what he told them. Five for a place at the front and one for the back of the line, and a person need not buy it and need not bring anything."

"And it is not the Board and it is not a plate and it is not a keeper, and I asked him where he got it off and he said he made it up in the last three days. And I asked him what happens when it is wrong and he said it will be right by the end of the month."

"He is not lying."

"He is not lying about one thing in about four, and he is not lying about the figure either, because he believes it," said Runa Sato. "He believes it because it is a figure and figures do not have to have anything behind them to be believed, that is what they are for, and he has worked it out in three days, and I have stood in front of him for about a quarter of an hour and I could not make him see a single reason why not."

She put her hand flat on the lane wall.

"So that is what the fourth day has," she said. "Five stools and a slate, and a figure of merit in chalk with nothing at all behind it, on a wall that a boy painted on at half past four yesterday morning with a number off a card."

"And not one instrument in this city can touch a person for writing a figure on a slate, and no plate anywhere says that figure is wrong, and I have been out on a round for nine hours and I could not find anybody who thought it was."

# The Claim The Church Makes

The fourth day of the hearing was the twenty-ninth of July and the room was full by a quarter to eleven, and the Vestry had brought its eleven hundred into a building that holds nine hundred, and about two hundred of them were in the corridor and on the stairs and in the yard.

She was on the book under her own name and she had been on the book under her own name since the twenty-first of July, and the book is a bound book about two inches thick with two columns on the fly-leaf, and the second column said Agnete Lindqvist and not the household and not a man, and under it one name where the line was ruled for a witness.

## What She Put In, In Four Parts

She stood up at about eleven and she read it off the book with the book shut and she had learned it.

"First. The reservoir was never held by the subject. It has been filling for thirty years out of things done in doorways by strangers who never met one another and never met him, and he is not the holder of it because nobody was ever the holder of it. A man is an address on a thing, and a thing that is filling has no owner until somebody spends it, and he has spent a part of it, and I do not put that against him and neither should you."

"Second. It cannot be returned. You cannot return a thing to people who are not recorded, because not being recorded is the whole of what they have in common, and there is no list, and he has said in this room and to me and in his own head since the fourteenth of March that he does not know how many there are and does not know how many there have been."

"Third. Therefore the disposition is distribution. Not return. Distribution. Given out, to people below a floor, in the open, by a body with a book on it and more than one name on the book, and not by a person, because a person is what the four of us have been arguing about for four days and I am the fourth."

"Fourth," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I am going to say this one slowly and I am going to stop halfway through it because the other half is the part that will be argued about for eleven weeks, and the half I am going to say now is that a return is a man deciding on his own, in his own head, who was owed what, and that there is no list, and that therefore the most he can be asked to do is decide it alone."

She stopped.

"Three hundred and ninety words," said the officer, "and I have got three hundred and ninety words and the date first and the hour with it, and I am going to read them back before I enter them and you are going to tell me if I have got one of them wrong."

## What Was Already Under It

That is what she had put in and it is four sentences' worth of a woman's ordinary reasoning and there was not one word of it that a clerk could have got wrong.

What was under it was two hours and fifty minutes on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh of July.

She had told that room in advance that she was going to build it out of the three hours, and she had done it, and the building was on the record and the record was kept on a shelf in that building and could be read by anybody who put their name in a day's book at the counter.

Sixty names and a hundred and forty particulars about a city doing the unmeasured thing in doorways. A keeper who filled a block from memory. A woman with a stall whose son scored nine. A counter with no door behind it. A boy of nineteen in March who was laughed at by four people and stopped.

Not one word of it could be used against a person. All of it was in a book.

That was the architecture. A claim is not made out of the four sentences. It is made out of the four sentences plus the two hours and fifty minutes that came before them, and nobody in that room could tell her to take the foundation out, because the foundation was true and free and had been given freely and had cost her three hours outside a room she had not been outside in eleven years.

## The Part Of The Room That Believed It

He watched it happen from the fourth row and he could not have stopped it with anything.

By about half past eleven about two thirds of that room had finished being an audience. By about one o'clock there was a queue at the day's book at the door of people who wanted to speak against her, and about forty of them said some version of the same three sentences, and every one of the three sentences was wrong.

It is his. It is the Board's. It was stolen off somebody.

And about nine hundred people in that room went out at about half past two believing, on the balance of the evidence and on the balance of who they thought they were listening to, that a chaplain of twenty years who had spent three hours on the record naming ordinary people who had been uncounted by their own city was more trustworthy on this question than a man who had been at the foot of a wall taking hands from strangers for eight days and had a cough.

He could not say a word. Not because of the rule about a subject instructing a room, which he had sat through for six hours and forty minutes on the twenty-eighth and had got exactly what he expected from it, but because every word available to him was either a document or an authority or a rank, and he had brought none of the three into that building and had not any of them.

## The Chair At Four O'Clock

The record of a public hearing is made on the afternoon of the day and the chair of the Correction Office is not the chair of the Concil of the Nine and has never been in that room in her life, and what she said at about ten to four on the twenty-ninth of July was the shortest thing anybody said in four days.

"The disposition of this matter is not mine to decide and it is not this room's to decide. It goes to the room that can decide, which sits on the first of October, with this record on the standing sheet and the record of the twenty-eighth on the shelf."

"Nobody in this building can order it and nobody in this building can stop it, and if either of you had come to me on the nineteenth I would have told you the same thing and you would have found it a very thin answer."

"Then what happens."

"A claim has been entered on the fourth day against a subject on the first day, and the subject has not answered it, and a claim that is entered in a hearing anybody may attend has to be answered in a room anybody may attend," said the chair.

"I have entered the answer for the thirtieth of July at ten in the morning, in this room, in the ordinary way, and it is not a fifth day of this hearing. The hearing was four days and it closed. The answer is a separate thing and I have entered it and it is not in anybody's gift to move."

He had not asked for it. That was the sentence that went round the room afterwards.

## What Had Been Done To Him By A Woman Four Days Early

On the twenty-fourth she had told him two things and he had taken them for a courtesy and they were not a courtesy.

She had told him that in her room he would not be able to refuse. He had heard that as a woman about to put him in a chair.

She had told him that she was going to be holding it on the second day and that she had never held it in a room with a hundred people in it in her life. He had heard that as a risk she was taking.

What she had told him, in a room about twelve feet by nine with a jug of water on the table that was not for drinking, was that she was going to build a case for four hundred days in public out of three hours of ordinary people's private lives, and that she was going to tell him about it in advance in case he had a better idea, and that she had taken four days over telling him, which is exactly enough to stop a man arranging anything.

She had built it out of people who had been told, in rooms where nothing could be compelled, that nothing could be taken out of them.

That is the whole of it, and it is not a mechanism and it is not a mechanism at all: it is eleven years of a woman sitting in a room where nobody can be made to say anything, and then standing up in one of them for two hours and fifty minutes in front of three hundred and forty people and giving away every true thing she had been given.

He had wanted to spend her on the twenty-fourth. She had beaten him to it by four days and had told him she was going to.

## One Hundred And Fifty-Nine

He walked out into the yard behind the Correction Office at about ten past four with about two hundred people from the Vestry going out ahead of him and a clerk behind him shutting a door and the word on a notice at the head of the Reading four streets away.

One hundred and fifty-nine days. Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty, and one hundred and thirty with twenty-nine is one hundred and fifty-nine, and on the first of October it will be one hundred and ninety-two, and on the first of October a room at the end of a corridor sits again for the first time since the thirtieth of June with two documents from this week on the standing sheet in front of it.

Two documents. A claim in three hundred and ninety words with two hours and fifty minutes of foundation under it. And four lines and an hour on the twenty-eighth that say it is held by no person and there is no list and the record is kept.

He had until ten in the morning.

There was no version of it that he could win with a brass in his coat, and there was no version of it that he could win with a clause, and there was no version of it that he could win with anything he had said out loud in a corridor in the last four years, because everything he had for four years had been about a thing that had a name on it.

On the twenty-eighth of July at about twenty past four in the afternoon a clerk had written four lines in a building off a third court that said it has never had a name.

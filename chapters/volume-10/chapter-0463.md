# Four Days Set By A Clerk

The clerk of the Correction Office was on the ground floor behind a counter that had been put in during the last war and had never been taken out, and on the morning of the twenty-third of July she was entering the days, and he had come in at about nine with about nine minutes in his head and no plan at all beyond the one thing, which was that four days could be four days in September.

She did not look up.

"I am going to ask you for a later month," he said.

"I am going to enter the days," said the clerk. "You may stand there while I do it."

## What A Clerk May Do

"A clerk may enter and may not be asked to hurry," she said, without any particular unkindness, because she said the whole of it in the same voice she said everything. "That is the first thing anybody learns at that counter and it is on the board behind you and you are not the first man to walk past it for twenty years."

"I have read the board."

"Then you know that a clerk cannot be argued with, and a clerk is also not a person you can hurry, and there is not one thing in that sentence that is going to open if you stand here long enough to be cross about it."

Her pen was not moving.

"What do you want."

"The first of October. Or the second week of September. Anything after the thirtieth."

"I cannot enter September," said the clerk. "A day that has not been entered cannot be moved into, and the days are entered out of the month they belong to, and the month they belong to is this one, and if you want me to enter a day in October you will have to come back in October and I will tell you the same thing in a different month and you will not enjoy it as much."

## Four Days, And Three Between The First And The Last

She wrote four things on a line and did not hurry them.

"The twenty-sixth," she said. "The twenty-seventh. The twenty-eighth. The twenty-ninth."

"That is four days."

"That is four days," said the clerk. "And twenty-nine taken from twenty-six is three, and three days between the first of them and the last of them is four days in a room, and a hearing has four days and not a fifth, and this office has had the power to call one since before I came here, which was before I came here."

She read it back. "The hearing sits on four days. The twenty-sixth. The twenty-seventh. The twenty-eighth. The twenty-ninth. It opens at ten and it closes when the room closes and nobody in this building decides when that is except the man who calls it."

"There is no way to make the last day shorter."

"There is no way to make any day shorter, and I will tell you the reason because nobody has ever asked me for it in a way that made me feel I was being clever." She put the pen down. "The rule is not that a clerk may only enter. It is that a clerk is the only person in this city who can enter a day without anybody having to have agreed to it first."

"If a day could be shortened by asking, then it would have been agreed to, and then the four days would be four days that you and the man who called the hearing had both put your names to, and a hearing that anybody may attend has to be a hearing that somebody could not have arranged."

## One Hundred And Fifty-Three Days, And Seven To The End Of The Month

He stood there while she sealed it, and the sealing took about eleven seconds and a clerk did it herself.

"One hundred and fifty-three days," said the clerk, not looking up, the way she said every figure that came out of her. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and thirty is one hundred and thirty, and one hundred and thirty with twenty-three is one hundred and fifty-three."

"How do you know what it is counted from."

"Because it is on the heading and the heading is what I enter against," said the clerk, "and I have entered four hundred and something headings in this room and not one of them on here fails to carry a date the reader can do the sum on, and that is not an accident, it is the only thing in this building that is mine."

Then she said, "Seven days to the end of the month. Thirty taken from twenty-three leaves seven, and a month in this city ends at the thirtieth with nothing after it, and if you have been counting on a calendar with a thirty-first in it then you have been counting on another city and I have seen that go wrong three times in nineteen years."

He had not been doing it on another calendar. He had never been able to make himself do it on another calendar, and it had taken him four years to understand that this was not a talent.

## The Man From Cordwain

The man from the Vestry came in at about half past eleven with two words and a piece of paper with a name on it, and he was in a hurry, and he got as far as the counter.

"Our witness is on the second day. If she is on the second day we can have the matter put first. Can you put the matter first."

"No," said the clerk.

"Can you ask him. He is standing there."

She looked past the man at him and she did not take the paper.

"I cannot enter an order of the day," she said, "and I cannot ask him anything on the order of the day, and if I could I still would not, because the order of the day belongs to the man who called the hearing and not to a person who has brought me a piece of paper with a name on it."

The man said something about eleven hundred people and about journeys and about a woman who had not left a yard in eleven years, and the clerk listened to all of it with her hands flat on the counter.

"The days are the days," she said at the end of it. "You may take her evidence on whichever day she stands up, and she may stand up on whichever day she stands up, and nothing that has been said at that counter this morning will move any of it. Come back at four when the days go out and read them off the board like everybody else."

## The Room Is Not Set

"Who is in the room."

"Not entered yet."

"How many."

"Not entered."

"Does she know. The woman who put her own name on a book in a yard off Cordwain."

The clerk looked up for the first time.

"That is a question about a person and I am permitted to answer questions about a person," she said. "She was told at about eleven this morning, because everything after the day an application is entered is automatic and nothing after that day can be news in this building."

"She will be in that room on the first of the four days and not on the last of them, because she entered the application for the second day and the second day is the day an applicant is heard."

"She has never asked her board for anything in twenty years and she asked it on the twentieth of July, and she has just been told by an office that she is on the second of four days."

"Yes."

"And the board of nine does not choose her."

"No," said the clerk. "Nothing does. That is the trouble with this office and it is the trouble with this city and it is not going to be fixed this month."

## What Went Up On The Wall At Four O'Clock

He went to the wall at about ten past five because it is what a man does, and there were eleven or twelve people at the north end of it and the clerk from the morning was not there and the boy had the ladder up.

The notice had four days on it now, and under the four days there was a heading, and the heading had a name in it.

Not his name.

A word.

One word, in the office's own hand, at the top of a page that had been blank of words for four days, and about forty people at the foot of that wall had stopped to read it, and a woman who had been at the rail on the nineteenth was saying it out loud to a man who had come in from a side street, and it took her a second time to get it right.

He stood in the square and let it happen. He could have gone up the ladder and taken it down and everybody in the square would have watched a man do that and about half of them would have worked out what it was within the hour, and the other half would have had to be told.

Instead he stood in the last of the light with his hands in his pockets while the word went round the north end of the Reading for the fourth time in ten minutes.

And it occurred to him — late, the way things occur to him now, about nine seconds after they have finished happening — that the only way to take a notice down off that wall in this city was to walk into the office that put it up and ask who put it up, and that the office which could put a notice up on that wall about a matter carried under five seals was one floor below a room that stands at five.

He did not go and ask.

## What He Had, And When

He got back to Ferrier Street at about eight and there were six of them at the kitchen table and not one of them asked him where he had been, which is what eight women who have all decided independently that they are not going to ask you anything looks like from the other side of it.

He sat down and worked out the whole of the rest of the month on the back of a hand.

Four days. The twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh, the twenty-eighth, the twenty-ninth. Three days between the first of them and the last of them, and four days in the room, and a clerk in a corridor with a counter bought in during a war who had sealed it herself in about eleven seconds and could not be asked to hurry and could not be argued with and would still be at that counter on the first of October.

Seven days to the end of the month, and then August, and then September, and then the first of October, when the room at the end of the corridor would sit again for the first time since the thirtieth of June.

Twenty-nine taken from twenty-six is three, and one hundred and thirty with twenty-three is one hundred and fifty-three, and there was not one figure in the whole afternoon that anybody could do anything with.

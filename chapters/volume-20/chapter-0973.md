# Two Prices On One Desk

Four thousand four hundred and fifty-six was the seventeenth of this one and the twenty-fourth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and sixty-three, and four thousand four hundred and sixty-three less four thousand four hundred and fifty-six, the seventeenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## A Desk And Two Chairs Standing On The Wrong Side Of It

The room was the first floor over a yard off Cordwain: a broker's room with one wide desk in it and two chairs standing on the wrong side of that desk. A visitor's chair goes on the near side, where he can sit back and be handled. These two were on her side, and the near side was bare boards.

Marguerite Vale was behind the desk with her sleeves turned back past the elbow, and Lin Hao came in and was put where a person is put who is being dealt with.


"Sit," she said. "You are on the correct side for what I do and it is the wrong side for about an hour, and I am not going to apologise for it."

The ends of her fingers had gone blunt on the right hand, the way the ends of a thing that has been against paper for a long time goes blunt. She noticed him noticing and kept the hand where it was.

"You have come because a man who walks people up four flights for a small amount has been in a room like this one," said Marguerite Vale. "Before I answer, tell me what you are frightened of."

## Why He Wants To Know

"He has been told something good," said Lin Hao. "I want to know whether it was good."

"A symptom, not a reason." She turned a chair round with her foot. "You are not here about a man you have never met."

Lin Hao stood on the bare side, where there was nothing but boards, hands on his knees.

"I am frightened that he is right," he said.

"There is a stair with four flights on it and a door at the top, and I gave that away at a counter for about two years for nothing, and then in February a man worked out that four flights is worth something to somebody who cannot manage a stair. He charges for it and he has never lied to anybody."

"And I have stood at the bottom of that stair saying out loud that no person will ever be charged for being told where that door is, and it has done nothing at all."

"And now you want to know whether the price on the street is the price you would have set."

"Whether it is bigger than the one I would have set," said Lin Hao, "and if it is, whether that is my fault or his."

## What A Man In A Street Was Told Here

"He stood where you are standing and asked me what a service is worth. Not what a stair is worth. A service. He had his price as a number in his hand and wanted to know whether to charge it."

"A service is worth what it saves and not what it costs." She put her blunt fingers together on the desk. "He took it the way people take the first sensible thing said to them all day, and went straight back and charged more."

"He told me in February that he says his price before he names the door. It out loud first, and then which door. I thought that was a trick."

"That is not a trick. That is a man who has learned what a favour costs against what a door costs, and he learned it in nine seconds in my room and has never had the benefit of a desk in his life."

Marguerite Vale looked at him directly for the first time. "A price said before the door is a price for a favour. It can be refused and it goes down. A price said after the door is a price for the door, and refusing it means walking four flights back down."

"He moved his price from one side of that sentence to the other and that is all he has changed, and it was the right thing to do, and I told him to do it."

"That was the best advice available in this city on that subject," said Marguerite Vale, "and it is not the advice you would have given, and you cannot give advice from where you are standing."

## The Rule Of That Desk

"The rule of this desk," she said, "is that there are always two prices on it and the second one is the one that gets remembered."

"There is one price on it."

"There are always two and only one of them is ever said. The other one is what the person walks out with."

She turned her hand over and looked at it. "The price that is said is the price of the work. The price that is remembered is the price of having been made to feel small by having to ask."

"And it is always the larger, and it never comes down, and a person who comes back a second time does not remember the first figure at all. They remember the walking down."

"And you cannot lower it."

"I cannot lower it and neither can the person charged by it. There is one instrument in this city that lowers a second price and it is a page a person can go and look at. Everything else is kindness across a desk."

"A service given away for nothing," he said, "has a second price as well. It has had one since November and a bigger one since the twenty-third of May."

"Yes," said Marguerite Vale, "and I am going to say this once and then we will stop talking about price."

She leaned back. "The second price of a free thing is the largest kind there is, because it is not about the work at all. It is about the person."

"The man in the street found yours in about three weeks and has been charging for it since, and he is not stealing it and he is not a thief. And in about four years the only people who will remember that this was free are the people you gave it to, and there are fewer of those every month."

## She Prices Him Before She Touches Him

"Hang your coat on that peg and come back to the desk," said Marguerite Vale. "You are going to stand on this side of it. I am not going to have a conversation about the price of a stair and then walk out of the room with a piece of paper between us."

He hung the coat on the peg and came back and stood on the broker's side of a desk with a woman behind it.

"I am going to do something I have not done in this room for about eleven years," she said, "and I am telling you first, and I do not lie down after anything."

"Say it before, not after."

"I am saying it before."

She got up and came round the desk and sat on neither chair. "I have had men at that stool since you were last in this city and three came about money and the fourth did not get to the end of it, and I was not disappointed about him either."

"Here is the second thing: I have been reading your three lines in other mouths since November and I have never asked you for them in my own, because I am a woman who writes things down. So I am going to take them out of your mouth and not write them down, and that will cost us both and I would do it anyway."

She did not touch him at once. She turned one of the two chairs a quarter of a way, as she had turned one of the books in the binder's room without noticing she was doing it, and sat down in it backwards with her arms along the back of it and looked at him standing on her side of the desk without his coat on.

"Twelve marks," said Marguerite Vale. "That is what you are worth this afternoon, and I am telling you the whole of it before I touch you, because a price said first is a price you can refuse and I do not want you to be able to refuse this one."

"I am not refusing anything."

"You will be in about four minutes. And when you are, I am going to be the one who takes it, because there is not one office in this city that can produce the thing I am about to produce, and I am not going to be told about a stair by a man in a white plate."

She undid his belt herself with one hand, because a broker does not ask for things twice, and pulled his trousers down and his shirt with it in the same movement, and took hold of his cock and told him the figure when it happened, standing there with her own skirts still down and her hands on her hips.

"That is a spent chest," she said. "It has been spent since the fourth year, and I have read the file, and I am not going to waste a good afternoon asking a man about a rib." She dropped his trousers on the boards.

"Now come here. I have not had this room in about eleven years and I am not going to have it against a desk."

## What She Did With The Two Chairs

She pulled the two chairs round against the wall by the window with her foot and made him sit on the lower one, then sat sideways over him on the higher with her skirts up round her waist done with her own hands, in the dark, without hurry.

"Both arms behind you on the chair back. Not because I want to hold you. Because I want to be able to stop without having to stop."

He put his arms behind him and she leaned over him and took him in her mouth and took her time about it, unhurried, talking between it in the voice she used for a valuation, telling him what she thought he was worth with her hand moving and her mouth moving at once and not stopping to let him answer.

It went on long enough that he stopped hearing what she was saying, and she noticed and laughed against him and pulled back an inch.

"There," she said. "Now I have got you, and the rest of it is going to be done sitting down, and you are going to be here until I have finished."

## After It, And What She Said Standing Over Him

She took him into her from above and worked down onto him a hand's width at a time and then held there and rode him with her own weight and no help from him at all, taking her time to the point where he had stopped counting, and then she got a hand up and pushed the window another foot for the air.

She came first and she came loudly enough that anybody in that yard would have heard it, and she did not stop for it, and afterwards said that this room had a ceiling in it that had not been asked to hear anything like that in about eleven years and that she was sorry for the ceiling.

Then she made him finish it inside her, with her own hand closing on him at the root and her mouth at his ear, and she took all of it, and told him afterwards, pulling her skirt down and stepping over his trousers, that he had paid at the desk and received at the desk, and that most men in this city did the other thing round and thought they had got away with it.

## Afterwards, With The Curtain Down

She put her skirts down, did up her sleeves and sat on the edge of her own desk with her heels off the floor, which was the first ungraceful thing he had seen her do.

"That was not a favour," she said, before he had got a word out. "Do not thank me and do not pay me. Men try to turn it into a thing in this room and not one of them has ever been owed a favour by me."

"I have not paid for it and I am not going to."

"Good. Now. What did you come down here actually needing, because it was not the man in the street."

He told her about the binder, the four open doors, and nine days in a room with the door open.

"You are taking the only copy of nine years of other people's answers out of the building it is in," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you have bought yourself a second price and it is the largest kind."

She went to the only window in the room. "A thing that has never been outside is worth what it was worth inside. The same thing on a street in daylight in a cart, under a man who does not know what is in it, is worth what people will believe about it, and they will believe whatever they like."

"And a belief cannot be checked against a page, because there has never been one written about a cart. I have valued four hundred lots of paper that had left its building and not one came back worth what it left for."

"And after nine days it comes back in."

"It comes back in with a price on it that was not on it when it went, and nothing in this city takes a price off a thing that has been outside." She turned round. "I would still send it. Send it."

## What She Would Not Touch

"I will not take it," she said.

"Take what."

"Your debt." She closed the subject like a lid. "If I take it then in about two years it comes due on a day you have not chosen, and paying it will be the most expensive afternoon of your life and it will not be about this afternoon. I do not collect off people who cannot name a figure."

"Then what do I owe you."

"You went down a road and asked a binder a question and he answered you," said Marguerite Vale. "That is between you and him. What I have given you is a valuation and a piece of advice, and the advice was not free, it was only cheap."

"And you are going to do the thing anyway and come back and tell me what happened, and when you do I shall find out that you did the right thing for the worst reason anybody has ever had, which is that you wanted to stop being afraid."

Lin Hao stood at the door of a broker's room with his coat on and had no sentence for that, and had not had one ready.

"That is a price," he said. "Two of them."

"That is the second one," said Marguerite Vale, "and I have not lowered it, and I could not."

Four thousand four hundred and sixty-three days. Less four thousand four hundred and fifty-six, the seventeenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven, and a woman behind a desk told a man that the thing he had been giving away for nothing had a second price at a broker's table and that the only instrument in this city that lowers one is a page somebody can go and look at.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is eighty-four, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and forty-three days, which are sixty-eight months and three days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

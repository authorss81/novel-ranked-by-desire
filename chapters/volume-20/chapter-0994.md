# Somebody Else Saying It

Four thousand six hundred and one was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand six hundred and eight, and four thousand six hundred and eight less four thousand six hundred and one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Two Women Under The Arch

He was at the bottom of his own stair from a quarter to ten on the nineteenth of November and he had come out of the four flights at the top of the morning having read his own three lines off his own door for the first time since the thirtieth of December last year, which is a thing he will come back to and is not going to here.

There were two women under that arch.

The older of them had a bundle on the ground at her feet and was standing the way a person stands when they have been expecting something for a while and have stopped checking. The younger one was maybe twenty and had a shawl over her head and was not looking at anything.

The older one was saying the three lines out loud, at the length, in the ordinary voice, to the younger one.

He was about nine feet from them and he did not move.

## She Got All Of Them

The street, which she had. The door at the head of four flights off a third court, which she had. And the hours, which she had exactly, from a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven and at no other hour, including the half of the second line at the end that has gone soft at two corners and which most people cannot make out at all from the bottom of a court.

He had said that line out loud perhaps nine hundred times and he had never once had to look at it while he said it.

She got all three of them and she did it to keep a young woman occupied, and she had no idea that she had done anything at all, and when the younger one gave them back to check she did not correct her.

The younger one gave them back with the hours as four in the afternoon.

"That is not it," said the older woman. "It runs from a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour. Be early, not late. That is what the card says."

Then the younger one said them again with the right hours, and the older one said the whole thing over once more to be certain of it, and neither of them looked at the man in the white plate nine feet away with his hands behind his back.

## The Thing He Cannot Do About It

He could not thank her.

He worked out why in about nine seconds, and it is the same reason he has not thanked the counterman under the window for nine years. Thanking a person is a thing you do to a person in order to make a thing go on. If she found out he had stood there and listened for a quarter of an hour she would think about it next time, and a woman who thinks about it is a copy.

He could not tell her she had done anything either, because the moment he told her she would have done it deliberately, and a deliberate thing is a copy of a thing, and a copy of those three lines is the entire disease.

He could not send for a person to ask her about it. A question is a thing this office answers.

So he stood about nine feet from two women under his own arch and did the whole of what he had come down there for, which was nothing, and it was the first morning since March that nothing had been what he came down there for.

## The Second True Mouth, And What The Three Lines Are For

What he did instead was this. At about eleven he said them out loud, correctly, from his own second step from the bottom, at the length, in the voice, with the arch on one side of him and the wall about the height of a man on the other.

He said them so that there would be two of them in that arch.

The older woman turned her head and looked at him and then looked at the four flights and then looked back at him.

"You are saying it the same as me," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it is not only hers."

"That is all I came down here this morning to find out," said Lin Hao.

"What are the three lines for?"

"They are so that a person who cannot read that card can be told where the door is, without it mattering whether they can read anything at all."

She took a long time over that and looked at the bundle on the ground.

"My sister cannot read," she said. "Not the hours and not the street and not her own name in a shop. So what you have just told me is that the whole of what there is for her is a man at the bottom of a court for about seven hours in a week."

"It is."

"And there are four of us at that market who can read and a whole row of them at my end who cannot, and I have been at this ten minutes and I have found a second one, and there is a woman at the far end of it who has been saying it since before you were saying it yourself."

"She did it before me."

"Then a stranger at a market is a better instrument than a card on a door," said the woman, "and I have worked that out at the bottom of your own stair in a quarter of an hour, and you have had it on a nail since the thirtieth of December."

## What She Wanted At The Bottom Of That Stair

The bundle on the ground belonged to a man who was coming and was not coming, and she said so, and then she said the other thing without being asked, which was that the woman at the end of a market had told them the hours were four in the afternoon and that she had not believed it.

"She did not lie to me," the older woman said. "She told me the light goes off that street at ten past four and that a person wants to be there before it goes, and that is true, and I have said it to my sister and to my sister-in-law and I will say it to anybody who stands here. And then somebody has added it to the hours and now there are two sets and mine is the one that is in writing at the top of that stair."

"Then yours is the true one."

"Mine is the one that is on a card," she said. "I have never been up those four flights. My sister has not been up those four flights either and she is the one who sent me."

## Whether There Is A Charge

"Are you paid for standing here."

"No."

"Somebody came to the end of my Row in the summer," said the woman, "and walked an old man up four flights of a court for a small amount. My sister could not manage that stair and I could not either, and the old man could not have done it in the cold."

"Now there is a man who will do it and everybody knows his price, and not one person has asked me for it, because you are here. I have been going on four months being careful about which one of you I ask."

"You do not have to ask."

"I could not work out how to thank you for that without it becoming a thing," she said, "and I notice that is exactly what you are doing with your face right now."

He had been doing it with his face for about four seconds and he had not known it was happening.

"Then do not," said Lin Hao. "Stand here instead."

## At Half Past Five In The Dark

He said the three lines at twenty to five to an empty court because he does, and the older woman had gone and the younger woman was still under the arch with the shawl over her head and about nine feet of pavement.

She said them to herself, out loud, all three, slowly, to find out whether she had them, and she got all three right.

He stood on his own step and listened to a woman who had learned them that morning from another woman in the same court give them back to an empty arch without an error in them.

There was a thing he wanted to do about that and he worked out what it was while she was saying them. He wanted to go up four flights and stand at the top of that table with the door open and say that there are now four correct mouths in this city instead of one, and that is worth something, and the arrangement has to be changed to allow for it.

He did not do it, because a thing said once in that room is that room's, and because the four of them are three plus a woman at a market who has never met the other three, and the only way to say four is to have a number, and this office will not have the number.

He understood that this was now the ordinary way of it, and that in about four months not one person in this city would be able to say which of the four versions was the first one, and that he would still have the card.

Four thousand six hundred and eight days. Less four thousand six hundred and one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven.

A woman who has never been up those four flights said the three lines correctly from memory in a court under an arch. He could not thank her for it, could not tell her about it, and could not ask her one question. The only thing he had was to stand about nine feet off and be the second mouth.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is forty-nine, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and eighty-eight days, which are seventy-two months and twenty-eight days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Argument In That Room

Four thousand four hundred and eighty-three was the fourteenth of this one and the twenty-first of this one is day four thousand four hundred and ninety, and four thousand four hundred and ninety less four thousand four hundred and eighty-three, the fourteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

## Saying It With The Door Open

He said it with the door open, which is how he says everything, and which on this occasion turned out to be a mistake, because it meant she had to argue it in front of the room.

"The book goes out on the twelfth of the month after," he said. "Nine days. It goes down four flights and about four hundred yards on a cart, in daylight, into a room off the market, and it comes back on the ninth day."

He had the whole of it out in about nine seconds and then he stopped, because he had expected to be interrupted and had not been.

Wenna Raut put her pen down on the page and squared it against the edge of the book with two fingers, which she does when a row is finished, and then she left it there, which she does not do.

"Say the date again," she said.

"The twelfth of the month after."

"You booked a date before you booked a cart and then you told me about the cart and not the date."

"I told you neither, until now. I have told no person."

"You told a binder on the thirtieth of last month," said Wenna Raut, "in a room four floors down, with three other people in it, and you said out loud that it was other people's answers."

The room had about a dozen people in it and the door was open onto the landing and every one of them was busy, and Lin Hao understood that she had just made a point about the room rather than about him, and that the point was correct.

"I will not have this argument across a table," she said. "Say when you want it."

"Now," said Lin Hao. "If I do it now then I do it once."

## The Argument She Has Had Since November

"Sit down," said Wenna Raut, "and I am going to say a thing I have had since the fourth of November and have not said once, and I have not said it because you had not done anything, and because you saying a word is not the same as you carrying a book out of a building, and because I am not going to be the woman who stops a man from a thing that is going to be right."

He sat down on the end of the table, which he never did.

"In nine days there is no record of anything this office has ever said," said Wenna Raut. "Not of the answers. Not of the refusals. Not of the questions. Nothing. There is a table and there are two chairs and a drawer ordered shut with a man's name under the instruction, and there is me."

"That is the finding," he said.

"That is the finding and it is the smallest half of it."

She turned the pen over once and put it down again. "Answer it. Everything a stranger is ever given in that room, and every stranger I have turned away, and every paper that came up four flights with somebody's writing on it, has gone into a book."

"It has never gone into the room. It has gone into a book, which has a date against every row and an hour against the date and the words that were used, and every one of those rows is still there this morning, and has been since before you were at that table."

"And the practices."

"The practices never needed the book," said Wenna Raut. "Not one of them. An answer goes in writing and both question and answer go in the words used and nothing is taken from a person and every written answer is carried by hand and the person who carries it writes down whose hand took it. Do you know what that last one is for?"

"To say who took it."

"It is to say who took it," she said, "because in nine days I will answer a person from my own head, and my own head has no date and no hour and no hand on it, and when I am asked what I told them a fortnight ago I will be able to tell them, and I will be right, and there will be nothing whatever in the world to check me against."

## The Thing She Cannot Survive

"I want you to hear the second half," said Wenna Raut, "because you have been looking at the first half for a fortnight and the first half is the easy one."

"Go on."

"A person will come up on the fifth of those nine days about something they asked in the first week." She said it slowly and she watched his face while she said it. "They will have a piece of paper with a date on it and a line ruled and a bracket at the end, because that is what people bring, and they will want to know what this office told them, and there will be nothing on this earth that says it except me."

He found that he had no answer for that and looked for one for a long time, which in that room is a very long time.

"You could enter it afterwards," he said.

"Into what," said Wenna Raut. "It is out of this room for nine days and everything said in between is said in the air. I could put it in afterwards from my own memory and then the row would say the date I gave it and not the date it happened, and that is the sort of thing this office has spent nine years refusing to do about a copy of a piece of paper with three lines on it."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is nine years of practice arriving at the same place from a different direction."

## Three Answers He Did Not Finish

He tried three things and got a few seconds into each of them and stopped, and Wenna Raut let him stop, which was the worst thing she could have done.

The first was that nothing is to be entered, and she had already heard it and had already told him what entered-nothing means.

The second was that the office will be refused for the nine days and there will be nothing to remember, which was true and was worse, because then the person who comes up on the fifth day would be right to be angry and would have nothing to complain about.

The third was that he would be in the room, and he got as far as saying it before he heard what it sounded like.

"That is a man," said Wenna Raut. "That is not an answer. It is a man, standing in a room, and I have watched nine years of this office and you are most of what it is."

"Then what is the answer."

"That is not the office's question. That is your question. You have asked me what I want you to do, and the answer is not long, and I am not saying it in front of this room."

## What He Took Down With Him

He did not go down those four flights thinking about the ninth day.

He went down them thinking about the fifth, and about a person coming up with a piece of paper with a date on it and a bracket at the end of a line, and about a woman at the top of a table being asked to say what a room with a book in it told somebody a fortnight ago, with nothing to check and one person who would be certain.

He got to the bottom of the stair and stood there for a moment, and then he understood that he was not permitted to fix it, and that fixing it was the whole of what he had been doing for nine years, and that the thing he had found out was that this one was not fixable by a man with a document and a good reason.

The pavement was not empty. The people who had nowhere free to go were on it against the wall in their places, saying three lines badly to strangers, and he stood among them and said them himself for about twenty minutes, badly, and that was the whole of what he had.

## Said Out Loud Where It Is Spent

He said it out loud at the bottom of his own stair at about half past ten that morning, in daylight, in front of the pavement and the people on it, because a thing said once in a court belongs to the court and he had learned that in June.

Then he came back up four flights and put it in front of her with the door open and the room full, because that was where he had promised to put it and she had a right to be answered in front of the same people.

"She is right," he said. "Nothing goes in the book for nine days."

"That is not new," said Wenna Raut. "I have been telling you that since the seventh."

"Then it did not happen."

The room went on doing what it does. Somewhere below them a door went and somebody came up two flights and stopped.

"Then I will be it," said Wenna Raut.

And Lin Hao sat on the end of that table and understood that she had not meant it as a figure of speech, and that it was the only sentence anybody had said to him in nine years that he could not answer at all.

"Go," said Wenna Raut.

He stood up.

"And do not come back up here on the twelfth to say it has gone," she said, "because if you come up on the twelfth to say it has gone then this room has heard about it twice and one of the times it went, and a thing that is said twice is a thing with a history."

"What if it does not come back."

"Then I will be it as well," said Wenna Raut, "and I will be it for as long as I am here, and there will be nothing whatever to check."

Four thousand four hundred and ninety days. Less four thousand four hundred and eighty-three, the fourteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven, and a woman argued a man out of the last thing he had to stand on and told him to go and then told him not to come back and say it had gone.

*Less four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty-one, and the fiftieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-one of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and seventy days, which are sixty-nine months and no days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Standing At The Bottom

Four thousand four hundred and seven was the twenty-eighth of last month and the fifth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and fourteen, and four thousand four hundred and fourteen less four thousand four hundred and seven, the twenty-eighth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Four Feet Of Pavement

He was at the bottom of that stair by a quarter to ten with his shoulder to the wall and the morning bright. Four flights rose above a third court, and a card was nailed to the door at the top of them with the street and the door and the hours on it and nothing else on it whatever, and a name was not one of the things on it. He had stood there six weeks and today opened the seventh, and his body knew the wall the way his hands knew the table upstairs.

He wanted to find out whether any person had been using the bottom of that stair as a place to stand, and who, and what it had cost them to stand there. What he felt, with the court still empty in front of him, was a watchful tenderness he did not trust, because tenderness in him had always been the herald of a mistake.

By ten past ten he had his answer. A woman with empty hands came into the court and stopped at the mouth, looking up at the stair the way people look when they cannot afford what is at the top of it. She did not come to him and she did not start up. She stood four feet off to his left, with her back to the far wall, and waited.

Another came within the hour, an older woman with a basket, who took up a place on the other side of the stair mouth without a word to either of them. By midday there were two more, a man with a limp and a girl with a shawl over her head, each standing apart, each watching the stair.

None of them had been told they were welcome. None of them came up four flights. By the second morning none of them was a stranger to him, and none of them was a stranger to each other, though no introductions had passed.

They were the people who had nowhere free to go. The ones who could not pay the man with the coin, whose morning cost more than four flights twice over. The ones who did not read the card at all, because reading was not the way they learned a street. What they had found, without building it and without announcing it, was four feet of pavement at the bottom of a third court where a person could stand without paying.

## What It Actually Was

He found out what it actually was by standing in it for a morning and listening. At about eleven a stranger came into the court with his coat buttoned wrong and looked up, lost, and before Lin Hao could speak one of the standing women spoke, giving him the street and the door and the hours in a rush, out loud, in daylight, to somebody who was about to go up them.

She got the hours half wrong and the door half right, and the stranger thanked her and went up anyway. Another of them corrected her after he was gone, and they argued quietly about the hours without heat, and then they laughed. Every one of them was getting it badly, a word wrong here and a turn wrong there, and the badness did not matter because the address had been free before it was any person's job.

He stood with his shoulder to the wall and listened, and he felt the floor shift under his understanding. He had thought he was an obstacle, a man in the way of a door, and he was a floor.

A free place to stand existed and no person had built it and no person had announced it, and it was not a service and it was not an answer. It was a place where a person could say three lines out loud in daylight to somebody who was about to go up them, and be wrong, and still be useful.

He watched the man with the limp give the hours to a carter, getting the quarter wrong and the half right, and the carter nodded and went up without hesitation. He watched the girl with the shawl tell a woman the street true and the door sideways, and the woman found it all the same because the courts ran into each other. Each wrong telling worked after its fashion, because a mouth did not need to be true to be a road.

At midday he climbed to drink water and stood in the room with the door open, with the book on the table and Wenna Raut at her place. He told her what he had seen, in the ordinary voice, because it had to be said where it was spent.

"They have been standing there inside a fortnight," said Lin Hao. "Not one of them has come up, and not one of them has been told they were welcome. They say the three lines to strangers badly, and the strangers go up all the same."

Wenna Raut put her pen down.

"Is that a practice," said Wenna Raut. "What you are doing down there. Is it a practice."

"It is not," said Lin Hao. "And what they are doing is not, either. A practice has to be written on the practice sheet by a clerk, and this is not going to be written down. I say nothing to them and I do not stop them, and I am saying that here so that it is said in a room with the door open."

"Then nothing goes in," said Wenna Raut.

"Nothing goes in," said Lin Hao. "And I do not thank them, because thanking and paying and asking are the three things a person does to stop something, and I am not stopping this."

He felt the rightness of that come home in his chest like water finding its level. He had learned from two women who had stood four feet apart in a room and refused to be thanked, and he was using what he had learned.

What he wanted, standing at the table with his hands empty, was to protect the four feet of pavement without touching it, and he understood that the only way to protect it was to leave it alone. He thought of the market floor and the stopped stop, and how neither woman had asked for a wall about herself, and he was glad he had learned in time.

## The Floor

He stood the afternoon at the bottom with the others, shoulder to the wall, saying his own three lines when asked and leaving theirs alone when they spoke. A stranger asked him for the door and he gave it true, and a moment later one of the standing women gave it wrong to another stranger, and both strangers went up.

He felt affection rise in him so sharp it hurt, for the wrongness and the use of it, and for the fact that no person owned it. He thought of his own mouth saying the lines to an empty stair at ten to five, and how lonely that had felt, and how different it felt to hear them said badly in daylight by people who had somewhere to stand.

One of them, the woman with empty hands who had come first, caught his eye at about four and nodded. He nodded back.

Nothing was said and nothing needed saying, because they understood each other the way people understand each other when they share a floor without sharing a roof. He felt the tiredness in his legs from six weeks of flags and the lightness in his chest from seeing the pavement used, and both feelings rested together without arguing.

The light went off that street at about ten past four and the card up four flights could not be read after it. The standing ones stayed where they were, talking quietly among themselves, saying the lines over to each other to get them straighter. He listened and did not correct them, because correction was a copy and a copy was a second copy, and the evening was too kind for copies.

At half past five he went up and got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards in the dark. The back board ticked under his arm about every four seconds, and he walked slowly because his chest was tight and because he was in no hurry to leave the court behind. He felt tired and glad together, the way a man feels when he has found something he was not looking for.

What the place needed would come in a fortnight, on the practice sheet and not on the standing sheet, and he had no reason on earth to expect it.

He knew only that a free place to stand had grown at his feet without his planting it, and that he was its floor and not its wall. That was the thing the decision at the end of the month needed, discovered a fortnight before it was needed, and it waited for him the way the fifth of May waited for the twenty-third.

A counter under a window was waiting too, and a man who was paid nothing, and a branch office at night that no person had asked him to look at. He thought of it on the way back with the book off his hands, and the thought of a man coming to look at something on his own account warmed him in a way he had not been warmed in weeks.

Four thousand four hundred and fourteen days. Less four thousand four hundred and seven, the twenty-eighth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven, and a man who thought he was an obstacle found out he was a floor and wrote nothing down.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is thirty-five, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and ninety-four days, which are sixty-six months and fourteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

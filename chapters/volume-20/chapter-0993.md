# The Room That Agrees

Four thousand five hundred and ninety-four was the fifth of this one and the twelfth of this one is day four thousand six hundred and one, and four thousand six hundred and one less four thousand five hundred and ninety-four, the fifth of this one, is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## Nine Steps At The Side

The Bellhouse has a stair of nine steps at the side of it with the paint worn off the treads in a stripe down the middle and a door at the top of them with a bell in it that has not been connected to anything since before the haberdasher's under it shut.

He went up those nine steps at about two in the afternoon on the twelfth of November because it is the only room in this city he has never been able to get a sentence out of, and he wanted an afternoon in which nothing would happen to him.

She opened the door before he knocked and she was not expecting him and she did not say so.

There was a piano in that room at the far end under a cloth that had been moved, because the cloth was folded over the rail of the chair beside it, and the lid was down.

## She Starts At The Piano Without A Word

She went across the room and lifted the lid off the piano without looking at him, and she sat down on the bench in front of it without a word, and she played.

The strings have been dead for about four years and the tuning had gone flat enough that everything she played was a little under itself, and she played about nine bars of something that was not a tune she had come in here to play.

The room took it.

He stood with his hat in his hands about six feet back from the bench and heard what came back off the wood and the plaster and the one window, and it was not what she had played. It was what she had played with the flatness taken out and the top of it lifted and about a third of a second late, and it was better than what she had played.

She stopped with her hand still on the keys and did not turn round.

"That is what I mean," she said.

## What The Room Does And What She Has Let It Do

"It sounded better than what you played."

"It sounded the way I meant it." She took her hands off the keys and turned about on the bench and gave him the whole of her attention for the first time since the door. "That is the trick and it has been the trick for twenty years and I have never once been able to find where it does it. I play a thing in here and what comes back at me is what I meant, and what I meant is never what I played."

"How is it different."

"It is the difference between saying a thing and being understood to say it." She put her forearm along the top of the closed part of the lid.

"I can sing a flat note in this room and I have never once heard it flat. I have gone in there and been wrong on purpose for four years and the room has agreed with every wrong note I put into it, and then I have sat here afterwards with my hands in my lap like a woman who has been praised."

"You have not sung in here in about four years."

"I have not sung in this room in about four years, and I have sung in nine others, and every one of them is a worse room than this one and every one of them tells me the truth about what I sound like." She put her chin on her forearm. "That is the whole of the illness. A room that agrees with you is the most comfortable thing there is and it is the only thing in this city that cannot tell you that you are wrong."

## She Makes The Room Agree With Something False

"Say something untrue about me," she said.

"What."

"Something about you that is not true. Say it in here and listen to what comes back, and then I will say something untrue about myself and you will listen, and then one of us will tell you which of the two it was, and you will not be able to work it out, and that is the afternoon I have got for you."

"I am not doing that."

"You are going to do it, because you have spent four months going round this city telling it that a mouth is all anybody has, and you have not the least idea whether a mouth can be checked," said Isolde Marchetti, "and I am the only person I know of who can put your own sentence back in front of you and have it be wrong."

So he said it. It was about his own office and it was not true, and the room gave it back at him about a third of a second late and it sounded like a man who had been in charge of it for nine years.

Then she said one about herself, which was that she had stopped singing because her voice had gone, and the room agreed with that too, and he could not have put his hand on the difference between the two.

"That," she said. "That is four years of my life, and you have had ten months of yours, and you did it with a door and a card and a white plate, and I did it with a piano and a cloth."

## What She Wants, And When She Says It

He said: tell me about the four years.

"I have told you about the four years. I am going to tell you about the four years once more in about half an hour, and I am going to tell you the part of it I have never told anybody, and I am going to do that while you cannot get away, and you will not be able to put your hand on my shoulder and stop me, because you will not want to."

She got up off the bench. "Do not make it into a bargain. I have had a bargain this year from somebody who wanted twenty in it."

She did not take anything off. There was one chair by the window and a long low seat along the wall under it that had a blanket folded over the arm, and she took the blanket off the arm and dropped it on the floor and sat down on the end of that seat and put her boots up, and waited for him with her hands in her lap.

"I have not undressed for a man in four years and I am not doing it now," she said. "You are going to sit on the end of this and I am going to do what I like with you, and you are not going to be told the order, and the reason I am not telling you the order is that I do not owe this room one more thing about itself."

## She Comes First

She took him into her mouth standing up off the low seat and with her boots still on the floor, one hand at the back of his head and the other under him at the hip, and she was not in any hurry about it at all.

The room gave her back the sound of it about a third of a second after she made it and it sounded better than it was, and he heard that happen and had to put his hand on the wall over her shoulder to stay upright.

She worked him up the whole length of her and then went back down it, twice, unhurried, listening to what came back, and at one point she pulled off and said: that is what I am for, and I have not done it in this room in four years, and you may have the rest of it however long you want it.

He put a hand on her jaw. She let it stay there. She went back to it and finished herself with her hand under his jaw and the other flat against the back of his skull, and she came first, and it was quick, and she turned her face into his neck while she did it and afterwards told him that she had done that on purpose.

"You are not going to tell me why."

"You will get the rest of that in about half an hour and you will not enjoy a minute of it," said Isolde Marchetti.

## She Holds Him At The Edge And Says The Whole Of It

Then she got him up against the low seat and took him in and had him right up to the edge of it, and stopped there and did not move.

Not resting. She got her fingers closed round the nape of his neck and kept him at the exact place where one more push would finish it, looked at him, and waited for him to understand that she was not going to do it.

"There are three people answering in that room," she said.

"Yes."

"Three people who all learned it out of your mouth, and who have all promised to say so at the time if one of them does it differently, and none of whom can show anybody the sheet that says so." Her hand did not move.

"In about four years there will be a room in this city that agrees with all three of them, because that is what you have taught them to do. And when somebody comes up those four flights in the dark with a question, that room will agree with them."

"That is the arrangement."

"That is the arrangement and it is the whole of it, and you told me about nine minutes ago that it is a rehearsal." She shifted her weight and he came a quarter of an inch further and she held him there again without appearing to notice.

"Do you know what you have actually done? You did not give the office away. You gave it to three people who will sound like you, and in about four years the only thing that will come up those stairs is you, wearing two other faces."

"Say that again."

"That is exactly what it is, and I am the last person in this city who should be able to tell you, because I have spent twenty years letting a room do the same thing to me and calling it a gift." Her voice did not rise at any point in it.

"You have built a room that agrees with you. You have spent five months making it agree with you in three voices so that none of them can be checked, and the whole of what you have is that the room is nicer than mine."

He could not get a sentence out of it and he was held there while he tried, and the room agreed with his silence as readily as it had agreed with the false thing on the low seat.

"You are going to get all of it and none of it dressed up," she said. "You were nearly right, and nearly is the dearest place anybody can stand, and a woman told you that in September and you have been carrying it about like a coin ever since."

## One Finish

Then she let him go, all the way, and took all of it in her mouth, and held him there with a hand at the back of his head until it had finished going, and swallowed it and sat back down on the seat beside him and put her boots on the floor again and let about nine seconds go by.

The room gave them back about nine seconds later, better than they were.

"That is the room," said Isolde Marchetti. "It has agreed with everything you have said today including the two things that were not true, and it will go on agreeing with anything else you care to say in it. Nothing in this city has ever been so flattered and nothing in this city has ever been more use to anybody. It does not work. It is here."

## Afterwards, With The Lid Down

She put the lid down and stood with a hand on it.

"I am going to sing again," she said. "Not in here. In here I would be told I had done it well, and I have four years of being told I had done it well, and I do not want any more of it. I am going to sing in rooms that tell me what I sound like, and I am going to be bad at it, and the wages are worse and the stairs are colder and that is the arrangement I am making."

"Why are you telling me the office is a rehearsal."

"Because somebody has to say it to you once and I would rather it was a woman who does not need anything from you," she said. "And because in about four years, when somebody in a hall asks this city what that office does, three people will answer, and all three of them will sound like one man, and the answer will be correct, and there will be no way on earth to tell which of the three said it, or whether any of them was there."

"What do you want me to do with that."

"Nothing," said Isolde Marchetti. "You have never once been able to do anything with a sentence anybody said to you in a room like this. That is why people like you find rooms like this."

## Out On Bellhouse Row At Half Past Five

He came down nine steps and out into Bellhouse Row at half past five, and the light had gone off the brick over the haberdasher's some weeks before that, and it was the hour in which the card's third line does no work at all.

There was a man on the pavement at the top of a third court about four hundred yards off, on his own, with his hands in his pockets, saying the three lines out loud at the length, to an empty court, in the dark, and then saying them again more slowly.

Four thousand six hundred and one days. Less four thousand five hundred and ninety-four, the fifth of this one, is seven, and twelve less five is seven, and a woman in a room that has agreed with everything for twenty years put a man's own argument back in front of him at the edge of finishing and held him there until he had heard all of it.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is forty-two, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and eighty-one days, which are seventy-two months and twenty-one days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Four Inches Of Daylight

Four thousand four hundred and forty-two was the third of this one and the tenth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and forty-nine, and four thousand four hundred and forty-nine less four thousand four hundred and forty-two, the third of this one, is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## The Tick Through The Table

The six lines were read out first, because that is the order they are read in, and the book came onto the table with the strap still round it and went on lying there with the room going on around it.

Lin Hao had got into a habit in the last month of resting the heel of his hand on the corner of the table while the six lines were read, and today the tick came up through the wood and into the heel of his hand about every four seconds, and he took the heel away and stood on the other side of the table instead, which made the tick louder.

Wenna Raut read the six lines in the voice that office reads everything in. The fifth laid down the fold with the flap gummed shut and the reading aloud with both people in the room, and the sixth laid down that an answer read once with different people in it could not be read again.

"That is the whole of it," she said.

"That is the whole of it," he said.

Then the room got on with the morning, and people came up the four flights and got what they had come for, and he stood at the end of the table with his hands behind him and let the day come at the book the way it always did.

He wanted the split wider. That was the whole of his wanting that morning and he was not proud of the shape of it. He had carried a book with a daylight in it under his own arm for two years and the daylight had not got worse and had not got better, and somewhere in the middle of that he had stopped noticing the tick at all, which he found out on the tenth of June by pressing his hand on a table and finding it there.

## A Door Open In Daylight Four Hundred Yards Off

He went down the road at about one, on his own time, out of the three days off that this came out of.

The room off the market had a wide door on it and that door was open. It had been open in daylight every working day for two years. He had walked past it — counted, without ever putting anywhere — a good many hundreds of times, and he had never once gone in, and the reason had never once been the price.

Going in is agreeing.

That is all it is. A man standing in a street looking at a door that is open in daylight and then not going through it is a man who has not agreed to anything yet, and a man who goes through it has agreed, in daylight, with another man's hands on the thing he is carrying. He had worked that out in about the first month and had never tested it and had never needed to.

He stood on the far pavement for about two minutes with his hands in his pockets and looked at a man at a bench inside through eleven feet of open air, and the man did not look up, because a man at a bench in daylight is not looking for anybody.

## The Word He Has Not Said To A Person Since November

At about half past two he was back four flights up with the door open and the book on the table and the room half empty.

"The board," said Lin Hao. "There is a binder four hundred yards off who does bureau books. His name is on nothing anywhere and I know where his room is and I have not been in it. That is the word I have been not saying to a person since November, and I am saying it now."

Wenna Raut stopped with her pen about an inch off the page and did not put it down.

"Say the rest of it," she said. "You have said the part that sounds like a decision."

"There is no rest of it. I have said a word."

"You have said a word to me, and you have said it out loud with the door open, and that is a decision by the only rule this room has." She put the pen down. "What are you going to do about it."

"I am going to stand here and find out whether I am frightened of it," said Lin Hao.

That was honest, and she let it stand a moment before she took it apart, which is what she does.

"You are not going to find that out standing here, and if you were, it would not matter, because you said a word in November and then said it again to me on the eighth and neither of those times did anything at all happen." She looked at the book on the table. "What has happened is that you have just said it a third time to the only other person in this building who knows where his room is."

## The Thing That Is In Two Heads

"That is what I want to talk about," he said. "Say the rest of that."

Wenna Raut did not say it. She got up and came round the table to his end and put her fingers on the back of the book, not on the board, on the back where the leather was still whole, and she stood there a moment.

"On the eighth of November you told me three things," she said. "You told me there is one binder in this city who does bureau books. You told me he is in a room off the market. And you told me he is out between the fourth and the ninth of every month. You told me them in that order and you told me the third one twice, because you wanted me to have it."

"I remember that."

"Do you," said Wenna Raut.

He found that he did not remember the third one being said twice. He remembered the frost and the doorway and the argument going the way arguments go in that room, and he remembered losing it in about four seconds because he had expected to, and after that there was nothing in his head at all about any of it.

It was all still in hers. Seven months on, standing at the end of that table with her fingers on the back of the only copy of nine years of other people's answers, she had a street in her head that he did not have, and a window in her head that he did not have, and a date in her head that he did not have,

and if she was wrong about any of the three then the book goes to the wrong bench in the wrong town on a day when the door is shut.

That is the first thing this office has ever had that it could lose, and it has had it for seven months without knowing it.

"Say it again," he said. "Say it now, all three, and I will write them down."

Wenna Raut took her fingers off the book.

"On what," she said.

## Half Past Five With The Light Still On It

He came down the four flights at half past five the ordinary way, with the book under his arm and the wire round the buckle twisted twice in his own pocket, and he stopped on the second landing and put his forefinger into the gap in the dark under the bottom edge of the back board — and there it was, the daylight, a line of it about four inches long where the two halves had come away from each other.

He had carried it against his ribs and against his coat and against the crook of his elbow for two years and had never once put anything into it.

He put the tip of his forefinger in.

The split took his finger to the first joint and would have gone further and the daylight opened around it about the width of a coin, and the tick he had been hearing through a table leg all morning was suddenly a tick he could feel with a finger in the dark of the gap, and it was the same tick. About every four seconds. It had not got worse since November and it had not got better and it was not going to get worse because it was June.

Then he said the thing out loud in a stairwell at half past five in June, which was a foolish place to say it and the only place in that building where the words were his.

"When it opens further than a finger, then it goes, and on the day it goes I will stand in the street with it and I will not put a coat round it."

Seven months. He had lost the argument about four seconds after he had said it, on the second morning, in a frost, and then he had said the sentence and made his own self the loser of it, and the frost had gone off on about the ninth and had not come back and was not going to.

The winter had not come back. The board was not waiting for the winter. The board was doing what it was doing on the tenth of June, in June, without reference to any month of the year at all, and the only thing that had ever stood between it and a binder's bench was a piece of weather that lasted about a week.

## Ten To Five On A Third Court

He went the last hundred yards in the light, because in June that court still had daylight on the wall above the door at ten to five and it would not have it in October, and neither of those two facts was on the card in any form.

He stood with his back to the low wall at the foot of his own stair, with no coat round the book because there was no book in his hands, and he said the three lines out loud to an empty court.

The street, the door at the head of four flights off a third court, the hours between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour. He said them twice.

The second time was slower and he got the last of the second line the way it is on the card with the corners gone, because he has it in his mouth and the card has it in ink and the two of them are not the same and the one in his mouth is the one that works.

And then he went up the four flights in the light, which he had not done in nine years, and stood in the doorway of that room with his coat on and the book back on the table and said that he was going down the road in the morning.

Wenna Raut had her pen in her hand and the practice sheet beside the standing sheet, and the standing sheet kept six lines, and the last of the six was entered on the second of December of the ninth year.

She looked at the practice sheet for a while and then she looked at him.

"Write the three things down," he said. "On the practice sheet. In your hand. A street, a window, and the days he is out, and the name of the shop is not one, because he has not got one on anything."

She shook her head, and she did it slowly, and she was not being slow on purpose.

"You have spent a volume not writing things down," said Wenna Raut. "And it is the right thing and I have never once disagreed with it. And this morning you have wanted a thing written down more than you have wanted to not have it written down, and there is not one sheet in this room that would hold those three things without it being the second copy of them in this city. And it would be the second copy and I would be the one who made it."

He stood there and let that be true.

He wanted the three things on a sheet so badly that his hand had gone to the table. And the reason he could not have them on a sheet was the reason he had given away the stair, the pavement, the standing, and the saying of the three lines in daylight at the bottom of his own stair. One rule, and he had made it himself, and it had just cost him the only address he had.

"Then go and say them to him," said Wenna Raut, "and come back and tell me what he said, and do not write any of it down."

Four thousand four hundred and forty-nine days. Less four thousand four hundred and forty-two, the third of this one, is seven, and ten less three is seven, and a man put his finger into a split he had been carrying against his ribs for two years and found that the deadline had been set by his own mouth on a frosty morning in November and had nothing whatever to do with the weather.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seventy, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and twenty-nine days, which are sixty-seven months and nineteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

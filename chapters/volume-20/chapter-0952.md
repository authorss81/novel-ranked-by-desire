# A Man Being Helpful

Four thousand three hundred and nine was the twentieth of this one and the twenty-seventh of this one is day four thousand three hundred and sixteen, and four thousand three hundred and sixteen less four thousand three hundred and nine is seven, and twenty-seven less twenty is seven by the month count.

## The Man Who Came Up On The Twenty-Sixth

He came up four flights on the afternoon of the twenty-sixth with nothing in his hands at all, which is the mark of a man who has been told something by a person.

He got as far as the end of that table and said, "It shuts at four, does it not."

"It does not shut at four," said Lin Hao. "It says a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour."

"Then somebody has it wrong."

"Somebody has it wrong," said Lin Hao, "and I am not going to tell you which part, because if I tell you which part then you have a correction and a correction is a second copy of a thing that had three lines on it, and the second copy is worse than the first."

The man had stood there for about nine seconds working that out and did not get anywhere with it, and Lin Hao said the hours out loud to him at the top of the stair in the ordinary voice, nothing added and nothing taken off, and the man went down.

And then Lin Hao walked down his own four flights, because he wanted to hear it.

## Nine Words At A Counter Under A Window

The counter is in a passage off Cordwain and it is nine years old and the man behind it is paid nothing, and he has never once asked for anything.

He did the service first, because it comes first.

A woman with nothing in her hands and a bundle on her arm came up to that counter and he said the stair and the third door off the landing and the hour she should be there, in nine words, in about four seconds, and she wrote about half of it on the back of her hand and got the half wrong, and he watched her do it and did not correct her, which Lin Hao has watched him do about nine times a month for four years.

"What happens when she gets it wrong," said Lin Hao.

"She gets it wrong and she is late and she comes again," said the counterman. "If I stop her in the street to put it right she learns that a counter is a thing you can be wrong at, and about nine people a week learn that in a passage like this one anyway. I would rather she came back tomorrow."

## The Fourth Thing

The man who was turning a coin over between two fingers on the twenty-second came back on the twenty-seventh and stood at that counter, and Lin Hao stood four feet off and let him do it.

He said the street. He said the door at the head of four flights off a third court. He said the hours.

And then he said, "And be there before ten past four, because the light goes off that street then and you cannot read a thing."

Every word of that was true.

The light does go off that street at about ten past four. It has gone off that street at ten past four every working afternoon since the card went up, and the clerk has watched it happen, and not one person in that building has ever said so out loud.

That man had worked it out for himself. He had stood at the bottom of that stair on a bright afternoon in the last week of the month and discovered that a piece of card cannot be read once the wall goes dark, and he had decided, being a decent man, that the next person should be spared it.

## Two Sentences Travelling As One

"Four things," said Lin Hao. "You have just sent four things out of that passage and three of them are mine."

"I have only added the true one."

"That is not the trouble and I am not blaming you for it," said Lin Hao, and he meant it, and he was not going to be talked out of it either. "The trouble is that you have joined it to the end of the hours in your mouth and it has become one sentence, and every person who takes that sentence off you will pass the whole of it on, and about half of them will not mark where you stopped and I started."

The man said, "It is not a lie."

"It is two true things and one of them is mine and one of them is yours, and that is a worse shape for a sentence than a lie," said Lin Hao. "A lie can be found out by a person who goes and looks. This cannot be found out by anybody, ever, because there is nothing in it that is wrong."

"I could go and tell the people I gave it to."

"You do not know who they are and neither do I, and if you had a list of them I would not want it," said Lin Hao. "Tell them what? That you are sorry? You have not said a wrong thing. You have said a right thing that does not belong to you."

"The reason it does not belong to you is that the man who owns it cannot keep it and cannot give it away," said Lin Hao. "You have picked up the piece he dropped and you have not asked, and you are not the first person in this city to do that and you will not be the last."

"I have not done anything."

"No," said Lin Hao. "You have been kind, and it has cost me something, and I am not going to be the one who stands in a passage and tells a person at a counter that they were not to be kind."

## He Said The Hours Back To Him

He said the hours out loud then, at that counter, in the ordinary voice, with nothing added and nothing taken off.

The man listened to all of it and then said, "That is not what I said."

"It is exactly what the three lines say."

"It is what the three lines say," the man agreed, "and I have been saying it wrong since about the twenty-third and I did not know I was."

He put the coin in his pocket and went off up the street with the fourth thing gone out of it, and Lin Hao stood in that passage and worked out that a thing said out loud cannot be taken back even by the man who said it, because the people who heard it have it and he does not know who they are.

That is the whole difference between this and a piece of paper.

A wrong thing on a piece of paper can be traced back to a hand. A hand has a name on it somewhere and the name is in a book and somebody can go and look at the book.

This has got four hands in it and not one of them is on anything.

## What He Did Not Ask The Counterman To Do

He turned round.

The counterman had gone back to his rail and was not looking at him and had not been listening to any of it, or had been listening to all of it and had made himself small at a counter, which is a thing about nine years old men learn.

"I am not going to ask you to stop," said Lin Hao.

"I would not have done it."

"I know you would not. I am saying it in a passage so that if anybody asks you later you can say a person told you on the twenty-seventh."

The counterman looked up for the first time.

"Then say the other half of it, because you have never once said the other half of anything to me in about four years."

"The other half is that you are right and he is right and both of you are wrong in the same sentence," said Lin Hao. "You have been giving that address away nine times a day for nine years and nothing about it was yours to give, and he has added a true thing to it out of kindness, and if either of you stops then the other one has no reason to stop either."

"So what do I do."

"Do the service," said Lin Hao, "and I will go and stand at the bottom of my own stair."

"Then it is already too late to stop it and I have known that since about November," said the counterman, "and you have known it longer than that, and neither of us has said it at that counter, and I have been waiting about four years for a person to come and be annoyed at me about a street and you have never once come and been annoyed at me about anything."

"You have been looked through."

"I have been looked through," said the counterman, "and I have been paid nothing for it, and those two facts have never once been joined up in my head before and I have had about nine seconds to do it, so you will excuse me."

## The One Who Came Up On The Twenty-Sixth

He went back up the four flights at about twenty past twelve, and the woman who had come up on the twenty-sixth was at the top of them waiting, with a man behind her who had not been there before.

She said it before she got to the door. "I have been told it shuts at four and I have got a child asleep at the top of these stairs and about four hours to get back down them, and if I come back tomorrow at ten in the morning I will lose the whole day over it, so I would rather know now."

"Then it does not shut at four," said Lin Hao. "It says a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour, and it has said that since the thirtieth of December and it is not going to say anything else."

"You are certain of that."

"I am certain of what is on it. I am not certain of anything else about it, and neither is anybody, and that is a different thing and it is the only one I can stand behind."

The man behind her had not said anything at all, and when she went down he stayed about four seconds and then went too.

## A Room With A Stone Floor

Wenna Raut had the book open when he came in and she shut it.

"There is a thing about this morning I am going to say once," she said, "and then it is done."

"Say it."

"A wrong thing on a piece of paper has a hand on it. A wrong thing said out loud in a passage has a person on it, and a person is not a hand and cannot be looked up, and about nine years of this office being careful about paper has just met the first thing it cannot be careful about."

"Yes."

"I am not asking you to do anything about it," said Wenna Raut. "I am telling you that there is a room in this city with a floor and a ceiling that take a thing like that and hand it back, and I have been in it twice, and I have never once used it on anything of mine."

"Where."

"Records," she said. "And you would not be able to do anything about it afterwards."

Four thousand three hundred and sixteen days. Less four thousand three hundred and nine, the twentieth of this one, is seven, and a man in a passage off Cordwain added one true sentence to another man's three lines out of kindness, and three people heard it that morning, and there is nothing on this table that can find any of the three.

*Less four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is twenty-seven, and the forty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and ninety-six days, which are sixty-three months and six days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

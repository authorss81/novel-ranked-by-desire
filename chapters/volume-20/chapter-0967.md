# Three Floors Of Washrooms

Four thousand four hundred and fourteen was the fifth of this one and the twelfth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and twenty-one, and four thousand four hundred and twenty-one less four thousand four hundred and fourteen, the fifth of this one, is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## Eleven At Night

He was mopping the third floor of washrooms at eleven at night when the counterman came. The branch office off a third court was quiet, with three floors of washrooms and a stairwell and a bin room, and Lin Hao worked it four nights in seven from eleven until half past five because he had asked for that shift. He had the bucket and the brush and his sleeves rolled, and his plate was covered because a plate meant nothing at night.

The counterman stood in the doorway without knocking, with his coat buttoned and his hands empty. He looked at the washrooms and then at Lin Hao, and he did not explain why he had come, because no person had asked him to come and he did not explain things he had not been asked to do.

"You have come to look," said Lin Hao.

"I have come to look," said the counterman. "And I will not touch anything and I will not ask a question about the office, because I have not been sent and I am not an auditor."

He stood in the washrooms for about nine minutes, moving slowly from one floor to the next, looking at the basins and the pipes and the drains. He ran water in one basin and watched it go, and he put his hand near a pipe without touching it to feel for damp. He did the washrooms the way a man does a place he respects, thoroughly and without comment.

"What do you think of it," said Lin Hao, when they were back on the ground floor with the bucket between them.

"I think about the water," said the counterman. "The pressure is low on the third floor and good on the first, and the second drain runs slow. You do this four nights a week with a bad chest and no help, and no person in this city has ever described it to any person."

He wanted to meet the man who was paid nothing at the work he actually did, which was not the office four flights above a third court, and to find out what he thought of it. Standing there with a mop in his hands in front of the one person he had never been able to pay, he was clumsy in a way he had not been clumsy in years.

"That is not what I asked," said Lin Hao.

"That is what I am going to answer first," said the counterman. "Because the office is not the price and not the product and not the man, and I have worked that out in the last four days, and I have been paid nothing for nine years at a counter without once thinking about it, and I have thought about this for about four days without stopping."

## What A Person Pays For

They went out into the yard behind the branch with the night cold around them, and the counterman leaned against the wall with his hands in his pockets. Lin Hao left the bucket inside and stood with his arms folded, listening, because listening was the only thing he could offer that had any value.

"What a person pays a man in a street for is that they cannot manage a stair and a morning," said the counterman. "Not the address."

"That is what you should have asked me about," said the counterman. "The address is on a door in the open and has been for months, and the man has never touched it. What costs is four flights twice over in the cold before work, with a bad knee or a child or a shift that starts at ten. That is labour and it is done again tomorrow, and a walk is a thing a person does."

He paused and looked up at the dark windows of the washrooms.

"And the only office in this city that does what that man does for nothing is four floors of washrooms that no person has ever described to any person," said the counterman. "You scrub basins four nights a week where no stranger ever sees you, and it is the same job in another shape. A person who cannot manage the night gets a clean place in the morning, and no person pays, and no person is thanked."

Lin Hao felt the truth of it go through him like cold water. He had spent a month criticising a price from the top of a stair, and here was the man who was paid nothing telling him that he did the same job himself at night for nothing, in another building, with a brush.

"I have criticised the walking," said Lin Hao. "Out loud, in rooms and on steps. And all the time I have been walking basins at night for nothing, and no person has ever paid me for it and I have never asked."

"You have not criticised the walking," said the counterman. "You have criticised the charging. And you were right to, because charging makes a rate, and a rate is a thing a person can be put on. But you have just started doing the same job at the bottom of a third court for nothing, in daylight, and you have not noticed, and that is what I came to tell you."

He pushed off the wall and stood straight, and Lin Hao saw that he was tired in the way men get tired when they have carried a thought for days.

"I offer you nothing and I take nothing," said the counterman. "I have been looked through for nine years and I am not owed anything, and I came anyway because the thought would not leave me alone. I will see you in the morning at the stair."

He went then, without waiting to be shown out, into the dark with his coat buttoned. Lin Hao stood in the yard with the cold on his wet sleeves and watched him go, feeling small and seen through in a way that had nothing cruel in it.

What he wanted was to thank the man and pay him and ask him to come again, and all three were forbidden to him by his own mouth. He picked up the bucket with hands that shook a little, not from the chest but from the honour of being looked at truly, and went back to the third floor to finish what the night required.

## The Next Morning At The Foot

He was at the bottom of his own stair by a quarter to ten with his back to the wall and the morning bright. The counterman came at about ten past ten, walking into the third court with his hands empty, and stood four feet off looking up at the four flights. He did not go up. He had never been up those flights to that door, and he did not go up now.

The standing ones were there, in their places along the wall, saying the three lines badly to strangers. The counterman watched them for a while with his head on one side, and Lin Hao watched him watch, afraid to speak first.

Then the counterman turned to him and said one sentence, in the ordinary voice, with strangers close enough to hear.

"The man in the street is not stealing it off any person," said the counterman. "And this office has just started doing the same job at the bottom of a third court for nothing, in daylight, and has not noticed. When a free thing starts it should start where the paid thing started."

He asked for nothing at all, and he did not go back up. He nodded once to the standing women, who nodded back without knowing who he was, and he walked out of the court into the morning traffic.

Lin Hao stood with his shoulder to the wall and felt the reversal come over him like dust after a cart has passed. The court smelled of rain and bread from the lane, and the standing ones went on with their low talk, unaware that a judgment had been given and received in front of them. He leaned his weight hard against the wall behind him for a moment, feeling the cold stone through his coat, and then stood straight again.

He had been told by the one person he had never been able to pay that he had started doing the thing he criticised, out loud, by somebody who was owed nothing and had come anyway. He felt shame and relief together, the way a man feels when a doctor names the illness and says it is not fatal. What he wanted, with the morning still ahead of him, was to be worthy of the sentence, and he did not know whether worthiness was standing still or moving.

At midday he went up and told Wenna Raut the whole of it with the door open and her pen down. She listened without interrupting, and when he had finished she asked whether anything went in.

"Nothing goes in," said Lin Hao. "He came to look at washrooms and he looked at water and not at the office, and what he said was said at the foot of a stair in daylight. That is where it is spent, and that is where it stays."

He stood the afternoon at the bottom with the sentence warm in him. A lane off Cordwain was waiting, and a man with chalk who wanted to put a figure on the standing itself. He thought of it as the light went off that street at about ten past four, and he was glad to have a reckoning coming that was not his own.

Four thousand four hundred and twenty-one days. Less four thousand four hundred and fourteen, the fifth of this one, is seven, and a man who was paid nothing looked at washrooms at night and said one sentence in daylight and asked for nothing.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is forty-two, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and one days, which are sixty-six months and twenty-one days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Carried Book Comes Back

The thirtieth of this one and the seventh of the next one is day four thousand five hundred and sixty-six, and four thousand five hundred and sixty-six less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of this one, is seven, and the month ended in between and there is nothing in it to take out.

## The First Hour

He came in at a quarter to ten and the book was on the table where a thing that has been gone forty-eight days goes, in the middle, square.

The first hour is the only hour in nine years in which anything about that book is new, and he had said so out loud the evening before with the door open, and now the hour was here and nobody in the building knew what he was doing, which was the arrangement and was the reason for it.

He did it in about nine minutes with his hands and no ceremony.

He put two fingers on the bottom edge of the back board and went along it the whole way, and it was flat and it was sound and there was no daylight anywhere in it.

He turned the book over and put his palm on the front board and it was dry, which it had not been for about four days before it went in August, because a room with four doors open in daylight in September is not a dry room. He put his nose to the gutter of the binding and there was nothing in it. He opened the front board, looked at the strip of paper with the tradesman's mark on it, and put it back the way he found it.

Then he stood there with his hands on the table and worked out that that was the whole of the first hour, and that in about nine years of that office nothing at all had ever happened in the first hour, and that there was no rule anywhere in the building about it, and that there could not be, because every rule in the place had been written by somebody who was thinking about nights.

## What He Did Not Look At

He did not read a row.

That was the arrangement and he had made it deliberately the evening before, and it held for about a quarter of an hour, and then Wenna Raut came to the near end of the table with her sleeves back.

"I want to check it."

"No."

"That is not an answer, that is a noise."

"It is the answer," said Lin Hao. "I told you what I was going to do last night at about four in this room with the door open and you heard me."

"You said you would look at it."

"I have looked at it."

"You have put your hand on it."

"The board, the gutter, the front and the back and whether it is wet. That is what I have looked at and I have not said a word about it to anybody and neither are you going to."

He did not raise his voice and there was a room with people in it. "Say one more word about a check in that voice in this room this morning and I will do it in front of all of them. And then it is a practice, and in about four years this room will have a rule about checking in it, and every person in this city will assume it was always there, and the three of us will not be able to correct that either."

She put both hands on the table.

"That is the worst instrument in this city," she said, "and you have chosen it out loud."

"I have chosen it out loud. That is the difference between me and the man in the street, and it is the only one there is, and I would take it if anybody else offered me anything."

## Not Today

The carrier with the bad back wanted to open it at about eleven and he said not today.

"You have been carrying an empty strap for forty-eight days and there is a new board in that thing."

"There is a new board in it," he said, "and there is a row in it with a date on it, and I am not opening that row in front of anybody until I have sat on my own hands for one day. That is not a rule. It is a man who has been awake for two nights thinking about a strap."

"That is the first sentence you have said to me since the tenth that I have believed all the way through."

## The Seventh And The Box

He went down and round and into the passage off Cordwain at about half past two, because the seventh was the seventh.

The form went up on the rail on the nail above the one with the figure on it, with two drawing pins, and the counterman squared the bottom edge with his thumbnail and stepped back and left it there.

It wanted what the office at the top of that stair had done in a quarter, and the figure it wanted was a count of persons, and Lin Hao stood and looked at the empty box in it for about a minute and a half and did not put anything in it and did not say why.

"You are going to have to say why to me," said the counterman. "I am not asking for the office. I am asking for me."

"Because a man at a counter under a window told me in June that a thing which will not count its own work cannot be defended either, and I have thought about that every day since," said Lin Hao.

"And the answer I have come up with is that a thing which does not count itself has to be good enough to be believed. This office is not good enough to be believed by anybody who has not been up those four flights, and there is nothing I can put in that box that would make it so."

The counterman took a pencil off the string and wrote under the box in his own hand on his own paper, in the small hand he chalks the head of that office's own standing sheet in, in a lane off Cordwain. One line and no more.

The office at the top of that stair keeps no figure of any person and will not supply one. Ask it.

"That is the same sentence as the last one," said Lin Hao.

"It is the same sentence every time and it will be the same sentence every seventh for as long as I am behind this counter," said the counterman. "You have never once asked me what it costs me to write it."

"Not this morning."

"I did not expect you to, and I told you in June that you do not thank people for doing things, and it is no more true in September than it was in June."

He put the pencil back on the string. "Go up your four flights and leave me alone. That book has been outside this building for fifty-five days and there is a mark in it now that was not in it before it went, and there is no return on that rail for a mark. That is the only thing I know this month that is worth saying out loud."

## Not Being Said Either

He walked back up and he did not thank him and he did not offer to pay him and he did not stop him.

On the stair, about halfway up, he stood still for a moment.

A man who is paid nothing had just told him something he had not thought of, and he was not going to pass it on, and he was not going to write it, and he was not going to say it out loud to the two of them at the top of that room either. The moment a thing like that is said in that room it becomes theirs, and then it becomes a thing somebody can be held to in four years.

So he went up the remaining flights with it in his head and said nothing about it, which was a new thing to do with a sentence and cost him something he had not budgeted for.

## Half Past Five Without A Sound In It

At half past five he carried it down the four flights and out along that road in the ordinary way, the wire round the buckle twisted twice in his own pocket doing nothing that evening for anybody.

He got about a hundred yards down the road before he worked out what was different and then he stood in the middle of the street and worked out the rest of it.

There was nothing in it.

For two years he had carried that thing down those stairs and out along that road every working evening with a tick against his ribs about every four seconds, and on the way back up he had never once been able to put a name on the noise, because he had known what it was, and now there was nothing, and the wire was still round the buckle where it had been every working evening for two years, and it had never once been the thing that made the noise.

The carrier with the bad back came down behind him about four minutes later and found him standing in the road with a book under his arm and his hand on the buckle.

"You have stopped in the road," she said.

"I have."

"Then I will walk with you, because there is a man who hires carts on this road at about this hour and I would rather he did not do it twice." She took the other end of the strap where it came down past his hip, and they walked the rest of the way in silence.

"Fifty-five days," she said, at the gate.

"Fifty-five days out. Forty-six of them past the nine he said."

"Nine days on the bench," said the carrier with the bad back, "and forty-eight days in that room with four doors open, and a week of it since on that table. And not one person in this building knew it was due on the twenty-second of August except you, because you were the one who was told, and you told nobody, and that is not a thing I am going to argue with you about, and it is also not a thing I am going to forget."

Four thousand five hundred and sixty-six days. Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of this one, is seven, and the month ended in between and there is nothing in it to take out, and a man checked a book that had been out of a building for fifty-five days with his own hands in about nine minutes and would not say so out loud.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seven, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and forty-six days, which are seventy-one months and sixteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

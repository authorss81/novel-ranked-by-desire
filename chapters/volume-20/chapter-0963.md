# A Second Late

Four thousand three hundred and eighty-six was the seventh of this one and the fourteenth of this one is day four thousand three hundred and ninety-three, and four thousand three hundred and ninety-three less four thousand three hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of this one, is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## Beatrix Halm

He went out to the end of the Ferrier line at about half past five in the morning, with the light still low and the air cold enough to show his breath.

The back platform stood beyond the last points where the trams were stabled overnight, a plain stretch of stone with a drop bar across the rails and a carriage with no person in it drawn up beside it. A low wall ran along the far side and a lamp burned at each end, and the noise of the city came thin over the roofs.

She was at the far end with a flag in her hand and a board under her arm, watching the empty carriage the way a person watches a room she is responsible for.

He knew her by sight from the branch roster talk and by name from Brack's lane, and he had not come to ask her about herself. He had come to find out whether a sentence had been going down her line for two years without her ever putting a stop to it, before he did anything at all about it.

"You are early for the office," said Beatrix Halm.

"I am early for you," said Lin Hao. "And I have come to ask about a carriage and not about a person, because a carriage is the only thing I am entitled to ask about."

She looked at him for a moment with her head on one side, the way people look when they are listening with one ear and keeping the other for the work. She was deaf in one ear and it had been so since the Veil, and the city's noise lived in the other one, and she carried herself the way people carry themselves when hearing is a trade.

"What carriage," said Beatrix Halm.

"The one with no person in it," said Lin Hao. "And the ones before it for two years. I want to know what is said at one end of them and heard at the other."

## The Ferrier Line

She put the board down on the low wall and came nearer, and the lamp at the end of the platform laid a bar of light across her face. He felt the fear he had been carrying for a fortnight rise up in his throat, the fear of his own mouth, and he was glad to be standing in cold air where it showed.

"You have heard it," she said. It was not a question.

"I have heard that you have heard it," said Lin Hao. "And I have not come to ask you to repeat it, because repeating would make you the second mouth, and you have spent two years refusing to be that."

She was quiet for about four seconds, and a tram moved on the far road with a grinding of wheels. He wanted an answer and he wanted it true, and he wanted it the way a man wants a door to be open when he knocks. What he felt, with the empty carriage beside them, was shame at asking a woman to carry his own trouble for him.

"I work this line," said Beatrix Halm. "For about twenty years I have worked it, from the first platform to this one, and I know how a thing said at one end of a carriage is heard at the other end about a second late with the hard edges gone off it."

She glanced at the empty carriage and then back at him.

"A man at the front says something to a woman beside him, and I am at the back with the bar, and it comes to me late and soft, with the consonants worn off it the way paint wears off a rail."

"I have learned to hear what was said by what arrives, the way you learn to read a card by its soft corners. I can tell a passenger what he said without ever having heard him say it, and I am right often enough that the signalmen ask me."

"And for two years."

"And for about two years there have been three lines going up and down this line said wrong at the far end," said Beatrix Halm. "A street and a door and hours, with the hours wrong and the door half wrong, said out loud by people who learned them from each other and never from a door. I have heard them go past me about nine times a week and I have never told any person, because telling would make me the second mouth."

He stood still in the cold and let that sit between them. He had been afraid of his own mouth for a fortnight, and here was a woman who had carried a worse one for two years and had never once used it.

"Why did you not stop it," said Lin Hao.

"Because stopping it out loud is saying it out loud," said Beatrix Halm. "And a wrong thing said out loud in a carriage cannot be traced to anything, and that is worse than a wrong thing on paper. I have kept my mouth shut for two years and it has cost me sleep, and I am telling you now because you came to the back platform and asked about a carriage."

## The Drop Bar

They walked to the drop bar at the end of the platform, where the rails ran out into a buffer of old sleepers. The city was waking behind them and the lamps were going out one by one. She leaned her weight on the bar with her board tucked under her arm, and he stood beside her with his hands in his pockets, afraid to touch her and afraid not to.

"I want you to hear the rest from me and not from a lane," said Beatrix Halm. "There is a woman at the far end of this line who has been saying those three lines out loud for four months for nothing. She says them to people waiting in the cold because she is kind and because she learned them from a man who was kind, and she has them true enough to be useful and wrong enough to be hers."

She paused and looked down the line toward the waking streets.

"And she cannot now afford the man with the coin, and has not said so to any person. The price is small and it is beyond her, and she will not be walked up four flights because she has not got it, and she has told no person because telling would be asking."

Lin Hao felt the ground shift under the morning. He had spent a month thinking about people who paid and people who could not pay, and here was one he had never met, at the far end of a line, saying his own address for nothing because she could not buy the walk.

"I know about the coin," said Lin Hao. "A man in a street who says his price before he names the door, and walks the stair himself once more before he takes any person, and has never been up those flights to that door, and has not lied to any person. He is right about the walk."

"I know," said Beatrix Halm. "A signalman told me about him on the previous morning, which is how things get to me. The line tells me everything a day late, the way a carriage tells me everything a second late. I knew before you told me and I am glad you told me straight."

She turned to face him then, with the bar at her back and the empty carriage behind him, and he saw that she had decided something in the time they had stood there.

"I have been listening to other people say your three lines for two years," said Beatrix Halm. "And I have not said them once, not even to correct them, because I would not be a mouth. This morning I want to be one, and I want it to be yours first."

## Her Hands

He understood what she was asking and he felt heat go through him that had nothing to do with the cold. He wanted her and he wanted to be wanted, and he wanted it the way a man wants water after a night shift. What he felt, with her standing close enough that he could see the pulse in her throat, was tenderness so sharp it hurt.

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And quickly, because the morning is short and the line is waking."

She smiled at that, quick and real, and put her board down on the stones. She came to him and put her hands on his chest through his coat, feeling the spent chest under it without pity and without pausing, and she kissed him hard on the mouth with her eyes open.

"I have wanted this since you came onto my platform and asked about a carriage instead of about me," said Beatrix Halm. "Take your coat off and put it on the wall, because I am not having it on the stones."

He took it off and laid it on the low wall. She got her skirts up round her waist herself, quick and sure, and got her fingers between her legs with her drawers pushed aside, and she showed him what came back on them, wet and shining in the early light. She told him she had been that wet since the moment he said he would not make her a mouth.

"That is not a thing you are to be careful about," said Beatrix Halm. "I am not fragile and I am not tired, and I have decided the order of this before I started."

She got his trousers open herself and took his cock in her hand, working him hard inside about nine seconds, watching his face the whole way. He groaned and she laughed under her breath, pleased with herself and with him, and she told him how hard he was and how much she liked the weight of him.

"Hold my hips and nothing else," said Beatrix Halm. "You have got a chest that is spent and I am not going to have you holding on like a man on a stair."

She pulled him into her against the drop bar with one hand at the back of his neck, and she was slick and hot around him, and she moved on him hard from the first stroke, taking him deep and telling him to do it properly. The platform was empty and the empty carriage stood beside them, and she was loud about it, crying out with each thrust and telling him what it was doing to her.

He said her name, and then he said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was better than what she was doing. She came with her fingers dug into his shoulders, shaking hard against the bar, and the noise of it went out across the empty rails and came back off the stabled trams. She laughed in the middle of it, delighted with herself, and then she made him finish, counting under her breath in seconds and watching his eyes.

Afterwards she got down on her knees on his coat and took him in her mouth to finish him, two fingers round the root of him, and she watched his face and told him not to shut his eyes. He came with his mouth open and his hands in her hair, and she swallowed and wiped her mouth and stood up, pleased and unhurried, and pulled her skirts down.

That was the part he had not been ready for. She enjoyed the whole of it completely, the way she did her work, and she said so plainly, telling him what had been good and what she wanted again. None of it was reluctant and all of it was hers as much as his.

## Afterwards By The Wall

She got his coat off the wall and shook the dust off it and held it for him while he put it on. Her hands were red from the cold and from the bar, and she warmed them on his chest through the cloth without asking.

"Right," said Beatrix Halm. "The rest of it standing up, and then you can go, because my line is waking and I have a carriage to see to."

"Say it," said Lin Hao.

"I am not on anything and I am not going to be," said Beatrix Halm. "I have been outside every count in this city for twenty years and I am staying outside. What I gave you this morning was not a favour and I do not want one back. Count nothing."

"I count nothing," said Lin Hao. "And I am glad of this morning in a way I have not been glad of anything since the winter."

She picked up her board and her flag, and her face changed back into the face she wore for the work. At the steps down to the stabling road she stopped with her hand on the rail.

"Go and find the woman at the far end," said Beatrix Halm. "She has been saying your three lines for nothing for four months and she cannot pay for the walk, and she has told no person. That is the debt this morning pays, and you are to pay it on."

He went back up the line with the light full on now and the city loud around him. He felt wrung out and tender and awake, and his chest hurt less than it had in a week. What he wanted, walking back toward the third court, was to stand at the bottom of his own stair and be worthy of two women who had said his lines without ever asking him for anything.

A woman who had paid a man a small amount that morning for a walk and had been given the address true would be standing at the bottom of a third court in daylight, waiting for a man who could confirm it. He did not know her yet and he knew she was coming, the way Beatrix knew what a passenger had said without ever having heard him.

Four thousand three hundred and ninety-three days. Less four thousand three hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of this one, is seven, and on a back platform at the end of a line a woman who had kept silent for two years gave him the finding of four months and asked for nothing to be counted.

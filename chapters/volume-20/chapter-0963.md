# A Second Late

Four thousand three hundred and eighty-six was the seventh of this one and the fourteenth of this one is day four thousand three hundred and ninety-three, and four thousand three hundred and ninety-three less four thousand three hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of this one, is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## Beatrix Halm

He went out to the end of the Ferrier line at about half past five in the morning, the light still low and the air cold enough to show his breath.

The back platform stood beyond the last points where the trams were stabled overnight, a plain stretch of stone with a drop bar across the rails and a carriage with no person in it drawn up beside it. A low wall ran along the far side, a lamp burned at each end, and the noise of the city came thin over the roofs.

She was at the far end with a flag in her hand and a board under her arm, watching the empty carriage the way a person watches a room she is responsible for.

He wanted to find out whether a sentence had been going down her line for two years without her ever putting a stop to it, before he did anything at all about it.

"You are early for the office," said Beatrix Halm.

"I am early for you," said Lin Hao. "And I have come to ask about a carriage and not about a person, because a carriage is the only thing I am entitled to ask about."

She looked at him for a moment with her head on one side, the way people look when they are listening with one ear and keeping the other for the work. She was deaf in one ear and it had been so since the Veil, and the city's noise lived in the other one.

"What carriage," said Beatrix Halm.

"The one with no person in it," said Lin Hao. "And the ones before it for two years. I want to know what is said at one end of them and heard at the other."

## The Ferrier Line

She put the board down on the low wall and came nearer, and a lamp laid a bar of light across her face. He felt the fear he had been carrying for a fortnight rise up in his throat, the fear of his own mouth, and he was glad of the cold air, where it showed.

"You have heard it," she said. It was not a question.

"I have heard that you have heard it," said Lin Hao. "And I have not come to ask you to repeat it, because repeating would make you the second mouth, and you have spent two years refusing to be that."

She was quiet for about four seconds, and a tram moved on the far road with the grind of its wheels coming up through the boards of the platform. He wanted an answer and he wanted it true.

Standing there wanting it, he understood that he had carried his own trouble down a line at half past five in the morning and put it in front of a woman on her own platform and asked her to hold it for him. The shame of it went hot under his shirt, and he let it stand there, because there was nothing else to be done with it here.

"I work this line," said Beatrix Halm. "For about twenty years I have worked it, from the first platform to this one, and I know how a thing said at one end of a carriage is heard at the other end about a second late with the hard edges gone off it."

"A man at the front says something to a woman beside him, and I am at the back with the bar, and it comes to me late and soft, the consonants worn off it the way paint wears off a rail."

"I have learned to hear what was said by what arrives, the way you learn to read a card by its soft corners. I can tell a passenger what he said without ever having heard him say it, and I am right often enough that the signalmen ask me."

"And for two years."

"And for about two years there have been three lines going up and down this line said wrong at the far end," said Beatrix Halm. "A street and a door and hours, with the hours wrong and the door half wrong, said out loud by people who learned them from each other and never from a door. They go past me all day and they have gone past me every day of those two years, and I have never told any person, because telling would make me the second mouth."

He stood still in the cold and let that sit between them. Here was a woman who had carried a mouth worse than his for two years and had never once used it.

"Why did you not stop it," said Lin Hao.

"Because stopping it out loud is saying it out loud," said Beatrix Halm. "And a wrong thing said out loud in a carriage cannot be traced to anything, and that is worse than a wrong thing on paper. I have kept my mouth shut for two years and it has cost me sleep. I am telling you now because you came to the back platform and asked about a carriage."

## The Drop Bar

They walked to the drop bar at the end of the platform, where the rails ran out into a buffer of old sleepers. The city was waking behind them and the lamps were going out one by one. She leaned her weight on the bar with her board tucked under her arm, and he stood beside her with his hands in his pockets, afraid to touch her and afraid not to.

"I want you to hear the rest from me and not from a lane," said Beatrix Halm. "There is a woman at the far end of this line who has been saying those three lines out loud for four months for nothing. She says them to people waiting in the cold because she is kind and because she learned them from a man who was kind, and she has them true enough to be useful and wrong enough to be hers."

She looked down the line toward the waking streets.

"And she cannot now afford the man with the coin, and has not said so to any person. The price is small and it is beyond her, and she will not be walked up four flights because she has not got it, and she has told no person because telling would be asking."

The ground shifted under the morning. He had spent a month thinking about people who paid and people who could not pay, and here was one he had never met, at the far end of a line, saying his own address for nothing because she could not buy the walk.

"I know about the coin," said Lin Hao. "A man in a street who says his price before he names the door, and walks the stair himself once more before he takes any person, and has never been up those flights to that door, and has not lied. He is right about the walk."

"I know," said Beatrix Halm. "A signalman told me about him on the previous morning. The line tells me everything a day late, the way a carriage tells me everything a second late. I knew before you told me and I am glad you told me straight."

She turned to face him then, the bar at her back and the empty carriage behind him, and he saw that she had decided something in the time they had stood there.

"I have been listening to other people say your three lines for two years," said Beatrix Halm. "And I have not said them once, not even to correct them, because I would not be a mouth. This morning I want to be one, and I want it to be yours first."

## Her Hands

He understood what she was asking, and the heat that went through him had nothing to do with the cold. He wanted her, and he wanted to be wanted badly enough that his hands had gone hot and useless at his sides. She was near enough to show him the pulse in her throat, and the tenderness of it went through him sharp enough to hurt, and he had no practice against it of any kind.

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And quickly, because the morning is short and the line is waking."

She smiled at that, quick and real, and put her board down on the stones. She came to him and put her hands on his chest through his coat, feeling the spent chest under it without pity and without pausing, and she kissed him hard on the mouth with her eyes open.

"I have wanted this since you came onto my platform and asked about a carriage instead of about me," said Beatrix Halm. "Take your coat off and put it on the wall, because I am not having it on the stones."

He took it off and laid it along the wall where the mortar had gone dry. She got her skirts up round her waist herself, quick and sure, pushed her drawers aside with two fingers, then opened his trousers with her other hand and told him what her fingers had been doing since he said he would not make her a mouth.

"That is not a thing you are to be careful about," said Beatrix Halm. "I am not fragile and I am not tired, and I have decided the order of this before I started. Hands on the bar behind me, and if you put a hand on my head I will stop."

He put his hands on the drop bar behind her, the cold iron of it under his palms, and she went down on one knee on the ballast at the end of the rails and took him in her mouth. She was in no hurry about it. She looked up at him once to be sure he was watching and then stopped looking up for a while, and the two lamps threw her shadow long across the stones.

She brought him to the edge of it and held him there with her hand at the root of him, and she said the word before he could have said one.

"Not yet," said Beatrix Halm. "I have had two years of other people's voices arriving at the wrong end of this line with the consonants gone off them, and I am going to have this one arrive proper, inside me, when it finishes, and not one moment before that."

She said it in the voice she used for a fare at the front of a carriage, and he laughed out loud, and she took the laugh for the agreement it was and used it.

Then she stood, and turned him round, and got him in against the bar with her back to the cold air off the stabled road and her hands flat on the wood on either side of his ribs. She set the pace herself from the first stroke, deep and unhurried, and told him where his hands were to go, and when she wanted more out of him she asked for it in a voice that carried out over the rails.

He came first, with her fingers hooked over the top of the bar and her forehead against the wood of it, shaking hard against him, and the noise of it went out across the empty stabling road and came back off the carriage with no person in it. She laughed in the middle of that, delighted with herself, and then she held him there and made him finish inside her, one hand at the back of his neck, watching his face and telling him not to shut his eyes.

He came with his mouth open and both hands flat on the bar on either side of hers. She felt the whole of it arrive, and told him afterwards how much of it she had taken and how little she had been given, which was the wrong way round and exactly what she wanted.

Then she got down off him and pushed her skirts down and spat over the ballast and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, and she was not cold yet.

That was the part he had not been ready for. She enjoyed all of it completely, the way she did her work, and said so plainly, telling him what had been good and what she wanted again. None of it was reluctant and all of it was hers as much as his.

## Afterwards By The Wall

She got his coat off the wall and shook the dust off it and held it for him while he put it on. Her hands were red from the cold and from the bar, and she warmed them on his chest through the cloth without asking.

"Right," said Beatrix Halm. "Take your shirt down before you go up the line, and go when you are ready, because my line is waking and I have a carriage to see to."

"Say it," said Lin Hao.

"I am not on anything and I am not going to be," said Beatrix Halm. "I have been outside every count in this city for twenty years and I am staying outside. What I gave you this morning was not a favour and I do not want one back. Count nothing."

"I count nothing," said Lin Hao. "And I am glad of this morning in a way I have not been glad of anything since the winter."

She picked up her board and her flag, and her face changed back into the face she wore for the work. At the steps down to the stabling road she stopped with her hand on the rail.

"Go and find the woman at the far end," said Beatrix Halm. "She has been saying your three lines for nothing for four months and she cannot pay for the walk, and she has told no person. That is the debt this morning pays, and you are to pay it on."

He went back up the line with the light full on now and the city loud around him. He felt wrung out and tender and awake, and his chest hurt less than it had in a week. What he wanted, walking back toward the third court, was to be worthy of two women who had said his lines without ever asking him for anything.

A woman who had paid a man a small amount that morning for a walk and had been given the address true would be standing at the bottom of a third court in daylight, waiting for a man who could confirm it. He had never seen her and he knew she was coming, the way Beatrix knew what a passenger had said without ever having heard him.

Four thousand three hundred and ninety-three days. Less four thousand three hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of this one, is seven, and on a back platform at the end of a line a woman who had kept silent for two years gave him the finding of four months and asked for nothing to be counted.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is fourteen, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fourteen of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and seventy-three days, which are sixty-five months and twenty-three days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

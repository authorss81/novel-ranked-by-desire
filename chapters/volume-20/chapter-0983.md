# Nine Seconds Before An Answer

Four thousand five hundred and twenty-five was the twenty-sixth of this one and the third of the next one is day four thousand five hundred and thirty-two, and four thousand five hundred and thirty-two less four thousand five hundred and twenty-five, the twenty-sixth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## The Only Room Where Nothing Can Be Compelled

He went because he had to put nine days of practice somewhere and there was one place in this city where it could not be turned into a rule.

A chapel off a lane has a stone floor and plain benches and a lectern at one end and a door that stands open from morning, and in it there is nothing anybody can be made to do. He had been in it once as a boy, put there by a woman who had decided he was the one thing in the city she could not get away with, and he had got out of it in about nine years without ever explaining to anybody why that room had worked.

She was at the back.

She is forty-five and she has been right about this for twenty years and she was not going to make it easier for him. The people standing in that room were the chapel's own, and not one of them had come for him, and not one of them had heard those three lines before the fourteenth of November.

## The Question And The Silence After It

"I want to say a thing and I am going to ask you a question first," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I am telling you the order this time because you have not read a room properly since the fourth of March and you will assume I am doing what I did in my own rooms, and you are not going to do that here."

"What question."

She asked it. He had been in the building a quarter of an hour and had not got his coat off.

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came out of it, and he understood about four seconds later that it was not him.

There are rooms where a person decides a thing and answers, and there are rooms where a thing has to stand still for a while before it can be said at all, and about nine seconds is not hesitation. He stood on a stone floor in front of strangers feeling a question arrive in him and sit down in him and wait to be allowed out, and he had never once in nine years of standing at the top of a table been in a room that would not let him answer.

Nobody else in that room noticed. They were used to it.

## What She Said Once He Was Allowed

"You have been saying three lines out loud at the bottom of a stair since November," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Say them here."

"Why here."

"Because in here you cannot be asked to prove them." She put her hands on the back of the bench in front of her. "A thing written on a card can be produced by anybody who can find the card. A thing said in a street can only be produced by the man who said it, and a man can say it differently tomorrow and there is nothing behind him at all."

He said them.

He said the street and the door at the head of four flights and the hours between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and he said them into a room with a stone floor and a door standing open, and when he had finished about four seconds went by the way it always goes by.

Nobody said them back.

Not one of the nine repeated a word. They went on standing where they were standing, because they had heard him and heard him correctly and had nothing to say about it, and Agnete Lindqvist looked at the open door and then at him.

"That is what it is worth," she said.

## What She Wanted, Said Before She Touched Him

"Now I am going to tell you what I want, and I am going to tell you all of it, and I am not going to tell you the order."

"I want you twice this afternoon," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And I want you, after that, to go up four flights of stair and find out what your office said to a woman in August and what your office is going to say to her when she comes back, and I want to do it before you do it, because you will take a week and she will come back on the tenth."

"Who is she."

"That is my price and you can have it for nothing," she said. "The rest of it I want without anything at all, and it is that you go and find out, and I am telling you that you will not, and I am telling you why. You will not, because if you find out that you were wrong in August then you have to do something about it, and the thing you will do about it is nothing. That is what you are."

"Give me the name."

"No," said Agnete Lindqvist, "because you will go and look it up before you have got to the bottom of the stair and then you will not tell me."

He did not answer her.

He wanted to say something about the door and the card and the woman at the market end. The room took about nine seconds off him and then gave him back a stone floor and an open door, and what came out of him was not any of the things he had loaded.

"I do not know what she is going to be told."

"That is the first sentence you have said to me since November that I am going to let stand," said Agnete Lindqvist.

## Taking Her Own Clothes Off

Then she did not touch him, which he had been braced for.

She took her coat off and hung it over the end of a bench and stood there in a room where nothing can be compelled and said why she was doing it in her own clothes.

"I am not undressing you," she said. "I have come this far without asking you a single question and I am not going to start by taking your coat off a man who came out of a branch office at eleven."

"And I am not taking mine off because I want to. I am taking it off because this is the only room in this city where nothing can be taken off anybody, and if it is going to happen then neither of us is going to be searched for it afterwards."

She had her back to the open door when she did it and she did it slowly and she did it without any of the theatre of it, and there were people in the room and not one of them turned round.

## In The Aisle

She put him on the floor of the aisle with his back against the end of a bench row and his legs out in front of him. Then she came down over him and took him inside her herself, sitting on the stone and taking her own weight and going at her own speed, with her hands on the bench either side of his head.

He was already wet when she got him in and she made him take all of it in one push, and she did not care in the least what that cost him, and she watched his face while she did it and told him what she saw on it.

She was quiet.

That was the part he had not been ready for and it undid more than the rest of it put together. There was nothing in that room for a sound to go and come back from, and she was a large woman who had been on her feet in a yard in every month of twenty years and she was working above him in almost complete silence, and her breathing was the loudest thing in the building.

"You keep looking at the door," she said.

"I am looking at the door."

"Nobody is coming through it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is the whole of the building. Not one person in this city can be made to stand in a doorway because they were asked to by somebody with a reason."

She went on at that speed and let him have one hand when he put it up, her cock riding against him with every push and his own hands on the stone on either side of her, and she talked the whole time, not to him and not to anybody, in the voice she used in a yard.

"Do you know what the worst of it is," she said. "It is not that you are wrong. You are the first person in twenty years who has come at it from the other end, and you are nearly right, and near is the most expensive place there is."

"And then what."

"And then in about four years there will not be a page anywhere with your name on it and there will be ten thousand of us standing at the bottom of a stair, and every one of us will be right, and not one of us will be you."

## She Came First

She came first and she made almost no noise doing it, and afterwards she told him why in the same voice.

"I did not make a noise because in this room nobody has to," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is not modesty. That is the only respect I have got left and I am spending it on you."

Then she made him finish it inside her, with her own hand closed round him and her mouth against his jaw, and she took all of it and held him in against her while it went, and then she let go and sat back on her heels on a stone floor and put her hands on her knees.

## The Silence After

About nine seconds went by and nobody in that room said anything.

Somebody near the door shifted a foot. The woman at the front went on reading what she had been given. The open door stood open with the lane outside it doing what a lane does.

"That is the room," said Agnete Lindqvist. "You have just had a woman in a stone floor tell you the worst true thing anybody has said to you in four months, and about nine seconds after it finished every person in this building decided whether to answer it, and not one of them answered, because there was nothing to answer. That is the only instrument in this city that has never once been lied to, and it does not work, and it is here."

## The Whole Of It, Given To Him

He sat against the end of that bench row with his back on the stone and listened to the end of it, and she gave it to him without softening one word of it and without any pleasure in it at all.

"A sentence nobody wrote down is worth exactly what it says and no more," she said. "Not what it was worth to the person who said it. Not what it would have been worth if it had been written down. What it says. I have said that for twenty years and I have been laughed out of two rooms for it."

"You are right," said Lin Hao.

"You have not let me finish."

"You are right, and I have known it since the seventh of August, and I have known it because a woman in my own building answered the same person twice in a month and the two answers were not the same words."

He put his hands on his knees. "You have been right about this since before I was a man in a white plate, and I have spent four months going round a city selling the opposite, and it has worked, and it has worked because nobody can check a mouth. That is not an argument about mouths. That is just the truth about them."

Agnete Lindqvist looked at him for a long time, and then she got up off the stone floor and picked her coat off the end of the bench and put it on.

"And you are going to keep saying it," she said. "That is the other half. I want you to hear it in my mouth so that you cannot go away and decide that I wanted you to stop."

"I am going to keep saying it."

"I know. I have heard it in a great many other mouths since November and it has been wrong in almost all of them, and I stopped correcting them in the spring because a person who corrects it has become the second copy." She held the door open with her shoulder. "Get your coat. Go and find out what you told a woman in August. And when you have found out, do not come back up here and tell me about it, because I will not be able to check it and neither will you, and that is the entire difference between this room and yours."

Four thousand five hundred and thirty-two days. Less four thousand five hundred and twenty-five, the twenty-sixth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven, and a woman in a room where nothing can be compelled told a man that he was nearly right and that near was the most expensive place there is, and he conceded the whole of it on a stone floor with his back against a bench.

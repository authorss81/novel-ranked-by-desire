# Four Flights Alone

Four thousand four hundred was the twenty-first of this one and the twenty-eighth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and seven, and four thousand four hundred and seven less four thousand four hundred, the twenty-first of this one, is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## Half An Hour Late

He was late on purpose on the morning of the twenty-eighth. He woke with his chest tight and his breath short, the way it went at the end of the fourth week when he had done the stair too often, and he lay for a while in the dark listening to it.

He had dreamed of the stair with no person on it, four flights of empty stone going up into dark, and he had woken angry at the dream for showing him what he was afraid to test. He wanted to find out what the absence of a man at the bottom of a stair did to a door, and he wanted it the way a man wants to take his hand off a wound to see whether it bleeds.

The dread came first, cold in the stomach, and the curiosity came after it and was the worse of the two, because the curiosity wanted the stair to fail and had not admitted to wanting it. He had spent his three days off for five weeks on a step, and some part of him hoped the door would not manage without him, because a door that failed would prove the step had been worth something. Another part hoped it would stand, because standing would mean the office was larger than his vanity, and he did not know which hope shamed him more.

The two carriers went up at a quarter to ten without him, with the strap between them and the book in its cloth. He followed half an hour later, alone, climbing the four flights with long pauses on each landing. His chest burned by the third flight and his legs shook on the fourth, and he came into the room with the door open breathing hard, with the book already on the table and Wenna Raut at her place.

"You are late," said Wenna Raut, without reproach.

"I am half an hour late on purpose," said Lin Hao. "And I want you to tell me afterwards what the half hour did, because I am done guessing from the bottom."

He took his place at the end of the table with his hands on the back of the chair until his breath came back. The door stood open onto the landing and the light through the high window lay across the floor.

He felt exposed and useless, a man in a white plate who had arrived after his own office had opened. He watched the carriers coil their straps and take their stools by the window, easy in the room in a way he had never been, and he envied them their backs, which had a use whether he stood below or not.

## Nothing Happens

What happened then was nothing. That was the finding, and it was a finding and not a relief, because nothing happening meant the four flights had never been the thing.

They came up those four flights all morning, alone and in pairs, and every one of them knocked in the ordinary way and was answered in the ordinary way. Wenna Raut entered the questions in the words used and he gave the answers, written or read aloud with both people in the room, and the carriers carried the written ones down by hand.

The stair did not slow down for his being late on it. Not one of them turned on the second landing to look for a man who was supposed to be there; they knocked, and the door answered, and the whole business of the morning went on without him in it.

He watched it with a growing hollowness behind his ribs. The door worked because of a card and four flights and a table and a clerk, and none of those was a man.

The card carried the street and the door and the hours and not one name. The flights were stone and the table was wood and the clerk was a hand that only entered what she was given. He had been spending his three days off on the one part that was never load-bearing, and the morning proved it by ignoring him completely.

At about half past ten he could not bear the room any longer and went down the four flights alone, slowly, with his chest aching. He stood at the bottom of them in the light with empty hands, looking up at the stair he had just come down, and he understood that he had carried the book up by himself that morning with a bad chest for no reason any stranger could see.

A woman came into the court with a child and asked him for the hours, and he gave them from memory. She went up, and he watched her go with anger rising in him, hot and directionless.

He was angry at the stair for not needing him and angry at himself for needing to be needed, and there was nowhere for the anger to go because no person had done anything wrong. He pressed his shoulder hard against the wall until the stone hurt, because pain was at least a thing with a place.

## The Fifth Morning

He did it again the next morning and the morning after, late by half an hour, climbing alone behind the carriers. Both mornings were the same. People came up, were answered, went down.

The book went up at a quarter to ten by two carriers and came down at half past five by his own hands, and it was never upstairs overnight. The bundle stayed at twenty-six things on the table where it had sat for months. He counted the mornings on his fingers at night and found he had lost track, because one late morning looked exactly like another when viewed from a table.

On the fifth morning he came down at half past ten again and stood at the bottom in the light, breathing hard, with his hands shaking from the climb. A carrier passed him with a bundle of slips and nodded, and he nodded back, unable to speak for a moment because his chest was too tight.

He went back up at midday and said it to the clerk with the door open, because it had to be said where it was spent.

"A body at the bottom of a stair is not worth what it has cost me," said Lin Hao. "It has never once made any person come up. The door works without me exactly as it works with me, and I have spent five weeks of mornings on a step that changes nothing about who knocks."

Wenna Raut put her pen down and looked at him for a long moment.

"Then why are you angry," she said. "If it changes nothing, there is nothing to be angry at."

"Because I chose the part that was never the part that worked," said Lin Hao. "I chose to be a body because a body was the only thing I had left that no rule covered, and I have learned this week that the door never needed a body. It needed a card and a stair and a table and you. I am the ornament on my own office, and I paid for the ornament with the only mornings I owned."

He felt tears behind his eyes and blinked them back, furious at himself for the weakness. He wanted to be useful and he wanted to be necessary, and he wanted those things the way a young clerk wants promotion. The grief came out of him in the quiet room, plain and shapeless, five weeks of mornings spent standing on flags while the work went on above him without him.

"The branch night shift is not moved," said Wenna Raut quietly. "And the mornings were not asked for and not bought. You spent them and they are spent, and no person can give them back to you. But you are wrong about one thing."

"What."

"You are wrong that the step changes nothing," she said. "It changes you. A man who stands at the bottom for five weeks learns what the bottom is, and that is not nothing, even if no stranger learns it with him."

## What The Bottom Is

He stood the afternoon at the bottom with the anger still in him, watching people go past.

There were people in that city who could not pay anything and had nowhere free to go, and he had not known that until that afternoon, and they were already finding the four feet of pavement at the bottom of that court without being told. He watched a woman stop at the mouth with empty hands and look up, and then look at him, and then look away, and he understood that she had somewhere to stand because he was standing there, even if she never knocked.

That evening he carried the book down himself in the dark, four flights and about four hundred yards, with the back board ticking under his arm about every four seconds. His chest ached all the way to the flat file and back, and he stopped twice on the third court to get his breath. He felt old and spent and glad to be done, and he wanted nothing more than sleep and a morning without a decision in it.

But there was a decision unmade in him, and a practice already unmade, and both of them waited for the fifth of May.

What the step was actually for, he learned from people who had nowhere to go, and he learned it late and from the outside, and he never once learned it from himself. A place that cost nothing to stand in was already growing at his feet, the way moss grows on stone, and nobody had planted it. He knew only that nothing had happened when he was not there, and that nothing was the truest thing anyone had told him all year.

Four thousand four hundred and seven days. Less four thousand four hundred, the twenty-first of this one, is seven, and a man who was half an hour late on purpose found out that four flights worked without him and was angry about it with nowhere to put the anger.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is twenty-eight, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-eight of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven days, which are sixty-six months and seven days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

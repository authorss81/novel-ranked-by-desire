# The Morning Nobody Is Him

Four thousand five hundred and eighty was the twenty-first of this one and the twenty-eight of this one is day four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven, and four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven less four thousand five hundred and eighty, the twenty-first of this one, is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## Nine Feet

He had been standing at the foot of his own stair since the twenty-fourth of March because a man in a street had worked out that four flights of a third court is worth a small amount to a person who cannot manage four flights, and because the only thing that answers a price is a body on a step.

There were about four people at the bottom of that stair and all of them were within about nine feet of him.

They were not waiting for him. They were standing in the four feet of pavement in the way people stand at a stop when the thing they are waiting for is coming, and one of them was saying the three lines out loud, at the length, to another one, in the ordinary voice, and the other one was listening with her head slightly over on one side.

Then the second one said them back to check, and got them right.

He said them out loud himself from his own second step from the bottom, because that is what he has done every working evening since November, and none of the four turned round.

## A Court With Nothing Going On In It

He looked for the rest of that morning for anybody paying anybody.

That was the arrangement he had set out loud with the price before he had done it once, and it had worked, and the cost of it was about four hundred yards of distance gone and a door that cannot be knocked on without walking past a man and every carrier's back visible. He had expected the man in the street to still be standing somewhere with somebody to take up.

He stood there until about eleven with his hands behind his back and watched people go into that arch and up four flights, all morning, without a break, and not one of them went with another person and not one of them stopped anywhere on the way.

Four feet of pavement will say three lines faster than four flights of stair will carry one person up them. That is all it took. The man in the street was still out there somewhere with a price for a walk, and he was standing in a court with nothing going on in it, and the whole of the difference between the two was that about four people had worked it out between themselves for nothing.

He was not angry about it. He found out later, going up the stairs, that he had expected to be.

## A Man Who Looks Up And Goes Back Down

At about eleven a man came up the arch and stopped.

He was carrying a bundle and he was in no hurry, and he stood about four feet off the wall about the height of a man and looked up the four flights to the door at the top, and he looked at it for about as long as it takes to read three lines off a card from the bottom of a court.

Then he turned round and went back down the arch.

Lin Hao watched him go out under the arch and into the street and he did not follow him and he did not say anything, and the thing he wanted was not the man's face and it was not his name.

It was the reason. A man came up that arch in the morning, looked at four flights, and decided they were not worth it, and that decision was made somewhere he could not see, and by the time the man reached the street it had been made by about nine other people he would never meet.

He stood there and worked out what he wanted to do about it, and what he wanted to do about it was to ask, and asking would have been a question, and a question is a thing this office answers, and this office answers a question by sending somebody up four flights.

The man had just decided not to go up four flights.

He worked out the second thing as well, standing on his own step in the middle of the morning with the light coming off the wall above his own door earlier every week. In March he had put a body there to stop a man charging money for a walk. In October he had handed the saying of those three lines to three other people.

The two things have nothing to do with each other and they have been joined at that stair ever since, and he joined them.

## At About One, At The Top Of His Own Room

The clerk told him at about one with the door standing open, and she told him because she had agreed on the twenty-fourth of September in front of two other people that if one of the three of them did it differently then the other two were to say so at the time.

"A woman came up at about half past ten," said Wenna Raut. "She wanted a thing about a piece of paper she has had for eleven years and she has brought it to us because somebody in a lane told her this office reads them."

"What did you say to her."

"I said I could not tell her what was on it," said the clerk, "and I said that out loud and to her face and while she was standing there, and she thanked me and went down."

"Is that what I would have said."

"No," she said. "You would have read it. You have read it four hundred times. I have never seen it and I told her the truth about that inside about nine seconds and she went down glad, and she was glad because she had been told by a person and not by a building."

He stood at the end of that table and found that he had known this was coming since the twenty-first of October and that knowing it had not taken any of it out.

## What He Said On The Twenty-First

He said it on the twenty-first, with a room behind him and the door standing open onto the landing and two people in front of him, before he had done it once.

About nine answers a day would now be given by people who had not been in that room for years. One of them had never opened that book and did not intend to. One of them had carried a strap and had never read a row to anybody in his life.

And in about nine days one of them would say something to a stranger that he would not have said, and it would be better than what he would have said, and he would not be able to hear it and neither would they.

It has been seven days.

He said it in his own head at the top of his own room and then he said it out loud, because that is how he says things.

"Seven," he said.

"Say the rest of it," said the clerk.

"It has been seven and not nine, and you were better at it in nine seconds than I have ever been at it in a morning, and I am not going to be able to unhear it, and I said in October that I would not, and I was right about that too."

"Would you have gone on doing it yourself for another year."

"Yes."

"So now there are two of them," said Wenna Raut, "and one of them cannot read, and the one who can read is going to carry the thing down four flights at half past five in the dark for the rest of his life, and neither of those two things is what you were doing in March."

## At Half Past Five In His Own Court

He went down and said them to an empty court, because the light has been off the wall above his own door for about a month now and it is not coming back before the spring, and because he has done it every working evening since November and there was nothing about this evening that was different from any other evening except that he had just been told, in a room with the door open, by the person he had handed it to, that it was better.

The court was empty and he said them anyway.

He said them at the length and at the pause he has used since November, and he listened to them go out into a court with nobody in it, and they sounded exactly the way they had sounded every working evening for a year, and there was not one thing in the sound of them that had changed and the entire arrangement underneath them had.

A man came up the arch while he was standing on his own second step, and stopped when he saw him, and then went on down the arch without slowing, and Lin Hao did not say anything to him and did not go after him.

That was the second man in one day who came up that arch and went back down it.

He had no way of finding out why either of them had done it, and the only instrument in this city that could put a question to a man in a court was four flights of stair and a person at the top of them, and a man who turns round in a court is a man who has just decided that the stairs are not worth it.

Four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven days. Less four thousand five hundred and eighty, the twenty-first of this one, is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven.

A man stood at the foot of his own stair for a morning to be the answer to somebody who came up those four flights because of him. About four people at that stair said three lines to each other and none of them was him. Two men came up the arch and went back down it, and nothing in this city can put a question to a man who turns his back.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is twenty-eight, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-eight of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven days, which are seventy-two months and seven days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

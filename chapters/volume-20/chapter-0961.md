# Hands That Are Not Mine

Four thousand three hundred and seventy-two was the twenty-third of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, and four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine less four thousand three hundred and seventy-two, the twenty-third of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-three is seven by the month count.

## The Six Lines

He went up four flights at a quarter to ten with the two carriers ahead of him and the strap between them, and the book went onto the table in the ordinary way and the door stood open onto the landing. Wenna Raut had the standing sheet out from under the weight and the six lines at the foot of it uppermost, because that was the order they were read in and the order did not change because it was a quarter-end.

She read them in the voice that office reads everything in, steady and without hurry, with the person it was about in the room and the person who had asked in the room. The room was full of people waiting to be answered and the light through the high window lay across the table in a pale bar. He stood at the end of the table with his hands behind him and listened the way he always listened, with his weight off his bad side.

The first line laid down how an answer went in writing and the second laid down that both question and answer went in the words used. The third laid down that nothing was taken from a person and the fourth laid down how every written answer was carried by hand.

The fifth laid down the fold with the flap gummed shut and the reading aloud with both people in the room, and the sixth laid down that an answer read once with different people in it could not be read again.

When she had finished he said that was the whole of it and she said it was, and the book was opened for the day. He wanted to get through the quarter-end without the rail breaking anything, and he wanted it the way a man wants a landing to hold when he puts his foot on it.

The six lines were still in the air and he wanted to be out of that room, four flights and a court below him, where the morning belonged to nobody and therefore to him.

"You will be down there by ten," said Wenna Raut, without looking up from the page.

"I will be down there by ten," said Lin Hao. "And the book stays here and the door stays open and nothing about today changes the order of anything."

## Half Past Two In That Passage

He walked the four hundred yards at about half past two with his coat buttoned and his plate covered, because a white plate in a passage at midday makes people stand straighter than they need to. The passage off Cordwain was full of midday traffic and the counter under the window had a queue three deep at the rail. The quarterly sheet lay face up on the rail where any person standing at the counter could read it, with the caption under the figure saying what it was.

He did not go up to the counter. He stood at the far side with his shoulder against the wall and waited, because the man behind it was working and a man who interrupts work in order to watch it has not understood what work is. About nine strangers moved through in the time he stood there, reading the sheet and asking for directions and going off with three lines they did not have when they came in.

At about twenty past three the press thinned and the counterman came round the end of the counter and stood with his back to the frame. He looked at Lin Hao once and then looked at the sheet, and Lin Hao understood that something was going to be said that had been held for a morning.

"You have come for the head," said the counterman.

"I have come to hear it where it is said," said Lin Hao.

The counterman put his hand on the rail beside the sheet and did not touch the paper. He raised his voice enough that the strangers still in the passage could hear him, the way he raised it every quarter when the sheet was due to come down.

"Four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand eight hundred and eighty, which is thirty-two times ninety," said the counterman. "Thirty-three sheets and thirty-two quarters, differing by one."

The strangers near the rail turned their heads. One woman with a basket stopped with her mouth half open and then shut it again. A carter leaned his weight off one foot and onto the other and kept looking at the figure as if it might move.

"It is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them," said the counterman, in the same level voice. "It is not money and it is not people and it is not any person who has come through a door. The caption under it has said so since the third of June of the ninth year, and it will say so when this sheet comes down."

He lifted his eyes to the rail and then to the clock on the wall behind him.

"The forty-eighth sheet comes down at about four this afternoon," said the counterman. "And the forty-ninth goes up on the same two nails carrying no new line. That is the whole of what a quarter-end does to a rail."

Lin Hao felt the flatness of relief go through him, the kind that arrives when a weight is lifted by other hands. He had carried that head in his own mouth four times a year for nine years, and hearing it in another man's mouth with strangers listening made it smaller and cleaner than it ever was in that room. He wanted to thank the man and did not, because he had learned what thanking did to a man who was paid nothing.

"You will be there when it comes down," said Lin Hao.

"I will be here when it comes down," said the counterman. "And you will not, because you have a stair to stand at, and that is where you are wanted."

## The Seventh Morning At The Bottom

He was at the bottom of his own stair by ten, in the light, with his back to the wall about the height of a man. The stair rose four flights above a third court to a door with a card on it, and the card carried the street and the door and the hours and not one name.

From a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, that was where he stood, out of his three days off, and today was the seventh morning of it.

People went past him all day. Some looked at him and some did not, and most of them looked once and then looked away, the way people look at a porter who is part of the furniture of a court. A woman with two children asked him whether the door was open and he said it was and gave her the hours. A carter asked him for the street and he gave him the street and the door with it, from memory, in the ordinary voice.

What he had expected, in the week before, was that being looked at would feel like something. He had spent nine years arranging his mornings so that no stranger could look at him for longer than it took to go up four flights, and he had imagined the first week at the bottom as a week of being stared through.

What he found on the seventh morning was that being looked at did not feel like anything at all, and that was the reversal he carried up with him at midday when he went to drink water.

He climbed the four flights at about one with a stiffness in his chest that made him stop on the second landing. The door stood open and the book lay on the table and Wenna Raut looked up from a row she was entering. The two carriers were on their stools by the window with their straps coiled at their feet.

"How is the bottom," said Wenna Raut.

"It is a floor," said Lin Hao. "I thought it would be a stage and it is a floor. People walk across it and it holds them and that is the whole of it."

"And the quarter-end."

"The forty-eighth comes down at about four and the forty-ninth goes up on the same two nails," said Lin Hao. "I heard the head said at a counter under a window with strangers in the passage, and it was a count of paper and nothing else, and I am glad I was there to hear it said by hands that are not mine."

She studied him for a moment with her pen an inch off the page.

"You look tired," she said.

"I am tired in the way a man is tired when he has spent a fortnight being looked at and has found out it costs him nothing," said Lin Hao. "And I do not trust the tiredness, because a thing that costs nothing on the seventh morning may send its bill in the fourth week."

He went back down before half past one and stood the afternoon out in a thin rain that came and went. The light went off that street at about ten past four and the card up four flights could not be read after it, and he said the three lines twice to people who asked for them, slowly, from memory.

At about four, four hundred yards off, a sheet came down off a rail and another went up, and he was not there to see it and had decided before midday that he would not go and watch.

## Ten To Five In The Dark

At about ten to five the court emptied and the stair behind him was dark from the second landing up. There was no person on the step beside him and no person at the mouth of the court, and the wall beside him was cold through his coat.

He said the three lines out loud once, into the empty stair, from memory, in the ordinary voice, the way he had said them on the night of the twenty-third with his hands empty.

Halfway through the second line he heard himself and stopped, surprised at his own mouth. He had started saying them when there was no person there to hear them, and he had not decided to do it and had not noticed himself starting. It was not a practice and it was not a rule, because it concerned no person but him, and he let it stand the way a man lets a habit stand when he is too tired to pull it up.

He climbed the four flights at half past five by himself in the dark and got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards to the flat file. The back board of the book ticked under his arm about every four seconds, with a line of daylight between the two halves of the board that he could feel rather than see.

He put it down in the flat file and came back, and on the way back he stopped again at the bottom of his own stair and looked up into the dark where the door was.

Tomorrow was the seventh of April and a form would go up on a rail four hundred yards off, and the morning would be only a morning, with no quarter in it. He felt a flicker of anger at himself for the comfort he had taken in the head being said by another man, because comfort was a thing he had not earned and did not trust.

What he wanted, standing there with the book off his hands, was to find out whether a man at a stair changed the number of people who used it, and he understood as he turned away that he had no way of finding out and that the not-finding-out was going to be the arrangement.

Four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine days. Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-two, the twenty-third of this one, is seven, and a man who had stood seven mornings at the foot of his own stair heard the quarter-end said at a counter by hands that were not his and said his own three lines to an empty stair at ten to five.

*Less four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is ninety, and the forty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and it comes down at about four this afternoon and the forty-ninth goes up on the same two nails.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and fifty-nine days, which are sixty-five months and nine days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

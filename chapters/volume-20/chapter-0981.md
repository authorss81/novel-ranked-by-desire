# The First Morning Without

Four thousand five hundred and eleven was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand five hundred and eighteen, and four thousand five hundred and eighteen less four thousand five hundred and eleven, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## A Quarter To Ten In His Own Court

The court at a quarter to ten had the light still on the wall above his own door and not yet round onto the far side, which it does not come round to until nearer eleven, and he stood at the foot of his own stair with his back to the low wall and said the three lines out loud to whoever wanted them.

Nobody wanted them. There was a man going across the court with a sack on his shoulder and he did not stop and he did not slow down, and Lin Hao said them anyway, because the hours on that door run from a quarter to ten and not one minute of them is his to shorten.

Then he went up the four flights.

## The Room With The Table On It

The door at the top stood open on its landing, as it stands every working morning, and inside it there was the room, and the table, and two chairs, and a drawer with a man's name under the instruction that keeps it shut, and a coat on a hook by the door that is not his coat and has not been anybody's coat since the winter before last.

On the table there was a sheet with a date on the top of it and an hour under the date and a pen lying across the bottom edge, and it lay there all morning the way paper lies when there is one piece of it.

Wenna Raut was at the far end of the table with her sleeves turned back. The room was not empty, and it filled and emptied and filled again through the whole morning the way it does.

## What The Room Did Without The Book

The first hour tells you the rest. He took the near end of the table and stood at it with his hands behind his back and did not sit down, and watched her work, and what she did was answer.

People came up the four flights. They were answered. They went down. Other people came up the four flights. They were answered.

That is all it is on a good morning and that is all it was on this one, and the thing he had to stand at the end of that table and get used to was that she was not slow. She was not slow in the way a person is slow when they are about to be wrong. She answered in the voice that office reads everything in and she answered quickly, and she did not go and get anything, because there was nothing to go and get.

About the middle of the morning a man brought a piece of paper up with him and asked her what she had told him about it in June, and she said a thing about it in about nine seconds that made him sit down on the end of a chair, and then he got up and said it back to himself twice on the way down.

## Asking Her Where It Came From

At about twelve he asked her what she had said to him.

"You were standing there."

"I was standing there," said Lin Hao. "I want to know where you got it."

"Out of my head."

"That is the answer to a question about how you knew. That is not an answer to how you knew."

She put her pen down on the line and squared it against the edge of the table with two fingers, which she does when a row is finished, and left it there, which she does not do.

"I was there," said Wenna Raut. "That is the whole of it and there is no second half of it. I have been at this table nine years and I have been at the other side of it for most of those, and when a person comes up those four flights with a piece of paper in their hand, then somebody in this room answered them, and nearly always it was me."

"You did not look."

"There was nothing to look at," said Lin Hao.

She looked at him for a while over the top of a sheet that had a date on it.

"Right," she said. "Say the next part."

"There is no next part."

"There is always a next part with you and it is always the part that costs something," said Wenna Raut. "Go and stand at the bottom of your stair."

## One O'Clock And The Court

He went down the four flights at one and stood in his own court for about an hour in the light, and the court did what it does on a working morning, and at about two he went up again.

She was on the landing.

He knew her before she spoke, because he had been about ten feet off her in the dark on the twelfth of this month standing in a street four hundred yards away with his hands in his pockets, and a cart going round a corner had taken the sound away.

## The Woman Who Came Back Up

"You said it was free."

"I said nobody will ever be charged for being told where that door is," said Lin Hao, "and I said it at the foot of this stair with the court standing behind me, and it is still true."

"I know it is true. I have said it to two people this week because they asked me at a market." She had her hand on the rail. "I came up on the fourteenth. I stood on this landing and I did not go in because I did not have a question, and I want you to know that I knew that on the fourteenth and I came anyway."

"And today?"

"And today I have a question and it is not about me." She said it evenly. "My sister came in June. She came up those four flights and she was answered, and she has not been back since, and she will not tell me what she asked or what she was told or why she went down and did not come up them again. She lives two streets off this court and I have been trying to find out what happened to her on a stair."

Lin Hao stood on the landing of his own office and found that he had nothing.

He had been in that room nine years. He had put a date and an hour against every row in it. He had carried the only copy of it four hundred yards in daylight on the twelfth of this month and signed for it at a tailboard, and he did not know what that office had said to a woman in June, and there was nothing in this city he could go and look at.

"I cannot find out for you," he said.

"I know you cannot. That is why I came."

"Then why did you come."

The woman looked at him for long enough that somebody went up the stair behind her and had to wait.

"Because I wanted to see the room," she said. "That is all. My sister will not talk about it and there is a woman at that door who has not got a thing to be frightened of, and I thought that if anybody in this city was going to say something true about a stair it would be the two of you."

## Going Back Up

He went up the four flights behind her and she did not come in and he did not ask her to and she went down them again and out into the street, and he stood on the landing for a minute with his hand on a rail that is not the rail.

Then he went into the room and there were people in it and the sheet was still on the table and the pen was still lying across the bottom edge of it.

"Wenna Raut."

"Ask it."

"What are the practices."

She put her pen down again.

"Why."

"Because I have just stood on my own landing and been told that a woman has been carrying something about this office for two months and cannot put it down, and I stood there and could not tell her one thing about what we have said to people, and not because of any rule." He heard himself and let the rest of it come.

"I have run that table for nine years and I cannot say the rules out loud. I found out this morning that I do not know them well enough to say them."

"That is the first sensible thing you have said to me since the twelfth."

"Then say them to me. All of them. And I will tell you the ones I know and you will tell me the ones you know, and we will find out which ones neither of us has."

She looked at the room, and at the four flights of stair through the open door, and at the light coming off the landing.

"Half past five," said Wenna Raut. "Not in the morning. In the morning there are people in this room and anything you say goes to whoever is standing nearest the door, and I am not having the twelve of them said out loud in front of a division return."

"That was not the reason."

"It was a reason," she said. "The other one is that I have never said them out loud to another person in nine years and I would like to know whether I can."

Four thousand five hundred and eighteen days. Less four thousand five hundred and eleven, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven, and a man stood at the top of four flights of stair and told a woman to say out loud the whole of what he had been running for nine years, and the only person who had ever asked him for it had been a woman on a landing who wanted to know what happened to her sister.

*Less four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is forty-nine, and the fiftieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and ninety-eight days, which are sixty-nine months and twenty-eight days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

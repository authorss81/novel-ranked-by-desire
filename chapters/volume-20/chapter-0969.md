# The Debt With Nowhere To Go

Four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight was the nineteenth of this one and the twenty-sixth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and thirty-five, and four thousand four hundred and thirty-five less four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight, the nineteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## The Asking

She came up four flights at about eleven with her coat buttoned and her hands empty, and she waited until the room was clear before she spoke. The book lay on the table and the door stood open and Wenna Raut had her pen ready, and Lin Hao stood at the end of the table in his white plate with his hands behind him.

He wanted to answer her in the only way he was allowed to answer, and he wanted the whole of it said cleanly in one go. Looking at her steady face he felt a gladness so heavy it sat behind his eyes, because somebody had climbed four flights to offer instead of to take, and underneath it was dread, because he already knew the answer.

"I have come to ask whether I may pay you," she said. "Not the office. You. For saying those three lines at the bottom of that stair every morning, in all weathers, to any person who asks. I have been up twice and I have heard you give them true, and I have stood with the others on the pavement, and I want to give something for it."

The room was quiet. Wenna Raut kept her pen still, an inch off the page, and did not look up. The two carriers by the window stopped handling their straps.

He felt the offer go through him like warmth after cold. No person had offered him anything all year but trouble and questions, and here was a woman offering the only thing any person had offered him, plainly and without guile.

What he wanted, with his throat tight, was to take it, and he could not. He thought of his empty hands at half past five and the ticking book under his arm, and how a small amount would have bought oil for the winter and leather for his shoes. He put the thought aside the way a man puts aside a coat that does not fit, because fitting was not the question.

## The Answer Has To Be No

"The answer has to be no," said Lin Hao, in the ordinary voice. "Not because I do not need it, and not because what you offer is not worth having. It is worth having, and I need it the way any man who works four nights and five mornings needs rest. And not because of any rule, because no rule covers a mouth."

She nodded slowly, waiting, and he saw that she would hear him out, which made it harder and better both. The carriers by the window watched without staring, the way people watch a thing they know is important and know is not theirs.

"The moment a person may pay me for saying those three lines, the three lines belong to whoever pays," said Lin Hao. "I have given them away at a counter for nothing for about two years, slowly, from memory, in the dark. The man behind that counter has given them away for nine years for nothing, every day, to strangers."

"I cannot take a price off them without taking the giving away back off the man who is paid nothing, and I will not do that. If I take from you today, tomorrow I can charge, and the day after I must, and the free is gone not only from me but from him."

She looked down at her hands and then up at him, and he saw that she understood, though understanding cost her.

"Then I will give to the office," she said.

"You cannot give to the office for this, either," said Lin Hao, gently. "Because the office has two refusals in that book and there will not be a third, and that was said on the floor of a lane in August and has not been improved on since. A refusal on paper is closed to us now, and what is left is the only kind this office has left."

Wenna Raut looked up then, with her pen still off the page.

"Does anything go in," said Wenna Raut.

"Nothing goes in," said Lin Hao. "I will not write it and I will not enter it, and I will not put a line in that book about a woman being refused money, and I will not give her a paper. A thing refused out loud in a room is the only kind of refusal this office has left, and that is a cost, and the cost is that I cannot prove in about four years that I ever said it."

## Said Where It Is Spent

He turned back to the woman, who stood with her hands folded, listening with the patience of a person who has climbed four flights to be told no and intends to carry the no home whole. The light through the high window lay across her shoulders, and he saw dust in it, and he thought how young she was to be offering to pay for another person's trouble.

"I am saying no out loud, here, with the door open and you in the room," said Lin Hao. "And I am saying what I will not do, so that the no has edges. I will not write it and I will not enter it."

"The clerk has asked and I have answered, and nothing goes in that book. That is the whole of what the office can do for you today, and it is less than you deserve. I wish it were more, and wishing does not make it more, and I have learned this year to say the less without dressing it."

She was quiet for about four seconds, and he felt the silence as a weight he had chosen to carry. The carriers shifted on their stools, and Wenna Raut turned a page without writing on it, giving the quiet somewhere to go.

"I came to pay a debt," she said at last. "You stand at the bottom in rain and I stand beside you for nothing, and I wanted the standing to be even between us. You are telling me it cannot be even, and that my debt has nowhere to go."

"It has somewhere to go," said Lin Hao. "It goes to the next stranger who comes into that court lost, and you tell him the street true, badly or well, for nothing. That is where my debt went nine years ago, to a counter under a window, and it has never come back, and I am glad of it for the first time in nine years."

She stared at him, and then she laughed, short and surprised, with tears behind it.

"That is a hard answer," she said. "And it is a kind one, and I did not expect the two to arrive together."

"I did not expect them to, either," said Lin Hao. "And being unable to take what you offer is the first thing in nine years I have been glad of, because it proves the giving is still free. If I could take it, the free would be gone, and the counterman would be the first man to have sold it, and the man in the street would be the second, and I would be the third."

She nodded once, as if that closed the account in a book no person would ever see. She did not complain and she did not press him, and he was grateful to her for the dignity of her listening.

The grief he was carrying was not for himself and it was not for her. It was for the paper he could not give her, and under it there was a gladness in him he had not expected, for a room that could hold a no without writing it down.

He thought of the two entries in that book that were neither question nor answer, one in the clerk's own words and one in three sentences with no name, and how both had been written to close a door that paper could close. This door paper could not close, and the room knew it.

"Will you walk me down," she said. "I came up alone and I would like to go down with company, if the office allows it."

"I will walk you down," said Lin Hao.

He went down four flights with her in the light, past the second landing where the light fell in bars, to the bottom where the standing ones waited along the wall. She took her place among them without hesitation, with her back to the far wall, and nodded to the woman beside her. He stood at his own place with his shoulder to the wall and watched her become one of the floor, and he felt the loss and the gain of it together.

At half past five he got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards in the dark. Wenna Raut entered nothing about the morning, because a woman refused money out loud was not a question and not an answer.

He came down the four flights behind the carriers and stopped in the court, and the standing ones made room for him along the wall without being asked, the way you make room for a man who has been away from a place. She was among them already, with her back to the far wall and her hood down, and she nodded at him and went on telling a stranger the street.

The practice sheet waited in the room above with a date eleven days in the past on it, the twenty-third of May, and four feet of pavement waited below for a line that would make him unnecessary. He did not know the words yet, but he knew the shape of them, the way a man knows the shape of a stair in the dark.

Four thousand four hundred and thirty-five days. Less four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight, the nineteenth of this one, is seven, and a woman who came four flights to pay was refused out loud with the door open and nothing went in any book.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is fifty-six, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-six of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and fifteen days, which are sixty-seven months and five days, and the sixty-seventh month is named here once and is not an event, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

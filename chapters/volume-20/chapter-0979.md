# Entering Nothing At All

The twenty-eighth of last month and the fifth of this one is day four thousand five hundred and four, and four thousand five hundred and four less four thousand four hundred and ninety-seven, the twenty-eighth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Asking A Woman To Do It From Her Head

He got there at a quarter to ten and asked her for a pen and then put it back down again when she gave it to him, which confused her.

"Read me a date," he said.

Wenna Raut put her hand into the book without looking, which she can do, and read a date and an hour off a row, and there is no figure and no name against any row in that book and has not been since before he came to that table.

"Now ask me what went in against it," she said.

He did not want to do it. He had been up that morning telling himself that this was a thing he had a right to know about the state of an office he had broken, and standing there with his hand on a date that he was about to make her reconstruct out of her own head, he found that it was not a thing he had a right to do and was doing it anyway.

"What went in against that."

Wenna Raut said it.

She said it in about nine seconds, in the voice that office reads everything in, and it was the words that had been used, and it was long, and it had a person in it, and she did not say the person's name because there is not one in the book to say.

Then she waited, because she was waiting for him to go and check it.

Lin Hao stood at the other side of that table with his hands on the wood and did not go and check it.

## Not Checking It Was The Hard Part

"You have not looked," she said.

"I have not looked."

"That is the first time in nine years I have given you a page and had you not look at it," said Wenna Raut. "And I am not going to tell you that it was wrong, and I am not going to tell you that I was right, because I have been in this room a long time and I know what a book is for."

"How long."

"About four seconds to find it and about nine to say it," she said. "And I knew it was right while I was saying it, and you are going to sit there and tell me that means something, and it does not mean anything at all. It means my head is good at that book. My head has had nine years at that book. That is not a fact about the office, it is a fact about me."

"Give me another, then."

"Lin Hao."

"Give me another."

She gave him another one, from another part of the book, and she was slower on that one and had to stop in the middle and start again, and she got it right.

Then she shut the book, which she does not do, and put her two fingers on the cover, and looked at him across the room with about a dozen people in it and the door open on the landing.

"Now sit down," she said. "Because I am going to say something and I want to be able to say it once."

## What He Said Out Loud With The Door Open

He said it standing up, because he had promised himself he would not say anything like it sitting down.

"For nine days there is no book in this room," he said. "Not a closed one and not an open one. And that means nothing is entered for nine days, and it means that every row that comes back into the book afterwards is a row that was never entered, and it means that anybody who comes up those four flights in those nine days will never know that anything was written about them in this room at all, including the person who writes it."

The room did what it does.

"That is all of it," he said. "I am not going to put a figure on any part of it, and I want you to hear me say that out loud while the door is open, because I could put a figure on it and I could put one on it truthfully, and it would be the first figure this office has ever printed about what passes through it, and it would be a figure about people, and I am not going to have it."

"You have thought about it for a week."

"I have thought about it for a week and a bit," said Lin Hao, "and the reason I am not going to is not the reason you think it is."

"Then tell me the reason."

"Because the moment there is a figure there is a quarter," he said, "and the moment there is a quarter there is a return, and the return comes back with a box on it, and you were there when I stood next to that box on the seventh. So it is not that a figure is wrong. It is that a figure is the beginning of a sentence, and I have spent nine years not being the end of one."

## The Finding

"That is a good sentence," said Wenna Raut.

"It is the best one I have got and it does not answer your objection and it never will."

"I know it does not answer my objection," she said. "Here is what it does instead, and I want you to have it on the seventh and not on the twelfth, because on the twelfth you will be too busy. This office will not find out what the nine days cost."

"Not the answers given, not the people turned away, not the paper that went into the drawer of the person it was about. Not any part of it. Because to find out what a thing costs you have to know what it was, and you have spent nine years arranging not to know."

"And that arrangement has now extended itself into the only nine days this office has ever agreed to in advance."

He stood in the middle of that room and let it arrive, because there was nowhere in it for it to go.

"I have just arranged to lose nine days of the only record I have," she said, "in a way that neither of us will ever be able to put a figure on, or a word on, or a check against. And I want you to understand that I think that is very probably the correct thing to do and that I will go on thinking so at half past ten at night for the rest of my life, and that I will still be here in nine days."

## Not Writing It Down

He did not write it down.

He had said it out loud with the door open, which is where he says things, and the practice sheet was on the desk beside the standing sheet, and he put both hands behind his back.

"Is that a practice," said Wenna Raut. "Because if that is a practice I will write it and put the date on it, and if it is not a practice then it is a thing that has been said in a room and that is all it is."

"It is not a practice."

"Then it is not written down anywhere at all."

"That is correct."

"That is the first thing you have told this room in nine years that is only in your head," said Wenna Raut, "and it is the last week you will have a book to tell it to. If you had written it on that sheet in the last nine years I would have taken it and read it out and put the date at the top."

"You have a rule that will not let you do that, and on the first of those mornings the rule is going to be the only thing standing between that fact and a full room, and there will not be one sheet anywhere in this building that says what this office is doing."

He had not thought about it in those terms and it was the true shape of it.

"Then I will say it in the morning," he said.

"You cannot say it in the morning. You can say it once in a court and it belongs to the court, and you spent it at the bottom of that stair in June. You are going into nine days where the only people who are allowed to say anything are the three of us, and if one of us says it out loud to a stranger then it is said to that stranger and not to this room."

## At Ten To Five In August

He came down the four flights at half past five in the ordinary way and the book went down with him and the wire round the buckle was twisted twice in his own pocket, and out in the third court the light was still on the wall above his own door, thin and yellow and going, and it would be gone by the end of September and that was in neither the card nor any practice.

At ten to five he stood at the foot of his own stair with his back against the low wall and no coat round his hands because he had carried the book down with the book and put it in the flat file and come back with nothing.

He said the three lines out loud to the court.

Then he said them again, slower, and he heard his own mouth do the thing it does with the second line where the corners have gone off the card, and he understood that in nine days he would be saying them to a court with not a soul in it, or to a court with a stranger in it who did not know that was the first day, and that no person would ever know which.

The twelfth was a week off, and the cart would be there at half past five, and he had thought about it every morning for a fortnight and had not once got as far as thinking about the ninth day.

Four thousand five hundred and four days. Less four thousand four hundred and ninety-seven, the twenty-eighth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven, and a man asked a woman to say a page out of her own head twice and did not look at it either time and found out that the rehearsal of a disaster is a morning like any other.

*Less four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is thirty-five, and the fiftieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and eighty-four days, which are sixty-nine months and fourteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

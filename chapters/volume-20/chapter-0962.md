# Two Hands At A Rail

Four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine was the thirtieth of this one and the seventh of the next one is day four thousand three hundred and eighty-six, and four thousand three hundred and eighty-six less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of this one, is seven, and the month ended in between and there is nothing in it to take out.

## The First Ordinary Morning

He was at the bottom of that stair by a quarter to ten with his shoulder to the wall and his hands empty. The court was cool and the light lay in a thin bar across the flags, and four flights above him the door stood open with the card on it. The street and the door and the hours were on the card and not one name, and the hours ran from a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven.

He had been there seven mornings and today was the eighth, and the quarter was gone off the rail four hundred yards away and a form had gone up that morning with nothing on it worth naming. He wanted to find out what a body at the foot of a stair actually did, before he defended it to any person including himself. What he felt, with the morning still clean in front of him, was a hard curiosity that had nowhere kind to go.

Wenna Raut came down at about ten past ten with a folded slip for a carrier and stopped at the second step to look at him.

"You are in the way of the stair," she said.

"I am the stair today," said Lin Hao. "Go round me and tell me afterwards whether it cost you anything."

She went round him with the slip held clear of his coat and went on up the court. He watched her go and felt the lightness of a man who has made a joke he does not believe.

## What A Body Cannot Do

By eleven he had learned the shape of the morning. People came into the court in a thin stream, and those who knew the stair went past him to the foot of it and started up without breaking stride. Those who did not know it stopped at the mouth and looked up, and then they saw him, and then they had a choice they had not expected to have.

A woman with a string bag stopped four feet off and looked from him to the stair and back to him. He gave her the street and the door and the hours in the ordinary voice, from memory, the way he gave it to any person who asked. She thanked him with her eyes and went up, and he understood that she had used the man and not the door, and that she had not known she had a choice until she had used it.

That was the whole of the first hour, repeated with small changes. The card was still on the door and the door was still open from a quarter to ten, and a person who wanted a door no man stood in front of could knock on that door until half past five. The man who stood at the bottom could not be in the fourth flight at the same time, and that was not a defect. That was what a body was.

He felt the truth of it come home in his knees by midday. He had spent a fortnight imagining that standing still was a kind of wall, and a wall was in two places at once because it surrounded something.

A man was not a wall. A man was in one court and not in another, and the door worked in the court he was not in exactly as it worked in the court he was in.

At about one he climbed the four flights to drink water, and his chest complained on the third landing the way it always complained when he did the stair too fast. The door stood open and the book lay on the table and the clerk looked up.

"How is the bottom," said Wenna Raut.

"It is one place," said Lin Hao. "And the door is another place. And I cannot be in both, and I have spent a week pretending I could."

## Two Courts

He ate standing up in the room with the door open, because going down to eat meant losing the step to whoever came while he was gone.

The two carriers brought the midday rows up and took the answered slips down, and their backs went past him twice each, loud on the stair. He watched the woman with the bad back take the third flight without touching the rail, counting the noise in her head the way she had counted it for nine years, and he felt a pang of shame that had no paper to go on.

In the afternoon a woman came who would have knocked. He knew her kind by then, after eight mornings, the way a porter knows the difference between a person who wants directions and a person who wants permission. She stood at the mouth of the court for about nine seconds and looked at the stair and then at him, and he saw her decide to come to him first because he was nearer than the door.

He gave her the three lines and she listened with her head on one side. Then she asked whether she should go up, and he said the door was open until half past five and the book was on the table. She went up, and he watched her go with a heaviness behind his ribs that he recognised as the cost arriving.

What he understood, standing there with the afternoon light going thin, was that every person who had ever wanted a door to knock on had now got a man in the way of it.

Somebody who wanted a door to knock on had a man in the way of it now, and a person who would rather go without is not being turned away anywhere. She is choosing something else: a lane with no steps in it, a longer way round, a morning she does not spend on a stranger's question. He had put a body between that woman and a door and had gone on calling it a gift.

He had moved being findable about four hundred yards without being asked whether he was entitled to, and being findable and being usable were different prices. That was the finding of the second week, and it was worse than the finding of the first, because the first had been about his own comfort and this one was about other people.

## Said Out Loud In Two Places

He said it out loud on the step at about four, to a carrier who stopped beside him with a bundle of answered slips.

"Every person who wanted a door has now got a man in the way of it," said Lin Hao. "Some of them will find that out on the step and some of them will go the long way round to another court and never come up these flights at all. I cannot tell you which is which and I am not going to stand here and find out. That is the second half of what this costs, and I am saying it here because it is spent here."

The carrier looked at him for a moment and then nodded and went on up the court. He did not write it down and had not asked to have it written down, and that was the point of saying it on a step.

At half past five he went up the four flights in the dark with the book under his arm and said it again in that room with the door open, with Wenna Raut at the table and her pen down.

"The same thing, in here, because it concerns this room," said Lin Hao. "A man at the bottom is a man in the way, and people will find that out on the flags outside and go elsewhere instead. I am not asking you to put that in anything."

"I am not putting it in anything," said Wenna Raut. "A body at the bottom of a stair is not a question and not an answer, and that book cannot take it. And you are right to say it twice, because a thing said once in a court belongs to the court."

He felt anger then, clean and cold, at himself and at the stair and at the week. He had given his three days off to a step that made some people turn away, and he had done it without asking the office and without asking the people. What he wanted, with the book heavy under his arm, was to move, to be up four flights where the door worked without him, and he could not move because the morning he had chosen required him to be still.

"You are angry," said Wenna Raut.

"I am angry at the arithmetic," said Lin Hao. "I thought standing there was an act of ending a price, and it is an act of moving it. The man with the coin walks people up for a small amount and I stand still for nothing, and between us we have made a lane where there was a door."

"And you will stand there tomorrow."

"And I will stand there tomorrow," said Lin Hao. "Because going away now would be a second price, and I have not finished paying the first."

He carried the book the four hundred yards in the dark with the back board ticking under his arm about every four seconds.

The form that had gone up that morning was face up on a rail four hundred yards off, with nothing on it that was an event, and he had not gone to look at it and did not need to. At the flat file he set the book down and stood for a moment with his hands empty, listening to the tick fade into the wood.

He came down the four flights last, after the carriers, and stood in the dark court with the book under his arm and the tick going out of it by inches. A back platform at the end of a line was waiting for him in the morning, and a woman who heard things at the far end of a carriage. The pull of it went through him like a hand on his coat, and he was glad to have somewhere to be that was not a step.

Four thousand three hundred and eighty-six days. Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of this one, is seven, and the month ended in between and there is nothing in it to take out, and a man who could not be in two courts said the cost of it out loud on a step and in a room and asked for nothing to be written down.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seven, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and sixty-six days, which are sixty-five months and sixteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

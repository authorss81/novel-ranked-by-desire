# The Last Asking

Four thousand six hundred and thirty-six was the seventeenth of this one and the twenty-fourth of this one is day four thousand six hundred and forty-three, and four thousand six hundred and forty-three less four thousand six hundred and thirty-six, the seventeenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## A Woman Up Four Flights With A Sheet

She came up those four flights at about eleven on the twenty-fourth of December with a sheet of paper in her hand and she was not a stranger who had read anything on a door, and she had a piece of printed paper with a date on the top of it and a box ruled at the bottom of it.

She said the name of a division of a hall and she said it the way people say the name of a hall they have been sent away from twice.

"The box at the foot of it wants an answer from the office at the top of that stair," she said, "and it does not ask what you do. It asks whether the man in the white plate still stands at the bottom of that stair and still says those three lines."

## What Is In The Box

He read it. There was a date on it, and a bracket, and the question written in under the bracket, and a ruled box at the foot of the page about two inches deep with nothing at all in it.

"You have come up four flights to ask me that."

"I have come up four flights to fill in a box," said the woman. "The rest of the page is somebody else's business. If I go back down those stairs with it empty then in about four days a clerk in a hall reads an empty box on a return and writes no service against the address, and that is not a word anybody at that hall has to mean. It is just what an empty box says."

"Who put that question in it."

"Somebody who has stood at the bottom of that stair twice," said the woman. "I am the one carrying it and I have not got anything else to tell you about it, and if you were hoping for a name I would rather you did not ask me for one on a stair."

## What He Would Not Say

He could not say yes, because he had not decided, and he had said out loud on the twenty-fourth of September, in front of two people, that a practice of this office is a thing one of three people does out loud and that a thing said once in a court belongs to the court.

He could not say no, because he had said in that same court on the twenty-ninth of June that there is no version of that afternoon in which he is the man who stops anybody, and that had been true then and it was truer now.

He could not write it, because there will not be a third written refusal and he has said so out loud twice in that room and is not going to be the man who makes one on the last working day of a year to be kind to a woman with a sheet.

He could not tell her the truth either, because the truth is that he has not decided, and a true thing said in a street in December becomes a decision about that street in about four months, in about nine other mouths, with nothing behind any of them.

"You have to put something in it," said the woman, not unkindly.

"I know."

"Then say it out loud to me and I will write it on this and take it down four flights and it will go into a hall, and that is how a form gets filled in."

"There are two things written on that sheet already."

"There are the address and the date."

"There are the address and the date and nothing else, and in about nine years of this office that has not happened once," said Lin Hao, "and you have done it this morning by walking up four flights and asking me a question that is about whether I turn up, and it is the best-worked form anybody has ever brought into this room."

"No," said Lin Hao. "A thing said to you on the twenty-fourth of December about a year that has not started is not a promise. It is a mood, and a mood cannot be entered in a box."

He put his thumb on the edge of that table.

"And if I said one, you would carry it down those stairs in a week, and somebody in that hall would read it in January as a thing this office had said about itself in December. It would not be that. It would be a thing a man said to a stranger on a landing because he did not want to be asked."

## What He Said To The Three Of Them

He said all of it in that room at about half past twelve with the door open on the landing, and he said it to the three of them and he said that he had been thinking about it since the seventeenth and had not decided and was not going to decide before the turn of the year.

Wenna Raut put her pen down.

"You have now said that out loud to three people in one room," she said. "In October you said something in a room and told us it could be said once. This one has been said twice, in a room, with the door open, and it cannot be said a third time to make it better."

"I know that it has been said twice."

"Do you know who has heard it twice."

"You and he and she," said Lin Hao, "and the man in the arch on the seventeenth has heard a different half of it and carried that down out of this court, and I am the only person in this city who knows that both of those things are true at once."

## What The Carrier Said About It

The carrier with the bad back was at the end of that table with her hand in the small of her back and she let the other two finish before she said anything, and she said it in four sentences.

"If you are going to do it then do it in a morning and not on the last working day of the year in a room with two people in it."

"That is not a decision."

"It is not a decision," she said. "It is what you should do when you make one, and you have not made it, and the reason it matters that you have not made it is that the three of us now know you are thinking about it."

"Say the rest of it."

"In about four years somebody in that hall will ask one of us what this office was, and one of us will say a sentence, and the other two will not be able to say whether you meant it that morning or not, and one of the three of us is going to have to stand in a room and not know what you decided, because you never decided, because you told us you were thinking about it on the twenty-fourth of December in a room with the door open."

## The Woman Goes Down With It Empty

She waited at the end of that table for about four minutes and then said that she was going to have to go down, because the return goes up in the morning and she had been told to bring it back.

"You are not going to write anything at all."

"I am going to write the address and the date because I have to write those," said the woman. "And I am going to leave the box the way it is, and I am going to say to the clerk who gives it back that the man at the top of the stair said no figure and gave no sentence, and that is what I will say, and it will be true."

"You will not get anything for it."

"I have been sent away from two halls with nothing in my hand and one of them told me to come back with a figure," she said. "I am going to go down those stairs with the box the way it is and I am going to be the first person in about nine years to walk out of here having been told in advance exactly what I was not getting."

## What The Clerk Said About The Page Itself

The woman had it flat on her hand the whole time and he had not looked at it again.

"The page is not yours," said Wenna Raut, "and there is nothing on it about your name, and there is nothing on it about this office at all except a street and a bracket. Nine years at the end of this table and that is the first sheet that has come up those four flights which we could hand straight back."

"Then it goes back."

"It goes back," said the clerk, "and it is the first return anybody has ever brought up here that we were glad to see the back of."

## At Twenty To Five He Said Them Anyway

He said the three lines at twenty to five to a court with a few people in it, at the length, from his own second step, because that is the hour and that is what he does.

He said them with a sheet going up four flights behind him with an empty box on it, and with about four months in front of him in which he would either have said them for the last time or he would not.

He had told three people that he had not decided, which is the same as having said it in a room and being unable to take it out of the room again.

Four thousand six hundred and forty-three days. Less four thousand six hundred and thirty-six, the seventeenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven.

A woman went down four flights with a box on a sheet that a hall will read as no service, and a man said at twenty to five that he had not decided, and the not deciding is now in a room with three people in it and in a lane with a man's chalk in it and in a street with a stranger in it, and there was nothing on any paper anywhere in this city that says he ever said it.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is eighty-four, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand two hundred and twenty-three days, which are seventy-four months and three days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Fiftieth Sheet

Four thousand four hundred and sixty-three was the twenty-fourth of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, and four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine less four thousand four hundred and sixty-three, the twenty-fourth of this one, is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## Getting A Man Down Four Floors

It took two days to arrange and both days were wasted on the arrangement.

A binder will not book a run on a book he has not opened, and the book did not come up, and could not come up, because a bound Register that has been lying in a flat file on a filing floor for nine years is not a thing anybody lifts onto a stair for a conversation.

So the binder had to come down.

He came at about eleven on the morning of the last working day of the quarter, down four flights and then about four hundred yards, and he came on foot because he would not be carried into a building he had not been asked into. He had a leather bag and a shaving in his hand and a folding rule he never used, and he went down that stair looking at the walls the way a man looks at stairs he is being asked to work near.

The flat file room is on a filing floor in another building and it has no window in it and has had none since the building was put up. It is lit by four lamps on a bar across the middle. There is a rail on the far wall with a quarter sheet on it, and a good many other things on shelves, and a room that has been warm and dry and completely unvisited by daylight for nine years.

The binder stood in the middle of it and looked up at the bar of light.

"You work in this," he said.

"My predecessor worked in this," said Lin Hao. "I have put a book into it at half past five five days in seven since the seventh year."

"Then you have not once looked at daylight in it." He put his bag on the floor. "That is a fault-finding room, and about half of them do not show at all in a bar."

"I would be able to tell you three things about that board in ten minutes in a yard and about nine months in here," he said. "That is the second reason I want it out of this room and I am not going to charge you for either of them."

## The Head Said Where The Book Is

They stood in a row in the middle of that room: Wenna Raut, and the two carriers, and the binder. Those were the four people whose hands went on that book, and he was the fifth, and there was not room on that floor for a sixth.

The quarter sheet was on its nail against the wall and Lin Hao took it off the nail and turned it round so that the figure at the head of it faced the three of them and himself, and he said it out loud in the voice that office reads everything in, because there was nothing else available.

"Four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand nine hundred and seventy, which is thirty-three times ninety," he said. "Thirty-four sheets and thirty-three quarters, differing by one."

Not one of them said anything, which was correct, and he went on with the part that goes with it.

"It is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them. It is not money, it is not people, and it is not anybody who has come through a door."

Wenna Raut looked at him. He had said that sentence in that room four times in nine years and three times of those had been said to the two of them. He had said it on a quarter-end at a counter under a window to a passage full of strangers, which was a different room with a different set of people in it and a man who was paid nothing standing behind a rail.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"There is nothing else to it."

"There is a reason you said it in this room," said Wenna Raut, "and you have not given it to them and I am not going to ask you in front of a stranger."

## What The Binder Asked In The First Ten Minutes

He did not ask what was in it for a good while, because he was busy. He got the book out of the flat file onto a deal table and put it on its side and turned a small mirror to get the daylight off the lamps onto it, and he looked at the split along the bottom edge of the back board for a long time, and then put two fingers into it and took them out, and then did it a second time.

Then he said, "What is in it."

"Other people's answers," said Lin Hao.

The binder waited. Lin Hao found that he had said it in the plainest possible way and had not managed to add anything to it, and that the two words had come out of his mouth with no weight on them at all, and that this was the whole of the difficulty.

"How many years," said the binder.

"Nine."

"And it is the only one."

"It is the only one," said Lin Hao, "and the two women who carried it up for two years have never opened it, and the woman who answers out of it has never read it, and I am the only person in this city who has ever had it open in front of him."

The binder took his hand off the table and wiped it on his apron and said nothing for a while, which in that room was a considerable thing.

"Then I will not look at a page," he said, "and I want that understood while I am standing here, because I am about to work on it for nine days and I am telling you now so that neither of us is surprised in a fortnight."

"Understood."

"I have wanted to look at a page of something for about four years," said the binder, "and I am not going to look at one of that, and you will not pay me for not looking and you must not thank me for it either, because you would then be able to do it again in about four years."

## Booking It At The Rail

At about four he took the forty-ninth sheet off that rail and put the fiftieth on the same two nails, carrying no new line, which is what a quarter-end does to a rail and is not an event and has not been an event for nine years. The binder watched a piece of paper go onto a nail in a room with no window and said nothing whatever about it.

Then they booked the run.

It was done on the back of a board on the end of a bench because there is no form for it and no office has one and never has. The binder wrote the date out and the number of days out and Lin Hao said the money again out loud so that it would be in two mouths and not one, and the binder said the cart had to be taken as well and could not be got on the day, and a cart was got.

The tenth of the month after. Nine days off the bench and back on.

"Then that is the whole of what is being arranged," said Lin Hao.

"It is the whole of what is being arranged and it is a third of what is being decided," said the binder. "I am going to say the other two thirds once, in this room, with no window in it, because you have just told a stranger what is in that book in four words and I would like you to have heard what you have done in your own voice."

## The Cost Of It Said In A Room With No Window

"You will carry it down four flights and about four hundred yards yourself," said the binder, "because you have carried it down yourself for two years and you are not going to hand it to a stranger on the last hundred yards for the sake of a morning."

"And then it will stand in a street in daylight on a cart under a man who is not me and who does not know what is in it, and it will be there long enough for somebody to look at it, and there is nothing you can put over it. Not a cloth."

"You are a man who has never put a coat round it and you are not going to start with a cover on a cart."

"That is not the cost I wanted said."

"The cost you wanted said is that it will not come back the same," said the binder. "It will come back nine days old with a price on it that was not on it when it went, and you cannot take the price off it and you cannot find out what the price is."

"And if you ask the man on the cart what he thinks is in it he will tell you what he thinks is in it and he will believe it, because he will have carried it four hundred yards and had nothing to go on."

The lamps on the bar across the middle of that room were the only light in it and none of them moved, and the four of them stood in front of them and let it stand where it was put.

"That is what it costs," said Lin Hao. "Say the rest of it if there is a rest of it."

"There is a second cost and it is worse," said the binder. "I have been in this trade thirty-one years and I have watched men book work they should not have booked and not one of them ever booked it for the reason he gave me. They booked it because they could not stand the tick."

## Walking Up The Four Flights Afterwards

He went up the four flights afterwards and the door was open and the room was half empty and the sixth of the six lines at the foot of that standing sheet was still the last one in it, and the second column still had four lines in it and was not going to get a fifth.

Wenna Raut had the practice sheet out. He looked at it and she moved her hand off it.

"You are not writing anything on that," she said.

"I am not."

"You booked a thing. You have just booked a nine-day run in a cellar with a stranger and paid a man for nine days of a bench and taken the only copy of this office's record out of the building it lives in, and you have done all of it out loud in a room with four people in it and the door was shut and I did not hear it open once."

"It stays shut," he said. "The rule is that it stays shut."

"The rule is that it stays shut and a person is not to be in the room unless the person it is about is in the room, and you have just told a man who does bureau books what is in that book, and he has promised not to look, and he is a stranger, and no practice in this building or this city covers a stranger hearing a thing and promising."

She shut the practice sheet. "I am not saying it was wrong. I am telling you that from today there is a fact in this world that was not in it this morning, and it is in a man's head, and neither of us can put it back."

"I know."

"Say what you want done about it."

"Nothing," said Lin Hao. "That is the answer and it took me a fortnight to arrive at it and I have got it on about the third of the month after with a form on a rail that wants a figure out of me, and by then I shall know whether it was worth it, and I am not going to know on the day."

Wenna Raut looked at him for a while.

"The seventh is in a week," she said.

"The seventh is in a week," he said, "and the tenth is the day after it, and there is a man in a passage off Cordwain who puts a thing on that rail every month on the seventh and does it in his own hand, and I am going to go and stand next to him and read it, because it is the only other rail in this city with a sheet on it that anybody can stand in front of."

He went down the four flights at half past five with the book under his arm and the wire round the buckle twisted twice in his own pocket and put it into the flat file himself, and shut the door of it, and stood there a moment longer than a man needs to stand where a book is.

The fifty-first of nothing went on a nail that afternoon and the fiftieth had been on it since about four, and the run was booked for the tenth, and the binder had a stranger's fact in his head and no page anywhere in this city that said what the fact was.

Four thousand four hundred and sixty-nine days. Less four thousand four hundred and sixty-three, the twenty-fourth of this one, is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six, and a man took a head off a nail in a room with no window and said it to four people instead of to a passage, and booked nine days of a stranger's bench and a stranger's cart for the tenth of the month after.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is ninety, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and at about four this afternoon it came down and the fiftieth went up on the same two nails carrying no new line.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and forty-nine days, which are sixty-eight months and nine days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

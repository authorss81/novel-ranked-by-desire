# Nine Days And A Cart

Four thousand four hundred and forty-nine was the tenth of this one and the seventeenth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and fifty-six, and four thousand four hundred and fifty-six less four thousand four hundred and forty-nine, the tenth of this one, is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## A Wide Door In Daylight

He went down the road in the morning on his own three days off and went through the door, which took about four seconds and cost him more than going down four flights had.

The room was long and low and there was no window in it that he could see, because the whole wall was doors — four of them, standing open, and the light came in off the yard through all four at once and lay along the benches in four bars. It smelled of glue and old leather and wet paper. There were seven books on the benches and a press at the far end with a screw handle a man could put his weight on.

The binder was an old man with a shaving in his hand and a board under his arm, and he put both down before Lin Hao got to the fourth bench.

"You will be the four flights," said the binder.

"You will be the four flights," said Lin Hao.

"Nobody comes in here for anything else. There is one of me and I do bureau books and you have the look of a man carrying something." He put the shaving in a block. "I am out between the fourth and the ninth of every month. I am in. What is it."

## The Small Half

"Back board along the bottom edge," said Lin Hao. "Open about four inches. A finger's width. It ticks about every four seconds and it has done since the second of November."

The binder held out a hand for it and then took the hand back, which was the first thing that told Lin Hao anything.

"Not here," said the binder. "Not off that table. I do not quote on a book I have not had my hands on and I am not having that one here, because if I had it here in daylight with those four doors open and somebody came in off that yard, I would have a stranger's eyes on it in about nine seconds and I could not un-see it myself afterwards."

He wiped his hands down his apron. "That is the first thing you do not get. You bring it to me or you do not."

Lin Hao stood there in the middle of that room and found that the argument he had built his whole delay out of had just been taken off him in nine seconds by a man who had never seen the book.

"What do you want to look at," he said.

"The edge of it. That is all. I am not going to read a page."

## The Figure For The Work

"You are out the fourth to the ninth," said Lin Hao. "What is the run?"

"Nine days off the bench and back on. That is what your board wants and not less. You take the back off, you open the spine where it has let go, you wash the leaves and press them and make a new back and sew it and let it stand before I put it in a cover again, and nine days is not me being slow. It is what the work takes in daylight."

He said it the way a man says a thing he has said to a division and had never had to soften. "And it is nine days of a bench that does other work, which is why you book it and not turn up."

"And the money."

The binder told him.

It was two figures and they were both small, and the smallness was the thing Lin Hao was not ready for, because he had come down that road with his stomach tied up about the size of it and he had had a picture of a man in a street who had said a price before he named a door.

He had not thought, once, in nine years, what any part of this office cost.

Not one thing. Answers on paper, carried by hand up four flights to whoever asked, and a book carried up by two people every working morning and carried down by one man every working evening, and a clerk's wages, and a branch office cleaned at night.

All of it free in the only sense that matters, which is that it had been free to the people who used it, and the price of it had never once entered his calculations because there had not been any.

There was a figure now. He did not write it down. He gave it back to the binder by nodding, because writing it down would have made it the second thing in this city that had that figure in it.

"That is the small half," said the binder.

## The Other Half

"I know what the other half is," said Lin Hao.

"You do not, because you have not said it out loud and neither has the woman who keeps your sentences, and I have been in this room thirty-one years and I can tell the difference between a man who has thought about a thing and a man who has only been not thinking about it."

He went along the benches and turned one of the books a quarter of a way round and left it. "Nine days. That book is in this room, and this room has four doors open and there is a yard off them."

"And anybody off that yard can walk in here and see a book on a bench for nine days, and they will see it because I cannot close these doors and work in a lamp, and I will not."

"You will not."

"I have not worked in a lamp since I was a young man and I am not going to be told about it by a man who carries one down four flights."

He turned round. "And here is what you have not thought about, and I am telling you because you asked me a question and not one person has asked me one in about four years."

"You are not buying a repair. You are buying nine days in which the only copy of nine years of other people's answers is in a room off a market with the door open, in daylight, and anything that is said about it in that room goes out of that room in a mouth."

"And I have watched men be ruined by a sheet of paper in a worse position than yours. I have not watched one be ruined by a mouth, because a mouth does not leave anything to sue you on."

Lin Hao heard it go into the room and stand there.

"That is right," he said.

"It is right and it is not a reason to refuse the work," said the binder. "It is a reason for me to say it before you have paid me, and for you to hear it in this room and not in yours, and for you to go away and decide whether a thing that is wrong in general is wrong for you in particular. Most men find out afterwards. You have come in before paying, so you will not."

## Asking For The Third Thing

"Why have you told me two things," said Lin Hao, "when you could have told me one? You quoted me. If the money had been ruinous I would have gone up the road and been back in a year. You knew that."

"I knew that." The binder sat down on the edge of the press. "You are the first man in about nine years to ask me that. Everybody else asks for the figure and goes. You are asking why there was a second figure, and that is the correct question and it is the one that costs you, and I am going to answer it and then you are going to leave."

"Answer it."

"You are buying nine days," said the binder. "The figure is for the work. The other thing I said is for the nine days. And the nine days is the part that does not go back, and a man who has only heard the figure has not heard what he is buying, and I have watched a division send a book off on a figure once and never ask the second question at all."

He got up off the press.

"Bring it down," he said. "Four floors and about four hundred yards and a room with no window in it, I am told, and I should like to see the light you have been working in."

He went back to his board. "The tenth of the month after. Not the fourth to the ninth, because that is my month away. Not the eleventh either, because there is a bureau book on the bench from the eighth."

"The tenth, and it goes in the morning and it comes back on the ninth day, and you have to be the one who is standing on the far side of it when it does."

## Going Up The Road

Lin Hao did not take the tenth.

He told himself on the road back that it was because he had not brought the book, and that was true, and it was also the smallest of the three reasons and the one he would have given to anybody who asked him.

The other two were that a cart had to be booked before a tenth of the month could be a date, and that he had not yet asked the person who owned the cart, and that he had no idea what a cart cost.

The third was that he had gone down that road at about eleven in the morning and come out of it at about a quarter past twelve with a figure in his head he had not asked for and a sentence in his head he could not put anywhere,

and that both of them had been put there by a stranger for nothing, the way the counterman under the window had put the three lines into strangers' heads for nothing for nine years, and that this time the stranger had taken something out of him instead of handing over anything.

That was the shape of it and he stood in the middle of his own court at one in the afternoon and let it be the shape of it.

A binder with four doors open had just told him that repairing the only copy of it put the only copy in a room with the door open for nine days, and had been right, and had not taken his money, and had offered him a date.

And in a room off Cordwain on that same street there was a desk where a man who had been told what a service was worth went and found out.

There was not another desk in this city he could ask, and he had known that all along, and he had spent eleven minutes in a binder's room finding out that a stranger will answer it for nothing if you ask the right question.

He turned round at the bottom of his own court at about a quarter past one and went back down the road. He did not go into the binder's room and he did not go up to the broker's.

He sat on a wall with his hands empty, because a man who has found out where the answer is does not go and get it the same afternoon, and he had been doing this for nine years and it had never once been wrong.

Four thousand four hundred and fifty-six days. Less four thousand four hundred and forty-nine, the tenth of this one, is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven, and a man asked a binder what a repair cost and was given two figures, one of which was money and one of which was the nine days it would spend with the door open.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seventy-seven, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and thirty-six days, which are sixty-seven months and twenty-six days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Same Card Same Nails

Four thousand six hundred and fifteen was the twenty-sixth of this one and the third of the next one is day four thousand six hundred and twenty-two, and four thousand six hundred and twenty-two less four thousand six hundred and fifteen, the twenty-sixth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## The Top Of Four Flights

He came up those four flights on the third of December at about ten in the morning and stopped on the landing outside that door, which he has not done since the twenty-third of August, and put his face about a foot from a piece of card on three nails.

It has been on those three nails since the thirtieth of December of last year, through one winter and about eleven months, and the holes are about a quarter of an inch wider than they were in the summer.

## The End Of The Second Line

He could read the street.

He could read the door at the head of four flights off a third court for about two thirds of the way along and then the rest of it went, because the card had gone soft at the two top corners of that line about a year ago and it had gone further since the twenty-eighth of October.

He stood on that landing with his face a foot off it and tried it four times, from the left and from the right, and the last four words of that line were a grey thumbprint on a piece of card.

The third line was the only part of it he could read from where a person actually stands.

## He Has Not Read It Since November

He said those three lines out loud every working evening for a year from his own second step in the dark, about nine feet below that door, and he never read them while he said them.

He had not stood still in front of that card in daylight since the day he put it up. He knew the street and he knew the door and he knew the hours because he had said them several thousand times, and it had never once occurred to him that a thing you can say without reading it is a thing you have stopped being able to check.

There are two true copies of those three lines in this city. One is on a nail at the top of that stair and the last four words of one line of it are gone, and the other one is a man standing on a landing with a poor corner in his eye and a chest that has been spent since the fourth year.

That is the arrangement he chose in the winter of year twelve, and he made it on purpose, and it has been correct for a year.

## How Thin It Is

He put his thumb against the top left corner and pressed and the card gave at it the way paper gives, and he took his thumb off and the corner stayed down and there was a shine on it now where there had not been one.

The card is about a year old and it has been off that wall in a winter and a summer and a winter, and it is pinned at three places and it is free at every other place, and it moves in any wind that comes round the corner of the building, and the two top corners of that second line are the two places it moves most because the door is on a hinge and the draught comes out of the room.

He has known all of that for a year without ever once standing on that side of it.

## He Asks Her To Read It Back

He went in and put his coat over a chair and asked Wenna Raut to come out to the landing with him and read the second line of that card to him.

She came out. She stood about a foot from it the way he had and read it, all of it, including the last four words, without moving her head.

"It is not gone."

"It is not gone," he said. "I could not get the end of it from the bottom of my own stair this morning and you got the whole of it standing next to it, and I want you to understand that I have never once asked anybody to read that thing back to me and I have asked you now."

"Nine years," said the clerk.

"Nine years."

"You have said it out loud about nine hundred times and you have never once read it while you were saying it, and I have never once said so, and I am saying so now."

She looked at the three nails and at his face. "You have been standing at the bottom of that stair since November being a copy of that card. A copy does not wear out. That was the arrangement, and it is the best thing you have ever done, and you have just found out what it costs in about four seconds on a landing."

## What It Will Take To Fix It

There is one repair and he knows exactly what it is, because he has done it once before, on the thirtieth of December of last year, and it took about four minutes with a hammer and a card of the same thickness.

You come down four flights at half past five and you go the next morning and you take it off its three nails and you put a new one up on the same three nails with the same three lines on it and not one name.

That is the whole of the instrument this office has ever had. It has one instrument and it is a piece of card and a hammer, and the last time he used it was a year ago, and it is the one thing he has spent five months not using.

He has been asked about that by two people in nine years, both of whom wanted to be able to rely on it, and he has given them the same answer both times, which is that a card with a date and an hour on it is a thing a person can be held to and a piece of card on a door is not.

Both of them were disappointed and both of them went away and one of them came back.

And the reason he has spent five months not using it is not sentiment, and it is not the rule that the card gets nothing added to it. It is that a renewal is a moment at which somebody decides what a door is, and he has spent five months giving that decision to other people, and a renewal puts it back in his hands on an ordinary working morning with a hammer, and nothing in this city can take it back out of their hands.

## What He Decided About The Hammer

He did not decide anything that morning, and he wants that on the page, because he has spent five months making decisions out loud and this one he made in his own head standing next to a nail.

What he decided is that he will look at the end of that second line every working morning from now until the middle of February, and that he will do it from a foot away, which is the distance a person stands at when they read a card.

What he did not decide is what happens when the word is gone. He knows the three possibilities and they are a hammer in December, a second card, and nothing at all, and he is not going to say the name of the middle one out loud on a landing.

## The Carrier With The Bad Back At The Top Of It

She came up at about eleven with nothing on the strap and found him on that landing instead of at the bottom of it.

"You have come up here to say them."

"The end of the second line has gone."

"Then say them louder and do not come up here," she said. "I have carried that strap up four flights twice a working morning for two years and you have said those three lines at the bottom of that stair in the dark every evening since November and not one person has ever heard you at the top of it, and I have worked out what that means, and I have not said it, and you have just made it impossible for me not to say it."

She put the coil of the strap down on the step.

"It means the free place has moved upstairs."

## From The Top Of The Stair

He said the three lines out loud on that landing at about a quarter past ten, in the ordinary voice, with the door behind him and the stair going down away from him on the other side.

He said them because he could not read the end of the second line and there was a woman on the second landing who had come up about a paper.

Then he went down and he stopped on his own second step from the bottom and he noticed that from up there he could not see the bottom of his own court at all.

He had never gone up there to say them in a year of saying them, and he had never once thought about what a person can see from four flights up.

It turns out that what a person can see from four flights up is a lit doorway and the top of his own head, and nothing at all of the four feet of pavement where about four people had been standing nine days before, saying three lines to each other.

Four thousand six hundred and twenty-two days. Less four thousand six hundred and fifteen, the twenty-sixth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven.

A man came up his own four flights in the morning to read three lines off his own door and could not read the end of the second line. He said them from the top of the stair instead, and found that from up there he cannot see whether anybody has come.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is sixty-three, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand two hundred and two days, which are seventy-three months and twelve days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

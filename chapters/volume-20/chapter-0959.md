# Two Women In A Lane

Four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight was the ninth of this one and the sixteenth of this one is day four thousand three hundred and sixty-five, and four thousand three hundred and sixty-five less four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

## The Two He Would Not Say On The Twenty-Fifth

He was in that lane at about half past one and Brack had the street door shut before he got to the second stool.

"Say them."

"I said on the twenty-fifth that I would not say them to you that day and I have not said them since and I have not written them down, so they are two names in a lane and nowhere else." Emil Brack took the chalk out and did not use it. "A woman called Sanna Ruhl who works a floor at the end of a market. And a woman called Ilva Tern who stands at a stop off the Ferrier line where the last service has stopped calling."

"Where do you have those names from."

"Off a woman who sells bread in the third district, who has been saying them at a door for about a month and who came in here in February and could not remember whether he had heard it from a floor or from a stop." Brack put the chalk on the rail. "They have never met."

"How do you know they have never met."

"Because a floor and a stop are about four miles apart and one works in the evening and one works in the morning, and I have nine districts and I have asked about four people this month and two of them said the other one had said it to them first and about nine of the others had not heard of her at all."

## The Sentence That Is Going About Them Is Not One They Would Recognise

"There is a sentence going round this lane about both of those women and I am going to say it to you so that you can hear how it sounds in here."

"Go on."

"That they are doing it for you."

Lin Hao did not say anything.

"Not that they are doing it badly and not that they are doing it wrong," said Brack. "That they are doing it for you. Which is what people in this lane say about a man with a slate, because that is the whole trade, and because the first man who gave something away is the first man that everything after it gets put on, and you did that on the thirtieth of December in front of about nine people."

"And you have let it stand."

"It is in a lane," said Emil Brack. "You do not put a person on a wall about a thing they have not said to you, and I have not got a figure for it and I have not got a subject for it, and I have been waiting four years for you to come down this lane and be angry at me about a street, and you have never once done it."

## What The Lane Has Instead

"Tell me what the lane has got, then."

"That the address went round for nothing and is now on the card, that a man will take anybody up four flights for a small amount, and that the man in the plate has heard about it and has not lifted a hand." Brack wiped the rail with his sleeve. "And that there is nobody at the bottom of the stairs, which is true until tomorrow and is not true after tomorrow, and I am not writing that one down either."

## He Went And Found The Second One First

He found Ilva Tern at the near end of that platform at about two in the afternoon with the last service gone and about nine people standing about, and the woman was at the timetable board with a bag of shopping on the ground beside her and she had a very ordinary way of standing still.

"I am not here about anything," she said, before he had said anything at all.

"I know that. I have come to ask you to come up four flights of a third court at about three o'clock this afternoon."

"Why."

"Because there is a sentence going round a lane off Cordwain about you and about a woman you have never met, and the sentence is that you are doing it for me, and I am going to say to you why that is not true and I am not going to say it in a street."

She looked at him for about four seconds.

"That is a lot of stair for a sentence," she said.

"Yes."

"I will be here at three."

## Two People Came Up Four Flights At About Three

Sanna Ruhl was at the top of that stair before Ilva Tern was a third of the way up it, which told him something he had not known about either of them.

The door was open and the book was on the table and Wenna Raut had her pen down, and two women came into a room four flights above a third court and stood about four feet apart in it and looked at each other.

"You are the stop," said Sanna Ruhl.

"You are the floor," said Ilva Tern.

There had been no introduction and there was not going to be one.

Lin Hao shut the door behind them, which he has not done in that room since the thirtieth of December, and said what he had come down four flights to say, once, to both of them, in the ordinary voice.

## What He Said To Both Of Them The Same

"You are not doing it for me," he said. "You have never done it for me and neither of you knows that I exist, and I have found that out this month at about a cost of four flights each."

"You are doing it for the person standing in front of you. That is the whole of it and it is the only thing you have ever been able to do about it, and it is what you were doing before I noticed and what you will be doing after I have forgotten about it."

Neither of them said anything.

"And here is the half of it that is mine," he said. "It is said for me because I gave it away first."

"I put a street on a door for nothing at ten past eleven on the thirtieth of December in front of about nine people, and everything anybody has done with those three lines since then has got put on the first man who gave them away, and the first man who gave them away was me."

"I have had about a fortnight to work out that this is not vanity. It is arithmetic. In a city the first gift is the address of the second one."

## They Compared What They Had Been Saying

"Wait," said Sanna Ruhl, and stopped in the middle of getting her coat off. "Say the second one."

"The second one."

"The door at the head of four flights off a third court."

"That is what it says."

"That is what he says it says," said Sanna Ruhl, to Ilva Tern, and not to him. "I have been saying third court for about four months. It is the second court. I have been telling people the third court for four months and about four of them have got to the wrong courtyard and gone round it twice and one of them has got to the right one by accident because the second court runs into the third."

Lin Hao stood against the edge of that table and did not say a word.

"Say yours," said Sanna Ruhl.

Ilva Tern said it, and it was right.

"Then I have been wrong for about four months, and the two of us have never once been in a room at the same time," said Sanna Ruhl, "and that is the whole of what a mouth is. I would not have found it out in four years."

"You would not," said Lin Hao. "That is not your fault and it is not this office's fault either and it is certainly not going to be fixed by a person standing in a lane telling two women they are wrong. It is fixed by one of them hearing the other one."

"You did that by getting us up here."

"I did that by getting you up here and by about four hundred yards of nothing else," he said.

## What He Did Not Say

He did not say thank you.

"I am not going to thank you," he said, "and I am telling you that I am not going to, because a man who has been at a counter being looked through for about four years has worked out that the three things you do to somebody in order to stop something are thanking them, paying them and asking them to stop, and I am not going to do any of them and I am not going to pretend that I have."

"You have not asked us to stop," said Sanna Ruhl.

"Neither of you is going to stop."

"No," she said, "and I have been waiting about nine days for a person to come and be pleasant about it and I would have said no and I am glad you did not, and I am not going to say that in a way that sounds as though I have been waiting."

"Two women have been standing outside a count for about four months," said Wenna Raut, from the table, "and neither of them knew about the other, and I am not going to enter this and you are not going to ask me to and neither of them is going to be on anything, and I am saying that out loud so that it is said in a room."

## What It Costs To Say It And What It Does Not

"Name the cost of standing at that door this afternoon," he said.

"I have given up half a day of a floor," said Sanna Ruhl. "And I have told about four people on that floor where I was and not one of them cared, and the four of them will go on saying them tomorrow."

"I have left a bag of shopping at a stop for an hour," said Ilva Tern, "and I have missed nothing, and I want you to understand that I did not come up here for a favour and I would have said no to a favour."

"I am not going to give you one," he said.

"I know that, and I have heard you say it to about four people in four months without ever meeting you, and I want to say that the reason those four people believed it is that you have never once said a thing that was not true."

Then he went down four flights with both of them and stood at the bottom of that stair in the dark with the light gone off the street about ten minutes before, and did not say the three lines out loud, because neither of them had asked him to and they had both been up those stairs already today.

Four thousand three hundred and sixty-five days. Less four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight, the ninth of this one, is seven, and two women who had never met stood four feet apart in a room four flights above a third court and were told by the man whose three lines they had been carrying for free that they were not doing it for him, and he did not thank them and neither of them asked.

*Less four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is seventy-six, and the forty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-six of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and forty-five days, which are sixty-four months and twenty-five days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Standing Given Away

Four thousand four hundred and thirty-five was the twenty-sixth of last month and the third of this one is day four thousand four hundred and forty-two, and four thousand four hundred and forty-two less four thousand four hundred and thirty-five, the twenty-sixth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## The Practice Sheet

He went up four flights at a quarter to ten with the two carriers ahead of him and the strap between them, and the book went onto the table in the ordinary way and the door stood open onto the landing. Wenna Raut had the practice sheet out beside the standing sheet, because he had asked her the night before to have it ready, and the standing sheet kept six lines with the last entered on the second of December of the ninth year.

He wanted to put on the practice sheet, in his own words and with a date on it, the one line that would make him unnecessary and could not be taken back. What he felt, standing at the end of the table with the blank sheet in front of him, was fear of the writing rather than of the giving, because the giving had been done weeks ago on flags and the writing made it true in a way flags could not.

"You have had it eleven days," said Wenna Raut.

"I have had it since the twenty-third of May," said Lin Hao. "And I have carried it twelve mornings without writing it, because writing it ends the only thing I built in the winter, which is a voice that replaced a number. Saying it ends it. Writing it buries it."

She waited with her pen ready, and the room was full of people who did not know what was about to go onto a sheet they would never be shown. The carriers were on their stools by the window. The bundle stayed at twenty-six things on the side table. The drawer stayed shut under its instruction.

"Everything else I have is a practice that only binds me," said Lin Hao. "A practice that binds a clerk requires a clerk to break it. This one binds no person and cannot be broken by any person, because any person standing at the foot of that stair is doing it for free, and a free act cannot be stopped or refused or priced by this office or by any person."

He paused, and the fear rose higher, clean and cold.

"And the cost of entering it is that about four people will be standing there inside a fortnight, and not one of them will say it the way I say it. About four hundred wrong copies will go on going, and the three lines will leave this office for good as the only mouth that said them true. I am saying that before the ink is dry, because it is spent here."

## In His Own Words

He dictated slowly, and Wenna Raut wrote in her clerk's hand on the practice sheet, with the date at the top in his own words.

From the twenty-third of May, any person may stand at the foot of that stair and say the three lines out loud, on working mornings, from a quarter to ten to half past five, and no person may be charged for it, and he will not stop any person.

When she had finished he read it back from the sheet, word by word, in the ordinary voice, with the door open and the room listening. The standing sheet lay beside it with its six lines untouched, and the sixth of the second of December of the ninth year was still the last.

The second column kept four lines. The card down four flights carried the street and the door and the hours and not one name, and it was not taken down and got no fourth line and no hour about the turn of the year, and got nothing out of today.

"It is on the practice sheet and not on the standing sheet," said Wenna Raut. "And it is dated the twenty-third of May, eleven days past, in your own words. That is where it lives now."

He felt the giving go out of him like breath after a long climb.

He had spent a volume being the only person in this city who could say those three lines true, and he had given the only thing he had left away on a date eleven days in the past, in a room with a clerk who had to write it down. What frightened him was not the giving but the writing down, because a line on a sheet no person had ever been shown would outlive his voice.

"Say the second cost," said Lin Hao. "Because I have said mine and there is another, and it should come from you."

Wenna Raut looked at the sheet for a long moment with her pen still in her hand.

"In about four years there will be a room with a rule about a stair in it," said Wenna Raut. "And not one person will be able to say who put it there or when, because the only copy is a line on a sheet no person has ever been shown. That is what writing does to a free thing. It keeps it and it hides it at the same time."

## Said Out Loud Before The Ink Is Dry

He went down the four flights at midday with the ink still wet on the sheet above, and stood at the bottom in the light with his back to the wall. The standing ones were there along the wall in their places, saying the three lines badly to strangers, and strangers went up past them without breaking stride.

He waited until the court was full enough that his voice would carry, and then he said the cost out loud where it was spent, on purpose, where any person standing there could hear him say it.

"A man who wanted a coin may have the same thing for nothing," said Lin Hao, in the ordinary voice. "On purpose, in a third court, from this morning, said by any person who stands here. The office will not stop any person and no person will be charged. That is the whole of it, and I am saying it at the foot of my own stair with the door open above me."

One of the standing women laughed, short and surprised. A stranger stopped with one foot on the first tread and listened, and then went on up. The man with the coin was not there and had never been up those flights to that door, and Lin Hao told the court so, because the market had to hear that it was not robbed.

"He says his price before he names the door," said Lin Hao. "He walks the stair himself once more before he takes any person, and he has not lied to any person, and he is right about the walk. What he sells is walking and what is here is standing, and the two are different labours. No person here steals from him."

He felt the irreversibility of it as he spoke, the way a man feels a nail go in. Rank one throughout, the branch night shift not moved, the mornings not bought and not asked for, and the thirteenth practice bought with nothing.

He had closed being the only person who could say those three lines true, and he could not take it back, because a free act entered on a sheet could not be unwritten by wanting. He thought of the winter card going up on three nails in front of strangers, and how that had felt like building, and how this felt like unbuilding with the same hands.

In the afternoon he stood with the others and said the three lines when asked, badly or well, for nothing. About four people learned the pavement that week who had never stood on it before, and not one of them said it the way he said it, and he listened to each wrong version with love he had not known he owned.

He felt glad and bereft together, the way a man feels when a child leaves home carrying his name wrong. He thought of Sanna Ruhl saying the third court for four months when it was the second, and how that wrongness had brought her to the same room as Ilva Tern, and how the pavement would now breed a hundred such wrongnesses, all useful, all uncountable.

At half past five he went up and got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards in the dark. The back board ticked under his arm about every four seconds, and he walked slowly because his chest was tight and because he wanted the day to last. Wenna Raut entered nothing about the afternoon, because a free act was not a question and not an answer.

On the way back he stopped at the bottom of his own stair, empty now, and looked up into the dark where the door stood open.

The tenth of June waited with four inches of daylight and a tick about every four seconds, and a binder four hundred yards away with other hands. He felt the night cold on his face and the tick of the book still in his arm though the book was filed, the way a carrier feels a strap after it is off.

He did not know it yet, but the book would have to leave the building, and the only copy of nine years of answers would ride in a cart. He knew only that he had given the standing away, and that the giving could not be un-been.

Four thousand four hundred and forty-two days. Less four thousand four hundred and thirty-five, the twenty-sixth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven, and a man put one line about another person's mouth on the practice sheet and said its cost out loud before the ink was dry.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is sixty-three, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and twenty-two days, which are sixty-seven months and twelve days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Somebody Else's Mouth

Four thousand four hundred and thirty-five was the twenty-sixth of last month and the third of this one is day four thousand four hundred and forty-two, and four thousand four hundred and forty-two less four thousand four hundred and thirty-five, the twenty-sixth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## The Practice Sheet

He went up the four flights at a quarter to ten behind the two carriers, with the strap between them, and the book came onto the table in the ordinary way and the door stood open on the landing behind him. Wenna Raut had the practice sheet out beside the standing sheet, because he had asked her the night before to have it ready, and the standing sheet kept six lines with the last entered on the second of December of the ninth year.

He wanted to put on the practice sheet, in his own words and with a date on it, the one line that would make him unnecessary and could not be taken back. Standing at the end of the table with the blank sheet in front of him, he found he was afraid of the writing and not of the giving, because the giving had been done weeks ago on flags, and writing it down made it true in a way flags never could.

"You have had it eleven days," said Wenna Raut.

"I have had it since the twenty-third of May," said Lin Hao. "And I have carried it twelve mornings without writing it, because writing it ends the only thing I built in the winter, which is a voice that replaced a number. Saying it ends it. Writing it buries it."

She waited with her pen ready, and the room was full of people who did not know what was about to go onto a sheet they would never be shown. The carriers were on their stools by the window, and the bundle sat where it always sat on the side table, twenty-six things.

"Everything else I have is a practice that only binds me," said Lin Hao. "A practice that binds a clerk requires a clerk to break it. This one binds no person and cannot be broken by any person, because any person standing at the foot of that stair is doing it for free, and a free act cannot be stopped or refused or priced by this office or by any person."

He paused, and the fear rose higher, clean and cold.

"And the cost of entering it is that there will be people standing there who are not me, saying it their own way, getting it wrong, and going on getting it wrong after every one of them is dead. There will be no end to it."

He let that stand in the room before he said the rest of it, because it was the half of the cost that nobody could argue with.

"Nobody will ever know how many of them there are, and that will include me, and that is the price of a mouth: what it says after you stop saying it. The three lines will leave this office for good as the only mouth that said them true. I am saying that before the ink is dry, because it is spent here."

## In His Own Words

He dictated slowly, and Wenna Raut wrote in her clerk's hand on the practice sheet, with the date at the top in his own words.

From the twenty-third of May, any person may stand at the foot of that stair and say the three lines out loud, on working mornings, from a quarter to ten to half past five, and no person may be charged for it, and he will not stop any person.

When she had finished he read it back from the sheet, word by word, in the ordinary voice, with the door open and the room listening. The standing sheet lay beside it with its six lines untouched, and the sixth of the second of December of the ninth year was still the last.

He said the street and the door and the hours out loud as well, off the sheet, so that the practice and the thing it was about would be in the same room at the same time.

"It is on the practice sheet and not on the standing sheet," said Wenna Raut. "And it is dated the twenty-third of May, eleven days past, in your own words. That is where it lives now."

He felt the giving go out of him like breath after a long climb.

He had spent a volume being the only person in this city who could say those three lines true, and he had given the only thing he had left away on a date eleven days in the past, in a room with a clerk who had to write it down. What frightened him was not the giving but the writing down, because a line on a sheet no person had ever been shown would outlive his voice.

"Say the second cost," said Lin Hao. "Because I have said mine and there is another, and it should come from you."

Wenna Raut looked at the sheet for a long moment with her pen still in her hand.

"In about four years there will be a room with a rule about a stair in it," said Wenna Raut. "And not one person will be able to say who put it there or when, because the only copy is a line on a sheet no person has ever been shown. That is what writing does to a free thing. It keeps it and it hides it at the same time."

He sat down on the edge of the table, which he never did, and asked her the question he had not been able to put to her the night before, which was whether the office could keep it.

"That depends what keeping is," said Wenna Raut. "I can write a practice and I can enter the date you gave it, and that is the whole of what I am. A practice sheet is not read out to a stranger, because a stranger who asks me about that stair is asking a question, and this office answers questions. And a question answered out of a practice is a refusal, and we have used both of those already."

"So there is nobody who can say it twice for me."

"You can say it twice to me," said Wenna Raut. "You can say it once out loud where it is spent. After that it is not yours to hand about, and I will tell you why without being asked twice: a thing said once in a court belongs to the court. You said that to a carrier on a step in the second week of this month and I heard you say it, and you will not argue it with me tonight."

He understood what he had built. He had written himself a sentence he was allowed to use once, and now he had to choose the one place to spend it in. If he spent it in that room it would belong to the office. If he spent it in the court it would belong to the court. Either way it would stop being his before the ink was dry.

"Then it is the stair," said Lin Hao. "Down, in daylight, where the people are standing."

"Then go and say it there," said Wenna Raut, "and take your coat, because I am not having you say a thing like that in my room without your coat on."

## Said Out Loud Before The Ink Is Dry

He went down the four flights at midday with the ink still wet on the sheet above, and stood at the bottom in the light with his back to the wall. The standing ones were there along the wall in their places, saying the three lines badly to strangers, and strangers went up past them without breaking stride.

He waited until the court was full enough that his voice would carry, and then he said the cost out loud where it was spent, on purpose, where any person standing there could hear him say it.

"A man who wanted a coin may have the same thing for nothing," said Lin Hao, in the ordinary voice. "On purpose, in a third court, and since the twenty-third of May, said by any person who stands here. The office will not stop any person and no person will be charged. That is the whole of it, and I am saying it at the foot of my own stair with the door open above me."

A stranger stopped with one foot on the first tread and listened, and then went on up without changing his mind about it. The man with the coin was not there and had never been up those flights to that door, and Lin Hao told the court so, because the market had to hear that it was not robbed.

"He says his price before he names the door," said Lin Hao. "He walks the stair himself once more before he takes any person, and he has not lied to any person, and he is right about the walk. What he sells is walking and what is here is standing, and the two are different labours. No person here steals from him."

He felt the irreversibility of it as he said it, the way a man feels a nail go in. Rank one throughout, the branch night shift not moved, the mornings not bought and not asked for, and the thirteenth practice bought with nothing, and not one of those things was going to be what the sentence cost him.

The standing woman with empty hands, who had come to that court before any of the others and had never once been told she was welcome, laughed and then stopped laughing.

"You have given the ground away," she said, and she said it out loud in front of the court, because she had learned four months earlier that a thing said badly in daylight outlasts a thing said well in a room. "Do you know that?"

"I do," said Lin Hao.

"Then say the other half of it, because you have said the half that makes a giver of you." She came three steps out from the wall and stood in front of him where anybody in that court could see her do it. "If a man comes up here tomorrow with a price, and stands where I am standing, and says a number at the top of his voice, what does anybody in this court do?"

Lin Hao found that he had no answer which was not already in the sentence he had written down twenty minutes earlier.

"Nothing," he said. "That is in the line I have just given you. I said I will not stop any person. I did not say any person else could stop one either, and I made certain of that in my own words before I came down those stairs."

"So a man may charge here."

"A man may charge here," said Lin Hao. "And if he does it properly he will be doing me the courtesy of a competition I handed to him with my own handwriting on it. And there is no version of this afternoon in which I am the man who stops him, because the stopping was the thing I sold. I have not got a second sentence, and there is no second use of the one."

"You have not got a first use left either," said the woman. "You spent it standing there."

"I spent it standing there," said Lin Hao, "because it was spent there."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she went back to her four feet of pavement and took up her place against the wall, and the two of them stood in that court saying the same three lines at the same hour of the morning, one of them right and one of them not, and neither of them owned the place any more.

In the afternoon he stood with the others and said the three lines when asked, badly or well, for nothing. People learned the pavement that week who had never stood on it before, and not one of them said it the way he said it, and he listened to every wrong version with a love he had not known he owned.

He felt glad and bereft together, the way a man feels when a child leaves home carrying his name wrong. He thought of Sanna Ruhl saying the third court for four months when it was the second, and how that wrongness had brought her into the same room as Ilva Tern, and how the pavement would go on breeding wrongnesses from this afternoon that nobody could ever count and nobody would ever be able to mend.

At half past five he went up and got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards in the dark, and the back board ticked against his ribs the whole way, because he could not hurry and because he wanted the day to last. Wenna Raut entered nothing about the afternoon, because a free act was not a question and not an answer.

He put the book into the flat file, shut the door of it, and stood with his hand on the frame and no reason whatever to be holding it.

The tenth of June waited with four inches of daylight and a tick about every four seconds, and a binder four hundred yards away with other hands. He felt the night cold on his face and the tick of the book still in his arm though the book was filed, the way a carrier feels a strap after it is off.

He knew only that he had given the standing away and that the giving could not be un-been. What he could not have been told in any room that day was that a record which cannot stay in the room where it was made is going to start asking to go out of the building, and that the only person in this city able to let it go is him.

Four thousand four hundred and forty-two days. Less four thousand four hundred and thirty-five, the twenty-sixth of last month, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven, and a man put one line about another person's mouth on the practice sheet and said its cost out loud before the ink was dry.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is sixty-three, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and twenty-two days, which are sixty-seven months and twelve days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

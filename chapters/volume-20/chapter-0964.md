# The Last Link

Four thousand three hundred and ninety-three was the fourteenth of this one and the twenty-first of this one is day four thousand four hundred, and four thousand four hundred less four thousand three hundred and ninety-three, the fourteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

## Daylight At The Bottom

He was at the bottom of his own stair by a quarter to ten with his back to the wall and the morning bright around him. Four flights rose above a third court to a door with a card on it, and the card carried the street and the door and the hours and not one name. He had stood there eleven mornings and today was the twelfth, and his legs knew the flags by now the way his hands knew the grain of the table upstairs.

He wanted to be asked the one question he had been waiting a fortnight for, and he wanted it the way a man wants a knock he has been listening for. What he felt, with the court filling and emptying around him, was a tight readiness that made his chest hurt less than usual because his attention was elsewhere.

At about half past ten a woman came into the court alone, walking fast with her coat buttoned and her hands empty. She stopped four feet off and looked up at the stair and then at him, and he saw at once that she had come from somewhere with purpose and had paid for the coming.

"You are the man at the stair," she said.

"I am the man at the stair," said Lin Hao. "And the door is four flights up and open until half past five, and I can give you the hours if you want them."

She looked at him steadily, and he saw that she was measuring whether to trust a man who stood still.

"I have paid a man a small amount this morning," she said. "In a street off Cordwain. He said his price before he named the door, and he walked the stair himself once more before he took me, and he brought me to the mouth of this court and showed me the stair. He has never been up those four flights to that door in his life, and he told me so before we started."

Lin Hao felt his pulse quicken. This was the question, arriving in daylight with its price still warm in her hand.

"And he gave you the address true," said Lin Hao.

"He gave it true," she said. "Every turn of it. He charged a small amount for two walks up and one down, his and mine, and he counted the walks out loud before we started. I want to know whether it is right."

## True

"It is right," said Lin Hao. "The street is the street and the door is the door at the head of four flights off a third court, and the hours are from a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven. He was right about the way and he got you here, and the small amount was for labour and not for telling."

She let out a breath she had been holding since the mouth of the court. He saw relief move across her face and then something harder behind it, the reckoning of a person who has paid and is working out what was bought.

"Then I will go up," she said, and turned toward the foot of the stair.

"Wait," said Lin Hao. "There is one more thing and I am going to say it to your face, because you have paid and you are standing in front of me, and it is the only thing this step is for."

She stopped with one foot on the first tread and looked back at him. The court was quiet around them, with no person close enough to hear, and the light lay full on the flags.

"I am the last link in a chain that cost you money," said Lin Hao. It took him about nine seconds and he said it in the ordinary voice. "You paid a man for the walking and he walked you here, and now I am telling you the address for nothing, standing still, where you have already arrived. The two of us together are a chain and I am the end of it."

She stared at him, and he saw her understand all at once what she had bought. She had bought being brought to a man who would tell her for nothing what she had just paid to be brought to. The price was not in the telling and it was not in the walking. It was in the distance between the two, and he had just closed the distance by saying so.

"Then the walking was wasted," she said slowly.

"The walking was not wasted," said Lin Hao. "The walking got you here. But you do not have to go up those flights this morning, because the man was right about the stair and I am right about the hours, and you have had both now without climbing. If you go up, go up because you have a question for the book, and not because you paid to be brought."

She stepped back off the first tread onto the flags. He watched her weigh the morning, with the stair in front of her and the man behind her, and he felt the old belief that standing there was an act of obstruction fall away from him. The only thing the step was for was that it could be said out loud to a person who had just paid, face to face, where no paper could do it.

"I have no question for the book," she said. "I came to find the door, and I have found it, and I have found you standing in front of it telling me I do not have to knock. That is a strange morning."

"It is a strange arrangement," said Lin Hao. "And it is the arrangement. I will give you the three lines now, from the step, from memory, so that you have them from the man who put them on the door, and then you can go home."

## From The Step

He said them slowly, the street and the door and the hours, in the ordinary voice, with his back to the wall and his hands empty. She listened with her head bent, memorising the way people memorise when there is no paper to take it on. When he had finished she repeated the hours back to him and got them right, and he nodded.

"The man was right about the stair," said Lin Hao. "And I am right about the hours. Hold both of those and you have the whole of it, and neither of us has lied to you."

She did not complain and she did not thank him. She stood for a moment with her hands in her coat pockets, looking up at the four flights and then at him, and he did not tell her not to thank him, because he had learned that from two women who had stood four feet apart in a room and had refused to be thanked.

"I was ready to be angry at you," she said. "On the way here I had it ready, that you would send me up and keep the price. And now there is nothing to be angry at, and that is harder to carry home."

"I know," said Lin Hao. "And I am glad you said it, because anger is the true thing about a price, and a price that cannot be angry is a price that has gone underground."

She nodded once, as if that closed something, and turned to go. At the mouth of the court she stopped and looked back, and he raised a hand to her without moving from the wall. Then she was gone, and the court was empty again, and the stair behind him was quiet from the bottom to the door.

He stood a while with his shoulder to the wall and watched the light move across the flags. A boy ran through with a hoop and a woman followed with washing over her arm, and neither of them looked at the stair. He felt the morning loosen in him, the way a knot loosens when the rope is no longer pulled, and he was grateful for ordinary traffic that wanted nothing from him.

He felt a fierce gladness rise in him, sudden and unwarranted, that had nothing to do with being right. He had thought standing there was an act of getting in the way, and it was an act of being the place where a price stopped.

The address was confirmed by a man who could not be led anywhere and did not charge, and the truth about her own transaction was told instead of only the door being pointed at. That was why the price died here, on the flags, in daylight, and not in a lane.

At half past five he went up and got the book down with his own hands and carried it the four hundred yards in the dark.

Wenna Raut listened to the whole of it with her pen down and entered nothing, because a chain that cost money was not a question and not an answer. He felt tired in his bones and light in his chest at the same time, and he wanted tomorrow to be a morning when the four flights stood empty and he could find out what the absence of a man did to a door.

Four flights with no person on them were waiting, and a morning when the man who stood at the bottom would not be there. He did not know it yet, but his chest knew, aching on the third landing in the dark.

Four thousand four hundred days. Less four thousand three hundred and ninety-three, the fourteenth of this one, is seven, and a woman who had paid that morning for a walk was told on the flags what she had bought and did not go up.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is twenty-one, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-one of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and eighty days, which are sixty-six months and no days, and the sixty-sixth month is named here once and is not an event, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Cheapest Man In Cordwain

Four thousand four hundred and twenty-one was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight, and four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight less four thousand four hundred and twenty-one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Nine Stools

He went down to the lane off Cordwain at about half past one with his coat buttoned and his plate covered. The lane held nine stools and nine districts and a slate and twelve books, and Emil Brack stood behind the slate with chalk in his hand and his street door shut against the midday traffic. The sooner figure stood on the left in small chalk, unrubbed, and the head of the office's standing sheet stood on the right in four-inch letters, and the two were not separated.

He wanted to find out what a lane had made of a man who would not move, before the lane made it into a rate. What he felt, stepping into the familiar smell of paper and dust, was a wary amusement that had been missing for weeks, because a lane was at least honest about what it did to people.

"You have got something," said Lin Hao.

"I have had it about four days," said Emil Brack. "And I have not written it anywhere and I am not going to, and you have come at the right hour to hear it before it goes cold."

He put the chalk down on the rail without using it, and Lin Hao understood that whatever Brack had worked out was still in his head with no unit after it that he could write, and that it troubled him.

"Say it, then," said Lin Hao. "And do not spare me, because sparing is a price and I have had enough of prices this month."

## The Price Of Standing Still

Brack looked at him for a long moment, the way he looked at a district before pricing it.

"You are standing still," said Emil Brack. "At the bottom of four flights, five mornings a week, out of your own time. And a lane has spent nine years getting you wrong, and for the first time it has got something right, which is that there is nothing to price."

He tapped the slate with one finger, not touching the figures.

"A man who walks somebody up four flights is a rate, because it is labour and it is done again tomorrow. A man who stands still is not a rate."

"Standing still is not done and it is not done again, because it is never finished. I have a figure for the first and I have had it four days, and it is the price of a man who will not move and not the price of a service, and the two are not the same and a lane cannot tell them apart because a lane has only one column."

Lin Hao felt the odd relief of being inventoried after being threatened. For a year the lane's sentence had been that the man in the white plate knew about the walking and had not stopped it, which was true and was worse for being true.

Now the lane had discovered that a man standing still was not a thing to be priced, and he preferred the inventory to the accusation. He thought of the winter mornings he had kept through snow and dark, and how the lane had priced everything but the keeping, and how strange it felt to be finally unpriced.

"And the second figure," said Lin Hao.

"The second is what a person will pay to be told that a door is where somebody is standing," said Emil Brack. "Which is nothing. A person looks up and sees a man and sees a stair behind him, and the telling is done by the standing. And a lane cannot put nothing on a slate, because nothing is not a figure and a slate is for figures."

He picked up the chalk and put it down again, restless.

"I have had a week to work out the difference and I have got it right at last," said Emil Brack. "The walking has a price because legs get tired. The standing has no price because standing is not sold."

He glanced at the door as a cart went past in the lane, and then back at the slate.

"And the telling at the bottom has no price because the man is already there. Three things, one column, and the column is wrong about two of them. That is the first honest arithmetic this lane has done about you since the second year."

## Do Not Write Either

Lin Hao looked at the slate with its two figures side by side, the sooner figure on the left and the head on the right, neither moved and neither argued.

The third figure was still in Brack's head with no unit after it, still owed and still out by more than half a quarter, and it would not be chalked this year. He thought of the small chalk beside the four-inch letters, and how the two had stood together since the autumn without either harming the other, and how rare it was for two figures to share a slate in peace.

He felt a sudden protectiveness toward the lane, strange and warm, because the lane had tried honestly to price him and had found honestly that it could not. What he wanted was to keep both figures off the slate, the one for standing and the one for telling, before either hardened into a rate.

"Do not write either figure," said Lin Hao. "Not the standing and not the telling. Leave the slate as it is, with the two that are there not separated and the head beside them not moved."

Emil Brack nodded slowly.

"I will not write them, and I will tell you why in my own words," said Emil Brack. "A figure in my lane is a rate in about a fortnight, and a rate is a thing a person can be put on, and I do not put people on things. I put districts on things and walks on things, and I have never put a man on anything and I am not starting with you."

He wiped his hands on his coat, leaving chalk dust on the cloth. The dust hung in the light from the high window, and the twelve books stood around them like patient animals.

"The two on the slate stay where they are and are not separated," said Emil Brack. "The sooner figure is not rubbed out and the third is still in my head with no unit after it and still out by more than half a quarter. The four-year sentence stands and I am not arguing it, because arguing is a price and I have done enough pricing this month."

Lin Hao felt gratitude rise in him, plain and unguarded, the first easy feeling he had had in a lane for a year. He had come expecting to fight a rate and had found a man refusing to make one, and the relief made his chest ache less.

"You have been described in this lane all year," said Emil Brack, almost gentle. "First as a threat and now as an inventory. I thought you would prefer the second."

"I prefer it," said Lin Hao. "A threat wants me to move and an inventory lets me stand. And I have stood six weeks to earn the standing, and I am glad the lane knows the difference, even if it has only one column to put it in."

He looked round the lane once more, at the stools and the books and the shut street door. He had come down it angry four years ago and afraid three years ago, and today he was neither, and the absence of both felt like health.

He said the four-year sentence himself then, in the lane, in the ordinary voice, and did not argue it, because it stood beside the head on the slate whether he said it or not.

Brack nodded, and no names were written down, and no list of any person was started, because a lane that did not put people on things did not write them down either. The chalk stayed on the rail where Brack had left it, untouched, and the afternoon went on outside with carts and voices.

## What The Lane Got Right

They stood a while without speaking, two men who had priced and been priced for nine years, with twelve books around them and dust in the light.

Lin Hao thought of the standing women at the bottom of his stair, saying the lines badly for nothing, and of the counterman saying that a free thing should start where the paid thing started. He thought of nine stools worn smooth by nine districts of trouble, and how the lane held them all without breaking.

He thought of the woman who would come tomorrow to ask whether she might pay him instead, and how the answer would have to be no.

"What happens now," said Emil Brack.

"Now the standing goes on," said Lin Hao. "And the telling goes on badly at the bottom, for nothing, by people who were never asked. And the lane knows there is nothing to price, which is the first true thing it has known about me in nine years, and that will have to be enough."

He walked back up the lane into the afternoon with the light going thin. He felt lighter than he had in weeks, inventoried and found to be worth nothing, which was the only price he had ever wanted.

What pulled him forward was not relief but the knowledge that the last chapter of the movement was waiting. A woman would offer him the only thing any person had offered him all year, and he would have to refuse it out loud, in a room with the door open.

Four thousand four hundred and twenty-eight days. Less four thousand four hundred and twenty-one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and in a lane off Cordwain a man with chalk agreed to write nothing and the two figures on the slate were left where they were.

*Less four thousand three hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March, is forty-nine, and the forty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it went up on the same two nails on the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand and eight days, which are sixty-six months and twenty-eight days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Paid Nothing, Said Anyway

Four thousand three hundred and fifty-one was the second of this one and the ninth of this one is day four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight, and four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight less four thousand three hundred and fifty-one is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## Nine Words To A Woman With A Bundle On Her Arm

He went down at about half past eleven and along about four hundred yards and one street off to the counter under the window, and he did not go up to it.

He stood at the far side of that passage and let the counterman do his day for about eleven minutes, because that was the reason he had come and he had not known it until he was standing there.

A woman with a bundle on her arm came up with nothing in her hands and he gave her the stair and the third door off that landing and the hour to be there, in nine words, in about four seconds, and she wrote about half of it on the back of her hand.

He did not correct her. He had watched him not correct her about nine times a month for four years and had never once known what it cost him, and on the ninth of this month he found out that the man knew exactly what it cost him and had decided it in about four seconds every single time.

## And Then He Said The Other Thing

"Whose stair," said the counterman, without turning round.

"Yours."

"It is yours in the way a road is yours," said the man behind the counter. "I am paid nothing and I have been paid nothing for nine years and I have never once been asked to be paid anything, and every person who comes to this counter goes away out of it with three lines they did not have, and they are not getting them off a sign."

"That is not what I came about."

"You came about the man in the street," said the counterman.

"I came to hear you say a thing."

"I am not going to say a thing while I am working," said the counterman. "I will say it at about twenty past twelve when there is nothing at that rail, and you will stand over there and not come up to this counter, and you will not tell anybody afterwards that I said it, because the day I stop being paid nothing is the day somebody finds out what I am for."

## The Sheet Face Up On The Rail

At twenty past twelve there was nothing at that rail and the man came round the end of the counter and stood with his back against the frame of it.

The quarterly sheet was face up on that rail where anybody standing at that counter could read it, and it is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them, and the caption under it says so, and it has said so since the third of June of the ninth year.

"You have read it."

"I have read it four times a year since the beginning of the second year," said the counterman. "It is a good sheet. I want to say that before anything else, because in about four minutes you are going to tell me something and I am not going to be able to say anything else straight."

## The Caption Under The Figure

"There is a caption under that figure and it has been there since the third of June of the ninth year, and it says what the figure is and what it is not, and you have read it four times a year for nine years without anybody ever asking you whether you understood it."

"I have understood it."

"Then you know what that figure is and what it is not," said the counterman, "and I have got about nine people a week standing at that rail reading the first half of it and going off with it, because the first half of it is the half with the striking on the bottom."

"Go on."

"It is a count of pieces of paper," he said. "And what happens in that passage, every single morning, is that a person reads a count of pieces of paper and goes off believing it is a count of something else, and I have never once been able to correct it, because the office that put it there does not correct copies of itself in any form, and I am a man in a passage and I am not that office and I never have been."

## What He Said

"Every stranger who comes to this counter finds that door because of me," he said. "Not because of the card. The card is on a door four hundred yards off and about nine people a week read it and most of them get it wrong."

"They find it because a man who is paid nothing stands in a passage and gives it to them for nothing," said the counterman, "and I have been doing it since before that card existed and I did not stop when the card went up, and I would go on doing it if the card came down tomorrow."

Lin Hao said nothing.

"That card has been on a door since the thirtieth of December," said the counterman, "and it has been in about nine hands a week ever since, and a man in a street has started walking people up for a small amount, and neither of those things is me. But you know and I know that neither of those things would have happened if a man behind this counter had said no to a stranger in about the second year."

## The Argument He Was Not Going To Make

"You want me to stop," said the counterman. "You have come down here to tell me that it would be a kindness and that about nine people a week would go away without it, and I am telling you the answer before you spend the walk."

"I have not come to tell you to stop."

"You have not come to tell me to stop and I want to say that I would have done it," said the counterman, "which is what makes me trust that you have not come to tell me to stop. If you had come to tell me to stop I would have done it, and I would have gone on doing it, and I would have told about nine people a week that I had been told."

"And the telling would have been free and it would have cost you a street," said the counterman.

He turned round and put his forearms on the rail.

"Here is the argument I have been not-making for about nine days and I am going to make it once. A thing that has been given away every day for nine years, for nothing, by the same man, at the same counter, is not a thing anybody else can be the first to sell."

"The man in the street is not stealing it off me," said the counterman. "He is the second person in this city to hand it over, and the first one has a counter and a rail and no name on anything."

"That is not an argument, it is a grievance."

"It is a grievance," said the counterman. "I have had nine years to turn it into an argument and I have not managed it and I have had about nine minutes to tell you, so you will take it as it comes."

## What You Do To A Man In Order To Stop Something

"I am not going to thank you," said Lin Hao.

"I know."

"I am not going to offer to pay you."

"I know that as well and I would have had to think about it for a day."

"And I am not going to ask you to stop, and I am going to say why all three of those are in the same breath," he said. "All three are things a person does to a man in order to stop something. Thanking a man makes him feel he has done a service. Paying a man makes him a man who is paid. Asking a man to stop makes him a man who was stopped."

"I have been stood in front of that counter for about nine years and I have never once done any of the three, and I am not going to start in the ninth year because a street has got into it."

The counterman looked at him for a while.

"You have been looked through by me for about nine years and you have never once asked me a thing about me," he said, "and I want you to know that I noticed, and that I never took it as kindness, and that I have got about nine seconds' worth of something else to say and then I have to open."

"Take the nine seconds."

"There is going to be a morning when somebody comes in here and asks me what it costs and I am going to have to think about it for about four seconds longer than I do now," said the counterman. "And I want you to have heard me say that I have thought about it and that I do not know what I will do, because I will not get another chance to say it."

He went back behind the counter.

## The Nine Seconds He Was Going To Take

"You said nine seconds."

"I said nine seconds and I have had three of them and I am spending the other six on something else," said the counterman. "You came down four hundred yards to say a thing to a man in a passage and the thing has been said and now there is about a minute where a person could ask a question and you are the only one in this city who could ask it and you have never asked me one in about four years."

"Ask you what."

"Not here," said the counterman. "Not at that rail, because anything said at a rail in a passage belongs to everybody going past it, and I have watched that work for nine years without ever being on the wrong end of it, and I am not going to start being on the wrong end of it now that there is a price on four flights."

He picked the chalk up off the rail and put it in his pocket.

"You come back in a week," he said, "and you do not come at about twelve, and you do not say anything about this, and I will ask you."

## What Was Left Of The Ninth Of The Month

Lin Hao went back up four flights and got in at about a quarter to two and told Wenna Raut the whole of it in about nine sentences in the ordinary voice with the door open, and she entered nothing.

"The man who is paid nothing is the reason a price exists," he said, "and he has been the reason for nine years, and he is right, and he is the only person in this city who has told me something about my own office this year that I could not have worked out on my own."

"Then what do we do."

"Nothing that is on a piece of paper," he said. "And I have been saying that sentence since the thirtieth of December and I am going to say it about nine more times this month and every time it will be less true than the last."

Four thousand three hundred and fifty-eight days. Less four thousand three hundred and fifty-one, the second of this one, is seven, and a man who is paid nothing told the man whose door it is that every stranger who comes to that counter finds that door because of him, and he had been saying so since the second year and would go on saying it if the card came down tomorrow.

*Less four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is sixty-nine, and the forty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and thirty-eight days, which are sixty-four months and eighteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

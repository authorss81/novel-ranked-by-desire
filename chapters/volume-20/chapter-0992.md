# A Mistake About The Light

Four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven was the twenty-eight of this one and the fifth of the next one is day four thousand five hundred and ninety-four, and four thousand five hundred and ninety-four less four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven, the twenty-eight of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## A Lane Off Cordwain

He went into the lane off Cordwain at about half past ten on the morning of the fifth because a man had stood at the foot of his own stair on the twenty-eighth of October, looked up four flights, and gone back down the arch, and Lin Hao had been thinking about that for eight days and had run out of anywhere else to look.

The lane has nine stools in it and a slate, and it is busy most of the morning, and the man who keeps it is right about every fact he has ever had and writes down what people tell him and not one of his own figures.

There were four people telling him things at once, which is normal there, and he was writing, and he put his chalk down when Lin Hao came in.

"You have come about the hours," said Emil Brack.

## A Line In Somebody Else's Hand

It was in one of the twelve books and it had been in it for about six weeks, entered by somebody who was not Brack, in a hand that leaned the wrong way for a man from that lane.

The three lines were there, with the street and the door at the head of four flights off a third court, and the hours were four in the afternoon.

Lin Hao stood in that lane and read a card in a stranger's handwriting on a page that belonged to a man who sells nine things a day, and the third line was the wrong hours, and the wrong hours had been written down by somebody about six weeks ago because somebody had told them.

"Have you had anybody else tell you that."

"Four," said Brack. "Two of them in October and one of them the day before you walked in here and one of them on the way in this morning with a bundle."

"You are not going to tell me who."

"I am not going to tell you who," said Brack. "You did not come here to find out who."

"Then tell me what you think a man would have to do about it."

"Buy the book off me," said Brack. "And then you would own it, and then in about four years you would have to say out loud what you did with it and where it is, and I am telling you now that you will not do that, so you would be buying it to feel better for about a fortnight."

## What The Author Was Doing In January

The line was there because of the light, and the light part was true, and Lin Hao has not been able to get the true part out from under the other part for ten months.

It started at a counter under a window in a passage off Cordwain in the last week of January, in a sentence a man said out loud to a stranger who could not make out the end of the second line, and it was a kindness, and it was this: the light goes off that street at about ten past four and a card cannot be read after it, so a person ought to be there before ten past four.

Not one person lied. That is what makes it a thing that cannot be traced, because a wrong thing said out loud has no hand on it, and the man who said it was being kind to a person in a hurry and he has never known that it went anywhere.

"It was a true thing about the light," said Lin Hao.

"It is a true thing about the light," said Brack. "That is not what is in my book."

## The Part Where It Stopped Being About The Light

Here is what happened to it, and it happened in about nine weeks and not one person did it.

The kindness had a number in it. A kindness with a number in it is the most easily carried thing in this city, because it is already true and a person does not weigh it. And the number in it was a time, and a time that has been said next to a door stops being a time of day and becomes the hours of that door.

So somewhere between the counter in January and this lane in November, the light went from the reason to the instruction, and the instruction stopped being about the light and became the third line of a card, and the third line of a card is the part a person uses to decide whether to climb four flights.

Not one person put those hours there. That is the whole of it. The hours got there the way a price gets there, by being true of something else first.

He stood in that lane and understood the thing he had spent ten months congratulating himself on.

He had spent ten months standing in the middle of this city arguing that a mouth is the whole of what anybody has got to say a thing with, and in the last week of January he had used one to add a fourth thing to three lines, out loud, to be kind, and the fourth thing had become the third line, and there was no hand on it and there was no page anywhere in this city with his name against it.

A man in a street had worked out that four flights is worth a small amount to a person who cannot manage four flights, and that took this city four months. He did it with a kindness in a single sentence, on his own, at a counter, to one person in a hurry.

"I want it out of that book," he said.

"I know you do."

"Not because it is wrong. Say the rest of it."

"Because if I take four words out of a line somebody else put in a book in my lane, then in about four years somebody in that lane will remember that you came in here and asked me to take it out," said Brack. "And that is how a lane learns what a thing is worth. Not from what is in the books."

## What Brack Would Not Write

"There is a figure in your head," said Lin Hao. "You have had it since the summer and you have not put it on the slate and you have not written it in a book, and it has no unit after it."

"It is still owed."

"Is it out."

"It was out by more than half a quarter in June and it is further out now and I am not going to tell you what it counts, and if I did you would not be able to do anything with it, and you would do something with it."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody in this city has said to me in four months."

"It is the second," said Brack. "You said the first one yourself on the twenty-fourth of March and you said it to a court and it cost you three days off a week you did not have."

He put the chalk down and picked the book up and shut it, and did not offer to show Lin Hao the page again, which was its own kind of sentence.

## At Ten Past Four In The Dark

He said the three lines out loud at ten past four in the dark at the foot of his own stair, on his own second step, in the voice, at the length, with the light already off the wall above his own door for the season.

There was a man at the foot of that stair who had been there about ten minutes, with a bundle, and who had heard every word of it.

Lin Hao stopped.

"Say them again," said the man. "Say the hours. I want to be sure I have got them."

"They run from a quarter to ten to half past five," said Lin Hao, "on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour, and that is the whole of what is on the card."

"I have got them four in the afternoon," said the man. "I have had them four in the afternoon since a woman at the end of a market told me, and she was not trying to be clever, she was telling me the light goes off that street at ten past four and I wanted to be there before it went."

He said them back at the foot of that stair, all three lines, correctly except for the hours, and he said it to be sure, and he said it because he had just heard the right ones and had decided he had the other.

"You heard me say them."

"I heard you say them," said the man. "I have got the other ones off a woman at the end of a market and she was not lying to me and she is not a liar, she just had it the way she had it. You do not think I would go up four flights on the word of a woman at a market when a man in a white plate has told me the other thing."

He looked up the four flights in the dark with the bundle under his arm.

"Tell me which one you want and I will go up."

He said the right hours again, slower, in the ordinary voice, the whole of all three lines, standing on his own step with the arch on one side of him.

Then he went up the four flights.

Four thousand five hundred and ninety-four days. Less four thousand five hundred and eighty-seven, the twenty-eight of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven.

A man stood at the foot of a stair in the dark and heard three lines said to him exactly as they are written on a card at the top of four flights, and went up the stairs with four in the afternoon, and said nothing at all to the woman at the top of that table about having heard the other version first.

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and seventy-four days, which are seventy-two months and fourteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

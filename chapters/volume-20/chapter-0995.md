# The Last Of The Light

Four thousand six hundred and eight was the nineteenth of this one and the twenty-sixth of this one is day four thousand six hundred and fifteen, and four thousand six hundred and fifteen less four thousand six hundred and eight, the nineteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## The Part Of The Day The Card Does Not Serve

The light goes off that street at about ten past four and the card runs to half past five, and for about an hour and a quarter every working day the door at the head of those four flights has an address and a street and no way of being read at all.

He has said the three lines in that hour every working evening since November. He has never once put a lamp in that arch and he is not going to now, and the reason he is not going to now is the same as it was in the summer, and the reason is that a lamp in that arch is a thing a stranger can be told about, and in about four years there will be a man in a lane selling the lamp.

So on the twenty-sixth of November he stood in it again from ten past four, and this time he let himself look at what it actually is.

It is a court with nothing lit in it and about four feet of pavement and an arch a person comes up, and the whole of the instrument available inside that hour is a man saying three sentences.

## What The Paper Version Was For And Who It Is Not For

Everything this office ever did with paper was done in daylight. Every answer that was ever written went into a fold with a date on the outside, carried by hand, and every one of those carries happened between about ten in the morning and about four in the afternoon.

The people the paper version was for are the people who can read a card and turn up in daylight, and the paper version was never for anybody else, and that has been true for nine years and it is not a fault anybody committed.

And the last hour and a quarter is the hour the people who cannot read a card turn up in.

They come later. They come later because they work later, because they are on a floor until it ends, because the whole of what they have done in the day is the reason they cannot get to a court at ten in the morning. They come at twenty to five and they stand under an arch and they ask a person, because a person is the only thing still open.

## What He Decides Not To Build

He worked out, standing there in the cold at about half past four, what would have to be built for them, and he worked it out properly, because he is a man who thinks a thing through before he says it and he had three weeks to say it.

A second card. A board with the three lines large enough to be read at ten past four by a person who cannot see well in the dark. A lamp over the arch. A woman standing at the bottom of that court in the last hour saying them out loud the way he has done every evening since November, on a rota, with a shift, and somebody above her in a list of mornings.

Every one of those five is a copy, and four of them are a list, and the fifth one is him standing there again for another year with his chest already spent since the fourth year.

He said all of it out loud, in the dark, on his own step, to a court with a stranger's bundle on the ground at the wall and not one person standing under the arch at all.

That is not a decision anybody has to keep. It is a thing said once in an empty court in the cold by a man who has to be able to say it again if anybody asks.

## A Man Who Came Up At Ten Past Five

The first of them came at about ten past five and he was in no trouble at all.

He had a question about a floor of a house and he had it in his head and not on a paper, and he wanted it answered out loud because he could not read the answer if it were written, and Lin Hao said it out loud at the length, standing side-on to him in the dark so that the words went along the arch and not across it, and the man got it, and asked for the hours twice, and got them twice.

That is the whole of what the last hour and a quarter of the card's hours has ever been for, and it works, and it has worked every working evening since November, and there is nothing about it that has changed since the twenty-fourth of March.

About half past four it stops working and it is not because anybody has done anything wrong.

## A Woman Who Could Not Hear Him

There was a woman at the foot of that stair at about twenty to five with a shawl over her head and a parcel and she had come up that arch from the street and she had not seen him standing a foot to one side of his own door.

"Say that again," she said. "I could not hear you the first time."

He said it again, all three lines, correctly, at the length.

"I still cannot hear you."

He said it a third time, louder, from the same place.

That is when he understood. He was standing on his own second step from the bottom with his face to the arch and his back to her, and there was nothing in that court to light the face of anybody standing at the top of it, and she was about four feet off him in the dark and the whole of the instrument was the wrong way round.

A man in a white plate can read a card at four feet. A man in a white plate in the dark at four feet, with his back to a woman who cannot read, is a thing she can hear and not understand.

"Say it over my shoulder," she said. "Say it walking."

## What He Did Instead Of The Lamp

He turned round and said it over her shoulder, into her ear, with his mouth about four inches from it, and she got it, and she asked him the hours once more to be sure, and he gave them to her twice.

Then she went up the four flights and he stood on his own step and listened to her go up.

He has stood at the bottom of that stair for about seven months in the last hour of every working day and told himself that the dark was the reason the mouth was better than the number.

It was. He said those three lines to strangers off a piece of card for a year because a man can say three lines in the dark and a card cannot, and that was the whole of the winter of year twelve and it was good.

It was also the reason the mouth does not work. He worked that out standing on his own step at about ten to five on the twenty-sixth of November with a woman four feet off him saying say it again, and both of those facts are the dark, and he has been carrying them as two for ten months.

The light going off that street at ten past four is what put a woman at a market in front of a stranger at a counter in January, and it is also what stands between that woman and the four flights of a court every working evening from November to the middle of February.

It is the same fact. He has spent ten months treating it as two.

## What He Said Upstairs About It

He went up the four flights at about ten to five and said it again in that room with the door open, because a thing said once in a court belongs to the court and a thing said once in a room belongs to the room, and he wanted it in the room.

He said: this office is not going to put up a second card, and it is not going to put a lamp in that arch, and it is not going to put a person at the bottom of that stair in the dark on a rota. He said the reasons in the order he had worked them out.

Wenna Raut wrote nothing down, which she has not done at that table since the twenty-first of October and is not going to start for a sentence he has said out loud.

"You have just described a practice," she said.

"I have described four things we are not going to do, which is a different thing, and you can prove it is a different thing in about four years by asking the three of us separately."

"And what will we say."

"That we decided it in November," said Lin Hao, "and that we will each say it differently, and that a woman can come up those stairs in about four years and ask which of us was right and get three answers, and that is what she will get, and she should have it said to her at the time."

## At Half Past Five

At half past five he said the three lines to an empty court at the length and at the pause, because it is the last working day before the month turns and because that is what the hour is for.

Four thousand six hundred and fifteen days. Less four thousand six hundred and eight, the nineteenth of this one, is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven.

A man stood in the dark at the foot of his own stair for an hour and a quarter and found out that the instrument he put there in November is the only one in this city that works after ten past four. It does not work at four feet. The people it is for are standing at the bottom of that stair saying say it again.

*Less four thousand five hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fifty-six, and the fifty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-six of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand one hundred and ninety-five days, which are seventy-three months and five days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

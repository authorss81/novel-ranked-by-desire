# The Bottom Of His Own Stair

Four thousand three hundred and sixty-five was the sixteenth of this one and the twenty-third of this one is day four thousand three hundred and seventy-two, and four thousand three hundred and seventy-two less four thousand three hundred and sixty-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## Six Things, And A Woman Who Counts Them

He did not bring it up at a quarter to ten. He brought it up at about half past eleven, with the book on the table and the door standing open and the room full of people who are about to go down four flights, because a thing decided quietly in a room is decided by whoever wants it decided.

"Count something for me," he said.

Wenna Raut put her pen down.

"Count the six of them," said Lin Hao. "Not the rows. The six. I want to hear them said out loud in order and I want you to tell me whether that is the whole of what is available."

"Say the first."

"A list of comers is refused. It has been refused out loud with the door open and it was refused on the twenty-first of October and not one list goes in anywhere, not a roll, not an index, not a bracket, not a figure for a quarter and not a number handed to anybody in that room."

"Second."

"A correction of a copy is refused. I have said that about nine times this month and I am going to keep saying it."

"Third."

"A copy is refused. The three lines go up on three nails and not one word of them goes down on a piece of anything, in his hand or anybody's hand."

"Fourth."

"A transcript is refused, and that is a different thing from a copy and I have watched a woman travel a long way with one."

"Fifth."

"A third written refusal is not coming. There are two in that book and there will not be another, and that was said on the floor of a lane in August and has not been improved on since."

"And the sixth."

"The sixth is not a refusal," said Lin Hao. "There is nothing in this city that covers a man standing still. Every instrument this office has is about a person who has come to a door and asked for something, and a man who is not asking for anything is outside all of them, and has been outside all of them for nine years."

## She Did Not Agree With The Count Of Six

"That is five," said Wenna Raut.

"It is six."

"It is five and an absence," she said. "You have dressed an absence up as a refusal because you wanted the list to come out even, and I have watched you do that four times this year and I have watched it work every time and I have never once agreed with it."

"Then what is the sixth."

"The sixth is the thing that is not there," said Wenna Raut. "And you cannot refuse a thing that is not there, and you cannot say its price out loud with the door open either, because the moment you do you have turned it into a rule, and rules go on a wall four hundred yards off and a wall is what this office is."

The room was quiet for about four seconds.

"That is better than mine," he said.

"It is not better than yours. It is the same thing with the ribbon off, and you have got very good at putting the ribbon on." She picked her pen up and then put it down again. "Say the next thing."

## What Is Left When The Paper Is Gone

"There is one thing left that is not paper," said Lin Hao, "and I have known it since about the fourth of February and I have not said it to anybody because it is the last private thing I own."

"Say it, then."

"Where I am."

He turned and looked at the stair through the open door.

"If a man is standing at the bottom of four flights of a third court between a quarter to ten and half past five, then nobody can be taken to that door, because anybody who wants to go there has to walk past him first. A man being shown the way is the whole of what is being sold, and a man who is at the bottom is not a man who can be shown. He is the arriving."

"And they cannot charge for reaching him."

"They cannot charge for reaching him because the reaching has already happened," he said. "You do not buy your way to a man who is standing where you are going. You buy a man with a coin who walks you up four flights, and you do it because he is not there, and the whole of the price is that he is not there."

## The Worse Half

"There is a second half and it is worse and I am going to say it now rather than let somebody find it out in a lane."

"Say that part."

"A man at the bottom of his own stair is a man standing in the only public room this office has ever had," said Lin Hao. "I have spent nine years not being found. That was not modesty. That was a man who had worked out that every road anybody can follow to that door arrives at a person who can be described afterwards, and he has been hiding from the describing."

"And from tomorrow morning I am going to be standing in the one place in this city where a stranger can look at me for as long as they like without a piece of paper between them," said Lin Hao.

He put his hand on the back of the chair and left it there and did not say the next part for about four seconds.

"And there is nothing in this city that can stop that either, and I am not going to write a rule about it, because I have understood this month that the moment I write it down it is a thing that can be copied, and I have been beaten by a copy before and it took two years."

## The Price Of It, Said Before It Is Spent

"Then say what it costs," said Wenna Raut, "because you have said the part that sounds like a decision four times this month and not once the cost."

"It is this, and I am saying it with the door open and before I have done it once."

"I lose about four hundred yards. Every person who comes to that door now walks past a man standing at the bottom of it, and every one of them will know exactly who is under it before their hand is on that door."

"Every carrier's back is visible from that stair for as long as this goes on, and the woman with the bad back has counted the noise of that stair for nine years and I have just made it the loudest thing in it."

"And about four people a day will learn my face before they learn anything else, and I am the only person in this city whose face is worth learning."

He stopped.

"That is the part of it I have the least idea how to put right," he said. "And there is a fifth, and the fifth is the one I care about."

"I work four nights in seven at a branch off a third court and I have three off, and this is five days in seven on the mornings. It comes out of the three off. I am not moving the night shift and I am not asking for anything. What I am doing is spending the only thing I have left that nothing in this city has ever cost me."

"That is the whole cost."

"That is the whole cost," he said, "and I have not done it once yet, and I am telling you all of it now because in about four days somebody is going to tell me I had said it and got it wrong, and I want the saying of it to be in a room with the door open and not on a wall."

## From Tomorrow

"From tomorrow," he said. "The twenty-fourth of this month. A quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, at the foot of that stair."

"That is a practice."

"It is not a practice, because a practice has to be written on the practice sheet by a clerk, and this one is not going to be written down, and it is not a rule because it does not concern anybody but me." He looked at Wenna Raut. "You write nothing. Not the date, not the hour, not the fact that I said it."

"I am not writing it," she said.

"Then from tomorrow there will be a man at the bottom of that stair and no page anywhere in this city that says so, and in about four years somebody will come up four flights and ask me why there was a man at the bottom of that stair for a year, and I will tell him that I stood there, and he will not be able to check it, and that will be the arrangement."

## What He Has Also Decided And Has Not Said

There was one more thing and he did not say it out loud, and nobody argued it, because it had not been said to anybody and he had not done it once.

He had also decided that he was not going to be the only one.

That decision was in no room, in no book, and on no piece of paper, and he had not mentioned it to the carrier with the bad back and was not going to mention it to the clerk, and on the morning of the twenty-fourth of this month he was going to stand at the bottom of four flights on three mornings a week that he had told no person about.

## Half Past Five

The book went down at half past five by his own hands and four flights and about four hundred yards, and it was not upstairs overnight, and the light had gone off that street about an hour and a quarter before, and the card on that door could not be read by anybody standing in front of it.

He put it down in the flat file and came back, and on the way back he stopped at the bottom of his own stair at about twenty to six with his hands empty, which is the only way he ever comes down it.

There was a wall about the height of a man on the other side of that landing and nothing else to stand on, and the stair was empty, and he stood there for about four seconds and then said the three lines out loud, once, into an empty stair, from memory, in the ordinary voice.

Tomorrow was the twenty-fourth. On the thirtieth a sheet comes down off that rail at about four in the afternoon and another goes up on the same two nails, and a man who is paid nothing will read a thing out loud at a counter under a window with about nine strangers in that passage, and neither of those things is his business any more, and the first morning at the bottom of that stair is tomorrow.

Four thousand three hundred and seventy-two days. Less four thousand three hundred and sixty-five, the sixteenth of this one, is seven, and a man who is rank one and works four nights in seven at a branch off a third court decided at about half past eleven on the twenty-third of this month, with the door open and a clerk in the room who wrote nothing down, to spend his three days off standing at the bottom of his own stair from a quarter to ten to half past five.

*Less four thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is eighty-three, and the forty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand nine hundred and fifty-two days, which are sixty-five months and two days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

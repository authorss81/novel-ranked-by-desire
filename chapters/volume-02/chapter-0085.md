# The Locked Office Opens

On the fifth of the month Colm Deveraux unlocked his office door at the branch for the first time since the ninth of the season, and Lin Hao was mopping the corridor when the key turned.

He felt a jolt of something between hope and dread. He wanted the man to come out and say the thing plainly.

Colm stood in the doorway with a bad shoulder and a duster in his hand he had clearly not used. The office behind him was dark and smelled of paper left shut.

"You can come in," Colm said. "You cannot clean it. I am going to clean it."

Lin Hao put the mop against the wall and stood in the doorway. He did not go in.

The room was smaller than the corridor. There was a desk, a chair, a shelf of returns that had gone grey at the edges, and a window that gave onto the back of the returns store.

Colm looked at the corridor board with the two lines on it, typed, and the strip on the spike.

"A woman in a dark coat came here on the twentieth of last month," Colm said. "She asked me whether anybody had asked me for names. I said no. She said that was very useful. Then she asked me about arithmetic and I told her."

"I know. Ola Bright has written her down twice in a week and neither time was near this floor."

"She came to the counter and then she came to the floor and then she stopped coming to me and started on the porters." Colm put the duster down. "The Office asked me the same three questions last week. I answered them. I did not tell them about her until yesterday, because I was frightened."

For Colm, who had kept a door shut for months, opening it meant every clerk on the floor could see how small the room was.

Lin Hao kept his voice level. "What did you tell them."

"That I gave her two answers for nothing in an ordinary voice at my own counter. That I have been frightened since. That I am still frightened." Colm looked at the floor. "You refused them in a room and it cost you a calendar. I gave them away at a counter and it cost me nothing at the time, and now it is going to cost me the room."

He moved the chair out from the desk and then did not sit on it, and then sat on it, badly, with the shoulder propped up.

"There is a sitting," he said. "The twelfth. They are taking the arithmetic as a district matter because it came out of a corridor that has a finding number on it, and they want it read out by the man who held it, and I have been told to attend and I have not been told what happens if I attend and say the same three answers I have said all year."

"Who told you."

"A clerk with a card, in the corridor, at ten past one, who had a list of eight names on it and mine was the sixth." Colm rubbed the shoulder. "Eight names, and the other seven are on the second floor, and it is a thing they could not do by asking one man at his own desk, so they have made it a sitting. That is the part I want somebody to hear. They have not trusted the counter. They have gone and made a room."

He opened the second drawer and took out a folded sheet and put it on the desk, squared.

"You told me once to write my own episode up. I did. It is in the drawer and it is nine pages and it has the date on every line of it and my name at the top and I have not shown it to a living soul, because showing it to a living soul is what a man does when he has decided that being seen is cheaper than being wrong about it."

"You have decided that."

"No." Colm pushed the sheet a half inch toward him. "I have decided that sitting on the twelfth and saying the same three answers in a room is worse than showing one page of paper, and I have not decided anything else. I have not decided to be brave. I have decided to be arithmetic."

Lin Hao looked at the sheet and did not touch it.

"I am not going to read it."

"I know. I am not offering it." Colm took it back and put it in the drawer and shut the drawer with his good hand. "If you read it, it becomes yours. And then at the twelfth, if they ask you whether you have seen my account, you have to decide in front of them what a man who has read it does with what he read, and you will not have had a night's sleep and I would rather not do that to you."

An attendance clerk came down the corridor with a sheet and stopped when he saw the open door, and stood there long enough for it to be a decision rather than a pause.

Colm handed him the duster. "I am opening the office. I am not asking to be moved. If there is a finding against the arithmetic I held, I will answer it."

The clerk wrote that down, and wrote down the door, and went on.

Two of the returns clerks from the second floor came down after him and stood in the corridor and looked into the room, and did not come in, and one of them said, "Colm, is this a thing you are doing or a thing that has been done to you."

"It is a thing I am doing."

"Will there be a sitting."

"On the twelfth."

The second clerk put his head round the doorframe and looked at the grey edges of the returns and the dark window and came back out again. "Then I would leave it open all day," he said. "Not because it helps. Because if it is shut they will say afterwards that nobody could tell what was in it."

He went up. Colm stood in the doorway with the duster still in his hand.

"That is the cost," he said. "Not the twelfth. The corridor. Nine people on this floor have now seen the inside of that room and there is nothing remarkable in it, and every one of them will now know that there is a thing in my life that I kept in there, and there was not, and that is the whole of what I have done. I have given nine people a reason to wonder."

"You could have said you had a chest cold."

"I could have said a chest cold," Colm agreed, "and then I would have opened it in June."

"Sit down for a minute," Lin Hao said, "before the corridor fills up."

Colm sat on the edge of the desk. "I am not asking you to forgive me. That is not on the table and I am not going to put it on the table at nine in the morning on my own floor."

"I know you are not."

"What I am going to ask for is one thing and it is small and I have thought about how to phrase it." He put his hands on the desk edge and left them there.

"Do not vouch for me. When they ask you at the twelfth, or when they ask somebody in that room who knows me, do not stand up and say I told you, or that I was straight with you, or anything of that shape."

"Because the moment you vouch, the finding is about both of us. And if it is about both of us then it is about a man who has spent nothing since the middle of last month and a man who gave two answers at a counter, and I would rather be the second one on his own."

The mop was still leaning against the wall and the corridor was filling up behind him with the morning shift.

"That is a strange thing to ask a man to do."

"It is the only thing I can think of that is worth anything to me and costs you nothing," Colm said. "You have got nothing to lose about me. I have got nothing but."

Lin Hao stood in the doorway for a moment.

"If they ask you what the dark coat wanted," he said, "she wanted three questions and a list of the names you had spoken to in a fortnight. She did not want the arithmetic. She already had the arithmetic by then. That is what nobody at the twelfth will tell you, because they will tell you it was the arithmetic that gave her the building, and she got the building off a man at a gate in the ordinary course of an ordinary morning."

Colm's shoulder came down an inch. "Say that again."

"You did not know that."

"I knew it came off a gate and I did not know it came off nothing." Colm picked the duster up and looked at it. "Forty days on my own floor and she never once asked me a number. She asked me a man. She has been asking my porters for names and she has had my names since the twentieth and she has not come back to me once."

"Then it was not you she was using."

"It was a corridor I was standing in," Colm said, "and that is the part I am going to have to say out loud on the twelfth, and I would like to have said it to somebody before I say it there."

Lin Hao picked up the mop. The hope stayed and the dread stayed with it, because a man who had hidden for months had come out and the coming out did not fix anything.

"You washed that date off twice," Colm said. "The eleventh. I saw you do it. Do not wash it off again where I can see you."

"I will not."

"I am going to open this door every morning from now on," Colm said, "including the mornings I do not want to, including the ones after the twelfth. And I am going to keep this desk and this chair and that finding number on the spike outside, and if they take the room off me at the end of it then it will be because they wanted it and not because I left it shut."

He went back to his washrooms with the office open behind him, and the corridor was different for a door being open, and it was worse, because now there was nowhere for either of them to pretend, and because in seven days a room would be full of men who came to a man with a bad shoulder and an arithmetic he had been holding for four months.

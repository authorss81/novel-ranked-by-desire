# The Empty Trolley

On the seventeenth, the only Thursday inside the hold, the machine on the fourth floor did not run and the bell did not ring and the strip did not come off it, and at eleven o'clock Drant put an envelope on the tray and stood looking at it for a while.

"There is a forwarding," he said. "It goes at four whether there is a strip in it or not, because a forwarding that goes late is a forwarding that did not happen, and the book upstairs has a line for late and a line for missing and I would rather be late."

"What goes in it."

"That is the whole question and I have had it since the eleventh and I have not asked you because I did not want to put it to a man at nine in the morning while it was still theoretical." He turned the envelope over. "I can send nothing, which is what has happened in this building for nineteen years, never, not once.

Or I can send a sheet with the figure on it and the date of the reading underneath it, which is a true document, because it says what it is and when, and it is six days old by the time it arrives, and it is in your hand or mine and I would rather it was mine."

"What happens upstairs if it arrives with nothing in it."

"I do not know," said Drant, "and I have thought about it for two days and I still do not, and that is the first time in nineteen years I have sent a piece of paper into that building without knowing what it will do. An envelope that arrives empty is an event. A figure that arrives six days old is a figure. And an event is the only thing in this business that makes people talk."

Lin Hao looked at the envelope.

He wanted the Office to have a number from him, any number, six days old or fresh, because for six days there was nothing anywhere in Ostara that said he was alive, and he had spent a fortnight teaching six women that a building with nothing arriving in it was a description of a man.

"Send nothing," he said.

Drant did not move.

"Say it again, because I am going to have to enter why."

"There is no why. You are a clerk and a forwarding went out on its day and there was nothing on the machine to forward, and that is the whole of the entry and it is the truest thing this floor has produced in a month." He put his finger on the envelope.

"And if you write a figure on a sheet with a date under it, then for the rest of this hold there is a number in a building in this city that says I read eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and somebody will copy it onto something, and within a month it will be in a return with my name at the top of it, and there will be no way afterwards to establish that it was ever true on any day.

I would rather be absent than stale."

That was the reasoning and the second half of it was true and the first half of it was fear, and Drant entered neither.

"Attendance," he wrote, "seventeenth of the month. Fourth floor. Figure not produced. Reason: no reading taken at the place of score. Forwarding made and empty."

He squared it and read it back and signed it, and did not ask Lin Hao to sign it, which was the third time in five weeks he had not asked.

The trolley went down the long road at half past three.

Lin Hao walked it as far as the corner, because he had walked it as far as the corner every Thursday for five weeks.

A trolley is a low flat thing on two wheels with a rail round the edge of it. On the thirty-first of last month it had gone down that road with a strip on it and a bell had already rung.

On the seventeenth it went down the same road past the same nine doorways with the rail empty and the spike behind it bare, and a woman selling fish outside the arcade called out to ask whether the machine had broken.

"Nothing has broken," he said. "It is a hold."

"A what."

"A hold," he said, and she went back to her barrow, and it turned out afterwards that she told two people on the line and that one of them was Ottilie Prebble.

At half past four a woman with a card came into the Vestry corridor and stood at the end of it by the office door with her coat still on, and she was not from the Exchange and she was not from the clinic, and she had the manner of a person who has been told to be punctual and has arrived early and is sorry about it.

"Lin Hao," she said, "I am Tove Marlin. I am a clerk in the Classification Wing and I have a form with your name on it and it is not a summons, and I would like you to know that before I ask you anything, because people hear the word and then they stop hearing the rest of it."

He felt the hollow thing come up, the one that is not fear and is worse: being looked at by somebody ordinary.

"Go on."

"You did not present at the place of score today. That is the whole of it. An absent reading is an absence of input and the office requires an account of the absence, and it is made by the member." She held the form against her chest and did not offer it. "I am not permitted to take your answer on this corridor.

I am permitted to ask you whether you will give an account, and if you say you will, I am to write the day and the hour and send a runner for you to come and do it in a room."

"And if I say no."

"Then I write account declined and I write the hour, and I go back up, and that is a lawful answer and I have written six of them this month for six other people and it is not a remarkable thing." Tove Marlin said it evenly and not unkindly. "That is the whole of my errand. I wanted to say it to you in a corridor rather than have it arrive as paper, because paper reads like a threat and a corridor does not."

"Then say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it. There is the account of the absence, and the account is why your plate was not read for six days, and there is a form for that with a heading on it, and the heading is not mine and I am not going to say the heading." She shifted her weight. "I will say this and I will say it once and then I am going to go back up and write what I have to write.

"There is a paper with your name on it going to a desk on the eighteenth. It was drafted this morning. I do not know what is on it, I am not permitted to read it, and I am telling you that it exists because a man who is told the day before a paper lands can spend a night doing something about it, and I would rather he did that than find out on the eighteenth what everybody else in that building found out on the seventeenth."

Lin Hao stood very still.

"You are not permitted to read it."

"I am not permitted to know whether I am permitted to read it," said Tove Marlin, "which is the honest form of it, and which is why I am still standing on this corridor at half past four on a Thursday instead of sitting down."

A ward runner came past them with a chart under her good arm and her bad wrist tucked in, and did not slow down, and did not look at the woman with the card, and went into the office and shut the door, and the office at the end of that corridor had a desk in it and a girl in it and two days left of not carrying boards.

"You have a floor to work on," Tove Marlin said, and looked at the doors on both sides. "How many quiet rooms is this."

"Four."

"Four," she said, and wrote nothing, and put the form back inside her coat, and said the last of it at the door on her way out.

"Thursday to Thursday. Six days is five intervals. The office counts intervals and it does not count days, and I do not know why I am telling you that and I have been told not to be helpful."

She went up the stairs and Lin Hao stood in a corridor with four shut doors on one side and an office at the end of it, and the grey at his temple was in its twenty-fifth day and it had come past the ridge and settled along the bone above the ear where it was going to stay for some time.

It was the twenty-fifth day of a thing that had gone in a day and a half for four years, and there had been no number printed anywhere in this city since the fourteenth to say that the man causing it was still being measured, and the only proof that he existed that had left a building in six days was an envelope with nothing in it that a clerk had entered and signed.

At eight that evening the empty forwarding was on a desk in a building he had never been inside, and somebody in that building would open it, and somebody would decide that an envelope with nothing in it was not a figure and was not a silence, and would write the word enclosure absent in a column in a book on the seventh floor, and that word would go down a corridor the following morning and be read by four people who had never spoken to him.

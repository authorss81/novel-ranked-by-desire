# The Unfiled Line

The sitting on the twelfth was held on the second floor of the Reading, in the records room, because the room on the third floor had been shut since the twenty-sixth with nine chairs standing in the corridor outside it.

Lin Hao went and stood at the back and did not sit, and there were eight names on the sheet and eight men in the room and Colm was sixth, and he had promised a man in a doorway on the fifth of the month that he would not vouch for him.

He felt the shame of it early and it stayed the whole hour. He wanted the finding to be about a counter and a bad shoulder and a fortnight of hand addition, and the only sentence that would have moved it off him was the one he had given away on the fifth, and he had made a promise with it.

The clerk read the arithmetic. It was four months of Colm Deveraux adding the fourth-floor readings against the branch aggregate by hand, every day, on a pad, because the printed aggregate and the floor disagreed by a constant and the constant did not move.

"Seventeen weeks," the clerk said. "A constant. And the constant is not a person and is not a shift and is not a season. It is present in every week of the pad and it is present on the days when the floor is shut."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"The source of the variation is not established," the clerk said, and wrote it, and read it back before he lifted the pen. "Finding against the arithmetic of the fourth-floor counter. Attendance of the holder of the arithmetic kept. The variation is unexplained and the office notes that the floor has been in use throughout and that the aggregate is not small."

Colm said, "That is true," and was told to stop, and stopped.

Then the man in the second chair asked where the pad had come from.

"From me," Colm said. "Out of my own head, on my own floor, on my own time, every day for four months."

"You added it against what."

"Against the aggregate, and against the corridor board, and against six months of strips I kept in a drawer."

"Which is where the pad should be," the chair said.

"It is where the pad should be and it is not there, because a pad kept by a clerk for four months is a document about the branch and I am not permitted to open a branch file, and if I could not find a place to put it I left it where I keep my own arithmetic, which is my own head."

The room did the thing rooms do.

Lin Hao put his hand flat on the back of the chair in front of him. He could have said that a woman in a dark coat had taken the building off a gate at four in the morning and had never once wanted the arithmetic, and that she had it by the twentieth and came back for a man instead.

He had said it on the fifth and he had said it well, and he had promised in a doorway not to say a word of that shape in a room with Colm in it, and a promise made in a doorway is worth more than a finding entered in a room.

He said nothing, and it took him about four seconds, and he counted them.

The clerk entered the finding and it went up the table and came back with a number printed in the corner, and the number was for the finding and not for the pad, and the pad was not evidence and the finding did not need it.

Then the chair said the last thing, which was read out of the minute by the clerk because he was not permitted to say it himself.

"The office notes that the variation is constant across seventeen weeks and that no document produced before this office accounts for it. The office further notes that a document exists, entered voluntarily on the seventh of last month by an agent of the district, which is not a district record and which the office has not been permitted to read."

The room was very quiet.

Lin Hao understood it in the ordinary way, the way you understand a thing that has already happened. The four-sided sheet. Side one, the line reading himself. Side two, the arithmetic and a heading and no names. Eleven places he had watched him stand in. Two questions. And under the twelve lines a twelfth, empty, with one word printed over it.

RECIPIENT. Entered on the seventh, by hand, by an agent, as a thing volunteered and received, and it had sat in a district file for five weeks being nothing at all.

The Office had known there was a paper since the seventh.

The Office had known there was a paper since the seventh and had not known what was on it, and had now written down in a finding that it existed, and a finding with a number on it is an instrument, and an instrument can be applied to a document.

Lin Hao went down the stairs and out into the square and stood in the cold with his hands in his pockets for about two minutes, and then walked to Lantern Row, because there was one person in Ostara who could tell him what had just happened and it was the woman the sentence was about.

She was at her own table with a lamp on it and four books open and none of them hers, and she had been at that table every working day for three years and had taught herself to do two desks at once.

"Sit down," she said. "You have come about the twelfth."

"You have heard."

"I have a sister who is on the intake sheet at that building and there are two clerks in the Reading who owe me small things, and one of them sent me a line at four this afternoon." Ines turned a book round so the head of it faced him. "Read that sentence back to me."

"It says a document exists, entered voluntarily on the seventh by an agent of the district, which is not a district record and which the office has not been permitted to read."

"That is the whole of it and it has a number on it now," Ines said. "That is not what I want you to hear. What I want you to hear is the last four words. The office has not been permitted to read it. No one refused them. No one has asked. There is no refusal in that sentence, because there was nothing to refuse when it was written."

"They are going to ask on the thirteenth."

"They asked twice before the fourth of this month," she said, "both times from the same desk, both times with a form attached and both times in a voice doing a job, and it was a production request under a heading about records held by an agent of the district, and it was general. Everything I have touched since the Review that has not gone into a district file. That is a form you answer with a list."

"And the third time."

"The third time is a form you answer with a document." She squared it. "A general request is a question about my work. A request with a finding number against it is a question about me. On the morning of the thirteenth there will be a piece of paper on this desk with a number in the corner of it that did not exist yesterday, and it will ask for one thing, and the one thing will be the sheet."

She put her hand flat on it.

"You have had that sheet in your coat since the sixth of last month."

"It is in my coat."

"Then on the thirteenth it stops being a question," Ines said, "and I would like to say what I have decided and I would like to say it once and I would like you not to answer it."

He waited.

"I am not producing it and I am not hiding it." She said it flatly, the way she said the heavy things. "There is no third position and I have looked for one every night since the fourth. I am going to refuse, in writing, in my own hand, signed and dated, and I am going to give the reason, because an agent who refuses without a reason is an agent who has something.

And the reason is going to be true and it is going to be about the document and not about you."

"That puts you outside procedure."

"It puts me outside procedure in front of a document and not in front of a man," she said, "and that is the only difference there is and I have been sitting on it since the fourth and I would like you to know that I have been sitting on it since the fourth."

"How much is it worth."

"It gets a number." She put her pen down.

"It gets a number on my ticket and it gets a review of whether this district should hold my ticket, and I have three private offers in a drawer, from three different people who do not know why I am free. I have not taken any of them and I am not going to, and my sister goes back to work on the first, and I would rather be an agent with a review on her than a woman with a flat and a habit and no ticket."

Then she stopped, and put both hands flat on the table, which was a thing she did when she was about to say something she had been carrying for three days.

"You did not say anything this morning," she said. "Colm asked me on the ninth whether the man who came to the fifth had been in the room at the twelfth, and I said I did not know, because I did not know. I have found out that you went and stood at the back and did not sit down."

"He asked me not to vouch for him."

"He asked you not to stand up in a room and say that a man told you something true." Ines's voice did not change at all, which was how he knew she had thought about it for three days.

"You have kept a promise given in a doorway and it has cost a man his arithmetic and his room and his own name in a finding, and you did the right thing and I want you to hear me say the other half of it, which is that the right thing and the useful thing came apart in that room at about a quarter past eleven and you did not know which one you were choosing while you were choosing it."

Lin Hao looked at his hands.

"I would have said it," he said. "I want that minuted. If you are writing, write that I would have said it and did not."

"I am not writing it."

"Write it."

"No," said Ines. "If I write that, then in six years a clerk will read it and decide that you are a man who holds a fact back under instruction, and there is nothing in the whole of this month that is more dangerous to you than being a man who holds things back. You did not hold it back. He asked you for it and you gave it to him and it is his." She picked her pen up again. "Let him have the doorway.

He has been on his own in it for four months and you are the only person who has ever gone into it with him and come out."

"My sister went on the ninth," she said. "It was a chest. That is all I am going to tell you about it, and I would like you not to ask me the rest of it while I am holding a decision about your coat."

The three books open on her table were not hers. One of them was the intake register at that building and it had a line on it, and the line had been struck through, and a struck-through line in an intake register is the whole of what a person gets when they go and come back.

"Tomorrow," she said. "It goes at half past eight in the morning by the counter hand and it is dated the fourteenth, because I wrote it last week on a day when it was true and I will not sign a thing I have not slept on.

And you are going to come to this desk on the twentieth and I am going to tell you what it says, and you are not going to like it, and I am telling you now so that you can spend the week thinking about it and not the day."

She turned the lamp down a half turn.

"Go and be somewhere else for a while," she said. "You have brought a number into my rooms and I would like it to leave by itself."

He went down the stairs with a finding number that had been attached to a piece of paper in his own coat for five weeks, and the paper was still warm, and there was nothing on the fourth side under the word printed there but his own hand and nothing else.

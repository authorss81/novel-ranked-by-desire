# The Forty Days

He did the arithmetic on the back of a paper bag on the counter in the kitchen of the room above the chop-house, and then he did it again on the table, and then he asked Nadia Ferrous to do it because she worked a timetable and could count without being asked twice.

Nadia put her token down.

"From what."

"From the last time it moved. Not the last time it was spent. Those are different and there is nobody in this city who knows they are different except a clerk in a sub-level and a woman with a book who does not know she has said it out loud."

"Then give me the date it last moved."

"The thirteenth of last month. In the afternoon. In the square."

Nadia Ferrous did not do it in her head. She took a pencil and she wrote it down, which he had never once seen her do for anything, and she wrote on the back of his paper bag and she used the counter as a table.

"Seventeen days to the end of that month," she said. "Twenty-three more into this one. Forty."

"Yes."

"The twenty-third."

"The twenty-third," said Lin Hao. "Five days ago. It is the twenty-eighth and it is five days past and I have been counting from the wrong end since the third of this month, because I have been counting from the day I stopped spending instead of the day it stopped moving, and those were the same day until the thirteenth of last month and are not the same day now."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"What does that mean."

"It means you do not get forty days from the last time you spent," Nadia said. "You get forty days from the last time anything moved in there, and nothing has moved since the thirteenth of last month, so the count started again that afternoon, and it ran out five days ago while you were cleaning washrooms."

The room above the shop was very quiet.

"I have been standing still for three weeks and I thought I was buying something," he said.

"You were standing still. That is all standing still is." She put the pencil down on the bag. "Do you want me to say the other part."

"Yes."

"You have been buying something," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is what you told Ines on the third of the month and what you told yourself in a room two floors under the Exchange on the same day. You have been standing still because a standing inquiry wants you to stand still and you have thought that refusing to spend was taking something away from it.

It has not. Every day you have not spent is a day the forty has been running, and it has been running since the thirteenth of last month, and it did not stop because you refused and it would not have stopped if you had spent either. Standing still is not a thing you do to an office. Standing still is what an office does to you and the only instrument it has is a duration, and a duration costs the person it is applied to absolutely nothing to run."

"You have thought about this."

"I have thought about nothing else since the tenth of the month in a lift with a woman who writes down what I say," said Nadia. "I gave her your name. I have had eighteen days to work out what to do with the fact that I gave a stranger your name, and this is what I have come up with, and it is arithmetic and I am sorry."

She put her finger on the bag.

"There are two of those costs and both of them are already on the page and neither of them is anybody's fault. One of them is that the mass goes flat, and a woman with a rack has been telling you about that since March and has had it at twenty-seven on a wall for eleven days because you have not spent once.

And the other one is a map with a dot on it that is getting truer every week you do nothing."

Lin Hao looked up.

"The dot is in a square," he said.

"The dot is where you stood," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is all a dot on a map is.

It is the place where a thing happened, and a thing happened on the thirteenth of last month and it will have happened again if you ever spend, and every week you stand still there is one week less between the two of them, and an analyst with a pencil and a month of marks can turn that into a line and a line into a name, and Ruta Vesely went up three floors on the twenty-sixth and closed that room and told you it was over.

It is not over. She closed it because you gave her a sentence that made it over. The next one she draws will be on a real event and it will have three addresses on it and there will be no sentence anybody can give her to make it not a map."

"And you have known that since the fourth."

"I have known that since the fourth of the month and I did not say it because I gave your name to a woman in a lift and I did not want to be the woman who came up here and added a thing to a pile," said Nadia.

"So I have been sitting on it for twenty-four days waiting for a better time and there has not been one, and I am saying it now because a man who has been counting from the wrong end since the third has not got eighteen days left in this and neither have I."

He did the second sum on the bag while she watched.

"You are wrong about one thing and I am going to correct you because somebody has to and you have been polite all evening."

"Go on."

"It is five days past and I have no way of knowing whether that is the good news or the bad news, and that is the part that has been sitting in the room since the twenty-sixth and will not go out of it." He turned the bag round. "A thing that goes flat is a thing that can be described. That is the rule and every version of it that anybody in this city knows says the same.

And on the fifth day after it went flat, my plate is reading eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and the margin is three and the strip in three days will say the same figure it has said four times.

So either it has not gone flat, which means the number is wrong, which means forty days is a number in a corridor and not a rule, which means the Office has been telling the truth for sixty years about a thing they do not have a machine for.

Or it has gone flat, and a flat figure is a figure that can be described. Four streets away there is an office that has had a strip of it every Thursday for a month and has spent all of that time asking me a question about the interval, and there is an Exchange that has been building a return form on the flatness of it.

And a girl at a counter in my own building has signed a true document twice that will be read as a drain on me.

And I have no instrument. There is nothing in this city I could look at and say yes or no."

"You have one," said Nadia Ferrous.

He looked at her.

"You have one and you have had it since the twenty-second and it is in a drawer in a room off a corridor with four beds at the end of it, and she is going to open it on the twenty-ninth at about nine in the morning, and you have already told her what hour and you have already told her to ask you at nine instead of in the afternoon." Nadia stood up and put the token in her pocket.

"So you do have an instrument and you are the only man in Ostara who is frightened of it, and I have watched you refuse five offices in five weeks and I would like to say one thing about that and then I will go downstairs."

"Say it."

"Every office you have refused wanted the figure." Nadia Ferrous stopped in the doorway. "She is the first one who wants the answer.

And you have given her your name in her own book and a date every month for as long as she keeps it, and you did that on the twenty-fifth, after the night before it. I would say it was a bad idea except that I have been trying to work out how to get a man who never asks for anything to ask me for one thing since September, and I have not managed it, and she got it out of him in a room with a day book."

She went down.

He sat at the table for another half hour with the bag in front of him and two sums on it and neither of them was any use.

It came out about nine and the chop-house was shutting and the man who owned it put the chairs up one at a time and did not ask him to leave, which he had never once done.

He walked down to the Vestry at twenty past nine and stood at the end of the corridor in the strip of light off the high window.

Four rooms. The first had a bed in it and a chair and a window. The second had two beds and no window and one of them was empty and laced shoes beside it, because Ilse Korvin had gone to the Exchange at four o'clock with a woman from the ward who had waited two hours in the street with her and had not once told her what to say when she got there.

The third and fourth were dark.

Tessa Okonkwo's own door was four feet along, and it was shut, and behind it there was a lamp on a cord and a rack of twenty-seven boards and a shallow drawer with a day book in it that had L HAO on the first line in his own handwriting.

He did not knock.

He had given her the twenty-ninth and she had watched him agree to it, and he was a man who kept a date he had given, and it was the twenty-eighth, and tomorrow was the morning.

He stood in a corridor for about two minutes and then he went home and did not sleep, and the grey at his left temple was on the sixth day of it, and a figure that had gone flat five days ago was sitting on a plate nine streets away reading eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and there was not one instrument in the city that would tell a man whether his own account had died.

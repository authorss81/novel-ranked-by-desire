# The Held Room

On the ninth of the month Tessa Okonkwo sent a runner to the chop-house with a message that had no clinical word in it, only a room number and an hour, and Lin Hao went at dusk because the runner waited while he read it.

He felt a heavy reluctance, the kind that knows the news is bad. He wanted to hear it in a room and not in a corridor.

The Vestry back corridor had four quiet rooms. The second was empty with the bed made. The fourth had its door open and a lamp on inside.

A woman sat on the bed with her shoes laced, ready to go somewhere. She was a tram conductor he had seen on Beatrix’s line for months, one who had stood in the street outside the Exchange on the twenty-ninth with Ilse Korvin and waited two hours without telling her what to say.

Tessa stood in the doorway. “Her name is Petra Hall. She paid for a berth in March and did not use it, same as Ilse. The standing against her does not close until she turns up. She turned up yesterday. The clerk was out. The date is still open.”

Petra Hall looked at him without much in her face. “I can get there. I stood outside it for an hour yesterday. I came back on the tram. I am here because while the date is open there is nowhere on paper for me to be discharged to.”

He sat on the chair because there was nowhere else to sit.

“This is not the third sub-level,” Tessa said. “This is not a classification. Nothing in this building is holding her. It is that I cannot stop paying for a bed that a date is sitting on, and there are four of these rooms and two of them have been full since the spring.”

For Petra, who had paid for a thing and not used it, being held meant she slept in a room with no window while a clerk was out for a day.

Lin Hao looked at the made bed. “What do you need.”

“A night,” Petra said. “One night. Then I go back in the morning and the clerk is in and I say I did not go, and the date closes, and I go home.”

Tessa put her hand on the frame. “She gets the night. That is the whole of what this room is.”

He nodded. The reluctance stayed, because a person was held for a date and the date was open through no fault of hers, and the precedent from the twenty-fifth was now a practice.

At the door Tessa stopped him. “Come tomorrow. First light. She goes at nine. You can walk her to the Exchange if you want to be useful.”

He went home by the arcades with a held room behind him that was not his and not the Wing’s, and the holding had a new name in it.

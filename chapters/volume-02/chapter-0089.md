# The Held Room

On the ninth of the month Tessa Okonkwo sent a runner to the chop-house with a message that had no clinical word in it, only a room number and an hour, and Lin Hao went at dusk because the runner waited while he read it.

He felt a heavy reluctance, the kind that knows the news is bad. He wanted to hear it in a room and not in a corridor.

The Vestry back corridor had four quiet rooms. The second was empty with the bed made. The fourth had its door open and a lamp on inside.

A woman sat on the bed with her shoes laced, ready to go somewhere. She was a tram conductor he had seen on Beatrix's line for months, one who had stood in the street outside the Exchange on the twenty-ninth with Ilse Korvin and waited two hours without telling her what to say.

Tessa stood in the doorway. "Her name is Petra Hall. She paid for a berth in March and did not use it, same as Ilse. The standing against her does not close until she turns up. She turned up yesterday. The clerk was out. The date is still open."

Petra Hall looked at him without much in her face. "I can get there. I stood outside it for an hour yesterday. I came back on the tram. I am here because while the date is open there is nowhere on paper for me to be discharged to."

He sat on the chair because there was nowhere else to sit.

"This is not the third sub-level," Tessa said. "This is not a classification. Nothing in this building is holding her. It is that I cannot stop paying for a bed that a date is sitting on, and there are four of these rooms and two of them have been full since the spring."

For Petra, who had paid for a thing and not used it, being held meant she slept in a room with no window while a clerk was out for a day.

Lin Hao looked at the made bed. "What do you need."

"A night," Petra said. "One night. Then I go back in the morning and the clerk is in and I say I did not go, and the date closes, and I go home."

"Why did you buy a berth and not use it."

"Because I am a fool with money in March," Petra Hall said, without heat. "I had a quarter and something left and there was a list of berths at the back of a shed and a man took my name and my money and gave me a card with a number on it and told me to bring the card if I ever wanted a night. I thought I would want a night."

"And then you did not."

"And then I did not, and there was no way to give the card back, because giving it back is attending, and attending is what it is for." She turned the mug round on her knee.

"So for five months I have had a card in my coat for a thing I did not buy and have not used, and every time somebody in an office asks me about myself the card is the reason there is a line about me. I have said no to six questions because of a card I paid for in a shed behind a tram office."

Tessa stood in the doorway. "Say the last part again."

"I have said no to six questions because of a card I paid for in a shed in March."

"That is the sentence I have wanted somebody to say in this room since the twenty-ninth of last month," Tessa said. "You have just described a holding. A card you cannot return, a thing that cannot be given up without the act that triggers it, and a woman who is asked about herself six times and answers six times for a reason she did not choose in a shed."

Petra Hall looked at her. "Is that what this is."

"That is what that is," Tessa said, "and it is not a room on my corridor and I am not going to build anything on it until I have read the card. What I am telling you is that you have been held since March by a piece of cardboard and a man in a shed, and that the fourth room on this floor is a courtesy, and the card is the arrangement."

Tessa put her hand on the frame. "She gets the night. That is the whole of what this room is."

"How many nights has this room done."

"Nineteen, since the spring." She said it without any pause, because she had the number and had been keeping it. "Four of them were Ilse Korvin. Two of them were a man off the Cutler's Row line who had bought a berth in a different quarter and been entered against this one. One of them was me, on the twenty-eighth of last month, and I am telling you that because it is in the book and it will come up if anybody ever looks."

"You were held here."

"I was not held, I was resting, and the distinction was entirely mine at the time." She took her hand off the frame.

"A room with no window and a lamp on it is not a cell in this city and it will never be a cell in this city, because there is no officer in it and no order and no register of who is in it. That is exactly why it is dangerous."

"If it had a register I could refuse it and be right. Without one, every night is a favour, and a favour can be withdrawn at nine in the morning by a woman with a bad back and a full floor."

Petra said, "I understand that. I would take it either way."

"What is the cost," Lin Hao said.

"You keep asking me that."

"You keep not answering."

Tessa went into the room and pulled the blanket up off the couch and dropped it on the bed.

"This room is a bed I cannot sell. That is the entire economics of it. I do not get a night's rent from any of them, so every night one of these rooms is full I have a bed out of service on a floor where the ward is four beds short and the Arcade clinic has been sending people here since I opened."

"I have been lying about it for five months in the ward returns," she said, "which is a thing I have never done and which I would do again tomorrow."

She turned round. "So the cost of keeping her for a night is somebody in a queue on the ground floor tomorrow, and I do not know their name, and I have decided four times this month not to know their name."

The runner from the ward, the one with the wrist, was at the corridor mouth with a chart under her good arm, and she looked at the open door and then at Lin Hao and did not say anything, which cost her a good deal.

"Fourth room," Tessa said. "That is the last one. If a fifth of these comes on a night shift I have a man off the tram line who sleeps in the office on a blanket and I have done that twice and it is not good for either of us."

"What happens on the twelfth."

"Nothing has happened on the twelfth. That is what a room is for." She pulled the lamp off its hook and hung it lower. "It happens tomorrow at nine, in a clerk's office, with a stamp, and then she goes home on a tram and I put a sheet on this bed and we go on."

"I can walk her."

"You can walk her if she wants you to and she has not asked." Tessa looked at Petra. "Do you want a man outside a gate at eight in the morning?"

"No," Petra Hall said. "I have had four of them in three days and every one of them wanted me to say something."

Lin Hao nodded and felt the refusal land in the right place, which was the one place it had been landing all month.

He nodded to her. "Then I walk you as far as the arcade and I leave you at the crossing."

"You go to work at that hour."

"I go to work at that hour," he said. "That is the whole of what I have."

She said, after a moment, "You can ask me the thing you actually came to ask."

"There is no thing I actually came to ask."

"You came down a corridor at dusk on nine days' notice because a runner waited while you read a message." Tessa did not move away from the door.

"You have a monthly arrangement with me that does not involve this floor, a reading day that does not involve this floor, and a piece of paper of your own in your coat that you have not asked anybody to look at."

"So either you came about one of those and you have got the wrong floor, or you came because there is a woman in the fourth room who cannot get out until nine in the morning."

"I came because there is a woman in the fourth room," he said. "And I came because in June a woman in the second room laced her shoes and got up and went out of this building in daylight for the first time in four years, and nobody came to this corridor to ask me whether I had noticed."

Tessa stood in the doorway a long moment.

"That is nearly the answer," she said. "Do not finish it in a corridor."

"You said you were not going to ask me about it until the twenty-ninth."

"I said I would not ask you what you are doing about it until the twenty-ninth, and I have not asked, and you have come and told me about it instead. That is a different thing and it is worth more."

She pulled the door to. "Go home by the arcades. And on the way, work out what a man who is not going to let an office hold anybody does on a night shift when the room is full. That is the question you have actually come down here with, and you have dressed it up as a question about a tram conductor."

She shut the door on Petra Hall.

He went home by the arcades with a held room behind him that was not his and not the Wing's, and the holding had a new name in it, and on a night shift the floor would run out of rooms and put a man on a blanket in an office, and nobody was coming down that corridor to ask him about it.

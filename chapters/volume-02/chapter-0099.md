# The Classification Chair

On the nineteenth the three costs came due inside nine hours, and none of them came due to him, and he spent the whole day finding out about them from other people.

The first was Tessa's, and it came at ten in the morning in a corridor, and it came because the runner's wrist had mended.

He had forgotten that. That was the honest shape of it: he had refused a closure at ten past eleven on the previous day and by ten the next morning he had forgotten that a girl on the Vestry floor would go back up two flights of stairs because the date Tessa had given in that same corridor on the thirteenth had arrived, and the consequence of the date was that the desk at four o'clock emptied.

"The boards went up yesterday at one," Tessa said. "She carried nine of them herself and would not let anybody take the second stack, and she has been up and down two flights all morning on a wrist that was no worse on the fifteenth, and she is not going to be at that desk at four o'clock and neither will I be, because I will be in a room with a man from this office at four o'clock."

"What is the second question."

"It went back on the twelfth and it has come a third time and it is not the same question." She had the form in her hand and she did not offer it to him, which was new. "The first two asked whether the clinic holds an instrument that reads a mass without an interval. I said no, twice, and I did say no, and I was accurate, because there is no instrument in this building that does that in a way anybody but me can operate.

"The third one asked whether the clinic holds an instrument that reads a mass, period. And then it attached a form for an answer, and the form had my name on it and not his, because a clinic that keeps an instrument is a service and a service is answerable, and there is a summary line on this floor that has been asked for twice and produced none."

Lin Hao understood it and understood what it meant for her, and then he understood the second thing, and the second thing was worse.

"You have to answer it," he said.

"I have to answer it or the form goes back unanswered and unanswered is a lapse against a service and a lapse against a service gets an officer up these stairs." Tessa turned the form over and there was nothing on the back. "And the answer that closes it is four lines long and I have been carrying it since the twelfth and I have written it about nine times and I sent four lines yesterday afternoon."

"Read me one."

"No." She looked at him. "I will not read you one and here is why, and you are going to hate it.

"If I say no, that is a lie from a clinician who has the instrument, and a lie from a clinician is a disciplinary matter and not a filing matter, and they will take the ticket.

"If I say yes and describe it, then the instrument exists in an Office file with my name under it and a description of what it does, and the next question is not about your account at all. The next question is what it reads.

And I have three hundred charts of people whose histories say they should be worse than they are, and I am the only body in this city with a mass and a clean history, and Lin Hao, the day that instrument is described is the day that drawer of mine stops being a clinic and starts being evidence about an ordinary person, and every one of the two hundred and fifty-eight of them goes in with me."

The arcade was cold and full of people going to lunch.

"So you have written four lines," he said, "and you are going to send four lines that say the instrument exists, that it is mine, that it is clinical, and that its readings are not produced."

"That is what I have written." Tessa put the form in her pocket. "And I would like you to sit down for the rest of this, because I have had since the twelfth to think about the second half of it and I have not told you and I am telling you now and it is the worst thing I have done in five months.

"In order to refuse to describe your mass I have had to describe your mass. The word is mass. The word is in a Wing file on the eighteenth of this month in my handwriting, in a sentence, in an answer to a question nobody in this building asked about you, and the sentence says a mass and not a figure, and a mass is not a plate reading and there is no plate in this city that returns one."

She put her hand flat on her pocket.

"The Classification Wing has a description of you now because I refused them one. That is what my four lines are. They are a door and I built it and I did it because the alternative was an unanswered form and a woman in a grey armband looking at a rack in a room with two doors in it."

Lin Hao did not sit down.

"That is not you," he said. "That is me. You refused them on the eighteenth at ten in the morning because I said no in a corridor at eight the same morning, and you did not have to—"

"Don't." Tessa Okonkwo's voice did not rise and did not soften either. "Do not do that. I have been taking what you bring me for five weeks and I have decided today, standing at this rack, that I am going to start refusing it, and if you take it off me it is you refusing for me, and then I have not refused anything.

"You did not do this. I did this. Put your hands down."

The second cost came at one in the afternoon and it was Runa's, and it came by runner with a folded sheet, and the runner would not give it to him and made him come down to the gate.

He read it standing in the street.

Runa Sato had written to the Classification Wing on the eighteenth, declining to deliver the subject to the controlled condition, in four lines, and beneath them six more lines which were not her own and were in the hand of somebody in her division.

The six lines were the household line.

For a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word on the sixth, at my request.

She had entered that on the sixth of this month. She had entered it into a Field minute, on her own paper, with her name and number on it, and she had told him at the time that it made the protection explicit and therefore answerable, and the Wing had answered it.

It had gone up on the eighteenth with the refusal, in the same envelope, in the same hand, in six lines, and a household in Ferrier Street was now a line in a file four floors above an inventory room.

Lin Hao sat down on a step outside a gate.

She came out at half past two and found him there and did not sit beside him, which she had never done.

"I told you on the sixth what it would cost," she said. "I told you I would be a woman with a file. I did not tell you that they would answer my paper with six lines of my own handwriting and that I would have to read them in a room with a man from the Wing sitting at the end of a table, in front of him, having written them myself."

"They answered it."

"They answered it and they read it back to me at eleven o'clock this morning as the grounds of my refusal being in my own hand and therefore available." Runa stood with her hands behind her back. "That is the sentence, Lin Hao. Available. Your household is available. I put it in a file to protect you and it has been read back to me as material, in a room, by a woman who has a legitimate interest in where the holder of a one-digit account sleeps."

"Who read it."

"A clerk called Tove Marlin," said Runa. "She was decent about it. She read it exactly as it was written and she did not enjoy it and she apologised for the room, which I thought was decent of her and which I want you to know about, because she is going to be the person who reads anything else that happens in this."

"You said on the sixth that the protection would be answerable."

"I said the protection would be answerable and I meant it and I would say it again." Her voice did not move. "Here is what I have not told you and I am telling you now so that you cannot tell me it was a surprise. I did not expect to be answering for it twice in twelve days. The sixth was protection. The eighteenth is obstruction of a dated lawful demand, written, by a serving officer, against a member who has already refused.

That is not a division matter any more. That is a disciplinary hearing and there will be one, in about two months, in a room, and I will be asked who the household is and I will say what I wrote, because I signed it and I meant it, and I have thought about whether I would unsign it and I have not and I am not going to."

"How long."

"I do not know and I am not going to find out this month." She finally sat down, on the step below him.

"What I do know is that I will not be carrying anything heavy for this division again before the end of the season, and there is a woman on the Ferrier line who has been counting for four years and a gate checker who has been standing at his gate for thirty-nine years and both of those people are going to be asked about me inside a month, because I have made myself a person with a household in it."

For Runa Sato, who had been heavier than she looked by a stone and a half and had not been held since she started carrying, the protection she had asked for had come back as a list of people to be asked about, and she had put herself on it with her own hand and her own number.

"That is my cost," she said. "It is not yours and I would like you not to take it, because I have watched you do that six times this month and it is a way of putting a woman down."

The third cost came at six in the evening and it was Sun Meiling's, and it was the only one of the three that he was in the room for, because Sun Meiling had asked him to be there and had been unusually firm about it.

She came down the outside stair of Bellhouse at six o'clock with her coat on, which meant she was not going in, and Marguerite let them both stand in the hall.

"Sit down," Sun said. "Both of you. It takes four minutes."

She put a sheet on the hall table, face up. It was not a form. It was an internal memorandum with a house heading at the top and a date on it, and the date was the eighteenth.

"There has been a request inside my house since the fourteenth," she said. "A provenance request.

It asks whether Veil-Research holds a record of a consult transmitted three years ago under a one-digit account number, and whether that number was ever a standing number or a holding number.

It is not addressed to me by name. It is addressed to the house. It has been sitting on a floor above mine since the fourteenth and has been answered by a man on my floor twice already, and both times he answered it correctly, and both times the answer went into a schedule, and the schedule has got my name on it because I am the officer responsible for the consult."

"You said no," he said.

"I said I could not recall," said Sun. "That was on the thirteenth and it was the exact truth and it was a complete answer and it bought five days." She put one finger on the sheet. "This is the fourth one and it is not a question.

It is a direction to my house to produce the consult, and it cites the refusal of an agent dated the fourteenth of this month as an indication that a document in this matter is being withheld, and it asks the house to produce its own record of a consult that it produced three years ago."

"So they have decided not to write to you again."

"They have decided correctly, and I want you to hear why that is the dangerous outcome and not the safe one." Sun Meiling looked at him and did not blink. "If they had written to me again, a woman with a card would have come to my door and said a name and I would have said I could not recall again, and a door can be visited twice and then it is a record. A direction to the house is not a visit to a door.

It is a thing that happens to everybody on a floor at once and there is no door in it, and my name goes on it as the officer responsible for a consult nobody on my floor has ever asked me about in three years of working two floors below a man who has a window on the yard."

"Will you fight it."

"No," said Sun. "I am going to say no, in writing, inside the house, on the nineteenth, and put my name and my number on the bottom of it, and it will be read by a man on my own floor this evening, and he will be extremely pleasant about it and he will know exactly what it cost to write, because he has been in that building for nine years and he has never once refused anything."

She squared the sheet on the hall table.

"And on the twentieth I will go back up that stair and be the only person on it who has ever said no to that house.

"There will be a woman in this city in a dark coat with no paper who has never been asked about me, and there will be a notebook on a third seat that rides four turns a day, and both of those women work by finding out things through people. I have just put my name at the top of a list of people worth finding out things about, in a house that is better at that than any building in Ostara."

The hall was very quiet. Upstairs a woman who did not share a name with any of them was reading a visitors' book with a glass beside it.

"You told us on the first of this month," Sun Meiling said, "that you would not be protected, and I said in this room that standing between me and a list makes me the reason there is a list, and I have been carrying that sentence for eighteen days, and it turns out that the list was not going to come to this house at all. It was going to be made by this house."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"Write it down," he said. "Not the request. The last thing you said."

"No," said Sun Meiling. "That is the first thing I have ever declined to write down in three years of keeping notes and I am declining it for the same reason I gave on the first. The moment it is on Ola's pad it is a sentence that a man on my floor could produce if he ever wanted to, and he will not want to today and he may want to in a year, and I am not going to be the reason that woman exists.

"It is said. Four of you heard it and two of you are going to remember it and one of those two is a man who does not keep records and that is the entire protection I have."

She picked the memorandum up off the table and put it inside her coat.

"The classification paper is dated the nineteenth," she said. "It is on Anneke Roos's desk at the register and it is unsigned and it is not in a tray. She did not tell me that. I read it over her shoulder on my way past at half past five because I have been doing that since the twelfth of this month and she has stopped pretending not to let me."

"What is on it."

"Five paragraphs and a heading at the top that I have not seen before and could not read at the angle I had." Sun Meiling got her key out. "And a sixth space ruled at the bottom with a line under it, and the line is the shape of the space in Anneke Roos's fourth book, and I have seen that book once and I do not forget things."

She looked at him.

"You have told me in this room that you will not be described, and I have believed you, and I believe you now. And I want you to understand that as of this evening there is a piece of paper in this city with your account's shape on it, and it has not been signed, and nothing has stopped it from being signed, and the only reason it is unsigned this evening is that nobody with a pen has decided they are the one holding it."

She went up the outside stair, and Marguerite came down the inside one with the lamp.

He stood in the hall of a house that had been a house for eleven years and was a register with a glass on it, where a sentence had been said that four people had heard and one of them would not write down. And on a desk three streets away and four floors up there was a paper dated the same day with a ruling space at the bottom of it and nothing in it.

# The Wing Stair

On the eighth of the month Drant took him down to the second sub-level stair and stopped at the top, and Lin Hao stopped with him because the clerk stopped.

He felt curiosity pull hard against caution. He wanted to see the drawer without opening it.

The first flight went down past a landing with a lamp on a cord and a rail worn pale at the top three feet, where a great many hands had gone down in a hurry. Below the landing the stair turned, and at the bottom of it a corridor ran off in both directions with doors on each side and every door the same.

Against the wall on the near side of the foot of the flight there was a cabinet with four drawers and one of them was open.

The sheet in it was a single thin page, cream, dated at the top in a machine hand, laid face up and squared. It was not turned round. There was a rule printed an inch and a half down it and a signature line under the rule with nothing on the line and nothing under it.

Drant did not go down. "That is as far as I go with you. I went down there nine years ago, once, with a junior clerk, and we opened nothing, and I have not been down since."

Lin Hao looked down the stair. He could see the date on the sheet and not the words, and he could see that the rule was ruled to take one line and one line only.

"What is it."

"A holding sheet with a date on it and no name signed to it." Drant kept both hands on the rail. "It was prepared for the account with one digit. It has never been turned round. Nobody has signed anything on it."

For Drant, who entered everything anybody did in a room, standing at the top of a stair and not entering it meant there was a paper in his building that his book could not take.

"How long has the drawer been open."

"That is the part I have been trying to work out for three days." Drant did not move down a step. "A drawer that is open is a thing somebody is working at. A drawer that is shut is a thing somebody has finished thinking about. It has been open every working morning since the twenty-fourth and I have come down this stair twice to look at it and I have never gone to the bottom, and you may draw whatever you want from that."

"Why show me."

"Because you asked what the order was on the thirty-first, and the order came up from three floors, and this is the floor it came past." The lamp on the cord moved very slightly in the draught and the corridor below went from grey to grey.

"And because Ruta's map is closed and Runa's minute is entered and the finding number is doing its work at the Exchange, and the next paper is going to come from down there. I would rather you saw it once from a stair than twice from a corridor with a desk in it."

Lin Hao stood at the top with the lamp light coming up under him and could feel the cold off the lower corridor, which was a different cold from the arcade cold and from the Vestry cold, a cold that came up out of a wall rather than down out of a roof.

He counted nothing. He did not go down the flight, and he did not turn the sheet round, and he did not sign anything, and there was a moment where the not-signing was the whole of what he could feel in his hands.

Then he heard the lift at the far end of the corridor below.

It was not the lift in the Exchange. It was a goods lift, the kind the Office runs for paper, and it came up to this level and stopped, and a door opened somewhere out of sight down the corridor to the left.

Neither of them moved.

A runner came into the bottom of the stair carrying a docket folder under one arm and going up, in the ordinary way, at a fast walk, and then saw the two of them standing at the top.

She stopped. She was young and she had a wax on a string and no docket in her hand for either of them.

"Drant," she said. "There's nobody signed for it."

"What is it," Drant said.

"It's a request for a holding order, second sub-level, to be prepared for a subject in the current attendance." She said it fast and flatly, the way a runner reads. "It came up on the twenty-fourth with four others. It's got no name on it and no number and the desk says it goes back down unanswered and they want it initialled by the sub-level clerk before four."

"Who is the sub-level clerk."

She looked at him. "You know who it is, you write them all."

Drant said, "Thank you," and she went up the last four steps and past them and up the second flight without stopping, and the stair was empty again.

Lin Hao heard his own voice before he had decided to use it.

"How many have gone down unanswered."

The runner stopped and turned round at the top of the second flight and came back three steps, and looked at him properly for the first time.

"There's four in the tray," she said. "All the same. No name, no number, no initials. They go back down at four and at four the desk stamps them and they go in a drawer with the other ones, and then somebody upstairs gets told there's four things nobody wants."

"Whose drawer."

"I don't know. It's not my drawer." She was looking at him properly now and he could see her deciding whether he was somebody. "Are you Drant's?"

"He is," Drant said.

She nodded, and went up.

The stair was quiet again. Down the corridor the goods lift shut with a sound like a book being shut.

Lin Hao said, "Four in a tray. That is four papers with a date on them and no name on them, and they go down at four with a stamp on them, and then they are a drawer."

"Yes."

"They are not for me yet."

"They are not for anybody yet," Drant said. "That is what a one-digit account is to this building. A sheet that can take whichever name gets put in it first. When there is nothing to write on it, four days running, it stops looking like a mistake and starts looking like a position somebody is holding open."

He felt something cold and clean go through the middle of him, the way it had in the yard in the spring when a load came down wrong.

"They are waiting for a name."

"They are waiting for a name that the Wing can put in itself." Drant took his hands off the rail and showed her palms, a gesture he had never made at him before.

"Which is why the desk stamps them at four. A stamp is a date on a thing that had no date." He put his hands back on the rail. "The next woman to open that tray is going to find four dates in the same week and a request that went round the building and was refused four times by nobody, and she is going to read that as the building having no clerk with authority. She will be right about the second half of it."

"You have authority."

"I have a pen and a book and I have had the same pen and the same book for nine years," Drant said, "and there is a difference between those two things and an authority and I have never once been given the third one."

He was quiet for a moment on the stair, in the cold coming up out of the wall.

"That is going to cost you," he said.

"That is going to cost me," Drant agreed. "And I would like you to notice that I have not asked you for anything and you are the one standing on my stair."

"I know."

"Then go down and look at it," Drant said, "because you have been doing that all month."

Lin Hao looked at the open drawer, at the cream page and the ruled line and the signature line with nothing on it, and he could have gone down eight steps and read the whole of it and put a name in the blank, or a date, or turned the sheet round so that a clerk coming up at four would have to look at the other side.

He did not go down the eight steps.

He went down three, and stood on the third step from the bottom, which was still the stair, and which put him inside the frame of the corridor and inside the light off the goods lift, and put his face at the height of the open drawer.

"That is the man from the Exchange," Drant said, from above him. He said it with his voice up and outward, so that it carried down the corridor in both directions. "He is here at my request on the eighth, going down. Put the four in the book with the time and the floor and my name against it, and do not ask him anything, because he will answer and I do not want him to answer anything."

The runner's voice came back from the second flight, flat and complaining. "There is a rule about a person on a stair—"

"There is a rule about a person on a stair alone and a witness," Drant called up. "He is not alone. Put the four in the book, the time, the floor, and my name, and the name of the man I brought down, which is L HAO, and the reason, which is that I took him to the top and stopped, and that neither of us went further."

There was a silence up there of about four seconds long.

"Do you want that in the tray one or in the book."

"The book," Drant said. "The book and not the tray, because the tray goes down at four and the book stays on the floor."

Lin Hao stood on the third step with his hands at his sides and did not go down four more steps, and did not touch the drawer, and did not turn the sheet round, and understood with total clarity that he had just been entered into a Wing book on a second sub-level by a clerk who was annoyed about it, and that no part of it was signed, and that he could not get any of it back.

He went back up the stairs. Drant came after him and did not touch the cabinet, and the drawer stayed open with the date on it and the line under the rule empty.

At the top Drant took the thin book out from inside his coat and wrote four lines in it and squared the book against his palm and shut it, and then he stood there with it shut in his hand for a moment longer than a man stands with a book.

"Entering," he said. "The time, the floor, the reason, my name, and yours. Not signed by either of us. It is in the book and the book stays on the floor, which means it is not going anywhere and neither am I."

"That was not what you said we came for."

"No," Drant said. "What we came for is a piece of paper in a drawer that has never been turned round and never will be while there is no name on it. And you have just made it so that there is a name on the floor above it. That is not the same thing and it is not worse, and I want you to think about which one you would rather have."

They went back up in silence. On the second flight Lin Hao looked back down at the foot of the first one, at the open drawer four steps below where he had been standing, and the lamp on the cord, and the corridor running off in both directions with its doors.

He had come to see a piece of paper. He had left having been written down next to it, and there was now a floor in this building where a clerk could say a name and a man would have to answer for being there, and he had done it to himself, on purpose, on the third step of a stair, because the alternative was that the four went down at four with nobody's name on them at all.

# The Cost Of Keeping

On the tenth of the month he walked Petra Hall to the Exchange at nine in the morning with Tessa Okonkwo on her other side, and the clerk was in, and the date closed in eleven minutes with a stamp.

He felt joy when the stamp came down, brief and disbelieving. He wanted to see Petra get on a tram home.

She did. She got on at the stop outside with her shoes still laced and waved through the window, and the tram went on.

"She came without being asked for," Tessa said. "That is not how it usually goes."

Back along the arcade the Vestry floor had the ordinary noise of a floor that was not quiet, and at the ward door they stopped because there was a woman at the desk with a cloth over her shoulder and a sheet of paper under it, and the woman was holding the paper the way you hold something you have decided to show.

"Sorry," she said. "Are you the one who walked out with me?"

"No," Petra said. "He is."

The woman took the cloth off the paper and gave it to Lin Hao. It was a printed request with a heading, and at the bottom of it a line for the person it was answered by, and on the back of it in an analyst's hand was a copy of the sheet that had left the building at four on the thirty-first.

"That came this morning," she said. "To the lift. A copy of the sheet that left on the thirty-first. My name at the bottom in an analyst's hand. And then somebody rang the desk about it before the building had finished opening."

"Who rang."

"A man who said he was from the office that has my name on it." She turned the sheet over and put her thumb under the last line. "This one. The last line. It asks for the days I worked the tenth and the persons I spoke to."

He felt the joy go out of him like light off a stair. He wanted to take the sheet and tear it and knew he could not.

"That is the lift log," Tessa said, looking at it. "That is a building with a log in it and a clerk who is required to produce it."

"And who else reads it."

"The building manager, and anybody the manager is asked by." The woman on the desk folded the cloth over her shoulder again. "The manager was in a meeting and the man said he would come back this afternoon, and he did not say to the manager."

Nadia Ferrous came up the stairs behind them with her hands in her sleeves and stopped when she saw the three of them.

"Here," she said, and held the sheet out flat so he could see both sides.

He read it and gave it back and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Say it," Nadia said. "Out loud. You told me on the first that if she uses it, it happens to me first, and you said it twice that morning like a man reading a docket, and I have been waiting nine days for the morning when I could use it."

"You want me to say it in a room."

"I want you to say it in a room with the paper in your hand and not from memory." She put the sheet on the desk. "It is different when it is in your hand."

He put his hand flat on the desk beside the sheet and looked at it.

"You gave her the building on the tenth on purpose. You worked it out four seconds before she asked. You told me on the twenty-second. You claimed it on the thirtieth. I put it in a file on the thirty-first. If she uses it, it happens to you first. That is minuted in this house and it is true in that building, and right now it is happening to you first, in a corridor, nine days after I promised you it would."

"That is the third time you have said it," Nadia said. "I have counted. The first time was the first and I made you say it twice, and you did it twice, like a man reading out a docket, and I let you do it twice because I wanted to see whether you would soften it. You did not soften it. That was worth something and it is why I am standing here asking for it again."

"And this time?"

"This time you have the paper under your hand and there is a woman at my desk who has just been asked about the tenth and a man coming back this afternoon." She turned to the woman at the desk. "What is your name."

"Fenn."

"Fenn, when the man comes back this afternoon, you do not answer him. Do not answer him and do not say you have been told not to answer him. Say you will have to check, and then come and find me, and I will tell you what to say."

"And if the manager lets him at the log?"

"The manager will let him at the log," Nadia said, "because the manager is a man who reads the heading on a piece of paper and does not read the paper."

She turned back to him. "That is what I want out of you and I am telling you what it is, so do not stand there looking like a man being asked for a confession.

"I have run that lift for four years and I have watched two women lose a job in this building for saying what they were asked to, and I am not going to be the third one. I am asking whether you will say the thing that makes that true out loud, now, in front of her."

"She is already on the paper," he said.

"She is already on the paper and she is still standing here," Nadia said, "and the difference between those two facts is the whole of what you have been doing since March."

He looked at Fenn, who had come down a floor and a half to tell a stranger about a phone call and was still holding the cloth she had folded her shoulder into.

"You are not in a file," he said. "Nothing that happened on the tenth involved you. If he asks about the tenth, you were on a different floor."

"I was on the floor he was asking about," Fenn said.

"Then you will be on the log as a person who was on the floor and nothing else, and a floor is not a name." He took his hand off the desk. "And I am going to say the rest of it out loud now because Nadia is right that it is different with the paper under my hand."

The ward office was not a soundproof room and two people at the end of the corridor could hear every word of it.

"You have been asked for the days you worked the tenth and the people you spoke to," he said, "and the person who wrote the sheet is the same office that has been asking porters at a gate and a tram guard on a line and a man at a counter."

"None of those people were asked to give a name and all of them ended up giving one. Nadia is the first woman in that building who worked out in advance that she was going to be used and chose to be used, and I filed her on the thirty-first so that it would be a fact in an office rather than a rumour in a canteen."

"It is still a fact," he said, "and it is still her."

Nadia folded the sheet and put it in her pocket. "Good. That is the first time I have had it with the paper in the room. It cost you eleven days to say it in the first place, and it cost me a file, and now we are even, and I am still running the lift."

Tessa went down to the rack. The runner with the wrist was sorting charts one-handed at the desk, slow and stubborn, and Tessa stopped to correct her grip and then left her alone.

"She is the reason I can come down here at all," Tessa said, without turning round. "The arm is the reason the desk is manned. If that arm mends on the nineteenth and the charts go up the stairs again then she goes up them too, and then she will not be at the desk at four o'clock, and I would be having this conversation in a corridor in front of eleven people instead of in a ward office with four."

Nadia went down to the lift. Fenn went back up the stairs.

"What happens when the manager lets him at the log."

"Then the log comes out and the log says who was in the car on the tenth, and the tenth was an ordinary working day, and the day a woman from that office stood in my lift and did not get out at any floor."

Nadia put her hand flat on the pocket with the sheet in it. "Whoever reads that log gets a list of between forty and sixty names off my own building's page, and they will not be interested in any of them, and they will write them on a sheet anyway, because that is the shape of it."

"Every one of those names will be in a document it had no business being in because of a decision I made in a car in eleven seconds."

"For Nadia, who had asked twice and claimed once, being answered out loud with the paper on the desk meant she could stand in her own corridor at four o'clock that same afternoon with the thing in her pocket and not have to pretend it was not there."

He stood in the corridor with the closed date behind him and the open inquiry in front, and at four o'clock the branch machine printed the strip with a bell and the runner brought it up the long road to the Vestry because Drant had forwarded the copy to the inventory room and the inventory room had sent it on.

He took the strip in the corridor with four beds at the end of it, and the figure on it was eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and the instrument had reached him where he stood, and somewhere below him a building manager was letting a man with a piece of paper in his hand past a desk where a lift log sat open on a table.

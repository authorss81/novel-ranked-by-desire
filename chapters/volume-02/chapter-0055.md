# The Question She Kept

Veil-Research is not on any of the maps Lin Hao had walked past for four years, and he found it by asking a runner at the Exchange gate, and the runner said third court, the iron stair, everyone knows it, in the voice of a man who had never been up it.

The side room was one of eight along a corridor that smelled of paper and cold stone. Long bench, two lamps, a wall of pinned sheets in three sizes with the top row overlapping the one below, a kettle, a chair that had been sat in so often the cane had gone flat. There were no ranks in the room at all. Nobody in it was going to be asked for a reading.

The woman at the bench stood up when he came in and did not take her coat off, which told him she had not intended to be long about it, and then she did not begin, which told him the opposite.

"You did not come about the memo," she said.

"No."

"You came because a clerk with a register asked me a question three years ago and initialled the answer with two letters, and you have found the two letters." Sun Meiling put both hands flat on the bench, one on either side of a sheet. "Ask me the question, and then I will tell you whether you are right, and then I will ask you one, and the one I ask is the one I have been holding since the eighth of this month and you will not enjoy it."

He asked the question.

She listened to the whole of it with her head slightly turned and did not interrupt once, and when he had finished she was quiet for a length of time that he understood, from four years of being looked through, to be the quiet of somebody rearranging what she already had.

"No," she said. "I cannot tell you what the memo asked about. I answered it in four lines and the four lines are in a drawer in this building with my name in the margin, and the drawer is mine, and I have not opened it in three years, because the answer I gave it was an answer I gave once and I have been checking it ever since and it has not changed." She looked at him.

"What I can tell you is that the memo was not about you. It was about a transfer with no destination column on the receiving end. That is the entire question I was asked and it is a nine-year-old question and somebody in that house has been asking it since before you were settled anywhere."

"Then why did a Wing clerk's sheet have your initials in the margin of a ruling about my figure?"

"Because the ruling is not about your figure and you have not noticed that." She turned the sheet on the bench toward him. It was covered in pencil and most of it was boxes. "Sit down. I am going to show you what is on that and then I am going to ask you a question that is wrong, and you are going to correct me, and you are going to correct me honestly rather than usefully, and that is the only test I have ever had."

He sat down.

She turned two sheets over so that they lay next to each other, and he looked at them, and after a while he understood that he could not read either of them, because they were not figures and not lists. They were sets.

Dozens of small loops drawn in pencil, grouped in threes and fives, some of them with a line struck through them and some with a line doubled, and the same loop-shape appearing over and over at different sizes across two sheets of paper that plainly came from two different buildings and two different years.

"I see the same thing in different data," she said. "That is the whole of my gift and it has a price attached and I will come to the price in a moment. First: your ruling is not a fact about your figure. A ruling is an event somebody did in a room. It was copied onto a register line because it was an event about an account, and the register line it went onto is the only line anybody in that office had that was about a person rather than about a figure, and it had to go somewhere and it went to the bottom row."

"And the bottom row is mine."

"The bottom row is the only one with a date inside a fortnight." She put her finger on a group of loops at the bottom left of the second sheet. "Here. Three of them, all on the same day, all in the same quarter of an hour, drawn by three different instruments in three different buildings because three people three streets apart all put something down in their charts in the same four seconds. Do you know what makes a shape in that data?"

"People," he said.

"No." Sun Meiling said it almost gently. "That is what your Wing clerk is going to say, and he will be right that it is people, and it will be the wrong question. In nineteen years of this house's records there is not one shape like that with a body at the middle of it. There are hundreds. Every one of them has a body at the middle of it." She turned the sheet over, and the back was blank, and that was somehow the most eloquent thing in the room.

"There is no middle. There is a cluster and a place, and the place is where it happened, and there is no one standing in the middle of it who made it."

She let that sit.

"That," she said, "is a question I have been asking since the eighth. Now. Here is the wrong question."

She said it in exactly the voice of a man reading a row off a card.

"Your figure is made of —" She recited it, flat, bored, a clerk's morning. "Four hundred off a woman with a basket at an interchange, two hundred off a door held in the rain, a Branch floor release of three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine behind a witnessed deed, and the balance accrual in the quarter where you stand. Correct so far."

Lin Hao felt the colour go out of his face.

"Now the part I am going to get wrong," said Sun Meiling, "on purpose, because in eleven years not one person has ever corrected me honestly, they have all corrected me usefully. I am going to say that the balance is nine years of somebody's grief — a man who lost something large and could not file it — and that it came to you because you were the only unsettled account the city had, and that you have been carrying another man's sorrow for nine years without knowing whose it was."

It was the most plausible wrong sentence anyone had ever said to him.

It was wrong in a specific place, a place he had not put his hand on in ten weeks, and it was wrong because he had been in a room with sixty witnesses and had spent the whole of a true answer to a question on the floor of it.

"No," he said. "That is not what it is."

"No," said Sun Meiling. "Go on."

"It is not nine years of anybody's grief and it did not come to me because I was the only account the city had. It came to me because I stand in corridors, and I did none of it, and there was nothing in the middle of it then either and there is not now, and the three of those people on the bottom row of your sheet are not yours, they are mine, and they turned four seconds apart on a Thursday in a room with nine chairs in it because I spent the whole of a true answer on myself in front of sixty people."

He stopped, because his voice had gone.

"That is what it is," he said.

"It is not grief. It is an accident that happened in a corridor where four strangers were holding doors."

"It has been going on for four years and then six weeks, and it went up eleven thousand in one night because ninety-one people stood in a building and watched me tell the truth. I have never done a deed that mattered. I cleaned the toilets."

"The figure is strangers being decent to each other near a man who cleans toilets, and I am the address. I did not choose it. I cannot give it back and I cannot stop it. And the last time I spent any of it on myself it came out in three bodies at once, and one of those three is a woman called Nell Ardery who has eleven weeks of figures in her pocket and has never once asked me for anything."

Sun Meiling had not moved at all. She had her hands on the bench on either side of the two sheets, and the loops were between them, and she was looking at him with an expression that had gone past listening into arithmetic.

"Say the last part again," she said.

"The last part."

"The three bodies. Say what it did to them."

Lin Hao had promised a woman in a cab at eleven at night at the end of a shift to spend on people and not on himself. He had broken it in a square in front of sixty people, and the breaking could not be mended, because a line in a ledger cannot be taken out by anybody including the man who put it in.

"It cost them a cold minute each," he said. "In the hand, or the chest, or an old injury, one to four seconds, and Nell had it in her shoulder where she has been carrying eleven weeks of figures, and Mr Neve had it in his back at a counter where he reads plates for forty strangers a day, and the man at the yard gate had it and asked whether he was supposed to report it." He put his hands flat on the bench.

"And I broke a promise I made out loud to a woman before any of it started, and I cannot mend it, because mending it would mean taking the line out, and nobody has ever taken a line out of anything."

"That is the wrong answer," said Sun Meiling.

"It is the true one."

"It is the true one and it is also the wrong one and I am going to tell you why, and then I am going to stop being a woman with a clipboard." She came round the bench. "You just described a transfer. You described it as a transfer and you did not hear yourself, and I have been listening to people describe transfers for three years and you are the first one I have ever caught doing it."

"I did not—"

"You said you spent the whole of a true answer on yourself, and that a true answer went out of you into a room, and that three people took something out of that room." She stopped in front of him.

"That is not an accident collecting in a man. An accident does not move when the man moves. Your own office has that written down — yours moves where you stand. This moved in three bodies three streets apart at four seconds, and there is a shape in my data from three years ago with the same geometry and there was no room in it and no man in it."

She reached over the bench and turned the second sheet over and put it down on the floor, and picked up a third one from under the bench, and laid it beside the other two.

"Your ruling," she said, "was not a fact about you and you have been carrying it as if it were."

She sent her own kettle down the corridor and had it back hot, and made tea badly in two cups because she only had two, and he drank it because he had walked forty-one minutes in weather.

"I am going to say a thing and then I am going to stop and let you answer it, and the answer is not the one you think it is going to be." She sat down on the bench opposite him with her knees a foot apart and her coat still on, and looked at him steadily.

"I have three years of the closest thing this city has ever had to a set of accounts that the whole city left unscored. I have spent those three years not sleeping well and not liking my own company, because every single thing in that set points at a place and not at a man, and I have told three heads of house that there is a man at the middle of it, and all three of them told me that there is no man at the middle of it, and the third one told me to stop."

"I am not the man at the middle of it."

"I know that. I knew it before you came up the stair and I have known it since the eighth, and I have known it since before that, and I did not ask you to confirm it because I do not need you to confirm it." She put her cup down on the bench.

"You are the address. You have been the address for nine years and you are not the middle and the middle is not a person, and I have been looking for a middle for three years and there is not one, and if there is ever going to be one then it is a thing that was sent somewhere and somebody sent it, and I cannot get to that from where I stand."

"Then what do you want."

"You," said Sun Meiling.

She did not smile and she did not qualify it and she did not look away, and it was the plainest sentence anybody had said to him in ten weeks and it arrived in a room with a kettle and two bad cups in it.

"I want you," she said, "not the figure and not the ruling and not the ruling's shape in my sheets, and I am aware that saying it in this room after saying all of that is a fairly cheap way to make a man stop being frightened. So let me pay for it properly and put my terms on the table first, because I have watched three women in your life manage that badly and I have no intention of joining them."

She turned and reached under the bench and took out a folded square of paper and put it on the bench between them, and he saw the ink of a plate transfer and a date that was three years old, and he did not touch it, and his hands wanted to.

"I will not be sworn," she said. "I will not be housed. I will not be filed. There are three reasons and you get all three."

She held up one finger.

"One: I am inside a house that keeps no registers, and the day my name is on a roll that a man of rank can be requisitioned against is the day somebody in my own corridor reads it off the wall in my corridor, and this house has spent a hundred years being the one room in Ostara where nothing is written down. I am not going to be the woman who puts it there."

Second finger.

"Two: by the time a roll matters, being on it will be the only protection any of you has, and it will protect me least, because I am not going to be on the plate and I will not be at your address and there is nothing on me to enforce. And when that day comes there will be a man with a requisition who knows exactly where I am, because you will have told him, in a room, in words, with a date."

Third finger.

"Three, and this is the real one." She put her hand flat on the paper between them. "You are going to be read in public eventually, and when that happens every person who ever got a cold minute in your hand is on the same page as you. I want to be the only one on that page who cannot be reached by reading it." She folded her hands.

"A woman who is not on your roll is not on your page. That is worth more to me than the plate and the quarters both, and you may think that is sentimental, and it is not, it is three years of watching what paperwork does."

"Agreed," Lin Hao said. "All three."

"Good." She did not move.

"Now. The other half, which is mine and not yours, and I am putting it out loud so there is no part of tonight that is a transaction."

"I do not want the terms. I have money. I have a house and a standing and a set of sheets that will keep me busy for the rest of my life. I want you because you are the only piece of data in this city that has never once been written down by anybody, and I have not been inside one of those before."

"And I am not going to pretend to you that tonight is about wanting a man, because it is also about that, and both of them are true and neither of them cancels the other."

"Four true things before anything starts," he said. "That is not a rule. That is what Ines says and she is right about it."

"Then say yours."

"You tell me to stop. I stop. If you say a thing is wrong I will believe you about it and not argue. If you want me to be somewhere I will ask before I do it. If I do not know what I am doing I will say so out loud instead of guessing."

"I am not going to ask you to be anywhere," said Sun Meiling. "You have been wherever somebody sent you for ten weeks and I am not going to be another party to that arrangement. That is my true thing. I am asking you to want me, and I am not going to pretend to be satisfied with less, and if it is not in you then it will not be in you tonight and I will still have the sheets."

"Then go on," he said.

She got up and took her coat off and hung it on the back of the chair, and pulled her hair back over one shoulder, and came and stood in front of him where he was sitting on the floor with his back against the bench, and took his face in both hands, which were cool and steady, and tipped his head back.

"You have been looked through since you were a boy," she said. "You have stood in one room in the last ten weeks and been looked at, and I am going to do the other thing and I am going to do it properly and you are going to have to stop being a document for about an hour."

"Yes."

"Say it."

"Yes," he said.

She kissed him there, sitting on the floor with his back against the bench, and her mouth was warm and unhurried and completely certain of itself, and she went on kissing him while she unbuttoned his coat with one hand, which took her a while because she was doing two things at once and neither of them was impatient.

"Boots," she said, against his mouth, when she got the coat open. "You have been walking forty-one minutes in my sheets and I can smell it. Boots off."

He put his boots off. She took his socks off. And then she worked his trousers down over his hips with the good hand careful of the forearm, the way everybody who has known about that arm for four months works around it without mentioning it, and he noticed her noticing it and did not say anything, and neither did she.

He got his shirt off and she got hers off, and she stood looking down at him and he lay on the floor of a room with no window and no register and no rank in it and looked at a woman who had been looking for him for three years and had found him by the shape of the hole he made in other people's charts.

"You are on the floor," she said. "Up. I am not doing this on a floor, we have had a very strange month and I am going to be comfortable."

She moved him — a hand under his good shoulder and a knee against his, and he let her move him, which was a thing he had not done in ten weeks with anybody — up onto the bench, and lay down on it beside her with the pinned sheets above them and the two lamps going, and the sheets were the closest thing to a canopy that room had.

"Tell me if your arm," she said, at some point.

"I will tell you if my arm," he said.

She laughed, and it was not a polite noise, and then she did not laugh for a while.

It was unhurried in the way she had said it would be, and she was entirely herself throughout it, and she did not once take anything from him that he had not put there on purpose, and at one point she stopped with her hand flat on his chest and made him say something, and what she made him say was not a declaration and it was not the ledger and it was not a single word that he could have spent.

"You are not the middle of it," she said, and made him say it back, and said, "Yes. Again. Louder, and stop looking at the wall, there are sheets on it and they are of you, which is going to be funny later and is not funny now."

They went on. She took her time and she was better at it than anybody had any right to expect from a woman who could not look at one thing, and he found out twice what that meant, because twice she stopped and made herself focus on a single object — his collarbone, once, her own wrist, once — and both times she got there by asking him for it out loud, and both times he gave it to her, and both times she thanked him by doing exactly what she had asked for.

Afterwards they lay on the bench on top of the pinned sheets in a room that would have been ruined for her if they had cared, and she got up, and brought water in a jug, and washed him herself, unhurried, thorough, unembarrassed, and then sat on the floor with her back against the bench beside his shoulder.

"Right," she said. "Terms first and then the unpleasant part, and I want you to hear the terms before the unpleasant part or you will hear the unpleasant part and start being a document again."

"Terms first."

"Not sworn. Not housed. Not filed. You said yes to all three in under a second, which tells me you had already worked out that I was going to ask and had decided in advance not to fight me about it, and I am not going to make a speech about what that says." She drank some water.

"What you get is this. I will not file you. I have not filed you, I have not filed the ruling, I have not filed the shape in the bottom row, and I have told three heads of house that there is no man at the middle of it and I will go on telling them, and that costs me something every time I say it and I am not going to itemise it for you."

"What do you want in exchange."

"Nothing you would recognise as an exchange, which is why I am telling you it is not one." She put the jug down. "Come up the stair. Twice a month, and not on the days you clean, because the day you clean is the day the figure is thickest and the day you clean is the day you are least able to be a person. And when you come up here I will ask you questions that are wrong until one of them is right, and when one of them is right I will stop asking, and that will be the only news I have ever given anybody in nine years."

"You will never know whether one of them is right."

"No. That is the price and I have paid it for three years and I will go on paying it." She looked up at the pinned sheets, which were of him and of a thousand other sets in the same city. "I cannot see a single thing any more. I see all of it or I see none of it. The day I can look at one man and know what he is, I will be worth a great deal more than I am worth now."

She reached down under the bench and picked up the folded square of paper with the plate transfer on it, and held it in her hand without opening it.

"And here is the thing I am not saying tonight," she said. "I have known since the eighth. It is on that sheet and it has been on it since before you were anywhere, and it is one sentence, and I have had it in my mouth for eight days and I have not said it to you because of what it does to you."

"What does it do to me."

"It takes the accident out of it." She put the square face down on the bench. "I am not going to hand you the middle of the shape and let you spend the rest of your life deciding whether to believe me. You will believe me or you will not and either way you will start behaving like a man who has been handed something, and you are at last a man who has been handed something, and it will be the fastest and the most dangerous way I have ever seen anybody go wrong."

"Then when."

"When you have stood still long enough for a mass to stop moving and have not gone flat doing it." She stood up and picked her coat off the chair and did not put it on.

"That is the condition and not a date, and I am sorry about that, and I would rather give you a condition than a date because I do not know the date and if I gave you one you would aim at it, and a man who aims at a date does not stand still at all."

Then she said, with the coat over her arm and the two bad cups on the bench and the sheets of him pinned up over both their heads:

"The worst of it is that I cannot promise it will help. I have three years of data and one sentence and a shape, and the sentence explains the shape, and the shape has not moved once in three years because it has never been touched by anybody who could find it." She looked at him.

"What moves it is the only thing you do, and you will keep doing it whether or not anything in this room is true, because you clean six floors of washrooms in the morning and you walk past a corridor at seven where forty strangers hold doors for each other, and that is not going to stop because I told you one sentence in a side room on a night."

"No," he said. "It is not going to stop."

"No." She held the coat and did not put it on and did not tell him to go. "Then come back in a fortnight, and I will ask you another wrong question, and you will correct me honestly, and we will do this again, and in three years one of my questions will not be wrong, and by then you will be far too deep in it to be frightened by anybody's office."

She put her coat on at the door, in the corridor, with eight other doors shut along it and no light on in any of them, and she walked him to the iron stair and left him at the top of it.

Going down the iron stair at four in the afternoon, with his arm aching in the way it always ached by then and the two bad cups in him and no panel, no figure and no faces and nothing whatever in the notation, because nothing had gone in and nothing had come out and for the first time since a square with sixty people in it he had spent an entire afternoon with a woman who had wanted him for the thing he carried rather than for the figure on his plate.

She would not be sworn. She would not be housed. She would not be filed.

And somewhere in a side room on the first floor, on a sheet nobody in that house was permitted to see, there was one sentence with a date on it three years old, and a shape that had not moved in three years, and a woman who was going to hold it until he had stood still long enough to earn it.

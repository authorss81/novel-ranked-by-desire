# Three Colds At Once

The cold store was eight feet by eleven and two degrees and one lamp on a cord and a couch nobody used and a table, and it was there because Tessa Okonkwo had said once, in June, that she would not do this in her own ward, and had then told him the room had to be the only one in the building where nothing belonged to anybody else.

She had the rack against the wall with the boards clipped in order and a gap at the top of it, which was new, and she was standing under the lamp with her sleeves pushed to the elbow before he had the door shut behind him.

"Twenty-two," she said.

"Say the rest of it."

"Twenty-two on the wall. Twenty-three before nightfall, and I have not added to it since, because I am going to add to it tonight and I would rather do it with you in the room." She nodded at the gap. "That is not space, Lin Hao. That is where the next eight go when the next eight come in, and I have been putting them in there since June and I have known for three weeks what the gap is for."

She turned round and put both forearms up under the lamp with the sleeves above the elbows.

"Left side," she said. "You have seen it since June. Grey-black stippling from the wrist to mid-forearm and it is eleven years old and it does not move. Then past the joint, and past the joint is a different colour — that is pale blue, and pale blue is not a bruise and it is not a burn, and there is nothing in the medical books about it because nothing in the medical books has ever needed it." She put her fingers on the blue part.

"That came across in the third week of August and it has spread a finger's width since you put your hand on a stock clerk in a counting house."

She turned the right arm up.

The right forearm had begun. It was nothing you could see from across a room. It was a stipple that came up from the heel of the palm about four inches, and she put her own thumbnail beside it so that he could see the size of it against something known.

"Four inches," she said, "in three weeks. That is the one that frightens me and it is not the arm that hurts, because the arm does not hurt, and it is not going to hurt, and that is the whole danger of it." She let the sleeve down.

"I do not run out of forearms for a year. I run out of room in a fortnight. I have four walls and I have been counting them for two years and I am at three and a half and the half is the chart rack, and a chart rack does not hold anything, it only stands there and lets you look at it, which is what I have been doing."

"Say the number."

"I have just said it. Four inches and three walls." She squared the boards. "And I am not going to say the thing you are about to thank me for, so do it now, get it over with, and then say the number you came to say."

He had it ready. It had been ready since the stair of the clinic.

"Tessa—"

"No." She put one hand up flat, the way Ines does with a folder and the way he does with a plate and the way a woman who has had eleven years of being the person who holds things does with a hand.

"Say it and I will refuse it, because I have been owed thankfulness by people whose bodies paid for it and it turns out to be a kind of invoice. You did not do this. I did this. There is a rack in this room that is mine and it has my damage on it and there is not one grain of it that came from a man in a washroom, and if you thank me for it you are thanking me for my own inventory and I have not got the patience for it tonight."

He did not say it.

"Say the number, then," said Tessa Okonkwo, "the number and not the names, because the names are on the board and I do not need a man to read them back to me."

"Twenty-two."

She nodded once, and squared the boards, and put her thumb on the top clip and left it there for a moment, and the cold store was quiet at two degrees with the lamp going on its cord.

"Right," she said. "You have come to ask me a medical question and you have been standing at the door not saying it, so I am going to answer the one I know you have got and then you can tell me if I have got it right."

"Standing still," Lin Hao said. "What does it do. To the three, and to you."

"Ah." She sat down on the edge of the table, which she never did. "That is the one. Yes. Sit down, this takes a while and I want the lamp on both of us."

She took the top three boards off the rack together and laid them flat on the table and put the lamp down beside them so the light came across the paper flat.

"Look at what I wrote for Nell Ardery on the Review afternoon, and then look at what I wrote for her in the week before, and then tell me which of those two you would have bet a week of your own on."

He looked. The two charts were in the same hand and the same tidy columns and looked like the same woman writing about the same shoulder.

"They are identical," he said.

"They are identical in every line except one, and the one is in the last column and it is not a figure, it is a mark, and I use a mark when I cannot make a sentence fit." She put her finger on it.

"Nell Ardery, second landing, number fourteen, eleven weeks of figures and a band that has not moved. She comes to me because her shoulder has been in the wrong place for eleven weeks. Every week for eleven weeks her chart has said exactly that. And on the Thursday afternoon in the sixth week her chart says the same eleven weeks in the same words and the same shoulder, and my mark says the body no longer agrees with the history, and the reason is that four seconds of cold went out of her in a room four streets away and the relief arrived early and settled."

Lin Hao looked at the mark for a while.

"That is what you have got in the drawer," he said. "Charts that are correct and bodies that do not match them."

"That is what I have got in the drawer and I have got nine hundred and something of them and I do not count them, and you asked me once in the summer and I told you that, and you have come back to it four times in as many weeks, which means you have worked out that I do not count them because counting them makes it a series and a series can be read."

"Nobody has ever—"

"Stop." She said it without heat. "Do not say that sentence to me. That sentence is a boy's and I am a woman with four inches of a thing in my own arm and there is a difference between the two of us that the whole of your life has been spent not seeing, and if you start being kind to me tonight I will put you out of this room."

"Sorry."

"It is fine. It is nearly right, which is worse than wrong, because I have to correct it and correcting it costs us both a minute." She squared the charts.

"Not one of those charts has ever been read by a second pair of eyes. That is the true thing and it is on me and not on you. I have been sitting on nine hundred of them for eleven years in a room with a lamp on a cord and the door has never once been opened by anybody who wanted to see the ones that are wrong, because the ones that are wrong are not wrong in a way that can be said out loud in a corridor."

"Then show me," said Lin Hao.

She showed him.

She did not take them out one at a time. She took the whole rack over onto the table and turned it face down and went down it with a thumbnail, lifting charts off the pile and putting them on the second pile, and it took her about nine minutes, and she did not speak for any of it.

Then she put the lamp on the second pile.

"Two hundred and sixty-one," she said. "Two hundred and sixty-one bodies that present better than their own histories say they should, across eleven years, in this clinic, with a bench rota and a night list. Not all of them are yours. I would say under a third of them can be traced to anybody, and the rest are people who got better in a way I cannot account for, and I have never been able to account for them, and I have not told anybody that until tonight."

She put her hand on the pile.

"Now here is the medicine, and I am giving it to you as a doctor and not as a Bureau." She straightened up. "A settled mass reads as too well. That is not my phrase, it is the only phrase. Right now your account is moving and what it leaves behind is four seconds of cold in somebody's shoulder, and a person who has had four seconds of cold is wrong on their chart — the history says one thing and the colour and the chest say another, and every machine in this building reads the colour and the chest."

"And if it settles."

"If it settles then it stops being four seconds of wrong and it becomes a direction." She said it carefully.

"People who are fed get better and then stay better. Not a lot. Not all at once. But they get better in one week and they are still better in the next and they come back in six weeks and they are better again and there is nothing at all wrong with them that anybody can find, and their charts agree with their bodies, and their bodies are better than any chart in Ostara would predict." She looked at the lamp. "That is not a signature anybody can mistake. That is a healthy district."

"You are telling me that a healthy district is a thing an instrument can see."

"I am telling you that a hundred and sixty bodies in one postal quarter, all of them improving faster than medicine can do it, over two years, is not a set of charts. It is a line." Tessa put her palm flat on the pile. "And Ruta Vesely put a ring in a second colour of pencil round a single point on a printed map eight days ago because she had three and a room. Give her a year and a quarter and she does not need the ring."

He stood in the cold store with his hands at his sides and looked at nine minutes of somebody else's work and understood that he had been carrying the wrong thing about his own account for four years.

He had thought the danger was being found. He had thought that if the thing moved, the mass grew, the faces came, and a number went up on a plate. He had thought that stillness was at least the one posture left to him that did harm.

Stillness was not the absence of danger.

Stillness was the office's ally, and it worked on the bodies of two hundred and sixty strangers who had never heard of him, and it did not require him to do anything at all, and it did not require him to attend a single date in a corridor to keep doing it.

"How long," he said.

"Six weeks from the day you did it in that room."

"From the Review."

"From the Review." She said it without qualification. "That is not my figure and I will tell you where it is from, because you will want to know and I would rather you had it from me than from an office that will only give it to you with a date and a signature. It is what a body takes to flatten a shape that has been building for four years. In a person it is eleven weeks and I have watched it happen to two people. In a mass with ninety-something sources in it —" she stopped. "How many."

"Ninety-one."

"Ninety-one." Tessa sat down properly this time, on the couch nobody used, with her hands hanging between her knees. "Then six weeks is not a guess about you, Lin Hao. It is a guess about how fast ninety-one people can be improved by something that is walking around a district at the rate you walk around a district. And I have to tell you that I do not have a figure for that and neither does anybody, and I am going to give you a date anyway, because you are going to work it out from this room whether I give it to you or not, and you will get it wrong."

"Say the date."

"The twenty-fifth of next month." She looked up at him. "From here that is four weeks and a bit. From the day you did it in that room it is six, and it is the second figure that matters and the first one is the one you will put on a door."

"And when I say that is the date on which it becomes readable, I am not telling you that on that day something switches. I am telling you that on that day an instrument in that office can read it, and it has been able to read it since about four days before, and six weeks is how long I would put out my hand before I would put my hand out."

He wrote the date on the back of his own hand with the stub of a pencil from the table.

"Six weeks," he said. "Twelve days of it so far, standing still."

"Twelve days, and every one of them the right decision. You spent on yourself on the Review day and you have spent nothing since because you have refused to, which is correct and is also the thing that is doing it." She stood up and began clipping the charts back onto the rack in order.

"Which is the part I would like you to sit with for one second before you go, because not one person in the history of this clinic has ever been in the position of knowing that the best thing they are doing is the thing that is killing it, and it is not a comfortable thing to hold, and I have watched two people try."

She walked him to the door and opened it, and Ines Duarte was standing in the clinic corridor outside it with her folder closed under her arm, in the exact place an auditor may stand.

Tessa said, "Twenty-two," to nobody in particular, and closed the door.

Lin Hao came out and Ines did not ask. That was the thing about her that had not changed in two months and had not changed in four years: she looked at a man's face and she read the room before she read him, and what she read off his face in a clinic corridor at half past ten at night was that he had come out of a room where somebody had paid for him.

"You can ask," he said.

"I can ask, and I am not going to, because I have asked you eleven times in eleven weeks and I have got an answer eleven times and I would like one week where the answer is not the only thing happening to you." She adjusted the folder under her arm. "I will say one thing that is mine and not a question."

"Go on."

"I have built the sheet." She said it in the flat voice she used for figures. "The line, the arithmetic, and the one question. It is four sides of paper and it is the smallest document in this city and it has been on the table in my rooms since the seventeenth and I have not shown it to a living soul including the woman it is about." She looked at the wall opposite.

"I am not telling you that so that you can read it. I am telling you because it is the largest thing either of us is carrying and I refuse to carry it alone on the grounds that I am the professional. From today it is half yours. That is not custody. Custody needs a court. That is me telling a person where the thing is."

Ines walked away down the corridor with her folder shut and did not look back.

He got his coat from the clinic rail and stood on the Vestry steps in the cold with two dates in his head.

The twenty-fifth of next month. Tessa's.

And the ones Silas Drant had written down in a room two floors under the Exchange at four o'clock the afternoon before, in a column, in front of him, because a man who has been told there is a line of dates coming ought to be able to count it.

The third of next month.

The twelfth of next month.

The twenty-first of next month.

Nine days apart, and the third of the three was four days short of the twenty-fifth, and it was on the same piece of paper in the same hand in the same building as a thin dated sheet that had been sitting in a drawer unsigned since some time in June.

He turned his hand over in the street light and looked at the pencil date he had written on the inside of it, and then he looked up at the Vestry roofline and out at a district where, on the twenty-first of next month, three hundred and forty women he had never met would walk into a clinic in the morning and be better than their charts said they had any right to be.

And in three rooms of a building he had never been inside, three men would come and stamp three pieces of paper and say the fourth name.

The calendar did not have to do anything. It only had to keep arriving.

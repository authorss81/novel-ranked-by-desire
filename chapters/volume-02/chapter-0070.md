# The Man Who Filed

"Sit down," said Drant. "You have come on a schedule date and I have a schedule sheet, so you are fifth of five, and that closes it, and nothing on the form says a schedule ever finishes, which is how a clerk ends up holding two calendars for one man.

After today the only one still running is the standing. The twenty-fourth and the thirty-first, and you have both of those, because I gave you the thirty-first on the seventeenth and a habit is worth something when it is in ink."

"That is not why you called me in before I had my coat off."

"That is not why. Sit."

He had the minute sheet squared and a second book beside it that Lin Hao had not seen in the room before, thinner, with a cloth cover and no corners on it.

"On the seventeenth you signed an acknowledgement," Drant said. "It is in the tray. It went up on the twentieth with a map that had your initials on two lines of it, and on the afternoon of the twentieth a woman at the head of this office read both, and on the twenty-first two things came out of her office in an envelope, and both of them are about you, and neither of them came to me."

"They went to a research house."

"They went to a research house." Drant put his hand on the thin book. "I do not get to see what goes out of this office. I get to register it. Everything that leaves this floor goes into this book at the desk, because a thing that leaves a building and is not in a book is a thing that has left the world, and in nineteen years that has happened twice and both times it was found."

He opened the book and turned it round.

Two lines of entries in a clerk's hand. The left one was the date and the hour. The middle one was the office it went to. And the right-hand side of the page, printed on the form and not written on it, was a name.

VEIL-RESEARCH.

The name under it was not initials.

"PERRIN SOWE, RESPONSIBLE."

"That is printed," Drant said. "It is printed on the form by the house that received it, so that the reply does not have to be looked for when it comes back. It means the house has answered the last one about this account and the same man signed for it, and it means that in this city there is one person who is allowed to answer questions about your file and his name is on the paper in front of you."

"I know the name," said Lin Hao.

He had said it before he had decided to, and it went across the room, and Drant looked at him for slightly longer than the sentence needed.

"Sit down," said Drant, again, for the third time, and Lin Hao was standing.

"You know the name."

"I have read it. Once. On a receipt in a folder in a side room with a door that closes, and it is in his handwriting, and it is the only handwriting I have ever known that I would have picked out of a room full of paper." He put his hands flat on the table, on either side of the register book. "Perrin Sowe. He was in the Reading Office. He was there three years ago."

"He was in your brother's building."

"He was in my brother's building."

The room had a lamp on it and a rolled map with initials on the tie and a thin book open on a cloth, and Silas Drant did the thing that Lin Hao had begun, somewhere in the fourth month, to think of as what an honest man does in this building.

He got up from the desk, and walked to the door, and looked out into the corridor, and came back and shut it.

"I am going to say something and I want you to hear the shape of it before the words, because the shape is that I have found out something about you that is not about your standing and it is the first time in nineteen years that has happened to me at this desk, and I am not going to be able to put it down again."

"Go on."

"That name is on an outgoing paper of this office, twice, and the second time is four days old, and I have registered it, and I have not read what the paper says and I am not going to." He put his hand flat on the register book.

"But the register carries the account heading, because a consult is headed with the file it is about, and I have seen that heading about forty times in nineteen years on this desk, and every one of them has been a number of two digits."

"And this one is one digit."

"This one is a single digit." Drant said it and stopped, and then he put his own hand off the book, which was the gesture he made instead of a face.

"Go on," said Lin Hao, because the silence had started to be worse than the sentence.

"No. That is all of it and you have the rest." He took his hands off the table.

"I am a clerk at an attendance desk. I do not decide what you are. I decided a long time ago that I did not decide, and I have been very comfortable in that, and it has cost this building nothing at all. A comfortable clerk is exactly what a standing inquiry wants, and I have been supplying one for four weeks with two rubber stamps."

"What has changed."

"You gave me a name on the seventeenth." Drant said it flatly. "Not the three names on the buff squares, which are on the sheet in my tray with your signature under them and which I am required to hold and am holding. The other one. You gave me the name of the man who is permitted to answer questions about your file, out loud, in a room, within about four seconds of seeing it, with no reason that is in your interest at all."

He sat down.

"That is an instrument," he said. "That is exactly what your brother did. I have never had one in my tray before and I have had this building's worth of refusals and I did not know until about nine seconds ago that they were the same shape."

Lin Hao did not answer.

"I am not going to do anything about it," Drant said. "I want that said in a room. I am not going to write it up, and I am not going to tell anybody, and I am not going to add a line to a sheet you have not signed.

I am a clerk and I have found out what you are and I have a list now and there is a thing in nineteen years that is worse than having a list and it is having none, and I am telling you which one I have got so that you know what you did in this room at four seconds past eleven in the morning."

He squared the register book and shut it.

"Attendance kept. Fifth of the schedule. And a note on your standing sheet, in my hand, that you were not asked for the name and that you gave it."

"I was not asked for it."

"No," said Silas Drant. "That is the entire instrument and it took under four seconds and I have been sitting here for twenty minutes with a piece of paper telling me I am the reason a man said a name out loud in a room."

Lin Hao got his coat.

He took the long way and went round the Reading and did not come through the square, and he stood in the street off the third court for about four minutes looking at the iron stair at the end of a court that was not on any map he had walked past in four years.

Then he went up it.

Sun Meiling had the wall of sheets back up and two hundred hooks in eleven years of paper and the same two lamps, and she had a cup of something beside the kettle and had not made a second one, which meant she had been expecting him and had not wanted two cups.

"Sit down," she said.

"You have a face on and it is not the face of a man who has come to be told something. And I have had a strip off a branch printer in my hand since the fourth, so I am going to let you get wherever you are going and then I am going to talk for a while."

"You are not to stop me. I have written out what I am going to say and I have had it written out since the sixth, and if you interrupt me I shall lose my place."

"Go on."

"Perrin Sowe," said Sun Meiling.

He had not said it. He had not said a word, and she had said it, and she had said it the way a clerk reads a heading, which was somehow the worst possible way for it to be said.

"He has been in this building for nine years," she said, "and he has an office on the third floor with a window on the yard, and I have worked two floors below him for three years and I have seen him in the corridor at the filing run about four hundred times, and I have never once spoken to him because there is nothing to say to a man on a filing run."

"Sun—"

"I am not finished and you are not going to be allowed to be polite at me." She put her cup down. "The consult came up to this house three years ago and it was headed with a number and it was about a transfer that carried no name. I drafted the answer. It was four lines. I initialled the four lines.

And I have told you twice that the four lines are in a drawer with my name in the margin and I have not opened it in three years, and all of that is true, and all of that is a true thing about my four lines."

She turned round and pulled the drawer out from under the bench and did not open it.

"What went back was not my four lines."

The kettle ticked as it cooled and neither of them moved.

"When a house answers a departmental consult, the reply is signed by the responsible officer of the wing and initialled by whoever drafted it, because that is how every house in this city has answered a departmental consult since the Veil, and because a junior does not answer a department, that is all." Her hand stayed on the drawer. "So my name is on the transmission.

That is what Anneke Roos has been copying eleven times and what you found in a stairwell in a building four streets from here. My initials are on the answer to a question about a transfer that carried no name, and they are on it because I was in the room when it was drafted, and the four lines underneath them are his."

"What do the four lines say."

"I have not seen them.

They are not mine and they are not in my drawer, and I have not read them in three years, and I am not going to, because reading them would mean being the woman who read them, and I have a discipline and you have heard it and it is the reason I am not going to put a man in a room." She shut the drawer.

"That is the whole of what I have. I do not know what he answered. I know that he answered, and that the answer was filed, and that the question went off the consult queue and into a file, and that a file is the thing you do to a thing when you cannot classify it."

"Does he know what it is."

"He knows the shape. He has known the shape longer than I have, because I have been here three years and he has been here nine, and there is not one set on my wall that he has not had in front of him at some point, because the responsible officer of a wing sees everything in the wing and I am a person who cannot see one thing and he is a person who does not have to."

She turned round and looked at him properly for the first time since he had come in.

"And he has never once come down my corridor," she said. "Three years.

Four hundred filing runs and a kettle on the bench and a woman two floors below him asking him, in front of the whole corridor, three times a year, whether he has read her latest — and he has never once come down."

"I have taken that for three years as a man being busy. It is not. I worked that out on the fourth of the month at about eleven at night, and I sat here and did not sleep, and I did not come up the stair and tell you."

"I want you to hear that I decided that on purpose and not out of cowardice, although it was both."

"Why on purpose."

"Because it is the second time." She said it without any weight at all. "The first time was in September when I told you what I would not be filed and I made you agree to three reasons before anything happened. I am not going to be the woman who puts a name into a room.

That is the whole of my discipline and I have built everything I am on it, and if I came up this stair tonight and told you a man on the third floor had answered a memo about you, then the next thing that happens in this building happens because I put it there, and there are eight doors on this corridor and every one of them is shut and I intend to keep it that way for the rest of my working life."

"You told me anyway."

"I am telling you because you have already written it on a piece of paper in my hand and it has gone to this house, and by the twenty-fourth there will be a second question with the same respondent's name printed on it."

"And you will learn it from a clerk in a sub-level in a room with a cloth on it, in the flat voice. And that is a worse way to hear a name than this one." She refilled her cup and did not fill a second one.

"Ask me."

"What do I know about him."

"That is not what I meant and you know it."

She stood at the bench with the drawer shut and the wall of two hundred sheets behind her and her hands flat on the wood.

"What I meant," said Sun Meiling, "is that you are standing in a room with a woman who has spent three years looking for the sender of the thing you are carrying. And the only question you have carried since you sat down on my floor is whether that person is in this building. It very possibly is."

"And I am telling you that you may ask me one question tonight and I will give you a true answer, and I will not ask you a single thing in return."

The corridor had eight doors shut along it and no light on in any of them and the kettle had gone cold on the bench.

He stood there with a name in his head that he had said out loud in a room two hours ago by accident, in a corridor of a building under the Exchange, to a clerk who was now sitting behind his own counter with a piece of paper that said a man had given a name he was not asked for.

The question was whether the man on the third floor knew where his brother was.

It had been in his mouth since the eighth of a month and it was the only question he had ever carried, and it was four feet away from being asked, in a room with a woman in it who had waited three years for somebody to ask her the right one, and she had told him plainly that she would answer.

He did not ask it.

Because the price was a brother's name said out loud in a room with a woman in it.

He had decided in a square in the sixth week that he would not spend on himself, and there was no instrument in Ostara that could measure what it had cost him to keep that. The only way to ask was to say the words Lin Wei to a person, and from that minute she would know that a man who cleaned washrooms had a brother who had disappeared.

And there was no taking it back, and every door on that corridor would be one she had put something into.

"The twenty-fourth," he said. "And the thirty-first. And there is a strip every Thursday."

"That is not a question," said Sun Meiling.

"No."

"Then you have come up an iron stair to tell me a day of the week." She did not look away from him. "All right.

I will keep it, and I will not spend it, and I will ask you about the rota when you are ready, and I will wait as long as it takes."

"And I am not going to pretend that I am fine about it, and I am not going to make it into something about you either. Because you are the fifth person and you did not ask, and that is not an accident, it is a habit."

"A habit is not a fault and it is also not a shield, and one day one of us is going to have to say so out loud."

She hung her coat on the back of the door.

"Go down the iron stair carefully," she said. "It is dark at the bottom and there is a man on the third floor with a window on the yard who has been in that building for nine years, and he has not come down it once, and as of four days ago he is the only person in Ostara who is allowed to answer a question about you."

He went down it in the dark.

On the twenty-fourth he had a date, and on the thirty-first he had a date, and on the twenty-fifth a district of two million people would walk into rooms and be better than their own charts had any right to be, and a document with a number on it was going to a house off a third court with a name printed on it in a form, and that name had been thirty feet above a woman's head for the whole of the last three years.

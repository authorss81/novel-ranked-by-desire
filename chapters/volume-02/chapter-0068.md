# The Cold Store Count

The cold store was eight feet by eleven and two degrees and one lamp on a cord, and Tessa Okonkwo had the top three boards off the rack and squared them on the table when he came in with his coat still on, which meant she had been waiting for him since the lamp was lit and not since the door.

"Sit," she said. "It is the sixteenth and you did not come and it does not matter that you did not come, because the arrangement was a day you would not have come anyway, and you came, and I want both of those acknowledged before I say anything else."

"Twenty-six," Lin Hao said.

She put her thumb on the top clip.

"Twenty-six on the wall and twenty-seven before tonight and I have not added since, because I am going to add tonight and I would rather do it with you in the room." She squared the boards. "Say the number again and do not put anything else around it, because you have started wrapping numbers in sentences and a wrapped number is a number somebody has to un-wrap before it counts."

"Twenty-six."

"Right." She let the clip go and pushed the three boards along the table towards him, and they were the three from the Review afternoon — Nell Ardery, Garrick Neve, and the gate checker with no address on him — and they were not the top three any more. They were in the middle, clipped in order, with four boards above them and seven below.

"They come down the rack as they get older," she said. "That is the one thing about a rack nobody understands from outside. The top is not the worst. The top is four days old and has not been read twice yet, and the bottom is a year old and has been looked at so often it has a thumbprint on it. Everything I have is in the middle and everything in the middle is me."

She squared the rack itself.

"Now. Tell me the month and then tell me what I am going to tell you, and if you get it wrong I will correct you and you will take the correction, and if you get it right I will say so and you will not be allowed to enjoy it."

"You want to know whether the count is the same rate."

"The count on the wall is not the thing I have been counting since the thirteenth of last month." She put her hand flat on the rack. "The wall is twenty-six. The drawer is two hundred and fifty-eight, and it was two hundred and sixty-one eleven days ago, and in eleven years it has not once gone down."

The room was very quiet at two degrees.

"It went down by three."

"Three of them were wrong and they are my fault and I want them out of the number before anything else happens with that number." She said it fast, the way she said things she had been chewing on.

"Eleven days ago I went back through the drawer with a lamp and a rule and I did the only thing I have never done in eleven years, which is look at the bottom hundred where my handwriting is worst. And three of them are not a mass settling. They are ordinary people who got better.

Old fracture, a course of something, a man who stopped drinking, a woman whose husband died in the spring and who slept for the first time in a year. Three bodies that present better than their history, for reasons a history can hold."

"And the other two hundred and fifty-five."

"The other two hundred and fifty-five are what I have been telling you about since the spring and I have never once counted out loud and I am counting now because I am going to have to." She put both hands on the table.

"Two hundred and fifty-five bodies in eleven years, in this clinic, on a bench rota and a night list, that got better and stayed better. Under a third trace to anybody. And the rate of it has not moved in a month, and I want you to say what that means, and then I am going to tell you why it means it."

"It means I stopped."

"It means you stopped." She nodded once. "Every one of them is a stranger who got a minute of something they were owed out of a corridor and took it home and stayed well.

And in eleven years that drawer has filled at a rate you could set a watch by, and for eleven days it has not moved, and there is a very simple reason for eleven days and it is that in eleven days the only man in this city who is doing it has not done it once."

The lamp swung a little on its cord.

"So that is the good news and I have been sitting with it for a fortnight and I have not told you because I did not believe it, and this morning I believed it." She took the top board off the rack and held it up.

"Tessa Okonkwo, no injury, no history, no complaint, presented at the Vestry on the sixteenth of the month, and every mark on this chart is the correct colour for a body that has never been hurt.

It is the only chart in this room of a woman who is fine and the reason she is fine is not in her history and it is in her own arms, and I have not put that on a chart in eleven years and I am not going to now."

"Then what is the rest of it."

She put the board down and sat on the table, which she never did.

"The rest of it arrived on the thirteenth." She reached under the table and got a sheet and put it on the cloth with the lamp across it.

It was a request form, one page, printed, with a box for the number of cases and a box for the period and a printed line at the top about subjects presenting in a manner inconsistent with recorded history.

"It came to the ward, not to me, because everything comes to the ward first, and the ward is good and the ward did the correct thing and sent it up to me with the wrong envelope on it, and I am not going to tell you what was in the wrong envelope because I have not looked and I am not going to."

"Send it to me."

"It does not have your name on it and neither does it have mine, and if you had said that sentence to me a week ago I would have read it to you as an auditor's convenience and it is not one." She turned it round so the printed heading was the right way up for him. "It is from this office.

It is a request for the number of patients at the Vestry clinic in the last eleven years who present in a manner inconsistent with their own recorded history. It wants a number and a period and it does not want names and it is beautifully and perfectly within the rules and it took eleven years' worth of somebody else's work to write it."

She tapped the box.

"Somebody asked, in a room, for a description. And they got one, and the description was a line, and a line has an address, and the address is the only clinic in this city that keeps a drawer."

"How do they know about the drawer."

"They do not know about the drawer. They know there is a line." She said it without heat at all.

"That is worse and you should hear the shape of it, because it took a strip of plate paper and a man who never spends anything and a flat column since the Review, and then it took an analyst who does not trust her own map, and the whole of it arrived at my ward in a printed envelope with no name on it and it is going to take me four weeks to answer and I have to answer."

"You could refuse."

"I could refuse and I would be a doctor who has refused an anonymised request, and there is a file on that, and it does not go in a drawer because I cannot put it in a drawer — it is about me." She squared the sheet.

"And I have not refused an anonymised request in eleven years and I am not starting on the eleventh day of the last month of the eleventh year, because the day I start refusing is the day somebody upstairs writes a line next to my name."

She put both hands flat on the table and looked at the request form and then at him.

"So I am going to answer it. And I want you to hear how, because you will not like it and you will not be able to argue with it, because it is the only version of this that is both true and useless to them."

"Say it."

"Two hundred and fifty-eight. Eleven years of this clinic's night list and bench rota, counted by me, by hand, on my own bench, at two degrees, and the count is two hundred and fifty-eight." She held up one finger. "And then, under the number, I am going to write the word cause: not established. Not one of them. In eleven years, in two hundred and fifty-eight bodies, not one case where anybody could say why, including me, including the two hundred and fifty-eight people themselves."

"They will know."

"They will know exactly what I have known for eleven years and it will be in a document with a number on it, and it is the first thing I have ever put in writing." She squared the boards. "And then, underneath that, on a separate line, I am going to write one case."

"Which one."

"Mine."

The cold store was eight feet by eleven and neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Not the charts," he said.

"The chart. Mine. One page, one case, no symptoms, no history, no complaint, and a line at the bottom in a column that does not exist on any form in this building because I have had to invent it and I am inventing it badly." She said it fast and then slowed down. "Presented better than history. Reason: established. And then the reason, in my own words, on a form that goes to an office: the treating physician's own record is consistent with the same finding, without cause."

"You have never charted yourself."

"I have never charted myself in eleven years because a chart is a thing you do to a person so that somebody can look at it later, and there was nobody later." She got off the table and stood up.

"And I have four inches of stippling in my right forearm and grey-black from the wrist to mid on the left with pale blue past the joint, and it is not going anywhere, and I am not going to be able to explain it to a twelve-year-old in twenty years, and it is on me now. So I am going to hand it to an office. On purpose.

Once. With a number on it."

"That is not medicine."

"It is the only thing I have got and I have had eleven days to find it." She put her sleeves up, both of them, under the lamp, and made him look at them, which she had not done before and which was worse than anything she had said.

"I am the only body in this city whose record shows a mass that has settled and whose history shows nothing. If I go up as a control then two hundred and fifty-eight of my patients stop being a line and start being a coincidence with a doctor in it. That is what a control is.

That is the whole of what a control is and I have never had one because I have never had anything to control."

She let her sleeves down.

"Four days' notice, Lin Hao. I would like you to be in the room when I write it and I would like you not to help and I would like you to hold the lamp."

"You could send me away."

"I could send you away and I would do it and you would go." She squared the boards for the last time.

"But you have been in this room six times in four months and every time you have come out with less than you came in with, and I am tired of that, and you are going to stand on the other side of this table and hold a lamp while a woman puts her own arms in a letter to an office that has never once asked her what she is."

She said the number and not the name, and then she put her own number in the wrong place, and Lin Hao understood about a second later that she had done it on purpose.

Twenty-six was what was on the wall.

What she put on the line was the wall, the two walls, the three and a half, and the store.

Afterwards she wrote the four words at the top of the form in her small square hand — cases presenting in a manner inconsistent with recorded history — and put the lamp down beside it so the light came across the paper flat, and then she sat on the couch nobody used with her hands hanging between her knees and looked at the door of a room that had been the only place in Ostara where nothing belonged to anybody else for eleven years.

She was going to send something out of it on the twentieth.

She had never done that before and she had no idea who was going to write back.

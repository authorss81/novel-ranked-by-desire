# The Paper He Will Not Sign

Drant had the strip on the desk before he had his coat off, and it was the fifth one, and the figure on it was eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

"Fifth Thursday running," he said. "Received. And I am going to say the two things about your calendar before I say anything else, because both of them have been sitting in my drawer for a week and I have been waiting for a day to put them on a table that had something else on it.

Your schedule is finished. Third, twelfth, twenty-first, all three kept, and nothing on it after the twenty-first because the hand that renews it is a hand that writes a column when a standing supersedes it, and it has not written a column since the third, and I cannot write one because I am not permitted to cancel my own instruments and renewing one is cancelling the other."

"So I have the thirty-first and a habit."

"You have the thirty-first and no other date in this city that anybody has written down." Drant squared the strip. "And the standing has five dates on it and no end date, which means that as of this morning you have attended everything that has ever been written down, and there is nothing after this morning, and a man in my situation who lets a man walk out of a room on that basis has failed at the only part of the job I am good at."

"Then what is there after this morning."

"Nothing. And the six will be on a Thursday if somebody upstairs decides there is going to be a six, and if nobody upstairs decides anything then you will turn up on the sixth Thursday because you are a man who turns up, and I have known you five weeks and I would bet my nineteen years on it."

He stamped the attendance. Then he took a second sheet out from under the tray and did not put it face down.

"This came up from three floors on the twenty-fourth with four other papers and it came up in an envelope with the other four and I was not told which one was which because I was not told it had come," said Drant. "It is prepared. It is dated the thirty-first.

It has not been signed by anybody and it has not been sent anywhere, and I have kept it face down for seven days because it was not mine and it was not addressed to anybody, and I have decided this morning that it is going to be face up on that table and I would like you to understand that I have decided it and not been told to."

Lin Hao read it.

It was four printed paragraphs on a single sheet and the fourth one was the whole of it.

The first paragraph named the office, the inquiry and the standing. The second named the days and the place and the hour, which were Thursdays, and the sub-level, and the room with a cloth on the table. The third gave a return to be made by the attendee on each attendance and said what the return was to contain.

The fourth paragraph said that the attendee was held at the attendance desk for the term of the inquiry, and that the account named in the preamble was in the custody of the office, and the number of that account was one digit long.

Lin Hao read the fourth paragraph four times.

"How long have you had it."

"Seven days. The twenty-fourth."

"You prepared this on the twenty-fourth," he said, "and I did not say that number out loud until the twenty-sixth, in a room with no chairs in it, to an analyst, and there were two people in the building and neither of them writes orders."

Drant did not say anything for a moment, and then he put his own hand off the sheet, which was the gesture he made instead of a face.

"I have had that in a drawer for seven days," he said, "and I have turned it over about ninety times, and I want to say the two things I have about it and then I am going to stop, because I am a clerk and there is nobody above me in this building who will read what I say.

The first is that you are right and I have known you were right since the twenty-fourth and I have sat here for seven days with a sheet that was dated for a number you had not said yet.

And the second is that it does not matter, and I have thought that through, and I would like you to have the whole of it, because you are a man who has spent a month learning that a thing being true about you does not stop it happening to you.

Whether they got there before you or the number is not what makes this order — and it is not what makes this order, because the fourth paragraph does not say that you hold an account. It says that the account is in this office's custody and that the man at the desk holds it. Those are two sentences and this building has been living on the gap between them for three years."

"It is a held-account order," Lin Hao said.

"It is a held-account order with a schedule attached to it so that it reads as a calendar and not as a room," said Drant. "That is what it is and I have had it face down for seven days and I did not put it together until about a minute before you came in and I have been sitting here for half an hour with a piece of paper I did not want to be holding.

You understand that I have no power to refuse it and no power to issue it. I can put it in front of you and I can enter what you do about it. I cannot enter anything else."

"I am not signing it."

"No. And I want to say something about that and I have thought about it all week and I am going to say it once." Drant put his hand flat on the sheet. "A refusal is an answer and this office has had five of them out of you in five weeks and every one of them has been in a tray, and every one of them has been a thing that gets answered, and a thing that gets answered is a thing that keeps you here.

If you say no, then there is a refusal on the thirty-first, and a refusal on the thirty-first is a finding, and a finding is an instrument, and I write it up and it goes up three floors at four o'clock and gets a number, and you will have handed that building six documents in a month, all of them in your own hand or your own voice, and all of them about you."

"Then what is the third thing."

"There is no third thing. That is what I have been trying to tell you since the twenty-ninth and I have been doing it badly." He pushed the sheet a quarter of an inch towards him and did not let go of it. "You can sign it. Or you can refuse it.

Or you can do nothing at all and say so, and I will enter this morning as attendance kept and the order as prepared and unsigned and unissued, and it will go back up three floors in the tray at four o'clock with nothing on it, and in nineteen years I have never entered a third thing and I do not have a column for it and I am not going to invent one at ten past eleven in the morning for a man who cleans washrooms."

The room had a lamp on it and a cloth on the table and a strip with a fifth figure on it.

Lin Hao did not touch the paper.

"You put it to me," he said, "and you have read it to me, and you have not once asked me to sign it, and I am going to say the reason and it is going to cost me something and it is not going to cost you anything, and I want you to hear the shape of it before I say it, because the shape of it is that I am not refusing this because of what it holds me with.

I am refusing it because of the last sentence of the fourth paragraph, and because that sentence is not true, and because a document that is not true is the only weapon anybody in this building has ever used on me."

Drant put his hand flat on the sheet.

"There is a second thing on it and I want you to have it before you decide anything," he said. "Not the number. The number was on it on the twenty-fourth and you have made your own point about that and I am not going to argue with a man who has made it better than I could.

The second thing is that the office that drafted this is the office that has had four strips, a map, a finding and two words on a sheet in my own hand, and it is the office that had a consult leave this building on the twentieth of last month with a one-digit heading at the top of it.

Which means this sheet was written by the same three floors that asked me for a return on your attendance and never told me they had asked for one, and I have not been able to decide in seven days whether that is carelessness or a decision, and I am a clerk and I am not allowed to decide it."

"Then the paper I signed on the seventeenth and the paper I am being asked to sign on the thirty-first have the same thing on them."

"They have the same single digit on them and they are about two different subjects, and I am a clerk and I am not permitted to know which is which, and I have not asked and I am not going to ask." Drant turned the sheet round so the preamble was at the top and read it aloud in the flat voice.

"The account named in this order is held in the custody of this office and the attendee named in this order is the holder of that account."

"That is not true."

"No," said Silas Drant. "And I cannot put that in a minute, and I have wanted to since the twenty-fourth, and what I can put in a minute is that the order was put in front of the attendee and the attendee did not sign it and did not refuse it, and that is all, and I am going to write exactly that and no more."

The runner opened the door with a sheet in her hand and Ruta Vesely came in behind her with her coat still on.

"I am here for the finding," said Ruta, "and then I am going up three floors, and I have got four minutes."

"Two women," said Lin Hao.

Ruta Vesely stopped.

"There are two women working on me in this city and neither of them is this office, and I am going to say it now and I am not going to be able to say it later in a room where it costs me less."

"Go on."

"The first has a notebook and a season on a third seat on the Ferrier line and has had it since the eighteenth and rides four turns and gets off at the yard end. She has got a season and no badge and she has been doing this for two years and she has four hundred and eleven counts and she does not write names down.

She got my name off a signalman at the yard gate on the twenty-fourth in about eleven seconds and she told me so, in a wall, standing up, and she told me that she had never once been refused and never once been asked to stop, and that is the whole of what she said and she said all of it in about two minutes."

"And the second."

"The second has a dark coat and no paper and no bag. She has been at my branch floor for forty days and at my yard gate for two of them and at the porters of a house in Bellhouse, and this office has had her on a list since the seventeenth and has not touched her, because she is not on an instrument and has not been near a counter with a form on it."

Ruta Vesely put her bag down on the floor.

"You are telling me this office is late."

"I am telling you that on the seventeenth a man with a pen in a room four streets from here asked the same three questions she answers at gates at four in the morning, and she has answered his office and mine and has not told either of them about the other, and that when this office finds her — and it will find her, because she has been on a list since the seventeenth — the third question is who she has spoken to.

And the people she has spoken to are a gate man, a porter in Bellhouse and a woman in a lift at the Vestry."

The room was very quiet.

"So you are here to stop us finding her," said Ruta Vesely.

"I am here so that when you find her, it is on the record that I told you first, and so that the third question does not start in a gate at four in the morning with a man holding a lamp." He put his hands flat on the table, one on either side of the edge. "And I am going to say one more thing and I have not said it in this room and I am not going to be able to unsay it."

"Go on."

"The name came out of a lift at the Vestry on the tenth of the month, from a woman who runs that lift, and she gave it out loud and on purpose and having worked it out four seconds before she was asked.

She has said to me twice since that if that name is used then the first thing that happens is to her and not to me, and she has asked twice for it to be said back to her in a room, and nobody has said it back to her once."

"Say it back to her."

"I cannot. Not from this room, because it is on a piece of paper in about four minutes and paper is not a room."

Ruta Vesely took her pencil out.

"Give me the name."

"Nadia Ferrous. She runs the Vestry lift. She gave the notebook woman her own name, the building and her floor, and she did it in eleven minutes on the tenth of the month and she has been sitting on it for three weeks."

Ruta Vesely wrote it on the sheet she had brought up with her, at the bottom, in her own hand, and turned it round.

"That is going into a sheet that leaves this building today," she said, "and it is going into a thin book at the desk, and the thin book prints the house it went to, and the house that answers questions about this account has one man allowed to answer it and his name is printed on the form by the house itself and not by anybody in this building.

I am going to write your lift operator's name on a paper about you because a woman in a dark coat has asked a gate man what he feels at four in the morning, and it is the only thing I have got, and I would like you to sit down, because you are not sitting down."

Lin Hao sat down.

"You have given me a person," Ruta said. "Not a name she gave you. A person. In your voice, in a room with a clerk in it, in front of the man who writes the register. And that is not a document you can decline to sign, because you have not signed anything and there is nothing for you to decline."

"I know."

"Do you. Because I have been an analyst for six years and I have had four people hand me a name in a room and every one of them was certain it was the generous thing to do." Ruta Vesely put the pencil in her coat. "Every one of them was managing what could be found. That is all it is.

It is a very good way to be generous and it still puts her name in a drawer in four streets where the dark coat will be in a drawer in four streets, and then there will be two of her in the same building and she will find that out from a clerk."

"I know that too."

"Then I am going to do it," she said, "because you asked me to, and because I have spent four weeks on a room with nine chairs in it that had nothing in it, and this is the first true thing anybody has put in front of me since the thirteenth of last month, and it is true and it is ugly and both of those are the reason it is going up."

She went out first.

Drant waited until the door had shut and then squared the strip and squared the order and put both of them in the tray on the left, which was the one tray, which was for everything.

"Attendance kept," he said. "Fifth of the standing. And there is no sixth date on it and there is no end date on it, and you have had every date there is.

And the order was put in front of you and you did not sign it and you did not refuse it, and that is the whole of what I am going to write, and I am going to write it now while it is true and while there are two people in this room, and if you want it in a different shape you can have it on the sixth Thursday and not before."

He wrote it, and read it back, and did not ask him to sign it, and put it flat under the tray.

Then he said, to the empty room, in the flat clerk's voice that Lin Hao had been listening to for five weeks and had never once heard him use for anything else:

"There is a third thing, then. And I have had it for nine minutes and not twenty-four seconds, and I am going to be a careful man about it and I am not going to say it again in this building this month."

He capped the pen.

On the thirty-first the corridor board outside the brass counter had two lines on it and the second was typed, and there was a strip on a spike with a figure that had not moved in a month.

In a tray on the second sub-level there was a dated order that had not been signed and had not been refused and had gone back up three floors with nothing on it. In another tray there was a sheet with a lift operator's name at the bottom of it in an analyst's hand, going out of the building at four o'clock.

He walked the long road to the branch at forty-one minutes and did not look at the Board. He had two dates and no end, and somewhere on the Ferrier line a woman with a notebook was riding four turns on a season she had bought herself.

And on the first of the month he was going to sit on a table in a room off a corridor with four beds at the end of it, while a woman with a day book wrote a date down and said nothing about it.

# The Weight She Carries

She opened the drawer at nine on the morning of the twenty-ninth and took out a single sheet on a board with a clip on it, and she did it standing up, in the room with the lamp and the rack and the window that gave onto a yard.

"You asked for it in the morning. You are here in the morning. Sit down and do not watch the machine."

She turned it so the scale was against the wall and stood between him and it.

"It has been reading since about the twenty-first and I have had it seven days and I have not put a number on it until now, and I want to tell you why, and then I am going to tell you the number, and you are going to do with the number whatever you were going to do with it, and I am not going to look at your face while you do it."

"You have been frightened of it."

"I have been frightened of being the woman who read it." She turned the machine on. "That is a different sentence and it is the true one and it has been sitting on me since the twenty-third and I have had six days to get it out in a usable shape and this is the shape."

The needle came up and stayed where it was.

"That is the first reading," she said. "I am taking three, ninety seconds apart, and you are going to hear me say the number three times and you are not going to be told what it means until the third, because I have watched you do arithmetic in your face and I want you to sit still for it."

The needle went on sitting where it was.

"The same," she said. "And the same again. And that is the definition in the Office's own word and I have never in eleven years been able to produce it in a clinic in this city for a living body."

She wrote it down. She turned the board round and put it face down on the table, which he had not seen her do in three years.

"It is not in grains. It has no interval. It is not a reading and it is not a score and it cannot be turned into a band, and if you take the figure on that sheet to any machine in this city you will get what the machine at that office does, which is three clicks and a scrape and a piece of paper with nothing on it." She sat down on the table.

"That is what is in you and it is real and I can read it and that is the whole of what I have got for you."

"Say whether it is settled."

"It is settled. It went on the twenty-third of this month and my instrument and your arithmetic on a paper bag in a kitchen in Ferrier Street agree to the day, and I did not know about the paper bag and there is no way on earth I could have known about the paper bag." She turned round and looked at him. "You did the sum yourself.

You did it last night and you did not come up here, and I want to be very clear that I am not praising you for that and I am not criticising you for it either, because a man who works out on his own that a clock has run out is a man who has decided what he is going to do about it, and I have been in this room for four days not knowing which way you were going to go."

"What does it look like," he said. "Tell me the shape of it and not the number."

Tessa Okonkwo put her hand flat on her own sternum and left it there.

"That is a settled mass," she said. "That is what a settled mass looks like. It is not slack and it is not tired and it is not the way a body looks at the end of a long thing. It is the way a room looks when something in it is being held at a constant."

"Held at a constant."

"Held," she said, "and constant, and it has not changed in the seven days I have had it, and I have taken it eleven times and it has not moved a hair.

And there is a reading on the plate in your bag that says eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and has said for five consecutive weeks. The two of those are both true and they are not contradicting each other, and there is no office in this city that can put those two numbers in the same sentence."

She stood up and went to the rack and put her hand flat on the boards, twenty-seven of them, in a column, clipped, each one with a date and a name.

"Now I am going to tell you the thing I have been carrying since March and you are going to find it worse than the number."

"Go on."

"That is what I have been holding for eleven years." She did not turn round. "Two hundred and fifty-eight charts, and under a third of them traceable to anybody, and a quarter of a district getting better faster than any medicine I have ever read about.

And every one of those bodies presents better than its own history says it should, and I have told you for four months that the reason is a mass that has not gone flat, and that was the best explanation I had, and it is wrong."

"It is wrong how."

"Because a mass that goes flat makes a body too well." She turned round. "That has been the whole of my working theory since March and it fits every chart in that rack and it does not fit you, and you have just handed me seven days of an instrument I have wanted for eleven years and the instrument says you are not the same as my charts and neither are my charts the same as you."

She came and stood in front of him.

"You are not a body that is too well. I have never met a body that was too well. I have met two hundred and fifty-eight." She put her hands on his shoulders and did not push.

"You are a load-bearing structure and the load is being held at a constant and the constant has been the same for three years, and if it has been the same for three years then it is not filling and it is not emptying and it is not a tank, and if it is not a tank then the thing it has been doing for three years is not accumulating."

He had not said anything for about half a minute.

"What is it doing."

"I do not know." She took her hands off him. "I have seven days of a reading, eleven years of charts, and a body that has held past the end of what it had for a hundred and fifty-eight people in a row.

And if I were going to make a sentence out of that I would not be a clinician. I would be a woman with an instrument and a theory, and I have got one of those, and I have never once been the second in eleven years, and I am telling you that tonight and not hiding it in a sentence."

"Twenty-three," she said, after a moment. "That is when it went, as far as anything in this room can tell, and it is the same day you worked out on the back of a paper bag in a kitchen, and I have had that in a drawer since the twenty-second and I have not been able to make it mean anything until about an hour ago."

He looked up at her.

"That is not a date I can give you," she said. "That is the thing I would give you if you asked me, and I am not going to be asked, because you have asked me nothing for four weeks and I have just told you about the whole of my frame and the whole of my rack in a room with a window.

That is more than I have given anybody in eleven years, and it is not a transaction, and if you turn it into one I will put the sheet back in the drawer and you can come back on the first."

"I am not going to make it a transaction."

"I know that and I am saying it out loud so that there is a date on it." She pulled the board round and turned the sheet face up and put it in front of him.

"That is yours and it is not a chart and it goes in no drawer of mine and it has no number on the face of it that the Office can use, and if you take it out of this room then it is a piece of paper and a piece of paper is worth nothing at all."

He looked at it for a long time.

"What do you want me to do with it."

"I want you to do nothing with it." Tessa Okonkwo took it back. "I want you to leave it in this room, and I want you to come back on the first of every month and put your name on a line in a day book and sit on that table while I write the date, and that is the whole arrangement and it has no clause in it about a figure."

She sat down on the table with her hands flat on her knees.

"And on the first of next month I am going to tell you something else and I am telling you on the twenty-ninth because you asked me a question this morning and it would be dishonest of me to answer it and then go back to being somebody who keeps things."

"Say it now."

"I am at the end of my rack." She said it in exactly the voice she used for a figure. "Twenty-seven on the wall and there are four walls and I have been counting them for two years and I do not have a fourth wall.

The last time I put a thing down that I had been holding, the person in the bed went in about four days afterwards, and that is one time in eleven years and it is the only one, and I have thought about it every week since, and I have not told anybody, and I have told you, and you are the first person I have told in a month and you are not a clinician."

"What is in the bed."

"Collapsed, and there was nothing wrong with him, and it was a man who came in with a woman who came in with a man, and all three of them had been on that floor the day before and none of them had been hurt, and there was no cause and I have looked for a cause for four days and there is no cause."

"What do you want."

"I want to be able to put it somewhere." Tessa Okonkwo stood up off the table and squared the rack and made the lamp cord straight where it hung. "Not you. Somewhere.

And I have had a clinic for eleven years and a flat above a laundry and a woman in Bellhouse who would take anything I asked her to hold, and I have not asked, and I have not asked because the answer to that question is that a woman in Bellhouse is a woman in Bellhouse, and a month ago you wrote a line in a book in this room and the first of every month is a day that does not happen to anybody else in this city.

And I do not know what it is yet and I have told you on the twenty-ninth because you asked me a question this morning and I would rather you had both than one."

She held the drawer open and then closed it.

"Twenty-seven on the wall," she said. "Twelve days, and nothing has come in since the thirteenth and nothing has gone out, and the one time in eleven years that a rack came down was the one I have just told you about.

And there is a new line in my book on the first of every month with your name on it and a date. I am telling you all of that on one morning because I am a clinician, and I do not keep things for people to find in a fortnight."

She gave him the sheet back and he took it.

"Left through the arcade and not the Exchange," she said, "and eat something, and I am going to be here on the first at nine and you are going to be late and I am going to say so."

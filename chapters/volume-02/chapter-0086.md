# Marks The Field Leaves

On the sixth of the month Runa Sato sent a runner to the branch with a folded slip that had no heading on it, only an hour and a court address, and Lin Hao went because the slip was in her hand and not a clerk's.

The court was off the Ferrier arc, a Field room with a table and two chairs and a shuttered window. She was there in field dress with her coat off and her sleeves pinned, and a file folder on the table with a requisition clipped to the front of it.

He felt desire and fear together, and the fear was the cleaner of the two. He wanted her, plainly, and he wanted to leave the room with the file still shut.

She put her hand flat on the folder. "Observation is closed. That was the twentieth of last month. The file is retained. That is the mandate. I am between two papers now and I am going to tell you what they are, and then I am going to ask you for something, and you can say no."

He sat. "Tell me the papers."

"One is an inventory summons. It names you for attendance off an analyst's sheet. Three names went up on the twentieth. Yours is the fourth they want." She tapped the folder. "The second is a requisition. It names the account. One digit. It wants the holder held at a desk for the term of the inquiry."

For Runa, who had lied in her own files since the spring to keep him out of them, having both papers on one table meant the lie had run out of rooms.

"The Wing wants me to bring you," she said. "The Field wants me to hold you where the Wing cannot issue. Both of those are orders I can obey. I am going to disobey one of them in writing tonight, and I want you to understand what that costs before you touch me."

He looked at the requisition clip. "What does disobedience cost."

"My standing. My division. The next time I carry weight nobody will sign for it." She said it flatly. "A field operative who refuses a requisition in writing is a woman with a file. That is what I am choosing."

She opened the folder and showed him the blank minute sheet under the two papers.

"I am going to write that I stood between the summons and the requisition and chose the summons. That I attended you to the inventory room and did not deliver you to the holding desk. That the Field retains the file and the Wing retains the inquiry. That is protection, and once it is written it is explicit, and once it is explicit the Wing can answer it."

He felt the weight of it. "Why tell me first."

"Because I want something from you and I am not going to take it without asking." She stood and came round the table and stood in front of him with her arms at her sides. "I want to be kept."

He looked up. "Say it plainer."

"I have kept you since the spring. I declined to write the report that would end you. I stood in a room on the seventeenth and refused to answer for you." Her voice did not shake. "I am tired of being the woman who keeps. I want a line in your keeping."

He stood. The room was small and the shuttered window let in a strip of light across her shoulder.

"Say the four things," he said.

She smiled, brief and real. "One: you tell me to stop, in any words, and I stop inside the second. Two: if I say a thing is wrong, I am right, and you do not argue. Three: I am not asking you for your plate or your file or a face or a number, and if you hand me those to be polite I will stop."

"And four."

"Four," Runa said. "I have wanted this since the yard in the spring and I have wanted it the way a person wants to put down something heavy. I want you to hold me down and not let me get up until I say so, because nobody has held me since I started carrying, and I am heavier than I look and I am tired of being the one who does the holding."

"Say mine," he said.

"You stop when I say. If you say a thing is wrong, I believe you. If I want you somewhere, I ask first." He held her eye. "And four: I do not know how to keep you. I have a room above a chop-house and a day book with my name in it and a household I did not ask for. I will put you in it if you want to be in it, and I will say so to anyone who asks."

She nodded. "That is four and it is true and it is enough."

There was a bed against the far wall that he had not looked at when he came in. It was narrow, made with a field blanket folded over a thin mattress and nothing else on it, and the shuttered window was above the head of it, and a jug stood on a crate turned upside down where a table should have been.

She saw him looking at it. "It is there because this is a room people are brought to. It has been used. I have had it washed twice today and there is cold water and there is nothing else, and if you want anything else you will not get it here, and I would rather you knew that now than at midnight."

"I have wanted less than that in worse rooms," he said.

"Then say what you want," Runa said, "because you have just told me where you will not stop and that is not the same thing, and I have watched you do it four times this year with women who were better at asking than I am."

She put her hand on the blanket and the room got smaller.

"Ask me for something out loud. Not the plate. Not a name. Something you want in the next hour that is about you, and do not be decent about it, because a decent answer in this room will get you the same two minutes you have been getting all spring."

He thought about it properly, which took longer than she expected, and she let him.

"I want you to stop counting," he said.

She went still.

"You count everything you carry and you count what other people carry, and you counted the seats in that room in the spring in under a minute," he said.

"You have had four numbers out of this table tonight and every one of them was about somebody else. I want you to put all of it down for an hour and put your weight on me, and not once ask me a question with a number in it."

"I do not want to be measured in here. I want to be the floor you put down."

"That is not what a man asks for," she said.

"It is the only thing I have wanted since March that I have not said in a room full of auditors."

She looked at him for a long moment, and then she laughed, once, with no amusement in it at all, and undid her belt and dropped it on the crate, and the sound of it went round the small room.

"Right," she said. "Then that is what this is. Come here."

She did not undress quickly and she did not make a ceremony of it. She unhooked the front of the tunic, pulled it off her shoulders and down her arms, and sat on the crate in her undershirt with her sleeves still pinned to the elbows, and let him look.

The harness had been on her for two years and it had left two pale bands across the tops of her shoulders and a darker line under the left one where the strap rode when she was carrying low on a descent. There was a bruise going yellow at the edge of one rib, and it was not fresh, and it was not his to ask about.

"Right," she said. "Now you are going to tell me the truth about that and I am going to believe you."

"It is two days old," he said. "You carried something on the second."

"Two days." She nodded. "You are right and it is not on any sheet. That is the arrangement and I am not going to stop it."

He put his hand on her shoulder, in the pale band, and she made a sound low in her chest that was not pain and he kept his hand there.

"Here," he said. "This is where you carry."

"Behind the bone," she said. "Under. It does not move and it is not going to move tonight, and if you press it you will feel how still it is, and I want you to feel it, because it is the only part of me that is not tired."

She took her undershirt off over her head. She was heavy in the way the book said she was, a stone and a half more than the eye agrees to, and the heaviness was in the shoulders and the hips and the backs of the thighs, and she stood square in the strip of window light and let him look at all of it without shifting her weight onto one leg.

"I am not going to be delicate about this," she said. "I have carried things that two men could not lift and I have put them down in front of a stranger and been told I was a marvel for it, and every one of those people wanted to look and none of them wanted to touch.

You are the first man in two years who has looked at me the way you looked at a landing with a load on it. Do not be tender with me about it. Be accurate."

She got him onto the bed with one hand on his shoulder, and the bed was not built for two people, and she dealt with that before he had a chance to.

"Side," she said. "Back to the wall. If I put all of this on top of you on a thing this narrow we will both spend the night on the floor and I have never once in my life got up off a floor gracefully."

He had his back against the plaster and her weight came down onto him and it was more than he expected and more than he could have described afterward, and she felt him take it and settled instead of lifting, which was the part he would remember: a woman weighing fourteen pounds more than she looked simply deciding to stay where she was.

"Good," she said. "That is the whole of it. Do not be gentle about it."

He got her tunic and her coat off the crate and her shirt and her smallclothes and set them in a stack, because she asked him to do it in that order and without hurrying, and he did, and she let him.

Then she came down onto him properly, and it was not a performance and there was nothing graceful in it.

She kissed him hard and with no doubt in it at all, the way she had done everything since he had met her, and he kissed her back and put his hand in her hair and she made a sound against his mouth that was relief more than want and did not apologise for either.

Her mouth went down his jaw and his throat and she bit him once, lightly, on the collarbone, and told him what she was about to do before she did it, out loud, in plain words, because that was in the four.

"Tell me where your hands are," she said, when she had her mouth elsewhere and the words came out ragged. "Both of them. Say it out loud and do not let me guess."

"Left one on your back. Right one under you, on the mattress."

"Keep both where they are for a minute."

He kept both where they were and she worked against his hand and made him feel every part of what she was asking for, and after a while she pulled up and looked at him.

"Your hands are fine," she said. "You are not shaking. I had you down as the kind who shakes. Do not tell me about that yet. I have enough on my plate tonight."

She took him into her mouth and she was not fast at it and not slow either, and she looked up at him about half way through with her weight on his thighs to hold him still, and when she had had what she came for she came off his mouth and wiped her chin with the back of her hand and said, "Right, that is one, and I am telling you it is one so that you cannot spend the next hour wondering whether it counted."

The plate did not move. Nothing was read off him and nothing was taken out of him and he found that he had stopped listening for it entirely, which he understood about four seconds later and did not examine.

They went on for a while without either of them counting it, and the shuttered window let a bar of light move a hand's width down the plaster over the head of the bed, and she used her arms to hold herself up and would not let him take any of that weight off her.

"You are being considerate," she said. "Do not be considerate. If you hold me up I am the one carrying again and I have had a week of carrying. Put your whole weight into it and let me decide what to do about it."

He did. She made a noise like something being set down on a hard floor, and her whole weight went over his hands where they were under her, and she said his name once, not loudly, and then made him say where his hands were again, and he did.

When she turned him over she was not careful with him and he liked it more than the care.

She got him face down on the narrow bed with his forearm folded under his forehead and came down along his back with both hands and her weight, and held him there with a forearm across the top of his shoulders and her hips against his.

She told him she was going to stay there until he asked her to move, and that he was not to be brave about it, and that if he wanted it lighter he was to say the word and she would take two of her pounds off immediately and without taking her knee off his spine.

He asked for it heavier.

The room was very quiet and the Field does not make noise and neither did he. There was the strap of her watch on the crate where she had put it down and her breathing above him, and the bar of window light crawling across the wall and then off it, and the two of them taking a long time over a thing that had no name on any paper in the building.

She moved him again after a while and he came up onto his back and she sat on him properly, all of her, with her hands under his shoulders, and that was the part where she stopped being the woman who keeps and was simply a woman on top of a man who had stopped counting.

"Ask me again," she said. "You have been sitting on what you wanted for an hour. Say it while I am on top of you and see whether you still want it."

"I want you to stay down," he said.

"You are a liar," she said, "and I am not moving. I asked you to stop counting and I am not going to stop you lying for me in the last ten minutes, because that is a different thing and I can carry both of them."

He got his hand round the back of her neck and she let him, and she came down onto him and stayed, and after that neither of them said anything at all for a length of time.

Twice she stopped him. The first time she put his palm flat on the blanket and made him say what was under it, wool and a mattress edge and the seam of the wall against his knuckles, and when he said it she went on.

The second time she made him say the room. The room was a shuttered window and a crate and a coat on a hook and a jug standing on the crate upside down, and he said it, and she said, "Good. Stay in it," and went on.

He understood then that this was not for him. It was a thing she did with people on a landing who had stopped being able to hear where they were.

She came off him and lay alongside with her head on his shoulder and her weight still against his side, and did not move away for a long time, and the shutter had gone grey.

"That is the first time since the spring," she said. "I am putting nothing on a chart. I am putting my name on something else."

He had one arm under his head and the other along her back, on the pale band, and he had been aware for some minutes that he could not feel his left foot and had decided that was a price and not a complaint.

"How long was it," he said.

"You did not ask me that for the first hour and you are asking it now." She lifted her head. "I do not know and I have been carrying for two years and I have never once known, and I am not going to start knowing tonight."

She got up and put her undershirt on and poured the jug over her own shoulders, which was what they did in a field wash, cold and graceless, and then she handed it to him and made him pour it over his own and stand there and count to ten while it ran off him onto the floor and got into the seam of the mattress.

"That is not washing," he said.

"That is the whole of washing," Runa said, "and I have done it two hundred times in doorways in the rain, and you can have hot water at the Vestry if you want to be a person about it."

She dressed and did her belt and pinned her sleeves and put her hair back under the field cap, and in the space of about four minutes the woman who had been on the bed stopped existing and a field operative stood at the table with a folder in front of her.

She sat down and put the minute sheet square and uncapped a pen.

"Now the paper," she said. "I am going to write it and you are going to watch me write it, and you are not going to sign it."

She wrote in her own hand, six lines, that she had stood between the inventory summons and the requisition on the sixth, and had attended the subject to inventory and declined to deliver to holding, and that the Field retained the file.

Then she wrote a seventh line, and read it aloud before she wrote it.

"For a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word on the sixth, at my request."

He felt the floor move. "That closes your way out."

"It closes my way out," she said. "It makes the protection explicit and therefore answerable. The Wing can now come at me instead of coming round me. That is what I am buying with it."

She signed it with her name and number and blotted it.

"You gave me a place," she said. "I am putting it where my division can read it. If they ask you, say I asked. Do not protect me from it."

He put his hand on the table beside the sheet and did not touch the pen.

"I asked," he said. "Say it once. Out loud. Not to the paper. To me."

"You asked," Runa said. "I chose. In writing. And I am glad."

He left with her weight still in his arms and no shirt and a file open behind him with his word in it in her hand, and the option of her being able to deny him was gone, and he could not walk it back.

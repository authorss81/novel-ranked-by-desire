# The Vestry Bench

On the thirteenth of the month he came down the arcade at four in the afternoon and sat on the stone bench outside the Vestry with his sleeve pushed up, because the bench had a brass plate on the end of it and he had never once read it and had decided today that he would.

Tessa Okonkwo came out of the ward office with the day book under her arm and stood over him without sitting down, and he understood that she was going to make him say what he had come for.

"Say it and then I will sit down," she said.

"I came to tell you there will not be a printed figure between the fifteenth and the twentieth."

She sat down.

The bench was warm on one half of it and cold on the other, which was how a stone bench works in a city in the afternoon, and about nine feet along it an old man was waiting for somebody who was late, and he had a folded bag between his shoes.

"Say the rest of it."

"A hold. A notice dated the ninth. Six days with no figure published, and the machine on the fourth floor of my branch does not run on the seventeenth." He put his sleeve down. "Your reading on the twenty-ninth has nothing to compare itself against. There is no number on a spike anywhere in this city between the fifteenth and the twentieth, and whatever I come in here with on the twenty-ninth will be a number I have nowhere to put."

Tessa did not answer for a while. She had the day book on her knees with both hands flat on it, and the book had his name on the first line and a date and no number, and the date was the first of the month and it was twelve days ago.

"Then that is the thing I have been waiting for somebody to say since the third," she said. "Not the grey. The grey is your own business and I have told you twice to eat. Sit still."

She turned round on the bench and looked back up her own corridor through the open door, at the four shut doors along the left-hand wall and the office at the end with a runner in it sorting charts one-handed onto three piles.

"Warden," she said, to the back of her own head, and then out loud, "leave that alone for a minute."

The girl looked up. Lin Hao started to say something about a name and Tessa put one finger up without looking at him, which stopped him dead.

"It is the twenty-second day," she said. "Of the grey. I have been counting since the twenty-third of last month because you would not and because a thing you are not counting is a thing that gets worse while you are looking at something else. I will put it in the day book on the twenty-ninth with the reading and you will not enjoy that."

"I would not mind."

"You would mind enormously and so would I," said Tessa, "and the whole of the point of that book is that it contains my name on eleven years of lines and your name on one, and if I start writing what I can see from a corridor in it then it stops being a clinical record and becomes a woman's diary about a man, and I have enough of those."

He wanted to tell her that the fourth floor of a clinic in a district of two million had been empty of arrivals for twenty-seven days and that it was him, and that saying it in the open air on a stone bench was going to be the worst sentence of his month. He had said worse sentences in rooms to analysts.

"The rack is twenty-seven," Tessa said. "It has been twenty-seven since the seventeenth of last month. That is twenty-seven days, and twenty-eight if you count the day it started on, and I have been putting both numbers in the ward returns for five months so that not one person in this building has to have the conversation that you are about to have."

"Have it."

"No. You have come down here to be kind to me about a notice and I am going to be unkind to you about a wall." She put her hand flat on the day book. "Twenty-six days of nothing arriving at a service I have kept for eleven years.

Every one of those twenty-seven days had somebody in it who needed a thing put down and had not one person in the building to put it down in, and about half of them went out of the ground-floor door with a worse headache than they came in with, and I know that because they come back and I see them."

For Tessa Okonkwo, who had refused for five months to build a sentence out of four days and eleven years, the wall had stopped being a number and become the reason.

"You have not spent since the thirteenth of last month," she said. "That is the whole of it.

That is the answer to twenty-seven days and it is the answer to a figure that does not move and to a mass that reads constant, and it is the answer to every question this office has been putting to you since the third, and I have known it since the third and I have not written it down because I am a clinician and not an auditor and I do not put things in files that I cannot support with an instrument."

"You can support it with yours."

"I can support it with mine and I will not," said Tessa, "because the moment that instrument is named in a sentence about you it stops being mine. It becomes a reference. And a reference is read by people who were never told anything about you and who will never ask you anything, and I have been sitting on that bench listening to you work that out for five weeks and I am telling you now that I am not putting my drawer anywhere near it."

The old man nine feet along had opened his bag and was eating something out of it with his fingers.

"So the twenty-ninth stands," Lin Hao said.

"The twenty-ninth stands at nine in the morning, and you will put your arm on the table and I will read it, and there will be no printed figure anywhere in this city to say whether it has moved.

"If it has moved then it is the first time since the thirteenth of last month and not one person but the two of us will ever know it. And if it has not moved then that is a fact about your mass held by one clinician in a building with four beds in it and no clerk at the desk after four o'clock."

"The clerk is at the desk."

"The clerk is at the desk until the nineteenth," Tessa said, "because her wrist mends on the nineteenth and after the nineteenth the boards go up the stairs and after that the desk at four o'clock is a corridor with a chair in it." She glanced at the girl and the girl said nothing and kept sorting, one-handed, fast, with the bad wrist tucked in against her ribs.

"She does four times the work at that desk as she does on the stairs and she will not go back up them and I have not made her, and on the twentieth of this month I will be running a floor where the desk at four o'clock is empty and an office that has decided your reading is absent is going to send somebody to ask about it."

She stood up and picked up the day book.

"There is one more thing and I am going to say it because you sat down on that bench and I am not going to make you climb four flights to hear it."

"Say it."

"The third of next month there will be three of them in a column." Tessa held the book against her chest. "A summary not produced, given by the holder in person, three times, and then it stops being a service failing to reply and becomes a service being asked properly and not answering.

I have until the third of next month to write one line that says eleven years of a rack without producing eleven years of a rack, and if I write it and it is true they will send somebody up these stairs to look at the floor.

And they will not send the girl with the wrist, because from tomorrow she will be back up two flights with a board in each hand, and a man who wants to look at a rack does not need a girl who cannot carry."

Lin Hao stood up off the warm half of the bench.

"They will send somebody," he said, "and you will tell them the number is on the wall and the reason is not at the counter, and that is the answer you gave the Exchange clerk on the third, and it has a shape that a clerk can carry up a staircase without you."

"It has a shape that ends in your name," Tessa said. "You are the reason that wall is where it is.

You are the reason there is a corridor with nothing arriving in it for twenty-seven days, and the day a man from this office puts his head round that door he is going to work backwards from an empty rack to a man who has not spent in a month, and it will take him four minutes, and I am not going to be able to stand in front of the rack and lie to him about a thing I can measure."

The arcade went grey outside the doors. Somebody at the far end was running the tap over in the trough, the long slow way it is run at the end of an afternoon, and the sound carried the length of the vault and stopped.

"So write the line," he said.

"I have not said I would."

"You did not have to." He pulled his sleeve up again and put his forearm out. "Read it now and I will sit here while you do it, and I will not offer to write anything, and I will not help, and that is the arrangement you made with the girl in this corridor nine days ago and it worked, so I am asking you to make it with me."

Tessa Okonkwo read his weight with two hands, twice, and wrote nothing down, which she told him afterwards was the result and not an omission.

"Read it again on the twenty-ninth," she said. "At nine. And bring your coat, because it has turned and the arcade is colder than the clinic and you sit on cold benches like a man who is waiting for something and I have watched three men do it this month."

She went in with the day book under her arm and the girl at the desk did not look up, and the old man nine feet along finished what he was eating and put the bag back between his shoes and sat with his hands on his knees, waiting for a person who had not come, and there were twenty-seven days on a wall in that building that had a name in them now and only one of them knew it.

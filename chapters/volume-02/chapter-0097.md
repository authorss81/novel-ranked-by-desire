# The Auditor's Answer

On the evening of the seventeenth he went to Lantern Row because the corridor at the Vestry had a woman with a card on it in his memory, and because there was one person in Ostara who could tell him in a sentence what had been decided about a refusal.

Ines Duarte had her table against the wall with four books open on it and none of them hers, and she had cleared about two feet at the near end, and there was a lamp on a stand with the wick turned down, and the little stove in the corner had been lit an hour ago because Lantern Row is cold after the fourth of the month and never before it.

"Sit down," she said. "Do not take your coat off yet."

He sat down.

"My refusal went into the counter hand at half past eight on the morning of the thirteenth," she said. "It is dated the fourteenth. It is signed, and the reason in it is four sentences long, and every one of them is about a document and not about you.

"It has been on a desk for four days and nothing has come back, and that is worse than a refusal, because a refusal at least gets a number. A thing that gets read and gets nothing has been decided about by somebody who did not need to write to me. Somebody has been told not to write to me. I am not important enough to be told, and I am going to find out in about a week from somebody being sorry." She put her pen down.

"Give me a glass of water now rather than in an hour. I am telling you that as a courtesy and not as a request, because I am going to ask you for something in about ten minutes and I would like there to be no debts standing in the room."

He got her the water.

"Now the other thing," she said. "The word on your line."

"Say the word."

"It goes faster than you think." She turned one of the books round and pushed it across the table. It was her own copy of side one, in her own hand, and it had one line on it and nothing else.

"One line reading himself," she said. "That is what it says, and that is what I wrote on the sixth of last month after nine days of not sleeping, and I want you to tell me what you have understood it to mean for four months."

"That I drew on myself. That it is the only entry in a hundred and twelve that could never have been relief, because there was nothing in me that came from me."

"That is what the Classification Wing has understood it to mean, and it is what the Review room understood it to mean, and it is what I understood it to mean for four months, and it is wrong." She put her finger on the entry.

"I have known it was wrong since the twelfth of this month and I have not told you, and I am telling you now because on the eighteenth there is a paper and you will be asked to answer it, and you cannot answer it with a reading I gave you in a drawer in February."

"The ledger reports in people and it does not report direction," she said. "Every one of the hundred and twelve lines is a pair. There is a person who was relieved and there is a person it came from, written side by side, and it has always been written that way since before either of us was at this table.

"Your line has only one name on it."

He looked at it. She was right. He had read that line perhaps two hundred times and had never once looked at what was missing from it, and what was missing was the other half of the pair, because there was nobody on that side of it.

"There is no word for the far end of your line," Ines said. "Not because it has not been found. Because the ledger has never needed one. Every other entry has a person at each end and the pair is the whole of the entry, and yours has one person and a relationship, and the relationship is himself, which is what a ledger prints when it has a party and no source to pair it with.

"So it does not read from him. It reads to him, from somewhere with no name on this side of it. You were standing in a room with sixty people in it on the thirteenth of last month holding something, and you gave it away, and the ledger printed a line for everybody you gave it to and one line for you, and it printed the only word it had."

Lin Hao sat with his hands flat on his knees.

"That was not a spend," he said.

"That was never a spend," said Ines. "It is the only line in that book that is not one, and every office in this city has read it as one for four months, because a spend is the only thing a ledger has ever been."

She turned the book back.

"Now here is the part I have been sitting on since the twelfth and I am going to say it once. If that line goes into a file, this office has a description of you. Not a figure. Not a band. Not a mass. A shape.

And the shape is one sentence long and it is the sentence that four offices have spent four months refusing to say out loud, and I am not going to say it in a room, and I am going to say it to you once at this table with a lamp on it because you are entitled to know what is being kept out of a file on your behalf."

She waited until he looked at her.

"You are not a tank," said Ines Duarte. "You are a door."

The two rooms on Lantern Row were very quiet, and the stove ticked, and he found that he had no answer and that the not having one was not fear.

"Everything that has ever settled in you came in through you from somewhere," she said. "That is not a figure with a settling horizon on it. That is a mechanism, and a mechanism can be described, and an office that has a description of a mechanism can write down what happens to it. That is all classification is.

It is a document that says what a thing is made of and what may be done with it, and every man ever classified in this city came back with nothing, and the nothing is not a punishment. The nothing is what is left when the description has been taken and there is no longer a use for the thing."

"Then you can close their inquiry."

"I can close it in a fortnight," said Ines. "That is what I have been telling myself since the twelfth and it is true and it is the reason I have not been sleeping. One line, one sheet, signed, filed under a finding number that already exists. They read it.

They write down that a settled mass is an accumulation and not a generation, they strike forty days of a settling horizon off their own file, the inquiry closes, and you walk out of this business a free man with a band you still cannot carry."

"And the file it opens."

"The file it opens is a file about a door," she said, "and a file about a door is a file about every person who has ever been through it, and it will be assembled twice a month out of four offices by somebody who does it well, and it will go to six floors, and Lin Hao, there is not one name on the eighty-odd that would mean anything to me that I could not find."

He felt the cold off the stove and thought about a notebook woman's season on a third seat and a woman in a dark coat at a gate at four in the morning and a hundred and twelve lines in a book that reported in people.

"So you are not going to file it."

"I am not going to file it on their schedule," said Ines. "Those are not the same words and you should hear the difference, because I have thought about nothing else for five days.

"If I hold it, I am an agent outside procedure on a document with a finding number on it. I did that once for a district in a matter with nothing whatever in it and lost four months of work and a good name with a man I would have liked to keep it with, and I did it again for a friend and it cost her a ticket. So I am not romantic about it. I will be reviewed. I may lose the district.

I will not be able to produce one thing again without a fight, and one day there will be a woman with a card at this door who is not as pleasant as the one who came to your corridor yesterday."

"And if you do file it."

"Then the only true document in Ostara about you is one I wrote, and it went in to close an inquiry, and in nine years somebody reads it and finds my name in the preamble under a finding number and cannot tell whether I filed it because it was true or because it was the fastest way out of a review. That question gets asked of me once. I have to get it right."

She sat back.

"I decided on the twelfth and I have carried it about four days waiting to tell you, because the decision is mine and you are not going to have an opinion about it and I did not want you in the room while I made it.

"I am going to refuse the production.

And when they come with a second request, which will be narrower and in a worse voice, I am going to refuse that one too, and at some point in the next year they will put me in front of a review and I will stand there and say that I declined to hand over a document that would have described a man as a mechanism, on the ground that the description was true and that being true was not the same as being owed to anybody.

"And I am not going to file it in a drawer and sleep. It stays with me, on this table, in my hand, legible to me for the rest of my career, and if they take my ticket then it goes with me."

She put both hands flat on the table.

"You have been wrong about yourself since March," said Ines Duarte, "and I have watched it every week for four months, and I have never once been able to correct you, because not one person in this city can read you, and you are the only man in Ostara who has never been described by a person who knew what they were looking at.

"I have spent three years reading every record that passes through this table, and the thing that happens to me when I hold one is that it stays and the years go. I know the sum of that exactly and I do it anyway, and I am not going to tell you the sum.

"So here is the part, and I have been putting it off since the seventh because I am a coward about this in a way I have not been about anything else in eleven years. I am going to touch you tonight and read you, and I am going to do it out loud, and I am going to tell you where you are wrong.

And I am telling you now that this is the thing I want and it is not a favour and it is not for your file and it is not me being kind to a man not one other person will look at. I want to be the one who reads you correctly. I want it more than I have wanted anything since I sat down at this table in the spring."

"That is a strange thing to want."

"It is the only thing I have ever wanted that is mine," she said, "and I have wanted to be the one who knows for eleven years."

She stood up and pushed the four books to the far end of the table and took her jacket off the back of the chair and hung it on the hook by the door where her own plate was, and then she turned round and put her hands out flat, palms up, the way she had put them flat on the table, and waited.

"Four things," she said, "and then I am not going to say anything else tonight until you have said yours."

"Go on."

"One. I have been awake since five and I have had three glasses of water and nothing else, and I am not impaired and I am not going to pretend to be." She said it flatly. "Two. Anything I ask you to do, I will ask you twice, and if you say the word wrong even once I will stop. Three.

I am going to correct you and I am not going to do it gently, and if you want me to stop then you say so and I will stop and I will not think less of you for asking. A man who has never been corrected does not know that it can be done kindly."

"And four."

"Four," said Ines. "I want you to have said something true and unpleasant about yourself and I want to be the one who said it. That is not fair to you and I am doing it anyway and I am telling you at the start so that you cannot say afterwards that you did not know the terms."

"Say yours," he said.

"One. I am not going to sleep tonight and you are not going to either and neither of us is going to pretend that is a complaint. Two. I will stop when you ask and I will believe you the first time. Three. I am not asking you for your plate, your file, your account number or a sentence you can use with anybody, and if you offer me one of those I will get up and put my coat on.

And four: I have wanted this since the eighth of last month and I have wanted it in the way that wants a thing to be made correct, and I have never once in my life wanted a man to be right about himself and I would like to find out what that is like, and that is the whole of my wanting and I am not going to dress it up."

They did not get up and put coats on.

She was cold to begin with and he was not, and she made him take the blanket off the back of the couch and put it round her shoulders while she worked the buttons of her own blouse with one hand, which took longer than it should have and which she refused to let him help with and then let him help with, which she told him afterwards was the first thing that had gone wrong and the only thing.

The lamp on its stand was the only light in the two rooms for a long time, and the stove went from ticking to a low pulse.

She had him sit on the floor with his back against the side of the couch and his legs out, and she knelt on the rug in front of him with his forearm in her hands. She began at the wrist, because the wrist is where a person starts when the person has been doing something with their hands for four years.

"That is wrong," she said, before she had touched him for ten seconds.

"What is."

"You have a hard place on the inside of your left wrist about two inches up and you have been telling yourself for about four months that it is the cold." She put her thumb on it. "It is not the cold. You clean six floors. The cold does not do that.

That is a thing you have done four thousand times a week with a cloth in that hand and it is in the muscle and it will be there when you are forty and it is not a wound and it is not a mark and it is not anybody's relief sitting in you. It is your own labour, in your own body, being paid for by nobody. Do you see what I have just done."

"You have told me a thing about myself that is not in any file."

"I have told you a thing about yourself that you could have found out by looking at your own arm," said Ines. "That is what being corrected is. It is almost always smaller and more boring than you were afraid of. Sit still."

She went up the forearm and found three things, of which one was real and two were stories, and she read each of them out loud in the flat voice, name, place, age in her own terms, verdict, and he found that being wrong about himself out loud in front of somebody who was right was the most exposing thing that had ever happened to him and the most restful.

"Say it back," she said. "Say what is in your forearm."

"A thing I have done four thousand times a week for four years that nobody has paid me for."

"Good. Now the other one. Say what you have been telling people about the grey at your temple."

"Ten days of it."

"Twenty-five days of it," said Ines. "And you have not been counting properly since the third because you have been counting from the day it started instead of from the day it started counting, and I have watched you do it wrong for a fortnight and I could not tell you which of two numbers you meant and neither could you. Do you know why that matters?"

"No."

"It matters because an entry with the wrong date on it is not a small error," she said. "It is the only kind of error that cannot be corrected later without somebody having to decide which of two true things you meant. I have spent eleven years arguing about that in rooms. Hold still."

He put his hand on her back and she leaned into it and did not stop reading.

After a while her hands went up under his shirt at the ribs and the reading went on there, and she told him that the fourth rib on his left was a thing he had been guarding since the spring, and that guarding was a habit and not a sensation.

He said she was wrong, and she said the word wrong in a voice so pleased with itself that he laughed out loud for the first time in a month.

"You like it."

"I have been correcting nobody for four months," she said, "and you have the single most wrong account of your own body of anybody I have ever read."

That was when it turned, and neither of them decided when.

She came up off the rug and into his lap and put her hands on either side of his face and made him look at her. He was hard against her thigh before either of them had done anything deliberate about it.

She looked down at the place where it was happening and then back at his face.

"That is also in the file," she said, "and it is also wrong."

"Say what it actually is."

"It is a man who has not touched anybody for three weeks being touched by somebody who is going to correct him. Those are not the same thing, and you know the difference, and you are not going to pretend you do not."

He got her blouse open and his hands were not steady and she took one of them and put it flat on her belly and said, "There. That one is not shaking. I have been watching your hands since the seventh of last month and that is the first steady one."

"That is a low bar."

"It is the only bar in this city and you have failed it since March," she said, and kissed him.

She kissed him the way she read, which is to say she did it in order and without hurry and with the evident intention of finding out what was there, and when she had found it she said so out loud, plainly, in words he would not have used, and she said it twice because the first time she wanted to see his face while she said it.

Her mouth went down his throat and her teeth grazed and she told him where her hands were before she moved them, both of them, one on his hip and one on the outside of his thigh, and when he put his hand on the back of her neck she let him keep it there.

"You are going to tell me where my hands are," she said, against his chest. "Both of them. Say it out loud and do not make me guess."

"Right one on your back. Left one on your neck."

"Keep them."

He kept them.

She sat back off him, got her own skirt open, and took him into her mouth without any preamble at all, which he had not expected and which undid him faster than anything that had happened in a month.

She looked up at him about half way through with her hands flat on his thighs holding him still, and she was neither slow nor fast. When she had had what she came for she came off him, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, and sat back on her heels.

"That is one," she said. "And I am telling you it is one so that you do not spend the next two hours wondering whether it counted, which is a thing you do, which is another thing about yourself that is wrong, and which I will come back to later."

There was nothing ceremonial about the rest of it and nothing careful.

The blanket came off her shoulder and then off the couch and ended up on the floor on top of the rug. He got the rest of her blouse open and she let him do it and then took over, because her hands knew what they were doing and had been given something to do for eleven years without a man in the room.

"Left," she said. "Go left."

"Left where."

"Where your hand wants to go. I am not going to steer you and then tell you afterward that I did not steer you."

It went left. She made a sound over him that was not a word and put her fingers into his hair and held on, and the thing he had been braced against all night arrived anyway, which was that he was not careful at all once he stopped, and that he had been careful for four years because nothing in his life had ever once let him not be.

"There," she said. "That. That is the man with eight turned lines in four years. Say his name."

"What."

"You have just described him exactly and you cannot hear yourself do it. Say his name."

"Lin Hao."

"Say it again, and slower, and do not stop what your hands are doing to say it."

He said it again, and she shook her head against his chest.

"You have been careful with four women for five weeks because you thought you were going to break something," she said. "You have not broken one thing. The carefulness is the single most expensive habit you have and I have watched you spend it on four women and get nothing at all for it."

"You do not know that."

"I have the only record of you in this city," said Ines, "and it has one hundred and twelve lines in it and eight of them are turned and one of them is him, and a man with eight turned lines in four years is not careful. A careful man has none. You have been spending yourself like a man in a hurry and calling it restraint, and I have watched you do it since March."

"Say it again."

"I am not going to say it again. I said it once and I will not be a woman who repeats herself to a man in a bed."

She turned him over onto his back and then turned herself and got him behind her, one hand back flat on his chest to hold him where she had put him, and she talked him through it in that same level voice, naming every place her hands were going before they went, which she had made him agree to at the start and which he now found he would have paid almost anything to be allowed to keep.

She told him what she wanted in the plainest words she had. It was not a performance and not a request and not a favour, and there was nothing tentative anywhere in it.

"Say it back," she said, when he had gone still for a moment.

"You are going to tell me where your hands are," he said.

"That is not what I said and you know it. That is my rule and you are using it as a door. Say what I want."

"You want me to stop being careful."

"I want you to stop being careful," said Ines Duarte, "and I want to hear you say it out loud tonight, because there is going to come a day when a room full of strangers asks you to stand up in front of all of them and say what you are, and the only version of that answer worth having is one you have already said to somebody."

He said it.

She made him say it twice more, and neither of them used the word please once.

The stove had gone to ash a long time before either of them noticed. Afterwards she lay along him with her weight on his side and one hand flat on his chest, counting nothing, and the room had gone cold at the edges and stayed warm in the middle of it, and neither of them got up for a long time.

"Nothing came in and nothing went out," she said. "I would know. I have been sitting against a ledger for three years and I know what it feels like when you move, and there is nothing in me this evening that is not still where it was, and I would like you to hear that from somebody who is paid to hear movement for a living."

"That is the only thing you have to say about it."

"It is the only thing anybody in this city can say about it, and it is worth more than every sentence the Wing has put in a file since the third of last month," she said, "and I have been trying to say that to four offices for five weeks."

She got up before he did and put the kettle on and found two cups that did not match, and came back with them and sat on the floor with her back against the couch and her shoulder against his knee.

"Say the line to me," she said. "Not what it means. What it says."

"One line reading himself," he said.

"One line reading himself," said Ines Duarte, "which is the only line in a hundred and twelve with no other end, and the only reason is that on the thirteenth of last month you were standing in a room holding a door open, and every person you gave it to got a pair, and the ledger had no word for the party at the hinge, so it wrote the one word it had and it has been reading itself ever since in four offices like a mistake."

She drank her water.

"I am not filing it," she said.

"And on the eighteenth you are going to be asked to describe yourself in a room, and you are not going to do it, and when you do not do it, I want you to know that there is one person in this city who has read the description correctly and is not going to give it to anybody. She read it correctly tonight, at this table, with you asleep on her couch, and she is not going to put it in an envelope in the morning."

On Lantern Row the four books were open on the far end of a table and one of them had a single line in it with a person at one end of it and no word at the other, and it was legible to the woman who had written it and to the man who had been in the room while she wrote it, and to not one other person in Ostara.

It was sitting on a table for nothing. That was the part neither of them had got round all evening: a true description of him, unwitnessed by any office in this city, held by one woman, and the whole price of it was a career and a night without sleep.

Within four days there would be a paper with the shape of his account on it going to somebody's desk, and the only person in Ostara who could have told that desk what the shape was had spent the night in his arms and was not going to.

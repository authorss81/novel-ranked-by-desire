# The House Behind Him

He came down to the room above the chop-house on the first of the month at eight in the morning with the day book date in front of him and the sheet from the building in his coat, and the room was already full.

Nadia Ferrous was at the table with her coat on. Beatrix Halm stood by the window with her hands in her pockets. Tessa Okonkwo sat on the sill with a mug she was not drinking. Ines Duarte had the four-sided sheet folded in her lap. Marguerite Vale was on the stairs with a porter's docket in her hand, and Ola Bright had a pencil and had already started writing before anybody spoke.

Sun Meiling was on the landing at the top of the stairs with her coat still on and a sheet of pins under her arm, and she came in last and did not sit.

He felt anger first, low and specific, not at the room but at the four o'clock tray three streets away. He wanted for the morning to get the name back off the paper.

"You have it," Nadia said. "Say it."

He put the sheet on the table. It was Ruta Vesely's hand at the bottom, and the name under it, and the hour the thin book had printed.

Nadia read it without touching it. "That is me. That is my building and my floor and the tenth. And it went out at four."

"It went out at four," he said.

Beatrix took her hands out of her pockets. "Porters were asked on the twenty-first and the twenty-fourth. The yard gate twice. The lift on the tenth. That is three places with your people in them."

Marguerite put the docket down. "My porter answered a question about arithmetic. He did not give a name. He gave a count of coats in a hall. The woman wrote the rest herself."

Ola looked up. "Do we want them to stop asking."

"No," Ines said. "We want them to ask in the right order. A gate man first is a rumour. A lift second is a list."

Tessa set the mug down. "Say what it costs. Not what it means. What it costs."

Nadia stood. She did it the way she did everything in that room, without checking who was watching. "It costs me being the person nobody had written down. I have run a lift for four years and I have never been in a file except as a roster line. Now I am in a file about him. That is a different file."

She looked at him. "Say it back to me."

He had been asked twice for that sentence and had not said it, and the paper had said it first. He knew what it would buy and what it would not.

"You gave her the building on the tenth on purpose," he said. "You worked it out four seconds before she asked. You told me on the twenty-second. You claimed it on the thirtieth in front of everybody. I put it in an office file on the thirty-first because the third question was coming."

Nadia held his eye. "Again."

"You are the first thing that happens," he said. "Not me. If she uses it, it happens to you first. That is minuted in this room."

She sat down. The anger in him eased enough that he could feel the rest of it, which was shame, plain and useful.

Sun Meiling came down the last two stairs and put her sheet of pins on the table by the mug, which was the only thing in the room she had any right to touch.

"I am going to say the wrong thing," she said, "and I would like it minuted as the wrong thing and not as a woman being difficult in a room she is not sworn into."

"Say it."

"I have been in four offices that protect people," Sun said. "In every one of them the people who were protected were the ones the protection worked for. The sworn are the ones who get requisitioned. The unsworn are the ones who get described."

She put her finger on the edge of the table and did not push on it. "You are all about to stand between me and a list. What that does is make me the reason there is a list at all, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that before the twenty-ninth of this month, when I am considerably more useful to them in a corridor than I am on a stair."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Write that down," Ines said. "Write it as said by her, in those words, and date it, and let it sit on the table where people can see it."

Ola wrote it down and dated it the first and did not soften a word of it.

Marguerite picked up the docket. "Then the house is on paper. Seven women with one man who has no address, and the paper has an address for every one of us except the one it is about."

"There are seven of you in this room now," Ines said, "and I am counting the one on the stairs, and the paper is wrong about him in a different way from the one it is wrong about us."

"He has an address now. One. He said it on the twenty-sixth. The shield is gone."

Ola wrote that down too.

Beatrix went to the window. "My tram keeps the season woman. She rides four turns. The guard has checked her four times. I am not refusing her. If I refuse her she finds another tram, and then she has somebody else's tram, and the next time she stands outside a gate at four in the morning I am not the one she sees."

"That is not a cost."

"That is the cost," Beatrix said. "The cost is that I am now a woman in a tram depot who is known to have been asked, and the only way to be un-asked is to be un-helpful, and I have decided to be helpful and I would like it recorded that I decided it myself at about six this morning."

Tessa stood. "My wall is twenty-seven. Nothing has come in since the seventeenth. I am not moving anything."

"For Marguerite, the typed line in the corridor means her porters would be asked again by the next woman with no paper."

Marguerite had gone very still on the stairs.

"Tell them," she said.

Ines said, "Tell us what."

"Tell me what the instruction says." Marguerite held the docket by one corner. "It came round the doors at seven this morning with a man who would not give his name. The hall porter has been told that from today every caller, every parcel and every inquiry about a resident is to be entered in a visitors' book at the desk, with the hour, and the book is to be read on request by anybody from the Exchange who arrives with a card."

The room was very quiet.

"That is not a question," Ines said. "That is a building that has been told to become a register."

"That is a building that has been told to become a register," Marguerite agreed, "and it is my building, and I have had it eleven years, and the porter on the desk needs a glass to read the hour column and will do it wrong."

"Will he do it wrong on purpose?"

"He will do it wrong because he is frightened and because it is easier." She put the docket down. "If I let it stand, I am running a visitors' book for that office and every name in my house is in it twice, once when they come in and once when I read it over. If I refuse it, the next woman with no paper does not get refused, because there is nothing left to refuse, and my porter loses a wage he has had since I was twenty."

Nadia said, quietly, "Do it."

"You do not get to do it for me."

"He is right," Nadia said, and put her hand flat on the table. "That is not me doing it. That is me saying a lift runner knows what an hour column does to a frightened man in about ten seconds."

Marguerite looked at her for a moment and then nodded once.

"Then it stands," she said, "and the glass comes off my mantel, and he writes it with it, and every name that goes in that book goes in twice, and I will read the book myself at the end of every week and know exactly what has been read."

"That is the cost," Ines said.

"That is one cost," Marguerite said. "You will hear the rest of it when it is charged."

Nadia put her token on the table. "I am still running the lift. I am not asking to be moved. If I move, the file says I moved."

He looked round the room. Seven women, none of them leaving, none of them asking him to choose, and one of them on the stairs who had just agreed to run a book for the office that was hunting him.

"What do you want from me," he said.

"Stay where we can find you," Beatrix said. "You walk the long road and you do not look at the Board and you come here when there is paper. That is all."

"Second that," Nadia said. "And one more thing. Do not come here less often to make it look like there is less to find."

He nodded. It was a small promise and it closed nothing, and the cost of it was that he could no longer pretend the household was outside the inquiry.

Tessa came off the sill and put the mug in the sink and washed it, which was the only thing she did in the room that had nothing to do with anybody.

"On the twenty-ninth," she said. "That is four weeks from the first and you are coming on the first of every month as well, and if you arrive here on a morning and there is nothing on the table you will not know whether nothing happened or whether we have stopped writing things down for you. That is the arrangement now. I am telling you so that it cannot be used against you later by either of us."

When he left for the Vestry at nine, Ola tore off the top sheet of her pad and gave it to him. It had the date and seven names and one line under them in her hand.

Under the seven names she had written Sun Meiling's sentence and the date against it, and under that she had written *visitors' book, Bellhouse, stands from the first*, because it was the only thing in the room that would have changed in the building by lunchtime.

He put it in his coat next to the analyst's sheet and went down the stairs with a household behind him that an office could now look up, and a visitors' book downstairs that would have his name in it twice by the end of the week if he walked through the door of the house again.

# The Quiet Floor

On the third of the month the Vestry floor was quiet in a way Lin Hao had never heard it, and he came up the stairs at ten with a bag of lint for the ward and found Tessa Okonkwo at the desk with the instrument sheet face down.

He felt grief before he knew what it was for, heavy and slow. He wanted to ask for the reading and was afraid to say the hour wrong.

She turned the sheet over. Three readings, ninety seconds apart, the same number. The shape under it in her hand: held at a constant.

“It has not moved,” she said. “The mass is settled and the plate is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and both of those are true.”

For Tessa, who had kept a rack for eleven years, the quiet meant the ward had stopped sending her anything to hold.

He looked past her to the corridor. The four quiet rooms were shut. The second had its shoes gone from beside the bed.

“Ilse Korvin went on the twenty-ninth,” Tessa said. “She stood in the street with a woman from the ward and she went in and she said she did not go. The standing closed. She went home on the thirtieth.”

“That is good.”

“It is good and it is not the thing.” She stood and took him down the corridor to the cold store, where the lamp hung on a cord and the couch nobody used had a blanket folded on it.

A girl sat on the couch with her hands round a mug. She was a ward runner, one he had seen carrying charts for two months, and her left hand shook against the mug in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

“She carried twenty-seven boards down two flights yesterday because I asked her to,” Tessa said, low. “She has carried them for two months. Yesterday the seventh board slipped and she caught it with her wrist. It is a sprain. It will mend in days.”

The girl looked up and smiled and was embarrassed.

Tessa bent and took the wrist and held it the way she held everything, and the girl stopped being embarrassed because the hands were sure.

“She will not carry boards for a fortnight,” Tessa said. “I have no other runner. The boards stay where they are. The charts do not get filed. The office form that asks for eleven years of numbers gets nothing this week.”

He understood the rule and what it did to one person. For the girl, a fortnight off the stairs meant she would sit in the ward and be looked at and have nothing to do with her hands.

Tessa wrote the wrist up and gave the girl the afternoon off and sent her to the kitchen for tea. Then she stood in the cold store with the rack behind her and the instrument sheet in her hand.

“I refused to make a sentence out of four days and eleven years,” she said. “I am still refusing. The floor is quiet because nothing is arriving. Nothing arriving is not the same as nothing wrong.”

He put the lint down. “What do you want me to do.”

“Come on the first and put your name down. You did that yesterday. Now come on the twenty-ninth and ask me again. That is the arrangement and I am not changing it because a girl sprained her wrist.”

He nodded. The grief stayed, because the floor had changed for the worse in a person and not in a chart, and there was no form that asked for that.

At the door she stopped him. “You look grey. Eleventh day yesterday, twelfth today. Eat something before your shift.”

He went down by the arcade with the quiet behind him, and the quiet was worse than the rack at twenty-seven.

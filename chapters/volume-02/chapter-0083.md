# The Quiet Floor

On the third of the month the Vestry floor was quiet in a way Lin Hao had never heard it, and he came up the stairs at ten with a bag of lint for the ward and found Tessa Okonkwo at the desk with the instrument sheet face down.

He felt grief before he knew what it was for, heavy and slow. He wanted to ask for the reading and was afraid to say the hour wrong.

She turned the sheet over. Three readings, ninety seconds apart, the same number. The shape under it in her hand: held at a constant.

"It has not moved," she said. "The mass is settled and the plate is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and both of those are true."

For Tessa, who had kept a rack for eleven years, the quiet meant the ward had stopped sending her anything to hold.

He looked past her to the corridor. The four quiet rooms were shut. The second had its shoes gone from beside the bed.

"Ilse Korvin went on the twenty-ninth," Tessa said. "She stood in the street with a woman from the ward and she went in and she said she did not go. The standing closed. She went home on the thirtieth."

"That is good."

"It is good and it is not the thing." She stood and took him down the corridor to the cold store, where the lamp hung on a cord and the couch nobody used had a blanket folded on it.

A girl sat on the couch with her hands round a mug. She was a ward runner, one he had seen carrying charts for two months, and her left hand shook against the mug in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"She carried twenty-seven boards down two flights yesterday because I asked her to," Tessa said, low. "She has carried them for two months. Yesterday the seventh board slipped and she caught it with her wrist. It is a sprain. It will mend in days."

The girl looked up and smiled and was embarrassed.

Tessa bent and took the wrist and held it the way she held everything, and the girl stopped being embarrassed because the hands were sure.

"She will not carry boards for a fortnight," Tessa said. "I have no other runner. The boards stay where they are. The charts do not get filed. The office form that asks for eleven years of numbers gets nothing this week."

He understood the rule and what it did to one person. For the girl, a fortnight off the stairs meant she would sit in the ward and be looked at and have nothing to do with her hands.

Tessa wrote the wrist up and gave the girl the afternoon off and sent her to the kitchen for tea. Then she stood in the cold store with the rack behind her and the instrument sheet in her hand.

"I refused to make a sentence out of four days and eleven years," she said. "I am still refusing. The floor is quiet because nothing is arriving. Nothing arriving is not the same as nothing wrong."

He put the lint down. "What do you want me to do."

"Come on the first and put your name down. You did that yesterday. Now come on the twenty-ninth and ask me again. That is the arrangement and I am not changing it because a girl sprained her wrist."

He nodded. The grief stayed, because the floor had changed for the worse in a person and not in a chart.

At the door she stopped him. "You look grey. Eleventh day yesterday, twelfth today. Eat something before your shift."

He went down by the arcade with the quiet behind him, and the quiet was worse than the rack at twenty-seven, and the form was already three days old.

It was waiting when he came back through at half past two, on the desk in the ward office with a clerk from the Exchange standing next to it holding it flat with both hands.

The form had a heading about five years of a service's holdings by weight and a line for a return and a line for a signature and Tessa's name on it in the top corner in a hand he did not know.

"Eleven years of boards and what I need is the summary line," Tessa said. "Not the boards. The summary."

"There is no summary line," said the clerk. "There is a summary line, and it is for the person who holds the service to make, and there is one of you, and the date on it is the twenty-fourth of last month and the return was due on the thirty-first, and the thirty-first is four days ago."

"That is correct."

"That is correct," the clerk agreed, cheerfully, and wrote on the bottom of it in pencil. He read the pencil back before he lifted the pen. "Answer given. Summary not produced. Given by the holder in person on the third. No substitute offered."

"You are recording that as an answer."

"I am recording what was given to me," said the clerk. "It goes back to the Exchange tonight on the evening run, and if the third of next month comes round with the same line on it, that is three of them in a column, and then it stops being a service failing to reply and starts being a service being asked properly and not answering. Those two things sit in different places upstairs."

"Say that again."

"I am not going to say it again," said the clerk, and folded the form, and put it in the bag he had brought it in, and stopped. Then, with the bag in his hands and a glance at the corridor: "Nobody has to be stupid for this to happen. It just has to happen."

He went. Tessa put the bag back on the desk where it had been and looked at it for a while.

"He was kind about it," she said. "That was the worst part. He read it to me so that I would know what it said."

For Tessa, who had refused to build a sentence out of four days and eleven years, the answer was now written on a form in a pencil in a boy's hand, and the service she had kept for eleven years had begun to look like a service that would not answer, which is a different shape of trouble and a much heavier one.

"Does the Exchange answer?" Lin Hao said.

"The Exchange does not answer anything of ours. We answer theirs." She unhooked the rack cover and looked at the board. "That is three lines in a column by the third of next month, and then somebody comes out here to look at the floor, and he will not send the girl with the sprain, because she will be off the stairs for eleven more days when he comes, and I will have made the returning by not returning."

"What is the cost."

"It is that I can keep a girl off two flights of stairs," Tessa said, "or I can keep this room looking like a room where nothing has stopped happening. I cannot do both and I have known that since this morning, and this afternoon it stopped being a thing I knew and became a thing I did."

She shut the rack cover.

"Come back at eight," she said. "Bring your arm, because I am going to re-read the weight and I would rather read it with two hands than one and a bad afternoon. And do not offer to fix the form. If a man comes up here with a pencil and an offer to write a summary line I have not got, it will be the worst week of this floor's year."

He came back at eight with his sleeve rolled and she read the weight twice and wrote nothing down, which she told him was the result and not an omission.

The runner with the sprained wrist was in the ward office at the end of the corridor, sitting on the edge of the desk with her feet off the floor and a mug in her good hand, sorting charts one-handed into three piles and refusing on the grounds that the piles were more use than the filing.

"You are supposed to be off," Tessa said.

"I am off the stairs." The girl moved a pile. "I am not off my hands."

"That is not the arrangement I made with your wrist."

"You made that arrangement with me," the girl said, "and you made it in front of him, and he is the only other person in this corridor, and I would like the arrangement to be true in front of both of us or I would like you to tell me plainly that it is not."

Tessa stood in the doorway with a chart in each hand and did not answer for about four seconds.

"I do not carry boards," she said at last. "You do not carry them. You file and you sort and you do what you can reach from the desk. And if a pile falls over you do not pick it up, you call me, and I will come, because I am the one who asked for it."

"That is the arrangement," the girl said. "Say it is the arrangement."

"It is the arrangement."

"What is her name."

Tessa looked at him. "Say it and it is on the pad and Ola will have it by tomorrow, and then she is a person in a file about you and she did not ask to be."

"Then do not tell me."

"I have not told you and I am going to keep not telling you, and you are going to keep coming up these stairs on a morning and seeing her, and that is the arrangement that works and it is the only one in this building that works." She closed the office door on the conversation. "Eat something before your shift."

The girl went back to her three piles and did not look up, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor and understood that something on this floor had been settled inside half a minute by a person who would not carry anything up a flight of stairs for a fortnight, and that it would have been settled in four minutes by anybody else, and that he had just watched the four-minute version fail.

Tessa came out into the corridor and shut the office door behind her.

"On the fourth," she said. "Same time. Bring the arm again and I will read it, and if it has not improved I will put a line in the day book about it with your name next to it, because that book is for the month my rack runs out and a wrist I sprained on my own floor is going in it whether you think it belongs there or not."

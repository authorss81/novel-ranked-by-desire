# The Sister's Stair

On the fourth of the month Ines Duarte asked him to walk with her to the stair behind the Cordwain office, and he went because she asked and because he owed her a walk without paper in it.

He felt tenderness toward her, sharp for how seldom he let himself feel it. He wanted to give her an hour with no office in it.

The stair was the one from the winter, stone with an iron rail, going down to an intake door where a queue stood two hundred long on bad days. On the twentieth of last month Ding D. Duarte had put her own name on an intake sheet for a chest, unprompted, and Ines had said her sister's name once in a queue at the Exchange.

Ines stopped three steps from the bottom. "She is not here today. I checked the sheet this morning. She is at home."

"Good."

"She asked me in April not to see anybody else about it. I told her not to see anybody else about it. She went and put her name down anyway." Ines put her hand on the rail. "That is what the stair is. A place where a person goes to be looked at because the person who loves her told her not to."

A clerk came up past them with a stack of intake sheets and nodded to Ines. Nobody asked for anything.

He waited. The pressure on the stair was the old one: a sister who had stayed away from doctors for four years and had now asked to be seen, and an auditor who could read any record in the city and could not read that one without breaking a promise.

"What do you want," he said.

"I want to stand here for ten minutes and not decide anything," she said. "Every office I work in wants me to file the line. Every room in your house wants me to hold it. My sister wants me to be her sister and not her auditor. I am tired of being asked to be one thing."

They stood. The queue below moved one place. A woman coughed and stopped.

Ines spoke again, quieter. "Ruta has your number now. Anneke has your heading. Tessa has your reading. Drant has your attendance. I have the only copy that says what it is, and it has an empty line with RECIPIENT printed over it, and it is in your coat."

"It is in my coat," he said.

"Keep it there. Do not give it to Runa. Do not give it to the Wing. If the Wing gets it, the line becomes evidence. If the Field gets it, the line becomes protection. While it is in your coat it is a question."

"For Ines, who carried records the way other people carried bags, leaving one record unfiled meant she woke at night and counted it."

"You are the only auditor in this city who cannot be made to produce a thing by asking for it politely," she said, "and I have sat across a table from two men this month who have discovered that, and neither of them did it in a room, and one of them did it by giving me a date."

"What date."

Ines took her hand off the rail.

"The thirteenth of next month." She said it without any weight, which was the way she said the heavy things. "It has come twice. Both times from the same desk, both times with a form attached and both times in the voice of a man who is doing his job. The form is a production request under a heading about records held by an agent of the district, and it is not addressed to me by name, it is addressed to the district, and my office has to answer it."

"What does it want."

"Everything I have touched since the Review that has not gone into a district file." She looked down the stair. "Which includes the line in your coat, because I have seen it and it has not gone into a district file, and it includes a good deal of ordinary work that some clerk out of a records room would be astonished to learn was being held back."

"Is it lawful."

"It is completely lawful," Ines said. "That is the part I want you to understand. There is nothing improper about it. I am an agent and I am withholding records from a lawful production, and in three weeks I either produce them or I do not."

"And if I do not, the answer I have to give is a refusal. A refusal from an agent to a production request is a thing that gets a number, and a number gets a file, and a file gets a review of whether my office should hold my ticket."

The queue below moved again. The cough had gone.

"What is the cost of producing it."

"That is the question and I have been sitting on it for eleven days." Ines turned round and looked at him properly for the first time since they had stopped.

"If I produce, the line becomes a document of the district. It gets indexed, and an indexed thing can be read by anybody with a district ticket, which is a great many people. Once it is read it stops being a question and starts being a thing that is known, and the moment it is known somebody in the Wing will ask what is on the empty line under the heading. I will have handed them the place to look."

"And if you do not."

"If I do not, I keep it, and I am a woman who refused a production request, and I will find out what that is worth in the next fortnight, and I am telling you the figure on it before it happens instead of after, because you are the only person I know who will actually do arithmetic about it."

"How much."

"Enough that I have three offers of private work this month from people who do not know why I am free," she said, "and I have not taken any of them, because a private agent who does not produce records on request is not an agent, she is a woman with a flat and a habit, and my sister goes back on the ninth."

He had no answer to that and let it stand, which she noticed.

"You are going to refuse them."

"I have written the refusal and it is in a drawer in my own rooms and it is dated the fourteenth and I will post it on the thirteenth." She said it flatly.

"Do not tell me I have not thought about it. I have done the arithmetic every night for eleven days and I am refusing, because the alternative is that a record goes into an index and comes out of a drawer and gets read by a man I will never see."

"I have watched a woman I work beside lose a ticket over exactly that," Ines said, "and she did not lose the ticket. She lost the work."

She looked back down the stair.

"You have a decision coming that costs you something you cannot put back, and everyone in your house knows it and nobody has said so to your face." She straightened up. "That is what I am not going to be tonight. I am going to be a woman on a stair with her sister on the ninth and a refusal in a drawer, and I am going to tell you about both of them and ask you for nothing at all, and that is the entire favour."

They walked back up. At the top she stopped and looked down once.

"You should not have asked me to come," he said.

"I asked you to come for ten minutes and we have had eleven," she said. "I am aware of the position I have put you in and I would have put you in it anyway, and if you have something to be angry about I would rather you had it now than on the fourth floor of a building with a window that shuts."

"What am I angry about, then, since you have asked me twice."

That stopped her for about three seconds, and then she answered it properly, which she had not expected to have to do.

"On the thirty-first," Ines said. "You filed her. You had a sheet and a clerk and an office to put a name into, and you put it in, and the reason you had a sheet at all is that the whole chain of that ran through you, and it cost her a building and it cost you eleven days of not saying a sentence out loud, and you did not tell her you were going to."

"You knew on the twenty-second."

"I knew on the twenty-second, and I did not tell you that I knew either." She said it without apology, which was worse than with one. "I wanted to see what you would do with it, and I have been standing in a queue for ten minutes working out that this is not a thing I am proud of."

For Ines, who carried records the way other people carried bags, this was the second one, the one she had not put on a list.

"That is fair," she said. "Write it down if you want. I will not stop you."

"Say it on the stair instead," he said. "You wanted to stand here and not decide anything. That is the closest anybody has come to asking me for one of those in four months."

She looked at him and something went out of her face, and she did not agree to it and she did not refuse it, and they stood at the top of the stair with the queue moving below them for another four or five minutes without deciding anything, which is what she had asked for.

"She goes back on the ninth," Ines said. "Not for a finding. For a chest. That is all I am going to say about it, and I am saying it so you know there is a date and it is not yours."

He nodded and did not ask the question he had not asked in the queue, and the restraint was still the debt.

At Lantern Row she left him at the door with a hand on his sleeve for a second longer than business needed, and the stair stayed behind them, open and unused, and the thirteenth of next month stood in the middle of the batch like a nail he had agreed to let somebody else hammer in.

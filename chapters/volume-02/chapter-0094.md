# Bellhouse Tea At Night

On the fourteenth, a Thursday, the machine on the fourth floor of the Fourth Branch printed at ten and rang its bell at half past, and the strip that came off it said eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and Drant entered it as the seventh of a standing and set it on the spike, and at four o'clock it went up the long road in the forwarding envelope to the inventory room because that was where it went now.

Then it walked home with him to Bellhouse in his coat, and Marguerite Vale had tea on the table in her good room with the glass standing beside it, and the glass had been off her own mantel since the first of the month.

Nobody said anything about the strip for the first four minutes. Ola Bright wrote the figure down without being asked and put a line under it, which was what she did with everything.

Marguerite pushed the visitors' book across the table. It was a plain book with a printed heading and ruled columns and about ninety entries in it, and every entry had an hour in the second column written with a glass by a frightened man who had held the pen the way you hold a pen you have not been taught.

"I have read it three times this week," she said, "and I want the room to hear what it says before I hear what anybody says about it, and then I am going to be unkind."

"Go on."

"Sixty-one entries since the first. Eleven are the same man, who is a courier and has a badge and gives his name and is not lying about it. Four are a woman who sells fish off a barrow and comes every second day and has never once given a name and writes carp in the column, which is the porter's decision and which I have not corrected. Nine are residents. The rest are strangers."

She turned the book a quarter turn.

"Twenty-two of the sixty-one asked about somebody. Not about the building, not about the rooms, about a person. And on nine of those twenty-two, the person they asked about is one of you."

Ola's pencil stopped.

"I know which nine," said Marguerite. "I am not going to say and I would like that noticed, because the reason I am not going to say is exactly the reason I agreed to the book on the first, and if I start reading the names out in my own dining room then the not-telling stops being a protection and starts being a thing that only survives because nobody has been cruel in a dining room yet."

Beatrix Halm had her boots off under the chair and had not touched her tea. "So somebody has been asking after us. Since the first."

"Somebody has been asking after all of you since before the first." Marguerite sat down. "Here is what I have worked out in three readings and I have not worked out any of it myself, I want that said, because I have a good clerk of my own and it is not her.

"The instruction that came round the doors on the first did not ask me to record my residents. It asked me to record every caller, every parcel and every inquiry about a resident, with the hour, and to have the book read on request by anybody from the Exchange who arrives with a card. That is a document about the pressure in this house. It was not written by a person who knew who was in it.

It was written by a person who wanted a building to become a place where the pressure could be seen."

"Which is what it is," Nadia Ferrous said.

"Which is what it is," Marguerite agreed, "and here is the cost, and I told you on the first there would be more of them than the one.

"I made it happen. I chose to make it happen, at my own table, with my own glass, and I would do it again, because the alternative was a woman with no paper coming to this door tomorrow and being turned away because there was nothing here to refuse.

And what I have got instead is a book that says which of you was asked about on which day at which hour, and it will say it again next week, and every name in this house is now in a register that did not exist five weeks ago and that a woman with no paper will never think to consult, because she does not know it is there and she never will."

For Marguerite Vale, who had had that house eleven years, the building had stopped being a house and started being a way of finding out who was in it, and she had put the glass there herself.

Sun Meiling had come up the outside stair at seven because the inside stair is the one people use and she did not want to be counted, and she had been in the room twenty minutes and had not sat down, and now she sat down.

"I have been asked," she said.

The room went quiet in a way it did not usually go quiet.

"Yesterday, at my own floor, by a man with a form, in the ordinary course, in a voice doing a job." She put her hands flat on the table, which was a thing she had learned from somebody in that house. "He asked whether Veil-Research holds any record of a consult headed with a one-digit account number, sent three years back, and whether the number was ever a standing number or a holding number. Both times he said please and both times he wrote down exactly what I said."

"What did you say."

"I said I could not recall." Sun Meiling looked at the table and not at anybody in it. "And that was the exact truth, and it was also a complete answer to the question he actually asked, and I gave it in under three seconds, and he thanked me and went away, and in about six weeks that conversation will be the reason there is a schedule on my floor with a name on it."

"He will come back," Ines's absence said from the middle of the table. Beatrix said it. "They always come back with the number."

"They came back twice this morning with two numbers," said Sun. "One of them was new. It was new this morning, and it came off a finding entered yesterday afternoon in a room on the second floor of the Reading, and I have spent three years in a house where papers arrive before their reasons and I am no longer surprised by it and I am still not used to it."

He felt the floor of the room tilt, the way it had in a Field room on the sixth of the month when a woman had begun writing a thing down that could not be taken back.

"They want the sender," she said. "That is what a provenance question is. That is all it has ever been.

Three years and four inquiries and every one of them has been somebody upstairs working out who sent a thing to a holding number, and the answer is in a consult that left this district three years ago with a responsible officer's name printed on the form, and the only person in this city who could open that consult is a woman who is not allowed to because she is not on the roll, and I have been not on the roll on purpose for three years."

She looked up then.

"And it has worked," she said. "Until the eleventh of this month. So I would like the table to register that I told seven people on the first of this month that standing between me and a list makes me the reason there is a list, and I would like it registered that I said it about protection, and I would like it registered now that I have been asked twice by my own house and the list is me."

Marguerite got up and filled the kettle.

"My terms are down in the drawer where they have been since the season before last," she said, with her back to the table, "and they are unsigned, and I am not going to sign them tonight, and I am not going to take them back. That is not an item for this room any more. Read them if you like.

They are an old woman's terms with a man in them and I have been able to ignore them for a year because they had not been tested against anything."

"Nadia," said Beatrix.

"I have been sitting here since half past seven waiting for somebody to say it and Tessa is not here and Ines is not here, so I am going to say it." Nadia Ferrous put her cup down on the saucer. "From tomorrow until the twenty-first there is no figure printed anywhere in this city.

Not one person in this city knows where you are on the seventeenth, and not one of them knows whether you have had a bad night, and I am not going to pretend to you that those are the same size of thing.

I have run a lift for four years and I have never once had a week where I could not go and stand at the counter and look at a spike and know you were alive, and on the fifteenth there is not going to be a spike."

"And so?"

"And so six of us are going to write it down." Nadia took a notebook out of her coat and put it on the table and opened it to a ruled page with a date already ruled at the top of it. "Where he is, at what hour, and who was with him. Not why. Not what he is. Where."

Nadia pushed the book across the table.

"The fifteenth to the twentieth," she said. "Six days. And then on the twenty-first we decide whether to keep it."

Silence.

Then Sun Meiling said, "That is what I meant on the first."

She had not raised her voice. She put one finger on the open page, on the ruled date, and left it there.

"That is the whole of the argument I made in this room on the first of the month and I made it in about forty seconds and then I stood on a landing in my coat and let all of you get on with it. You are all going to stand between me and a list. You are about to stand between this household and a list that says where Lin Hao is on the seventeenth. And what the standing does is it makes the household the register.

And the register is kept by women, and it is kept for him, and he does not get a copy and does not get a vote and does not get to know how many of his friends have written down that he was in a kitchen on the fifteenth."

She took her finger off the page.

"That is not me refusing the protection," she said. "That is me telling you that I was right, and that I would like somebody in this room to be frightened by it for about one day before we start finding it charming."

Ola Bright had written it. She read it back, twice, and dated it the fourteenth, and put it in the middle of the page under the figures she had already written down, and did not soften one word of it, which is what she had done on the first with the same woman's other sentence.

He wanted the notebook off that table more than he had wanted anything off a table in six weeks, and he did not reach for it, because a man who reaches for a notebook in a room with six women in it is a man who has decided what a notebook is for.

"Nobody writes anything," he said.

"Nobody writes anything," Nadia agreed. "It is still here."

"And on the fifteenth morning I will be in this room at eight and I will be here on the twenty-first at eight, and if you write it down anyway I will read it, and you will show it to me, and I will sign the first page with my name on it, and then it is mine and not yours, and you will hate that more than the writing."

Beatrix laughed once. "That is the most expensive sentence anybody has said in this house."

"He has been here six weeks," said Marguerite, setting the kettle down.

On the table there was a visitors' book with sixty-one entries in it and a glass off a mantel, a notebook open at the fifteenth with a date ruled across the head of the page and nothing under it, and a pad with a figure written down twice in two months and a sentence under it that six women had now heard twice from the same person in the same tone.

At half past eleven he walked the long road home past a Board that was showing a man six in a band he could not carry, and in the morning the machine on the fourth floor of the branch would not run, and the first page of a book that six women were going to fill in was ruled and empty and waiting for the fifteenth.

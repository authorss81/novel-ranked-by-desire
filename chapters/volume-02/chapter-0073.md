# The Plate Without Merit

The strip was in the tray with the day before’s date on it, and Drant looked at it for a while before he said anything at all.

"You put it in a day early."

"Yes."

"And I am going to enter it as received on the day before, because a clerk who corrects a man into his own calendar has taken over his own calendar, and I have watched that happen to two men in nineteen years and one of them was me." He squared it. "Attendance kept. Fourth of the standing.

And I have a thing and then I have two things, and I am going to give you the first one in the wrong order on purpose, because the right order is going to make you angry and I would rather you were angry after you have heard all of it."

"Go on."

"Thursday, strip received, figure unchanged." He put his hand flat on the sheet. "The strip you have been giving me since the third of the month is a forwarding. That is the word in your standing and it means the paper itself leaves your building, which I told you in a room on the third and you knew it better than I did.

Which means for four weeks now the actual paper of your figure has been out of the Fourth Branch, in this building, on a Thursday morning, and then on the day after each of them it has gone out of this building as well, because everything that leaves this floor goes into the thin book and the thin book says where it went."

"Where it went."

"It went to the Exchange." Drant turned the thin register round. It was the same book as the twenty-first, cloth cover, no corners, and the left column was a date and the right-hand name was printed on the form. "Not to the Reading Office. Not up to the Council. The Exchange, on the day after each one, four of them running, with a clerk's name printed under the receiving house that I have never seen in nineteen years and cannot tell you and am not going to try."

Lin Hao did not say anything.

"I did not know it was going there," Drant said. "That is the honest sentence and I would like it minuted.

I have been giving you a straight answer about the standing for four weeks and the straight answer has been straight about my own desk, and my own desk is not the whole of this building, and I found out on the fourth with a strip in it and did not have the standing to do anything and said nothing, which is the third time this month that the correct thing to do has been to say nothing and I am beginning to understand why nobody in this building sleeps."

"What is on the strip."

"Yesterday's was still on it." He said it without any weight at all. "On the reverse. Printed. One page. And I will read it to you because you have earned the wording and because I am not handing you a document you have not seen in a room."

He squared a sheet and read it in the flat clerk's voice.

"To the holding branch of the member whose plate and ruling appear overleaf. A standing which does not rise over four consecutive publications indicates an absence of input at the member's place of score. State the place of score and state the cause. A return is required weekly and not on request."

He put it down.

"That is the whole of it. It is one page and it is beautifully and perfectly within the rules and it took four years of somebody else's work to write."

"They have it wrong."

"They have it exactly right," said Drant. "That is the part I want you to sit with for about ten seconds before you say anything, because I have watched you do this five times now and every time it has been the same. They have read your figure and your figure is flat.

Flat means input and no output, and input and no output in a member is a thing that gets looked at, and they have looked at it and written down the only sentence an office can write when a figure does not move."

"And the sentence is that nothing is going in."

"The sentence is that nothing is going in." Drant put both hands flat. "It is correct about the surface. Your figure has not gone up one grain in four weeks and I have had it on a strip in my hand every Thursday. It is wrong about the cause.

I do not know what is wrong about the cause, because I have been trying for a fortnight to find a true sentence to put in a box and I have not got one, and I would have got one if you had been able to give me one, and you cannot, and that is not a complaint, that is the entire shape of your problem in one afternoon."

"Then it goes to the branch."

"It went to the branch on the sixth. To the plate office at your counter, because the form is addressed to the holding branch and your branch holds you, and the form wants a return weekly, and the return is signed at a counter." He put his thumb on the edge of the cloth.

"And I am going to tell you what a weekly return at a plate-office counter means, because you have worked six floors below one for four years and you have never once looked at what is written on the top of it."

The branch corridor was full and the plate office was a counter with a spike behind it and a book, and on the morning of the twenty-fourth the book was open and Ottilie Prebble was standing at the end of it with a form and a pen she had not uncapped.

She had been at the counter three years and four months and she was the person who read the strips into the branch book on a Thursday morning, which is the least interesting job in the building and the one that puts a name to a figure.

"I did the first one on the sixth," she said. "And I am doing it again this morning because it is weekly, and I would like you to know that I know who you are and that you clean the fourth floor and I have read your strip every Thursday since the third of the month.

I would like you also to know that I have never once been asked for anything, and this is the first time anybody in this building has handed me a piece of paper about a person I work under."

She put her hand flat on it, which Lin Hao recognised, because it was what people did to forms instead of touching them.

"And I want to say the true thing before you say anything," she said, "because I have been here three years and four months and I know what happens to a form when the man it is about walks in."

"Go on."

"The form says an absence of input at the member's place of score." She turned it round so he could read the line she had written under it.

"And the true thing is: input at the member's place of score is office aggregate, and you are an office employee on the bench rota, and the aggregate at this branch is released on a witnessed entry and it is not small and it is not nothing, and I have the figures for every week since March because I read them into the book.

So I wrote it. I wrote the number and I wrote the place and I wrote cause not at the counter and referred, which is what the box says a man writes when he cannot find the answer, and I put my initials at the bottom because I am the one who signs a return at this counter and Mr Faber is the counter and not the return."

Lin Hao read it.

He could not think of a single thing wrong with it.

"That is a true document," he said.

"It is a true document and it is going to be read as a confession." She uncapped her pen and did not write. "That is what I have worked out since the sixth and I am telling you on a Thursday morning because I have to hand it in at ten and I would rather hand it in knowing."

"Read as what."

"Read as: the member's place of score has produced no input and the counter declines to account for it." She looked up. "And when an Exchange reads that, the remedy is not to ask the member. The remedy is a place-of-score correction.

The standing gets recalculated against the aggregate that the Exchange thinks belongs to you, and the Exchange does not think the aggregate belongs to this branch, because the Exchange has never once in the history of that building been told that a washroom attendant on the bench rota produces anything at all. And the aggregate gets audited. Not you. The aggregate. Every figure this branch has put into the district line since March."

The spike behind her had nine strips on it and one of them was his.

"Mr Faber has been at that counter twenty-nine years," she said, "and he has never once been asked to account for a machine.

And on the twenty-third he was called into the front office at half past nine about the aggregate, and he came back at ten past ten and stood at the end of this counter for about a minute and a half and did not say anything to anybody, and then he went and did the fourth floor washrooms himself, which he has not done since before I came here."

She squared the form.

"And I am the name at the bottom of it. Every week. Because I am the one who signs a return at this counter, and it is not going upstairs as Mr Faber, and Mr Faber does not want it to go upstairs as Mr Faber, and he has not said so to me and I have not asked him, because I have been here three years and four months and I know which questions are for me."

[Ledger. One hundred and twelve lines. Eight turned.]

The branch was a hundred and forty people and forty-one of them were attendants and he had cleaned six floors of them every morning for four years, and none of them had ever been in a room with him.

That was the sentence he had been living inside since the Review and it had turned over this morning because a woman at a counter had put her initials under a true document and the document was going to do something to a floor she worked eleven storeys above.

"Say the piece I cannot get to," Lin Hao said.

Ottilie Prebble turned the form round again and put her thumb under her own handwriting.

"It says cause not at the counter and referred," she said. "And the true thing about that is that the cause is in this building. It is not at the counter and it is not in the district line and it is not in an aggregate and it is not in a plate.

And I have been the person at this counter who reads the strips into a book for three years and four months, and I have never once been able to write a cause for a flat figure, and I have never asked, because nobody has ever asked me a question about one."

She put the pen down on the counter.

"You are going to ask me for the cause and I am going to tell you that I do not have one, and then I am going to sign the return anyway, and it is going to go to a building four streets away that will read it as a drain."

"Then do not sign it."

"I am going to sign it." She looked at him steadily. "Because there is a version of this where I do not sign and it goes up unsigned, and an unsigned return is a return nobody answered, and an unanswered return is a lapse, and a lapse against a held band is a finding against the band, and you are three grains under a floor on a machine with a bell on it.

I know that because it is typed on a sheet on the second floor of an office you go to on Thursdays, and everybody in this building knows it, and that is a worse thing than my name at the bottom of a form."

He stood at the counter for a while.

"The figure is not flat because nothing is going in," he said.

"I know," said Ottilie Prebble. "You cannot write that either."

"No."

"Then I will write cause not at the counter and referred, and it will be true, and it will be read as a lie, and you will have to decide what to do about that on your own, because I have three years and four months and a counter and I am not going to be the instrument that carries it either."

He got the strip off the spike and put it in the tray without an envelope and went up the stairs, and on the second floor the woman with the bad finger read a plate off a stranger with the other hand and looked up at him and said his name for the first time in four years.

"Faber," he said, from the landing. "Is he in."

"Fourth floor. Since ten past ten." She said it the way she said everything, which was entirely without comment, and then she looked at his temple for slightly longer than she had ever looked at it.

"You are still grey," she said. "You should go and sit down."

He went and sat down on the bench outside the register room for twenty minutes with his hands on his knees, because there was nowhere else to sit and because it was the truth, and the grey was still there on the second day of it.

On a spike four floors below him a form with a junior clerk's initials on it was going to be read by an office in four streets as a man being drained of everything he had, which was the exact shape of the truth and the exact opposite of it.

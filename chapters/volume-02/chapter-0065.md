# The Ungiven Word

The Ferrier line came in off the arc with the wire going up and down over the joins and Beatrix Halm had his cab off the platform without being asked, which she always did, and did not say anything for four stops.

He had not been on a tram with her since the sixth week.

She drove with her left hand and her right hand in her coat pocket and she took the long way past the junction by the Reading because the long way is the one with the crowd on it, and at the crossing where the crowds come out of the Yard she said the thing.

"You are not on my list."

He had known the shape of that before she said it and he had been wrong about which way round it was.

"Since when."

"Since the thirteenth." She took the car in past a baker's cart without looking at it.

"You were on it from the seventeenth of June and you came off it in the sixth week and you did not ask me, and I have been waiting since then for you to ask me, because a man who has been removed from a list always asks by the next week, and you have not asked once in four weeks, and that has been four weeks of not knowing what to do with you."

"I did not know I was on it."

"You knew what a turned column was. You have known since June." She let a car go past and took the gap. "So here is the invoice and it is small and I am not angry about it and I want that clear, because if you think I am angry then you will do something stupid to make it better."

She put her hand over the change box.

"I gave you the count in a cab at eleven at night on the seventeenth of June and you put it on the wall next to your own name, and I did not ask for anything. I want you to hear the shape of that. I did not ask for anything.

I gave you the whole of the Ferrier arc for free, in a cab, on a shift, and I never once said what it was for, because I wanted to be wanted for it and not for the use of it."

"I know."

"You do not know what it was for, which is the correct arrangement and I have never once minded it, and you have just spent the whole of a true answer on yourself in a room in front of sixty people, and there are three people in this city who felt it go out of you and all three of them are mine." She said it level and she did not raise her voice at all.

"So the price of the thirteenth is that you are not on my list, and that is not a punishment I have invented, it is what happened to you, and I did it because if you are on a list I keep track of what you cost, and there is no version of this in which I keep track of what you cost and go on being somebody you come to at midnight."

"She will not tell you what you cost."

"No. That is why you are off it." Beatrix took the car past the Vestry end. "On the list I know what a minute costs you. Off the list I know a figure went up and I do not know who paid it, and there is no worse place in this city to be than in the middle of a crowd of people who cannot get warm and nobody upstairs can say why."

The car went quiet for about four hundred yards.

"You have lost your instrument," he said.

"I have lost my map of you and I have kept my map of the line." She rang the bell for a stop and did not stop at it. "And I will tell you the whole reason, because you have earned it by not asking, and it is not noble and it is not clever.

If you are on my column then every tick I write is about you and every tick I write is something I did to a man who was on my list. If you are off it then the column is the column and you are a passenger, and a passenger is somebody I am glad to see and somebody I could refuse."

"Would you."

"On a bad night. In a bad mood. With a tram full and somebody else's bad shoulder on me."

She looked at him for the first time. "It would be the only time in four years I have wanted to put a cold minute into a man, and I want you to know it went through me like a needle when I said it. And I am telling you because it is the honest reason, and not the one where I am a better person than I am."

He had not expected that and it sat in him the whole length of the arc.

"Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it. That is the price and it has been paid and I am not going to allow you to make it larger because it would be more comfortable for you." She took her hand out of her coat pocket. "I have been very good on my own since the thirteenth, by the way. In case you were sitting there wondering."

They came in over the viaduct and the city came up underneath them and he looked at four hundred people holding doors for each other between the Vestry end and the junction and did not say a word, and Beatrix let him have that too.

"Right," she said, coming down off the viaduct. "There is something else and I have been putting it off for two days because I did not want to hand it to a man who was on my list."

"Give it to a passenger."

"Yes." She took the car into the terminus and brought it to a stand with the brake on and did not open the doors. "Four turns last week, one a day. A woman in an ordinary coat with a notebook and no badge and a seat booked, which you do not do on this line unless you have a reason and a reason is not a badge. She rides four stops and writes in the notebook and at the Yard end she gets off and asks."

"Ask what."

"Asks the operators."

The terminus was filling up behind them with the sound it always makes and neither of them moved.

"Not the whole of it. Not our names. Our counts. How many we keep in a week, how many we had last week, and whether the number has gone up or down, and whether we can feel it when it goes." Beatrix said each of those as a separate item because she wanted him to hear four and not one.

"And she asks it as though she is doing us a service, and she is very good at it, and she says she is writing a survey and that it is for the Bureau and that there is no standing in it at all and that a survey is not an audit."

"And you answered."

"I answered every operator on this line with a figure and I made four of them different and all four of them wrong in different directions, and I got off at the Vestry and I came back on at the Yard end and I rode behind her for the rest of the week." Beatrix turned the brake off again because a car was coming.

"And on the fourth turn she got off at the Yard end and asked a man on the platform whether he knew the name of the attendant at the branch office."

"She asked for me."

"She asked whether the attendant at the branch office had ever ridden this line. And when the man said yes, on and off, at the Vestry end, for a plate, she wrote it down." Beatrix said it flatly.

"And it is you, and she got it out of a signalman in eleven seconds because I have spent four years telling eleven signalmen that the fourth-floor attendant comes and goes at that end and would they put a light on for him, and that was in June and it was the only thing I ever asked any of them for."

Lin Hao put his hand over his eyes for about a second.

"You did that in June."

"I did it in June because you were an interesting man who was carrying something and could not tell what, and I have been carrying a light for you at the Vestry end for four months and I am not going to stop, and that is the entire relationship and there is nothing in it that is not to do with doors."

"And the word."

She looked at him.

"You have been holding something since I got on," he said. "You have had a word ready since the junction and you have not said it and you have been not saying it in four places and you are going to go the whole arc without saying it. What is it."

Beatrix Halm took the car out of the terminus and down toward the arc and let the doors shut behind her.

"You do not get the word."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the whole of what I have." She drove. "There is a word. I have used it about four hundred times since June and it is a perfectly ordinary word that every operator on this line uses and it does not mean anything outside a cab, and I have never once used it about you and I am not going to start in a car, and you are not going to get it out of me by being good at asking."

"Why."

"Because the day I use it about you I will have to say what I think you are, and there are two answers and I do not have the right one, and a word is how a person commits to an answer in this city before they have got the evidence." She took the car over the crest. "So I will give you a date instead. September.

The ninth of September. I have had that date since June and it is the only appointment I have ever made with anybody on this line and I have not told one of them, and you are going to turn up on it, and I am not going to tell you what it is for."

"You said not to aim at dates."

"I said that about a different woman and a different stair and it was good advice." She let the bell. "You are going to turn up on the ninth of September because I have never once asked anybody to turn up for me and it would be a waste of a good one."

The arc came down over the yards and he could see the Yard end and the gate house from above.

The gate house was there. The tallies were on the wall of it. And a woman in an ordinary coat was standing at the gate with a notebook, asking the checker something, and the checker was answering her.

He was answering her because he was a man who had written up a four-second episode on his own record in the sixth week, and had decided he was a man who accounted for things.

Beatrix took the car down into the Yard end and slowed.

"Turn up," she said. "That is the whole of it and it is more than I have given anybody in four years."

"Why not tell me what it is for."

"Because you always get it right about the office and never once about me." She brought him to the kerb and put the brake on and looked at him with her hand on the change.

"And there is a woman in a notebook at the gate of a yard that has been counting your turned lines for a month out of a column she is no longer on, and she has got four operators and a name, and I have got a date, and you have got three grains and a weekly strip and a calendar, and every single one of us is now keeping something about you that we did not keep in June."

She opened the doors.

"September is mine," said Beatrix Halm. "I am not giving it to an Office and I am not giving it to a woman in a notebook and I am not giving it to you, and the ninth is a date and not a favour, and you will get it when I decide you have stopped asking."

She rang the bell before the platform and let the doors shut.

"I will tell you one thing about the ninth and then I am not going to tell you anything else about it, and I am telling you because you will work at it all the way to September and I would rather you worked at the right one." She took the car round the loop.

"You have been trying to work out what my date is after since the junction. You will not work it out, and you will not work it out because it is not after anything of yours.

It is a thing I have had since before the Review, and it was in my head before you could read a plate, and the only part of it that has changed in three years is that you are going to be in the car."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the whole of what you are getting. September." The doors opened and she did not look at him. "Turn up."

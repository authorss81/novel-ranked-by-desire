# Ink In The Margin

The Reading Office window opens at ten and he was on the step at ten with the buff square in his hand, because there is one office in this city that answers a question with an answer and he had spent two days finding out that it did not.

The clerk behind the window was the one who had put his reading into the machine on the 3rd.

She looked at the square through the gap and then at him, and she did not take it, and after a moment she leaned her head very slightly to one side, which from her was an enormous amount of feeling.

"That is not from us," she said.

"No."

"Then I cannot help you with it and I would like to explain why in about eleven seconds so you do not stand here for an hour." She put her hands flat on the counter on either side of the window.

"My query closed on Thursday. It closed on a finding, and a finding is an instrument, and an instrument does not sit with the office that asked the question. It sits with whoever has to act on it. Before Thursday it would have been a query on your standing and it would have been mine and I would have had to write to you. Now it is a ruling and it is upstairs and it is not mine."

"You wrote the entry."

"I wrote the entry. I do not own the number." She said it kindly and then, because she was not a kind woman, she said the rest. "You have come to the wrong window twice in three days and both times you wanted a person rather than a procedure. I am a procedure. I will tell you everything I am allowed to tell you, which is very little, and then you will go and stand in a queue somewhere else."

"Which queue."

"I do not know. I am serious. If I knew, I would tell you, because I think you are in trouble and I have never been able to leave anybody in a queue without saying so."

He stood in the window's silence for a moment. Behind her, up the counter, the machines were going through their morning, and a clerk at the far end was reading figures aloud in the flat voice that meant they were ordinary.

"What box am I in," he said.

"You are a rank-six band sitting on a figure with no interval on it. There is a shape for that in about four offices and I could not tell you which one is yours, and I have been doing this for eleven years." She turned the buff square over without touching it.

"That is not stamped. An unstamped notice is not an instrument, so nothing compels you to answer it and nothing compels me to open it. Take it upstairs. The Wing's intake corridor is on the far side of the Exchange and it does not have a queue, which should tell you something about how many people go there."

She went back to her machine. He took the square up to the Exchange and across it, forty-one minutes because the long road goes round the Reading and he did not want to go past his own tile in the square, and arrived at a building that is not on the front of any of the maps he had been walking past for four years.

The Classification Wing's public counter is brass-fronted, has three stools, a bell, and a form rack, and it is the only part of the sub-level that a citizen may stand in front of. He had been to it once, in the first week, with a reading wrapped in oilcloth and a woman called Hester Quill who told him kindly that the Office takes people for a reading for nothing they have done.

The sub-levels do not open to the public. That was true then and it was true now, and the corridor above the counter had changed in one respect only, which was that a young man in a Wing clerk's grey coat was standing at the end of it with a sheet of ruled paper in his hands, reading it the way a person reads something they did not write and cannot stop reading.

The counter clerk took the square without being asked.

"Summons," she said. "Attendance. Bring plate and ruling." She turned it over. "That came in without a box. Which is not possible, and I have not said that to a person in eleven years before."

"It came on the counter yesterday. There was a runner and no badge."

"Then it is an instruction, not a summons, and it is the office upstairs that sent her, and upstairs does not send runners." She put the square down on the brass and squared it with two fingers. "Attend on the eighteenth. Bring the plate. Bring the ruling. There is nothing on it about what happens after, because whoever wrote it did not get that far. You will find out what happens after in the room, not here."

"And which box am I in."

The counter clerk looked at him and then at the young man at the end of the corridor, who was still reading his sheet.

"Say that again," she said, "and I will write it down, and then there will be a record of a citizen asking an intake counter to identify which part of the Office holds him, and that record is a worse thing to own than the notice you are holding."

The corridor at the end of the room had a bench and a fire that had not been lit. He went and stood at the end of it and asked the young man the only question he had, which was what he was doing.

Anneke Roos looked up.

She was young enough that Lin Hao did the arithmetic on her and had to stop himself doing it, because the arithmetic would have been an age and this building did not need one from him.

"I am transcribing rulings onto the observation register," she said. "Thursdays. It is nine pages and I have been at it since the eighth."

"And this morning?"

"And this morning I am on the thirty-first line and the thirty-first line has a ruling number beside it in red ink, and the ruling is mine to copy, and there is nowhere on the sheet for the number to go." She held the sheet up so he could see it, which was either innocence or an invitation.

"Look at it. Thirty-one rows. Twenty-nine columns. Every column has a heading and every heading is about an account and an account is a thing with a figure and an interval and a state, and a figure and an interval and a state is what a ruling is not. There is no column for a number beside an unsettled entry, because for nine years no unsettled entry has ever had one."

She said it in the voice of a clerk reading out a heading that is not on the sheet, and she was entirely right, and it was the first time anybody had said it to him as a fact about paper rather than as a fact about him.

He looked at the sheet.

Thirty-one rows in a ruled hand, plate numbers and no names at all, his at the bottom and the only one with a date in the last fortnight. The red ink was to the right of his row and not inside it, sitting on the margin the way a note sits on a margin when the writer ran out of table.

"What happens to it," he said.

"That is the question I have had for two days and I have had it from a supervisor twice." She put the sheet down on the bench and squared it, which he recognised, because it was what Ines did with paper.

"A transcription goes up. I take it up. Somebody upstairs has to decide what a ruling beside an unsettled entry means, and upstairs has a rule for a figure, a rule for a band, a rule for a lapse, and a rule for a finding, and there is no rule that combines the two, so it goes in a tray, and then it goes on a desk, and then it comes back down with a mark on it, and the mark will say one of four things. It will say enter, or it will say refer, or it will say hold, or it will say query."

"And which does it usually say?"

"For a new shape, hold." She looked at him directly for the first time. "Hold is the fourth box. You have got three boxes marked and the fourth empty. Hold is what happens when nobody upstairs can say what a thing is and does not want to guess, and it is not a refusal and it is not a decision, and it does not go away. It comes back down with a date on it."

She went back to the sheet. He watched her read it again.

"Take it up yourself," she said. "The corridor on the left, second door, and ask for attendance at the desk. It is Thursday and Fridays and the mornings after a publication, and today is neither, so you will get a date and not a room."

"How long does a hold run?"

She thought about it honestly, which he had not expected.

"Until somebody upstairs can say what it is," she said, "or until the person stops turning up. Both of those end it. Nothing else does."

She was wrong about one thing, or he was, and it took him the rest of the afternoon to work out which.

He walked back round the Reading to the public register sheet on the wall outside the square — the one anybody can read, thirty-one accounts under continuing observation, plate numbers and no names, opened nine years ago by hand in pencil before the form was printed — and he stood in front of it the way he had stood in front of it eleven times in five months.

The red ink was not on the public sheet. The public sheet was printed from the register and printed sheets do not carry a clerk's pencil in a margin, and the whole time he had been looking at a printed wall and assuming it was the book.

The book was upstairs, at the desk in the corridor on the left, and the book had thirty-one rows and twenty-nine columns and a ruling number sitting in the margin of the bottom one, and the office that could not file it had sent a woman out in a grey coat with no badge to tell a man standing at a socket that he had been seen.

He got in at ten past three and asked for Anneke Roos, and the girl on the fourth-floor rota said Anneke did Thursdays and Fridays in the register room and could see somebody at the desk on the way out if it was short.

He waited by the dispatch pigeonholes. At ten past five the register room door opened and Anneke Roos came out with a coat over her arm and a folded sheet in her hand, and she saw him, and she stopped in the way a clerk stops when she has been the only person in a building who knows something for two days.

She did not say his name.

"The corridor on the left," she said quietly. "Second door. If you knock after six the runner takes your card and it goes in a tray and you wait a week for a date. Before six, a person takes it up. Before six is the whole difference between this office and the other one."

Then she said the thing she had plainly been carrying since the 8th, and she said it in a stairwell with nobody in it.

"There is a consult line on the docket that goes with this. It has been on it since the year before last. It is a research consult and it was put to Veil-Research three years back and it asked a question about a transfer memo that carried no name, and the answer came back and it is initialled S.M., and the initials are the only thing on the answer." She shifted her coat on her arm.

"I have never seen the memo. I have copied the initials eleven times. When you go upstairs — and you are going upstairs, I can tell, you have the look — ask whether S.M. still works there. Not what she said. Whether she still works there."

She went down two steps and stopped.

"I am not allowed to tell you that," she said. "I am telling you that I am not allowed to."

Then she was gone down the stair, and he stood in the fourth-floor landing of the Fourth Branch office of the Bureau of Merit with a plate in his pocket and four days to go to an attendance, holding the fact that an office which could not describe him had spent three years asking somebody else to.

S.M. still works there.

The question, now, was what a research wing had been asked about a transfer memo that carried no name, and why the only person left who could answer it was the woman whose initials had been copied eleven times onto the bottom row of a sheet she had never been allowed to read the rest of.

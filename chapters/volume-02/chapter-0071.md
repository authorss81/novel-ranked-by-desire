# The Unasked Question

The room above the chop-house had six people in it and two calendars on the table and neither calendar was mentioned for the first half hour, because the room does that, and because the woman who had put them both there had come in at half past eight and had said nothing since and had washed up instead.

Tessa Okonkwo had the cold of the Vestry in her hands and put them round the mug deliberately, one at a time, the way a person holds something they have decided to hold. Her wall was twenty-seven and it had been twenty-seven since the seventeenth, which was five days, and in eleven years she had never gone five days with nothing coming in, and she had said so once to a bucket of clean water and had not said it to anybody.

Nadia Ferrous had a tram token in her fist and was turning it over.

"There is a thing I am going to say and I would rather say it at the table than have anybody find it out in a canteen," she said. "It is not a small thing and I am not going to do the thing where I laugh about it."

The room went the way a room goes.

"Twelve days ago a woman came into my lift at the Vestry with a notebook and no badge and she asked me whether I could feel it when it went. And I said I could feel it and she wrote it down and asked me how often and I said a lot, and she asked me where and I said the Vestry, and she asked me who."

"You did not tell her who."

"I told her who." Nadia put the token down on the table and left her hand on it. "Your name, Lin Hao. In a lift, on purpose, at about eleven in the morning, and I had worked it out about four seconds before she asked, because she asked me four times about operators and never once about a clinic, and she came to the Vestry for you and not for me."

"Who was on the floor."

"Nobody. That is the whole of what I have done and I have made sure of it." Nadia took her hand off the token. "I did it because you have been standing still for three weeks and nothing is coming in and a woman with a notebook asked me a question I have wanted answered since September, and because you have spent nothing and I have watched you decide not to four times and every time you looked like a man putting a plate down in a queue."

Ola Bright, who had a page propped against the lamp and had been copying things out all evening because it is what she does, said, "There is a woman in a dark coat as well."

"There is."

"They are not the same one."

"They are not." Ola put her pencil down. "I have seen the dark coat twice, in the canteen at the Exchange and once at the end of Cutler's Row, and she has no paper at all, and the notebook woman has a booked seat and rides four turns, and I have written both of them down because that is what I am for, and I have been waiting for somebody to ask me about it instead of the other thing I do, which is make tea."

"That is not the same as asking," said Beatrix Halm from the stairs, coming down with her coat still on and her hands marked from the brake. "That is putting it on the table and waiting for the table to ask."

"Which is what I am doing now."

The door at the bottom of the stairs opened and Ines Duarte came up three steps and stopped, which is the thing she does when she has decided a room is a room and not a corridor.

"Sit down," Tessa said. "There is tea and there is a chair and there are five women who have just been told something by Nadia Ferrous and are going to be told something better by you in about a minute, and I would like you to have both before you start."

Ines put her bag down and did not sit.

"Two things and then I am going up the stairs again, because I have to be in Lantern Row by eleven and this is not my room." She said it as a fact about floors. "The first is that your sheet came back."

Lin Hao looked at her.

"It went in on the seventh. It is not returned, it is answered, which is worse, and the answer is on the fourth side under the line I printed, and somebody has typed under my word without asking me." She took her handbag off the table and did not open it. "I am an auditor. I know what it looks like when a document has been typed under a heading by a person who was not the author of the heading."

"What is under it."

"Two words. And they are not mine, and they are not a department, and they are not a name." She put her handbag down on the table with the strap over it so it could not be opened by accident. "They are a question."

"Ines."

"I am going to put the question on this table and then I am going to sit down, because I have been carrying it down a hundred and forty steps since the seventh and I am tired of it and because there is a woman at this table who copies things out for a living and will write it down, and if it is written down then it is not in a bag on a landing any more."

She sat down.

The room waited.

"Anneke Roos," said Ines Duarte, "is a junior clerk at the register desk of the Classification Wing, and she has been transcribing rulings for longer than some of you have been alive, and she is the only person in that building who talks to any of us.

In the last month she has said four things to this man that she was not supposed to say, and the last of them was that the head of that office keeps a fourth book with a page for every entry that could not go in the register."

"She said four weeks of dates," Lin Hao said. "Since the eighth. Five headings. And a ruled space under the fifth."

"And she said the head of the office has written that sixth heading twice and got it wrong twice." Ines put her two hands flat on the table. "And she said that when the head of that office gets it right, the first twenty days of your page get copied under it and nothing before that is lost."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Downstairs the chop-house finished its last orders and the man who owned it put a chair up against the door.

"So that is the first thing," Ines said, "and it is not the question."

"Give me the question, Ines."

"The question is who writes it."

She said it plainly, in the voice she used at a counter, and then she let it stand there without helping it, which is the single most expensive thing anybody in that room had ever done for him.

"That is it?" said Nadia.

"That is it, and I have watched four people in this room decide to hear something smaller. I am not asking what it should say. I am asking who owns it." Ines turned one hand over. "Anneke Roos says twice that the head of the office was wrong about the sixth heading and right about the arithmetic, which means the head of the office is a person who can do the sum and cannot do the sentence. That is not a failing of character.

It is what happens to a person who only ever sees figures. And she has been getting it wrong for two attempts and she will get it right eventually, because she is right about arithmetic, and when she does, twenty days of your plate will move under a sentence that is not yours and that you never saw and cannot take out."

"And you cannot stop it."

"I could stop it and I am not going to, and I want that said in this room before anybody accuses me of it in a month." Ines looked at him. "A heading on a page in a book in that building is a description. A form describes a thing that has already been described.

That is the entire principle on which that office functions, and it is correct, and I have spent fourteen years saying it out loud. And there is exactly one thing in this room that has never been described by anybody at all, including the six people at this table.

She cannot describe it, and I cannot describe it, and the only person in Ostara who can describe it has been refusing to do so at every desk in this city for a month with my full support and considerable professional help."

"You are asking me to write it."

"I am asking you who writes it." She did not soften it and she did not sharpen it. "That is a different question and you are a man who has been answering the smaller version of a question all month, and I have watched you do it at a counter to a woman with a rubber stamp and I have watched you do it to me twice and I have never once been able to make you stop."

He had been looking at the table. He looked up.

"I will not answer it with a speech," he said.

"Good."

"I do not know the answer yet."

"That is a sentence that has been used in this house four times since September and every time it was true and every time it was followed by somebody else saying the answer." Ines put her hands flat. "Lin Hao.

She has a ruling number in red ink in the margin of the thirty-first line of that register and it has been there since the thirteenth and there is nowhere in thirty-one rows to put it, and the printed sheet that goes on the wall is printed from the register and does not carry the ink. So a man standing in a square looking at a wall is looking at a print and does not know that a red number exists.

And the sixth heading is the place a red number goes when the register has run out of shapes."

She stopped.

"So that is the unasked question," she said, "and I have asked it out loud in a room with five women in it, and none of us has an answer and one of us has to write it, and I have watched you refuse five times this month and I am not going to be one of the five people who does it for you."

Ola Bright had her pencil up.

"Shall I write the question down?"

"Yes," said Ines. "Write the question down and not the answer, and put the date on it, and if anybody in this house ever wants to know what was asked on the twenty-second of the month they will be able to read it in Ola's hand and they will not have to ask me."

She got up and took her bag.

Then she stopped in the doorway and turned round, and for about a second she was not an auditor at all and she was a woman standing in a doorway in a room above a shop.

"There is a second thing," she said. "And it is not mine, it is a rank-two observation, and I am putting it on the table because Nadia Ferrous has already put a worse one on it. The strip goes up every Thursday in your own corridor and a second office has had four of them now.

I know there are four because I read the outgoing register for a different reason on the nineteenth and I did not tell you, and I am telling you now because Nadia Ferrous has told us a name is loose in a lift and I am not having a room where two people know different things about the same corridor."

"The second thing."

"Four strips. Four figures. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, four times." She said it without any weight at all, which was how she said the things that were heaviest. "The margin is three grains. It is printed every Thursday with a bell on the machine. And nobody has yet asked the machine a question that would change the answer, including the machine's owner."

She went down the stairs.

The room above the chop-house was quiet for about a minute. Tessa turned her mug a quarter turn and did not drink from it.

"Twenty-seven on the wall," she said. "Five days. It is the first time in eleven years and I would like it noticed by somebody, and I have noticed it myself, and that is not the same, and I am saying it out loud in a room because a thing I have only noticed myself starts to look like a thing I have imagined."

"It is noticed," said Beatrix Halm.

Beatrix had not sat down either. She was standing with her back to the stair rail in the coat she had come in, with her hands in front of her and her thumb across the heel of one palm, which was the brake and which she had been rubbing for four years and which nobody in the room was allowed to treat as a small thing.

"I am going to say the other half and then I am going to go down," she said. "She has taken me off her list since the thirteenth and she did not tell me and I did not ask, and that is my business and it is hers, and it has nothing to do with tonight.

Ines is right about the heading and Nadia is right about the lift and Tessa is right about five days, and all three of those are the same thing, which is that every single thing in this room that is being tracked is being tracked by somebody we have never met, and the man in the middle of this table is the only one of the five of us who is not allowed to write down what he thinks."

"I did not say that."

"You have said it every week since the third of the month," said Beatrix. "Nine days, then seven. And on the twenty-fourth and the thirty-first. And there is no sixth date anywhere and none of us has asked for one and all of us have been counting."

Ola's pencil stopped.

Lin Hao stood up and went to the shelf by the window where the pencils were kept and took one out, and came back, and stood at the end of the table with it, and did not sit down.

"Five headings," he said. "Attendances. Plate. Ruling. Figure. Margin. And a ruled space under the fifth."

"That is what Anneke Roos told you."

"That is what Anneke Roos told me in a corridor at ten past four on the twenty-eighth of last month and then she went down a stair because there is a runner on it who thinks she is still in there." He turned the pencil over once. "I have not written anything down about any of it since the twenty-eighth. Not the five headings, not the space, not the two words. It has been in my head for twenty-four days and I have not put a mark on it anywhere."

"And now."

He put his hand flat on the table the way he did at counters, one palm down, on the wood, and he did not look at any of them.

"Ask me again on the twenty-seventh and I will write it down," he said. "That is a date and I do not usually give dates, and I have given it because Ines Duarte is right that I have been answering the smaller version of a question for a month and because a woman who copies things out for a living has just written my question down in her own hand and dated it, and a thing that has been written down by somebody else has to be answered by somebody."

He sat down.

Tessa Okonkwo put her mug down.

"That is a date in a room," she said, "and it is five days off, and the twenty-seventh is an ordinary day with nothing written on it at all, and you have never in your life given a date for an ordinary day, and that is what has happened."

"I want it said now, in front of all of us, that you have agreed to be asked a question on a day and you will not be let off it on the grounds of a queue."

"I will not be let off it."

"Say it back in a week, then," said Nadia Ferrous, "and if you have got a new calendar out by then I want to see both of them, because I gave a stranger your name in a lift for nothing and I would like to have got something out of it."

And it was not a speech. It was a pencil, and a date, and five women, and a woman in a lift on the tenth of the month who had been asked four times about operators and never once about a clinic.

The room above the chop-house went on until eleven, and nobody mentioned either calendar, and at some point after ten Ola Bright read her own handwriting out loud to make sure of it and Beatrix Halm told her to read it again and she read it again, and it was the same, and she put it on the lamp where it stayed until morning.

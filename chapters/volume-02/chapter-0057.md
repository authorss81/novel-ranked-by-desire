# Cutler's Row After

Cutler's Row in the afternoon was the sound of a yard and the smell of hot brass and cut oil, and thirty-one doorways in a line with the numbers gone off them in June and the new numbers gone off in July, so that a man standing in front of one had to count from the end.

He counted from the end twice and got to number fourteen and Nell Ardery was on the second landing with her jacket off and her sleeves pushed up to the elbow, doing the small thing she did with her hands when she was thinking about a figure, which was turning one wrist over in the other and looking at the back of it.

"You are late for a man who walks forty-one minutes," she said.

"Something came up at the yard."

"Something always comes up at the yard." She let him in past her, which she never did, and shut the door behind him, and the room was the room of a woman who does accounts for a living in a house with a requisition on it.

"It has been in my shoulder since Thursday. It goes down the outside and into the joint and it is not the same as the eleven weeks, because the eleven weeks are in here." She touched her chest. "This is new and it is four seconds long and you have not said a word about it in five days."

"I have said a word about it to your doctor."

"You have said a word about it to the only person in this city who is paid to be interested and who told me to drink water." She kept her sleeve up. "It is the fourth of these. I have had eleven weeks of figures and a band that has not moved and now I have four of these and I am asking you for one thing and I am going to ask it in a plain sentence so you cannot pretend I was poetic about it. Make it stop."

The room was very quiet.

"Sit down," she said. "You have that face on, the one you had in June when you could not say a number. Sit down and say it in words."

He sat down.

"I am not going to."

She looked at him for a moment and then she laughed, once, without any humour in it at all, and put her sleeve down.

"That," she said, "is the second time. In July you came up here and made it go and you went grey at the temple for two days and you could not hold a bucket. I do not mind the grey. I mind that I asked you and you did it and now I am asking you again." She sat down on the end of the bed with her hands on her knees. "Say why. Do not make it noble, make it arithmetic. I am a machinist and I can do arithmetic."

"Because you have had four in five days," he said, "and a second one into a person who has already had one is a reportable episode, and three in a season gets a file, and you are at four."

"That is the least romantic arithmetic anybody has ever said to me on a landing," said Nell Ardery, "and I have been proposed to twice, and both times it was romantic and both times it was wrong."

She got up and made tea, because that is what you do on a landing, and she gave him the cup and kept her own and did not drink it.

"Go on," she said. "You have a face on that is not only about me."

Number twenty-two was Garrick Neve's, on the same side, three doors further down, and he was letting himself in with a key when Lin Hao came up the row, and he turned round and stood in his own doorway and looked down the row at the man walking toward him with an expression of enormous and entirely professional dislike.

"Attendant," he said.

"Mr Neve."

"Do not do that. I have read you out loud to a queue of forty people every Thursday for two months and I have put your answer in front of the Exchange twice and I have watched the machine print a band off a ruling." He came out onto the landing and shut the door behind him.

"You are also the reason I live at twenty-two and not on the Kiln side, and I have had three weeks to work out how to say that sentence without it sounding like a speech, and I am going to say it badly. You are the reason this row is a requisitioned row, and I am a man who reads plates for a living, and I have worked out precisely what happened to me on Thursday, and I would like you to tell me whether I have it right."

"Tell me what you have."

Neve did not go inside. He stood on the landing in his Exchange coat with his badge still on and his hands loose at his sides.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," he said. "Three grains under a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. A band of six printed off a ruling made in a room. And a figure that has not gone up by one grain in ten days while every plate in that corridor that has anything to do with the Yard aggregate went up four hundred." He looked at him.

"In the Review room the machine read your figure against the band line instead of against the floor. And that is when it happened. Not the ruling. The reading. Something came out of you in that room and it went into three people and I have been the rail for nine years and I know exactly how long four seconds is."

"Yes," Lin Hao said.

"Yes." Neve nodded once. "Thank you. That is all I wanted and I am aware of what it costs you to say it in a doorway on a Thursday. Now. I have to decide something and I would rather decide it with you than about you. Do I write it up?"

"That is your file and not mine."

"It is my file and I am asking." He came down half a step. "Here is what it comes down to. I have four in five days like Nell Ardery, and I have been at the rail nine years, and in nine years I have been the man who did not put a man down in the middle of a Thursday, and I am not going to start."

"But the other half is that the moment I do not write it up, there is a record somewhere with one fewer episode on it than there was, and the record is the only thing that has ever protected anybody in this city." He shook his head slowly.

"So I am going to write it up. Unexplained. Chest, four seconds, Thursday, in a room, with nine other people present who all had the same thing. And then something is on the record, and whoever reads it can add up the number of us, and there are three of us and I have been counting the three since that afternoon."

"That is not a safe thing to publish."

"No. It is the opposite of a safe thing to publish." He said it without heat. "It is the only thing I have that is not a rumour, and you have taught me in the last ten minutes that I would rather publish a fact than have a frightened man publish it first."

The yard gate was at the end of the row past the passage, and the passage behind the last six dwellings was the way he had gone on the nineteenth of the season with his forearm in to the wrist, and it was nine hundredweight of landing over eight feet of a back stair that came down in four seconds, and a hoist wire ran across it at chest height carrying barrows of cut brass from the shop.

The checker was under the gate house with a docket under his arm and a badge he had to lift his chin to see over, and he had been nodding Lin Hao through every morning for a month and had said nothing beyond the nod, and he put the docket down when he saw him and did not pick it up.

"You have come down here to ask me something," he said.

"I have."

"Ask."

"What you told the doctor. Have you written it up."

The checker looked at the gate, and at the wire, and at the row.

"I have not," he said, "because the woman in the clinic said not to, and because I have never had anything wrong with me that anybody could write down, and because I am not — Lin Hao, I have a badge and a docket and I am not going to be a man who brings a chart to a counter for a thing that is in his head." He shifted the docket to his other arm.

"That is why I asked her whether I was to report it. Because it is not in my head, it is on me somewhere and I have to carry it round."

"You should write it up," Lin Hao said. "Today. Unexplained, four seconds, in a room, with other people in it. Do not put my name anywhere near it and do not put the room number on it if you can help it, and I know that is asking you to carry something and I am asking you anyway."

The checker looked at him.

"You are the first person who has ever told me to write something down that would go on my own record," he said, "and the second person who has told me not to. The doctor was kind about it. You have not been kind about it at all."

"That is true."

"I am going to do it." He picked the docket up. "And I will tell you the thing you have not thought of, because you think about everything and you have not thought of this. If three of us write the same four seconds up in the same week, then it is not three episodes. It is a pattern. And a pattern gets an address, and I have been at this yard for six years and I know what the Office does with an address." He tipped his head at the row.

"It is that building. It is you. It is where you stand. So write mine up and I will put it on my own record, and put theirs up as well if you have to, and God help everybody on this row when it lands."

Halvard Rees was on the wall at number eleven with his back to the brick, which was where he sat every evening in every month he had lived on that row, because he was a wire-splicer and that was the warm side of the courtyard.

He had not turned. Forty-one climbs at the Ferrier arc had gone through him in four years and not one of them had come off him, and Lin Hao had used him twice as a control and had never been able to say thank you for it.

"Six," Halvard said, before he had got down off the wall.

"Yes."

"I read it. Somebody at the yard read it out on the yard road at six this morning in front of about forty of us." He stretched his bad shoulder.

"Band off a ruling. Plate three under the floor. So we are all looking at a man who is six and under his own rank in the same breath, and about a third of this yard has worked out that this is what he has been doing for four years, and the other two-thirds are still on the question I would be asking, which is where the figure comes from, and they will get to that in a fortnight and then somebody will say the word supply out loud."

"Then they will be wrong."

"Then they will be wrong, and a thing that is wrong and simple is worse than a thing that is right and complicated, because a man will build the wrong one out of whatever is to hand." Halvard looked up the row at the three doorways. "You have been up there today. Nell's light was on at four."

"Yes."

"And you came down here to find out whether it was still on her."

"Yes."

"You spent nothing," said Halvard Rees. "I have been sitting on this wall since ten past five and I have watched the door of that gate house, and you went in it and you came out of it and you have not spent one grain on anything on this row. I saw you not do it. I am not going to thank you for it, and I am not going to tell you it was the right decision either, because it was not my row and it was not my three people, and the cost of it is sitting on those three people this evening and not on you."

"I know what it costs."

"You know the number. Everybody in this courtyard knows the number." Halvard got down off the wall. "Get off my row and go home. And Lin Hao — I have told you once and I am telling you again and I would rather say it than not say it. Do not spend one of the eight on anybody on this row tonight. Whoever comes up that passage behind those last six doors, do not put a hand on them, and do not go into that yard at all after dark if you can help it."

The wire across the yard passage went up tight at about half past six and sang.

He heard it before he understood it, from the shop end, and it was the sound of a loaded barrow swinging on two feet of wire with a man holding the rope, and the man at the far end of the passage was Halvard Rees's neighbour and the barrow was coming down the line of the last six doorways at the height of a man's chest.

He did not think about it. He went into the space the way he had gone into a space on the nineteenth of the season with his forearm in to the wrist, and he took the weight of it on his forearm and turned his whole body so that it went over him and not through him, and the barrow went down on the cobbles a foot from the wall and the wire came off its bracket with a bang that brought two people out of doors.

His arm from the elbow to the wrist took the whole of it and went dead for about four seconds, and then it came back with the grey at the left temple arriving about a second and a half behind it, and he stood in the passage with his back against the brick and his arm hanging and did the arithmetic of what had just happened to him, which was that he had spent nothing and had hurt himself for nothing, in a yard he had no reason to be standing in, on behalf of a man he had already decided not to help.

"Do not do that," said Halvard Rees, from the yard mouth.

"There was a man in the line."

"There was a man in the line and the line is where it is every evening at six and it has been for six years and it was there on the night they came for the row." Halvard's voice was completely level. "You have been running at things in passages since the nineteenth. In September. That is the second time. And in September the passage took your arm and now it has had it again and you have got a week out of that at best."

"I know."

"Then go out of it and stop being the man who goes into the passage."

The gate house light was on and the checker was still under it with the docket on his knee and a pencil in his hand, and he had done about two thirds of a chart when Lin Hao came past on his way to the road.

"Wait a second," he said.

Lin Hao stopped.

The checker went through the docket the way a man goes through a document he has carried all day without knowing what is in it, front, back, front again, and then he stopped on something near the back and turned it round and held it up so that Lin Hao could see it in the gate house light.

It was a buff slip the size of a call-off chit, folded once, and the folding was soft at the crease the way paper goes soft when somebody has opened and shut it many times over several weeks.

On it, in a printed line and a space for a name: CLASSIFICATION WING — ATTENDANCE — UNSETTLED ENTRY, REFERENCE ATTACHED.

There was a name written on it in pencil. It had been written badly, and then rubbed out, and then written again.

It was not the checker's name.

"It was under the gate tallies," the checker said. "Slipped in with the week's tallies two days ago. I did not look at it because it looked like a gate chit and it was under the gate tallies and it is still under the gate tallies because I have not had a reason to move anything and the gate tallies do not get tidied in the middle of a week." He turned it back over. "Lin Hao. Who is that."

"It is me."

"Then you have been up here twice and this has been in a gate chit pocket under my own tally book for two days and I have not thought about it once, and I do not write gate tallies." He held it out. "Take it. Take it and read it, because I am a man who stands in a gate house and everybody in this city thinks a gate house is where nothing happens, and that is exactly what somebody has been counting on."

Lin Hao took it. The paper was warm from being under a tally book and it was not signed and it was not stamped and it had no box marked on it at all.

"There is no date," he said.

"There is no date," said the checker, "and it does not say what attendance, and it says unsettled entry, reference attached, and the reference is a sheet I have never seen in my life. Somebody has been standing about six feet from me twice a week for a month putting paper in my own chit pocket." He put his pencil down on the chart.

"You told me to write mine up. I am going to write mine up. But Lin Hao — if that paper is under my tallies and it has your name on it, then they are not coming to me for me at all, are they."

"No," Lin Hao said. "They came to you to be near me, and you are the only man on this row who had no reason to refuse to be near me, and they were right about that, and it took you two days to open it because you have never once in six years had any reason to open anything."

The checker nodded slowly. Then he picked up his pencil, and looked at the half-finished chart, and squared it on his knee.

"I will finish this tonight," he said, "and I will put it in the box at eight, and then I am going to write out my own attendance for this week and every week I have been on this gate, on the back of it, because a man who has been standing where somebody stood is a man who should have to account for it. And you can tell your office that when they come."

He looked up the row at the three doorways and at the passage and at the wire still swinging a little in the dark over six doorways that had all belonged to a row that was requisitioned in June because a man had put his hand on a chest in a doorway.

"You have refused to spend anybody since you got that notice," the checker said. "Is that why."

"Because spending a person who has already had one gets them a file," Lin Hao said, "and because you are right that it is not protecting you. It is only keeping me out of the room. Every one of you is still standing exactly where you stood on Thursday afternoon, and there is nothing on any of you that says why, and the only reason there is nothing is that I am the only person who knows, and the only reason they cannot get it out of me is that I have not given it up yet."

He put the buff slip in his coat beside the two dates.

"And they have started at the gate," he said, "which means they have stopped trying to get at me and gone to the yard instead, and a yard is easy, because in a yard a man says yes to the small thing four times a day."

It was not dark yet when he got out to the Cordwain road, and the yard was behind him and the row was above him and his forearm had gone from the elbow to the wrist in a stripe that was not going to be anything by morning and was going to be a bruise by the end of the week.

Nineteen days. He had twenty-three days until the second attendance and he had been in the building every day for four years, twice a day, twice a week at minimum, five days out of seven, and every one of those days had been a day of doors held for strangers and trays carried and a figure that moved where he stood.

There was no shift he could stand outside of. There was no day of the week where a man who cleaned six floors of washrooms was not standing in a corridor somewhere in the largest building in the district while the entire unmeasured half of that city's goodness poured gently and invisibly into his chest.

And the Office had found this out in nine days, and had not come for him, and had not come for the figure, and had sent a woman in a dark coat to a supply supervisor on the third floor and slipped a buff slip into a gate house chit pocket under a tally book that nobody tidies in the middle of a week.

He had been thinking of this as a room he had to walk into.

It was not a room. It was every room he stood in, and it had no door he could refuse at, and the only man in Ostara who could describe the whole of it was standing at a gate with a pencil and a half-written chart and had already decided to finish it.

# The Refusal At The Desk

The second attendance had a different room in it.

The first one had been a desk at the end of a corridor with a bench outside it. This was up two flights, on the second sub-level, with a door that closed and a table with a cloth on it and three chairs, and the corridor outside it had a runner in it who took his name and wrote it down before he went in.

Silas Drant had the ruling and the plate and the buff square from the first attendance laid out in three piles, squared to the edges of the table, and he had a minute sheet in front of his chair and a second chair against the wall with the chair back to the wall, which is the chair you give a man whose file is the reason for the room.

"Sit," he said. "There is a Field officer coming and she is late, and I am not going to start without her, because whatever you say in this room in the next twenty minutes is going to be read by somebody who was not in it."

He had said the last part in exactly the voice of a man who has learned it.

Lin Hao put the plate on the table and the Reading Office closure beside it, which was a slip with the query's number and the words CLOSED ON INSTRUMENT and the clerk's stamp and the date, and it was the third thing he had brought and the only one of the three that was an answer.

"The query is closed," he said. "On a witnessed answer, which is an instrument. The band is on the ruling. The ruling is signed by nine. Those are the three pieces of paper and I have brought all three and I would like this to end today."

"It will end today," Drant said. "It will not end the way you want it to and I have never yet seen it end the way a man in that chair wants it to, and I am going to be straight with you, because you were straight with me, and because the alternative is that you spend the next twenty minutes finding out that I was not."

He put his hand flat on the fourth pile.

"What is this?"

"That is the answer to your question and it arrived five days ago from a colleague of mine and I have not read it because I am not permitted to read it, and I am permitted to bring it into a room, and I am not permitted to know who assembled it." Drant turned it round and pushed it across the cloth. "I am told there is a wall map on it. That is the whole of what I am told."

It was a wall map of the district, printed, with a rectangle of newsprint pinned flat over the square where the Review had been held, and on the newsprint there were thirty-one small pencilled marks in a band, and thirty of them were in a line along the Ferrier arc, and the thirty-first was a single mark in the middle of the Reading square, under the room.

Three dots had been drawn around that one mark in a ring, in a second colour, close together, like three flies on a window.

"Ruta Vesely, analyst, observation support," said Drant. "She has had your plate for eight days and your band and your band line and the Review date. She has had no ledger. She has had nothing that is not on this table." He said the last four words the way a man reads a caution off a sign.

"And she has found three episodes reported in one week in three buildings three streets apart, and the man they were reported by has a badge and a place of work four hundred metres from the middle of Ostara, and she has found that all three of those people were in one room with you on one afternoon, and she has put a ring round a point on a printed map because that is what an analyst does when she has three."

Lin Hao looked at it.

Ines had said it in a room above a chandler's on Lantern Row, a fortnight ago, in a sentence she had put down and picked up and put down again: an analyst could put a dot on a wall map and the dot would be that room. She had said it while holding a list of nine of his fourteen and not knowing a single one of the faces.

She had said it to him and he had heard it as a warning about other people finding it out.

It was on the table, in a ring, in a second colour of pencil, done by a woman he had never met, off his plate and his band and the date of a room.

"What happens when that goes up," he said.

"It goes up to the desk of the head of this office," said Drant, "and she will want to know whether three episodes in one room are three episodes or one, and the answer is one, and if she is satisfied that it is one then the three of those people are three witnesses to one event and the room is the place the event happened in, and a room is a thing that can be attended."

A knock. The runner opened it.

Runa Sato came in with her hands behind her back and closed the door herself.

She was not in Field dress, which Lin Hao noticed, and he noticed it because he had spent five months learning to read what she wore on which days. She had come as an officer to a file.

"Attendant," she said.

"Field operative. You have been named on a certificate at the door. You will be present for the attendance and you will not be asked to give evidence unless you are asked." Drant had a sheet in front of the third chair and did not put it down.

"You have read that you are here as the holder of an observation file on this subject, and that the file closed on a finding in the sixth week, and that the declaration at the front of your file carries the number of that ruling."

"It does."

"Is it true that you hold it."

"It is true that I hold it. I closed it and I did not close it, the finding closed it, and there is a difference and I have written it down twice."

Drant nodded and wrote that down, and asked her to sign it, and she signed it standing up.

Then he put his pen down and looked at the two people in the room.

"Before anything else is written," he said, "I am going to tell you both what this attendance is, because the last one did not tell you and that was a defect in the room and not in either of you. This is an inventory of nature." He said it without any weight at all.

"Not of conduct. Not of your standing. There is a shape on a table and the shape has been wrong for nine years, and I do not know what it is and neither does anybody above me, and my job is to write down everything anybody tells me so that in four years somebody with a better head than mine can look at it and say what it was. I am the man who writes it down. That is the whole of what I do and I have never had an opinion about it."

He picked the pen up again.

"Now. On the eighteenth the attendee declined to supply names behind observation entries. That refusal is minuted and it has been read. I have been instructed to put the request a second time, and I am putting it a second time, and I want the request and the answer in one minute that nobody can pick apart."

He turned the sheet so the whole room could see it, and there was the request written out in full, and there was a space under it for an answer.

"Names behind the observation entries," said Drant. "In person. In a room. With a Field officer present, which is a formality and I am telling you it is a formality, because she has no standing to compel you and neither have I and the request is not an instrument and you could stand up and walk out of this room the same as last time."

Lin Hao looked at the sheet.

Then he said: "Write what I am going to say, and read it back, because last time you read it back and that is the only reason I am sitting in this chair."

"Write," said Drant, and wrote.

Lin Hao spoke slowly, because he had done this once before in a corridor in July, with Runa's notepad on the table and Tobias Renn's own career in the frame, and he had learned that a true sentence said in a room is worth more than an accurate one said at a counter.

"Write: the attendee declines to supply names, and declines for a reason that is not the reason on the first minute, and the reason is this." He waited until Drant's hand had stopped.

"Write: the faces are not instruments. A name is not a figure and it is not an interval and it does not advance anything, and an office that wants a name is not measuring me, it is describing me, and I will not be described by an instrument. Write that one exactly like that, because in four years somebody with a better head than yours is going to read it and they are going to want to know which of the two of us was holding the instrument."

Drant wrote it and read it back and it came out exactly.

"Write the second thing." Lin Hao put his hand flat on the cloth.

"On the eighteenth I said the entries were not mine to surrender and that is still true and it is not the whole of it. The whole of it is that three people on that map are on that map because of something I did in a room, and the cost of it is mine and the evidence for it is mine and neither of those two things belongs to this office. I am not going to hand you a list and call it a closing. If you want it closed then it closes with me still holding it or it does not close."

He stopped.

"Third thing, and this one is mine and nobody else's and nobody in this room is to write it as an instruction." He looked at Runa Sato for a second and then back at the sheet.

"There is a line in a ledger that reads myself. One. It came there on the Review day and it is permanent and it cannot be taken out by any of us, including me, and it is the reason your machine made that noise in a room with me in it. It is unfiled. It is not on this table, it has not been offered, and it will not be offered today or on any day this office asks me a question. Write it as unfiled. Write that it is the one line that exists and that the office has it by my mouth and not by any hand I have given it to."

The room was very quiet. Runa Sato had not moved from the door, and Lin Hao understood, watching her, that she had come into that room knowing she was going to stand in it and be asked a question she could not answer, and had come anyway.

"Write the last thing and then put the pen down and do not pick it up again." He looked at the wall map, at the ring in the second colour of pencil, at the three flies on the newsprint over the Reading square.

"Write that there is a point on Ruta Vesely's map and that the point is right, and that the three of those people are my cost and I will answer for them to whoever asks, and that the room is a room and it is a room in a building that publishes attendance, and that this office does not need to put a dot on a map of this city because it has already got a building that prints one."

Drant wrote for a long time.

He read all of it back in the flat clerk's voice, and then he looked up at the third chair.

"Field officer. You have heard this. Is there anything in it you must correct."

Runa Sato came away from the door.

"There is one thing and it is mine and not his." She did not raise her voice and did not come further into the room.

"The observation closed. I hold the file. The declaration at the front of my file was filed by the man in that chair on his own hand and his own signature, and it says his name under a stair and a forced door and a boy who was not settled, and the finding number sits above it because the finding closed the file. I cannot answer any part of that inventory for him, because answering it would mean filing something about the account, and the account is not in my file and never was, and I will file nothing further on it unless the division orders me to in writing."

She put her hand on the back of the empty chair and not on the table.

"I am here because a certificate named me and I came because the certificate named my file. If it is asked of me whether the man in that chair is an account, my answer is that a Field file is not evidence of anything and I will not give it to you."

Drant wrote that down and asked her to sign it, and she signed it, and the sheet went into the pile with the ruling and the closure and the buff square.

Then he stamped the minute.

"There," he said. "Two attendances kept. Two refusals minuted, both in a room, both with the answer read back, and one of them with a Field officer in it who has signed her own paragraph." He put the stamp down. "You have now been to this desk twice and given this office four sentences that nobody can use and that anybody in this building can read."

He squared the piles and then stopped, with his hands on them.

"You asked me at the first attendance what happens next and I said that somebody above me would have to decide. I have to tell you that is now true in a different way from the one you were expecting, and I am telling you because it is a fact about a room and you are standing in it and I would like you to have it while you are standing in it."

He took a sheet out of a different drawer — a thin one, from an office, unnumbered.

"Upstairs is going to ask whether there is a description of you that does not require the ledger. And there is not, and everybody involved knows there is not, and everybody involved has known there is not since about the eighth." He put the thin sheet flat, face up, on the cloth, and did not turn it round.

"So there are two available answers to that question. One is to describe you without the ledger, which cannot be done, or to describe you using something else. And there is one other instrument this office has and I have never had cause to hand it to anybody before today."

Lin Hao did not look at it.

"I am not going to turn that round for you and I am not going to read it, because it is not in front of you as far as I am concerned and I would rather you were able to say afterwards that it was never in front of you." Drant put his hand flat on it the way he had put his hand flat on the card at the first attendance.

"I will tell you one thing about it because I think you are owed one thing. It is dated. It has been dated for some time. It is prepared, unsigned, and it will not be taken off this desk by me, and the person who will take it off this desk is not me, and that person will be a person who has to sign their own name to it and put a date against their own name."

Then he took the thin sheet back, put it in the drawer, and shut the drawer.

"That is not a threat and it is not a favour," he said. "I am a clerk at a desk and I have told you the state of the drawer. Anything you hear about it from anybody else will be worse than that and probably false."

Runa Sato signed out at the door and said nothing to him in the corridor, which was the correct thing to do in a corridor with a runner in it, and he heard her boots go away up the stairs at a steady pace.

Drant came out of the room behind him with the attendance sheet in his hand.

"Second attendance kept," he said. "And the schedule is lengthened, which is the third thing you already know about and I am telling you anyway because the alternative is that you find it in a tray in nine days and I would rather you heard it standing up."

"How long."

"Every nine days at present, from the second." He held the sheet so they could both see it. "Three months."

Lin Hao looked at the line of dates running down the page, and after the second of them there was nothing, because Drant had not written them yet.

"You have not written them."

"I have not written them, and I am going to write them now, in front of you, with the first three in, because a man who has been told there is a line of dates coming should be able to see the line and count it and know how much of it there is." He took a pen and wrote three lines down the page and then stopped with the nib on the paper.

"Every nine days. Three of them written. After the third it renews by the desk's own hand, and every renewal is a signature, and in a year there are forty of them, and every one of them is a man who came to a desk and said no."

He capped the pen.

"Now I am going to tell you the thing that everybody in this building is going to tell you over the next nine days, and I am telling you first, because it is the only privilege a clerk has." He put the sheet down on the ledge by the stair.

"It is not a form. The man upstairs who wanted one has been told by her that there will not be one, and she is right, because a form describes a thing that has already been described."

"The Office does not need a form for you. It needs you to go on not being described, for long enough, and that is not a punishment and there is nothing on your plate that will say so. The only instrument this Office has against you is that it comes back every nine days and you keep coming, and you keep saying no, and every time you come it is written down."

He looked up the stair.

"You are going to be here a long time, and the reason you are going to be here a long time is the calendar, and no clerk in this building can put an end to it for you, and I have watched a thing like this run five years in another district and I am telling you as a man who writes it down what I have learned about it."

"What have you learned."

"That in five years the only thing that ever moved it was the account itself going quiet." Drant said it simply and without relish and he picked the sheet back up.

"A mass that stops moving for long enough stops being a thing that is still happening and becomes a thing that is finished, and a finished thing reads on an instrument, and an instrument is the one thing in this building that ends an inquiry instead of renewing it. That is not advice. It is the only sentence in this building that anybody has ever said to you that is true about the mechanics of it, and I am not going to say whether it is a door or a trap, because I genuinely do not know and I have never met anybody who did."

He went back up the stairs with the minute in the tray under his arm and a list of nine-day intervals to write down.

Lin Hao stood in a corridor two floors above a public counter where a woman had once told him kindly that nobody was taken for a reading for anything they had done.

Every nine days. Three of them written.

The mass did not have to be classified for the Office to keep him. It only had to go on being what it was, for as long as a man was willing to come and say no, and the calendar did the rest, and the calendar was free, and the calendar had a signature on it that was not his.

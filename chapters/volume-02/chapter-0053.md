# The Thirty-First Line

Lantern Row at seven in the evening, and Ines Duarte had a chair pulled out from the wall and a folder she had not opened, which told him before he had his coat off that she had decided the folder was not part of the evening.

"Sit down," she said. "You have that square in your coat and you have had it since yesterday and you came here before you went to the office. Say what it says and then tell me what you want, because you do not come to Lantern Row to be reassured, you come here to be told the price of something."

He put the buff square on the table.

She read it in about four seconds, which is what an auditor is for, and then she read it a second time much more slowly, which is what she did when she was frightened.

"Unstamped," she said. "Which means it is not an instrument, which means you cannot be compelled by it, which also means nothing compels the office to be bound by what it wrote on it. That is worse, not better. An instrument binds both sides. A piece of paper binds one man and can be changed by anybody upstairs between one morning and the next." She put it down and squared it, which she did to everything.

"And there is no fourth box. The runner told you that or you worked it out. Somebody wrote an instruction they could not finish."

"Then I go."

"You go. That is not the question." She sat down opposite him and folded her hands, and for a moment she looked exactly like the woman who had said there is a form for a man and nothing for a floor in a corridor in June, and he understood that she had been thinking about this for two days without him and had come to the end of a road he was only now starting down.

"The question is what it will do to you when you are in the room, and I cannot tell you, because I have never been in that room and neither has anybody I have asked in eleven years."

"You could ask."

"I did ask. I asked at the Exchange yesterday afternoon, in the ordinary way, as an auditor of rank two wanting to know the standing of a man she —" she stopped, and restarted, because she had been about to say something that was a lie by omission and both of them were too old for that.

"As an auditor of rank two wanting to know the standing of a man of rank six held on a ruling. I got as far as the corridor clerk, who told me that there is no procedure to ask about because there is no category, and that a procedure about a man with no category cannot be given to a woman standing outside it."

"So there is no procedure."

"There is no procedure about you. That is not the same as there being no procedure in the room." She leaned forward.

"Listen to me properly for a minute, because you have come here to be told the price and the price is this and it is the worst one I have to give you. Every office in this city closes a file by describing the person in it. If they can describe you, the file closes and you are a described thing forever. If they cannot describe you, they cannot close it — and an open file is not a safe place. It is a place people keep coming to. It is a place where somebody sits down with a new question every eight days and a man who has to keep answering it."

"How long."

"Until it can be described, or until you stop going." She said it flatly. "Those are the two endings and I have looked at both of them for two days and I do not know which one you are walking into and that is the first time in this business I have been unable to price a file."

He thought about the third possibility for a while and did not say it, because the third possibility was that he stopped going and the file stayed open and he kept ninety-one lines and a plate with a figure on it, and a man who stops answering an office keeps every door he has and also keeps the only thing he is afraid of, which is that one day the office stops writing and starts sending.

"There is one thing I need to ask you," he said, "and it is the only thing I have come to ask, and I am asking it as a man, not as a file."

"Ask."

"What does an audit do with a line it has never had to read."

She did not answer for a while. Outside, a tram went past on the Vestry side and the light of it came under the door and went away.

"I do not know," she said. "That is not modesty. There are about a hundred entries on that register and I have audited the reading behind nine of the eleven a year, and every one of the hundred is a figure with no interval, and I could not tell you, if you sat here tonight and asked me to put my hand on yours and tell you whether there is a line in that mass that says you, whether I would find it or make it up. That is the honest state of it." She stopped.

"I have been sitting with that for three days and I keep coming back to it because it is the only thing in this that frightens me and I am the person who should not be frightened of a line."

"You have decided about filing it."

"I have not decided. I have not filed it. Those are the two facts and neither of them is a decision and I am telling you both so you cannot tell yourself later that I chose." She unfolded her hands.

"But I will tell you what I have done, because you will find it out anyway and it is better that you hear it from me. I have drawn a sheet. On the sheet is the line, and beside the line is the arithmetic that puts it there — ninety-one lines and eight turned and one of the eight is a man spending on himself in a room with sixty people in it — and beside that is the only question I have ever wanted answered, which is what the Office does with a relief that had no relief in it."

"You are building a file."

"I am building the smallest possible piece of paper in the city, and I have not shown it to anybody, and I am not going to show it to anybody until I know whether the answer to my question is the same as the answer to theirs." She squared the buff square again.

"And I am aware that a woman building a private file about a man she sleeps with is the oldest possible shape and that I have thought about that for three days, and I am doing it anyway, and I would like it recorded that I knew."

The lamp on the table had a shade that was too low and it put the two of them half in the dark, which was how they had been sitting since the first week of June.

He put his hand out flat on the table, palm up, the way he had done on the Review floor, and waited.

She looked at it for a long moment and then put hers into it, and the cold went out of him into her hand and down into the four months she had been holding a sister's stair, and she shuddered once, all the way, and closed her eyes and said a word he did not catch and then opened her eyes and looked at him.

"Eighteen," she said. "And Tessa has it at nineteen before nightfall."

The Vestry clinic was twenty minutes' walk and the corridor outside the ward was the warmest place in the building. Tessa Okonkwo had a rack of boards against one wall and a lamp on a cord and a table, and the boards went up in a column from the floor in order, each one clipped, each one with a date and a name and a line of observations in a small square hand.

Tessa squared the boards as he came in. Eighteen. She said the number before he could ask for it, and he said the other one, because he had been in the room at the end of the Review and he had stood in the doorway and watched her put three charts up on a rack that had been seventeen on the week before.

"Eighteen," she said. "And a man standing in a doorway who has come to say a number that is not the one on the wall."

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen before nightfall yesterday, which is where you are getting that from, and I have not added to it since." She moved the lamp on its cord so the light came across the boards.

"Two came up from the ward this afternoon with charts that do not match what they present as, which is the ordinary version and the ordinary version fills a rack on its own. And there are three on this board that came up together, in one room, in one quarter of an hour, all three of them the same shape."

She turned the board round.

Three sheets clipped side by side.

Nell Ardery, machinist, fourteen Cutler's Row, second landing. Garrick Neve, Exchange public counter, Delta badge, nine years at the rail. And a third with a yard gate written on it in place of an address, because the checker had never given anybody one and Tessa had not insisted.

"Eleven weeks of figures and a band that has not moved," Tessa said. "Nine years at the rail and a spelling test every Thursday of his life. And a man who asked me whether he should report it." She looked at him over the top of the three sheets.

"I told him no, and I am telling you why I told him no, and you are not going to like it. Because I do not know what a report is yet. In eleven years of doing this I have never once seen what came back."

She took the three charts off the wall and laid them flat on the table, and put the lamp beside them, and the three of them lay there being ordinary, three people with three unaccountable minutes in the middle of unaccountable weeks.

"That is the thing about a chart," Tessa said. "It does not say anything happened. It says the body does not match its own history, and a doctor is supposed to put that in a file and a file is supposed to go somewhere. These three go nowhere. Not because anybody has refused them — because there is nowhere on the route they are on." She straightened up.

"I have been the person who decides what goes nowhere for eleven years and I have got very good at it, and what I have just done is put three of my own decisions on the wall where I can see them, and you may have the consequence of that."

"Which is."

"They are not mine now. They were a morning, and they are a fortnight." She squared the charts again. "And the reason I put them up is that tomorrow morning somebody with a desk is going to come and look at whatever comes out of that square, and I would rather they found me already holding them than found somebody else holding them and not telling me."

He looked at the three sheets for a while. Three people who had been in a room with him for four seconds on a Thursday afternoon and had walked out of it carrying something they would spend the rest of the week putting down.

"Is there a number," he said, "for what it costs to hold three like that overnight."

"No. That is what I have just told you and you have asked anyway, which means you already know the answer and you wanted it said in a room." She pulled the boards back onto the wall and clipped them in order. "The answer is the rack. It was eighteen this morning and it was nineteen before nightfall yesterday, and by the end of next month it will be thirty, and there are four walls in this room and I have been counting them for two years. I do not have a fourth wall. I have a store."

"Tessa—"

"You may say the number to me once a month," she said. "On a day you would not have come here anyway. The number and not the names, because the names are on the board and I do not need a man to read them back to me."

Then she looked at the lamp and not at him.

"There was a woman at the Clinic," she said. "Years ago. Before you. She was not a patient, she came to the desk with a slip and asked whether we had ever seen a man who came in with shaking hands at a figure and went out shaking, and I said no, and she wrote down the date and the figure and the word no, and left."

Lin Hao stopped breathing for a second, in the way a body does when it is waiting for a number.

"She came back twice more and there was no man and no figure the second or third time." Tessa turned the lamp cord out of the way of the boards. "She was research, not clinic. She gave her name as Sun and she was not from this building and she did not have the look of somebody who came to clinics."

"What research."

"Veil something. Veil-Research. She wrote it on the back of the slip and she did not ask me to keep it, which I have thought about since, because people do not give up the name of their own house for free." Tessa finally looked at him. "What?"

"Nothing yet," he said. "Give me the date on the slip."

"Second of May." She said it without looking it up. "Two thousand six hundred and forty grains, and a man walking out of my door shaking, four months before you ever aimed anything at anybody, and I have never told you that figure because you have never asked me for it, and I am telling you now because you have just come in here with an office on you and it is going to keep on coming in, and I would rather you got it from me than found it."

The cold store was eight feet by eleven and two degrees and one lamp on a cord and a couch nobody used and a table. He had been in it three times. It was the only room in the building where nothing belonged to anybody else and she had said so, the first time, as a condition.

"Lin Hao." He turned round. "When you go on the eighteenth — and I know you are going, because you went down to the Reading Office window first and you asked me what a Wing intake does to a person on the way here, which is not a question a man asks if he has decided to send paper — when you go in that room, you will be asked what you are. Not what your figure is made of. Not what instrument you hold. What you are."

"And I will not know."

"You will know by the third question." She squared the last board and hung it. "They will start with the plate because the plate is the part they can see, and then they will try the ruling because the ruling is the part they wrote, and then they will go looking for the part in between, which is the part that is yours. And when you go looking for a description of that, you will reach for the only true sentence you have got, and every true sentence you have got goes through a person."

She said it and then she put her hand flat on the last board she had clipped, which was not one of the three.

"And I am saying this as your doctor and not as anything else," Tessa said, "and I am saying it once. What happens in that room will not happen to your body. It will happen to the twenty or so people in this district who have a cold minute in them that they cannot explain, and it will happen to me, because I am the person you bring it to. There is a version of going in there where you come out the same and there is not one, and I cannot tell you which you will get, and I have never in eleven years had to tell a patient that."

"Then what do you do with a body that is already too well?" Lin Hao said, before he had decided to ask it, and Tessa Okonkwo took her hand off the board.

She looked at him for a long moment across a rack of eighteen clipboards in a room two degrees.

"That," she said, "is the question I have been waiting eleven years to be asked by somebody who is also in it. Come back on the twenty-fifth. I will show you the drawer."

He walked back up the Vestry stairs and across to the arcade and down the arc to the branch with four days on a notice and a name from a woman who had handed him a slip she had kept for three years in a room where nothing belonged to anybody else.

He was going in person.

Not on the understanding that it would go better in the room.

On the understanding that everything that had happened to him for six months had happened at a distance, to a piece of paper on somebody's desk, while he stood in a corridor and did not come — the four-page statement, the declaration, the eight names on the wall, the box at nine, the query, the finding, all of it conducted through a hatch by a man who cleaned washrooms and who had never once had to stand in a room with the person he was describing.

The runner had told him a missed attendance marks a lapse and a lapse on a held band is a finding against the band.

So he would be there on the eighteenth, in the room, on his own feet, and he would answer every question that had an answer, and he would let them get to the part in between.

And he would not give them the ledger.

That was the decision and he made it there on the Vestry stairs at a quarter to eleven at night with his coat over his arm, and it was not a decision he had been asked for by anybody, and it was the first one in eleven weeks that he had made entirely on his own and entirely for the worst possible reason, which is that he could not stand the arithmetic of ninety-one names belonging to people who had not given him any of it.

The copy slip in his coat was four thousand one hundred and twelve folios and one open, and the second line under the receipt said Held Account One, and there was no Held Account Zero, and somewhere in a building across the city a clerk with initials in a margin had been copying a name onto the bottom of a page for eleven years without being allowed to read the top of it.

He would go on the eighteenth. He would take the plate and the ruling and nothing else at all.

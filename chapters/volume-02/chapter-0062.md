# The Shape In Data

The seventh week of a standing that never went up was the shape in the data, and the shape belonged to Garrick Neve before it belonged to anybody else, because Garrick Neve read forty plates a day at a public counter and wrote down every one of them.

He had said it in a doorway on Cutler's Row ten days after the Review: a figure that has not gone up by one grain since the third of the season, while every plate in that corridor with anything to do with the yard aggregate went up four hundred.

Nobody had done anything with it since, because a man on a landing is not a ledger.

On the third of the month, in a room two floors under the Exchange with a cloth on the table and four chairs and one chair against the wall, Silas Drant did something with it.

He had a card. It was the same size as the card with eighty-one rows on it, and he turned it round so the man in the chair could read it, and there were two columns on it and both were ruled by hand.

"Fifty-one figures down the left," he said. "Fifty-one plates read at this desk since the start of the season, in the order they came in, with the interval between each one written in the margin. That is your column." He put his thumbnail under the first entry. "And that is mine."

The left column went 8,140. 8,139. 8,138. 8,138. 8,138. 8,138.

The right column went all over the place.

"That is every fourth plate at the same counter over the same period," Drant said, "a mix, and I have thrown out anybody whose band moved and anybody who changed employment, because a band that moves tells you about a band and I want a figure. So it is ordinary people in ordinary work. Watch the right one."

It went up two hundred, down ninety, up four hundred, up eleven, up thirty, up six hundred, down four hundred.

And the left one sat at the bottom of the page with three grains of daylight under it and had not gone up by one grain since the third of the season.

"That is not a machine error," Drant said, "and it is not a fault in your plate, and I have had the plate office check the socket twice because I would rather it were a fault in my building than what it actually is."

"What is it actually is."

"It is a standing that does not feed." He put his hand flat on the card, which was a thing he did instead of saying something with his face. "Every figure on that right-hand column is a person doing work inside a building every day and being paid for it at the end of the week.

Yours is a person doing work inside a building every day and not being paid for it at all, and I have been in this building nineteen years and the only other time I have seen a column behave like yours is on a card in a drawer in this office, and I am not going to tell you what is on that card."

"Then what are you telling me."

"That your figure is evidence," said Drant. "That is what I am telling you and I have thought about it for two days and I am sorry about the two days."

He drew the minute sheet round and squared it, and uncapped the pen, and did not write.

"You have been measured in public in this building three times since the Review and every one of those three times a machine printed that your band is on a ruling, and the three grains under the floor, and the number of the ruling. And the number of the ruling is the only reason that the plate office can tell anybody anything about you at all. I want you to understand what that means before I put the next part on the table, because you are about to choose."

"Go on."

"The Office has two instruments and no description. The first is a calendar and you have it. The second is the register, and the register holds addresses, and an address is not you." He put the pen down.

"There is a third thing and it is not an instrument and it is not on any sheet in this building, and I am offering it to you because I have been told to offer it and because I have read the paper it comes on and I have a view about it which I am going to keep to myself for about four more minutes."

"Say the paper."

"The plate office in your branch prints your standing every morning and prints the band line and the floor and the margin and the ruling. Nine strips on the spike.

A machine in this building that cannot get the third thing out of a plate can get every other thing out of a strip, and a strip is paper, and paper joins to a register line by a number in eleven seconds." Drant looked at him. "Once a week.

From the branch socket, on a Thursday, on your shift, in front of your own corridor, the strip comes off the machine and it goes up to this office. Not a copy. The strip."

"The corridor reads it out."

"The corridor reads whatever it reads out. That is not mine and I have no authority over your branch and I have not asked for any."

Lin Hao looked at the card for a while.

The left-hand column was the shape and the shape was flat, and flat was the thing. A figure that will not go up since the start of the season in a city where every figure in the room goes up is not a man who is unlucky and not a man who has been wronged, because both of those show in the next column after the next.

It is a man whose deeds are landing somewhere the plate cannot see, and the proof of that is that nothing is coming back, and the proof of the proof is that it is printed weekly on a strip in his own corridor with a bell on the machine.

He was going to hand them the evidence. Once a week, in writing, in a form they had designed for him and stamped with their own dull little seal, for the whole of the rest of however long the calendar ran.

"You understand what this is," he said.

"I am going to read it back to you and you are going to tell me I have it right, and then you are going to say yes or no, and either answer is correct and neither of them is free."

He read it back.

"Attendee agrees that the branch plate-office strip, taken weekly and forwarded, is accepted in place of a week record of attendances, persons spoken with, and addresses. The three ruled lines are discharged for any week in which a strip is received. The interval of seven days is not shortened further for the term of this standing. Both undertakings run from the third and neither has an end date written on it."

"The addresses."

"Are discharged," said Drant. "All three. Days attended, persons spoken with, addresses. All three, and I want that in the minute in those words, because I have watched two men sign this and one of them thought he had only given up two of them."

Drant wrote it, and read it back, and did not sign it.

"Now the part I have to say before you say anything, because you will not find it out from me later and you will find it out from a machine." He turned the minute sheet round so the man in the chair could see the rule printed at the top of it, in the Wing's own small type. "A held band carries a tolerance of three grains.

You are at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Four down and the band is gone and you are a five and you may reapply."

"It drifts down."

"It drifts down where you stand and it has drifted down three grains since the start of the season, a grain at a time, on the days it moves, and nobody in this building has ever yet worked out why it moves down when you are in a corridor full of strangers." Drant capped the pen. "From today it is printed. Every Thursday.

On a machine with a bell, on a strip, in a corridor with a queue in it, and if it goes down a grain in a week your margin is two, and the week after that it is one, and the week after that the band is on the Review floor and not on your plate."

"How long have I got."

"That depends on you and it does not depend on me and I would rather you worked that out than took it from a clerk." He put his hand flat on the minute. "I have watched five years of one of these in another district and I have never once watched it end because the man stopped wanting it. It ends because somebody signs something in a different building."

The room was very quiet, and the machine on the desk made the small sound a machine makes when it has nothing to give.

"A strip a week," Lin Hao said, "is the whole of what I am giving them."

"A strip a week is the only thing you can give them that they cannot manufacture for themselves, and that is why it is on the table." Drant pushed the minute sheet round. "It is a figure, and it is the only figure they have got, and it is a flat one, and a flat one is the sentence you would have written yourself if anybody had asked you to describe yourself to an office."

He read the minute back one more time.

"I am not going to argue with you," he said. "I have wanted to argue with you for four days and I am not going to, because a clerk who argues is a clerk who wants a different answer than the one that is true, and this answer is the one that is true.

You will hand them the only thing you have and it will cost you a grain a month on a plate with three grains on it, and there is no version of this week where the strip is harmless, because a strip is a figure and a figure is a thing they can keep."

Lin Hao signed it.

He signed it with the nib Drant gave him because he had not brought a pen and did not think about it, and then, about four seconds later, he did think about it, because it was the first thing he had given that office in a month and he had come in with a plate and a ruling and a counterfoil and two refusals and two stamps and had taken every one of them away again.

The minute went into the tray on the left, which was the same tray the refusals went into, because there was one tray and it was for everything, and he understood that when he saw it go in.

"There," Drant said. "First weekly strip, Thursday, from this week, and I want it in the tray by six on Thursday evening or I am going to have to send somebody to a branch office to stand in a corridor, and I am not going to do that and neither is anybody else."

"I will put it in the tray."

"You will bring it in yourself, on your own morning, and you will not put it in an envelope, because an envelope in this building is a thing that gets weighed." He stamped the attendance once. "First attendance of the standing. Kept. And the three ruled lines are discharged for this week and for every week after it until the standing is ended by somebody with a different opinion."

Lin Hao took the plate and went up the stairs.

Outside the Exchange it was still early enough to be cold, and the square was full, and the Board was nine streets away with a black six on it.

He stood on the kerb with the plate in a cloth bag and worked out, standing still on a pavement like any man waiting for a tram, what he had done at half past nine that morning. He had volunteered, in a room, the only proof that existed that nobody in the city could find where his merit was going. He had done it in exchange for two days in a fortnight.

Two days in a fortnight was the cheapest thing on the table, and he had taken it because he was tired.

It was a good trade. He could see that it was a good trade, which was the difficulty.

Two days a fortnight is four days a month and Tessa's figure on the wall was twenty-two on the twenty-fifth of the month before, and a good trade in Volume Two is a thing that looks like arithmetic and turns out to be a person.

On the desk behind him, in a room with a cloth on the table, Silas Drant entered the minute in the register of outgoing paper with the time and the date and the standing, and put it flat under the tray, and then stood for a moment with his hand on the tray.

Alma Quayle read a strip a week for four weeks, and then a month, and then longer, and it never once went up, and by the end of it there was nothing left in the data that was not flat.

And she wrote the sixth heading herself, in her own hand, on the twentieth, and it was two words long.

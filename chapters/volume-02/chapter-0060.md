# The Standing Inquiry

The queue at the Exchange went out of the doors and along the rail and turned at the corner, which it had not done since the Review.

Lin Hao stood in the marked square at nine with the rail on three sides of him and the notice screwed to the wall above his head saying what it always said, and Garrick Neve put his reading card into the machine and read it out.

"Standing, eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight."

The queue listened the way the queue listens.

"Floor of the band, eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Three grains down. Three is the whole of the margin and he has all three." Neve turned the card so the rail could see it, which he did every Thursday and had done since the twenty-second of the month before, and then he looked up.

"Band six, held. Not provisional. Struck on a requisition of property of the nineteenth, and on a Review ruling of the thirteenth, in a room, with nine chairs. That is the reading. I have not been asked a different question and I am not going to ask one."

He wrote the figure on the board and drew the line under it, which was the standing order, and then he took his thumb off the card and stood there.

Then he did the thing that had not been done before, and it took him four full seconds to get to it.

"I am going to say a thing that is not the question," Garrick Neve said, "and it is going to be the last thing said at this counter for four seconds, and I want the queue to hear it as a fact and not as an opinion."

Nobody moved.

"I was in the room on the thirteenth. I was one of the three." He did not raise his voice and did not look away from the rail.

"On the Thursday afternoon the machine read the figure off a ruling, and there were nine chairs and sixty people, and there were four people in that room who felt something they could not account for, and I was one of them, and I have spent a fortnight deciding what to do about it, and I have written it up myself, unexplained, with a date and a length and no reason, because that is what the record is for."

"You did not have to do that," said somebody at the back.

"No," said Neve. "And I would like the queue to understand that it was a decision and that it cost me, and I am making it in public because a decision made quietly in a corridor is not a decision. It is a piece of administration." He squared the reading card.

"The counter's question this week is what instrument seats your band, and the answer is on the board behind me, and you have all heard it twice already. Anything else is a different counter and I do not run it."

He put the card in the slot and the machine took it and the queue moved forward four feet and the thing was over, and Lin Hao stood in the square and said nothing at all for eleven seconds, which was eleven seconds longer than he had ever stood in it without answering.

He had come here expecting to be asked.

The woman who had come to listen was forty feet back in the queue, in a grey coat, with a notebook open on the rail and a man beside her who had not come to listen to anything.

Runa Sato had her hands behind her back. Silas Drant had a satchel and no badge, and he stood at the rail the way a man stands who has been sent to observe and has been told, correctly, that observing is the whole of his job.

Drant had not interrupted the discharge. He had not come forward. He had stood at the back of a queue in an ordinary coat for the whole of it, with his notebook shut, and that was the entire crime, and it was the first thing anybody in the Office had done for him that was not a question.

Neve read the next reading at nine minutes past nine and Lin Hao came out of the square.

He came out into an Exchange that had rearranged itself around him without doing it on purpose.

Garrick Neve at the counter, working, saying the reading. A Delta badge two places down who had been a queue on the Review day and was a queue now and had been in a room.

A woman at the rail who had stepped forward twice and gone back twice and had been looked at by forty people and had let herself be looked at, which was the single hardest thing he had ever watched anybody do. A porter holding a door for a man with a plate in a cloth bag and finding no way to make it look like nothing.

Nobody said his name.

Nobody said anything else either, and it took him about four minutes to understand that this was not discretion. It was that the Exchange on a Thursday morning is the loudest room in the district and that a queue of two hundred people who have all read a finding on a wall will talk about the weather in front of a man as readily as about him, and that the most extraordinary thing about being publicly visible is that it produces no conversation at all.

A month ago the branch corridor had carried a wall of ninety-one names and a column of figures and he had put five of them back by accident and five back on purpose, and a month ago there had been a reason for every person in that building to look at that wall and work out what it meant.

The wall had been empty for ten days.

The Board in the Reading Square was nine streets away with his name on a black tile, and every Thursday for the last six weeks he had walked past it on the long road to this counter and had not looked at it.

This morning he had come round by the Exchange doors instead, which was the first time in six weeks, and he had come through the square. The tile was there, black six on white, four printed figures under the name, and the fifth one still the largest number in Ostara, and a woman and a child were standing in front of it with their heads back because it was the big one.

Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. The number he had been carrying for four years on a machine that could not tell where it came from.

"He looked at it," said a man behind him, to another man, in the voice of a man reporting something he has not entirely understood. "That is the man that number is. He is the one that looks at it every Thursday."

"Leave him," said the other man.

They moved on. He stood there for a moment with the plate in the cloth bag and then went to the counter because there was a queue.

At twenty past ten the query clerk came out from behind the rail with a docket in her hand.

She was the woman who had put his reading into the machine in the third week of the season, and who had closed the query on a finding in the voice of nine chairs and sixty witnesses, and who had told him a fortnight ago that a finding does not sit with the office that asked the question.

She was going along the rail with the week's docket going to the sub-clerk, and it was the way she handed it across the gap between two counters that stopped him.

She took it in her left hand under the docket. Then she took it with her right as well, and her thumb went down the outside edge and pressed a corner flat that had started to lift, and she held it for a moment longer than the hand-over needed, with her thumb on a page she did not open and had no reason to touch.

Her eyes came up. They went across him once.

She did not stop. She went on down the rail with the week's docket held flat at a corner that nobody in that building had asked her to hold flat, and she handed it on, and the sub-clerk wrote the date in the book, and the week was done.

Lin Hao stood at the rail for some time.

"The box."

Drant had come up the side of him without hurrying, and he had his notebook out and a rubber stamp in his other hand that was not the classification stamp and was not any stamp at all that carried a category.

"A discharge is a discharge," he said, "and this office keeps attendances, and I am here to observe and I have observed, and the observation is that a rank-six band was discharged in public at a public counter with a hundred and eighty people behind him and every one of those people heard the instrument and the margin." He wrote for about eleven seconds.

"That is an attendance kept. I am logging it, and it counts, and I am telling you it counts because you have paid twice for it and I would like you to have one of them free."

"You are not obliged to."

"I am not, and you are not going to persuade me to stop, and I am a clerk at a desk and I do not have authority to stop." He shut the notebook. "There is also one piece of administration and I am going to do it in front of you and then I am going to go back down two flights and stand behind my own counter until somebody needs me."

He put the date sheet on the rail on a dry patch between two puddles and uncapped the pen.

"First attendance kept, the eighteenth. Second attendance kept, the twenty-fourth, with four refusals minuted and one Field paragraph signed." He wrote. "Third attendance: the third of next month. Fourth: the twelfth. Fifth: the twenty-first." He signed the sheet, capped the pen, and turned it round so the dates were facing up.

"The renewal after the fifth is mine to sign and it goes on without you having to do anything at all, which is the design of it, and the design is not mine, and I told you in that room that the calendar is the instrument and I am telling you again now that it has five dates on it and you have not yet been to three of them."

He put the sheet away and put the stamp that was not a stamp away.

"You do not have to come on the third," he said. "You have to come on the twenty-first. Everything between the two of those is paper."

At eleven Ines Duarte was in the queue with her folder shut.

She came and stood four feet away, which was further than she had stood in six weeks and exactly the distance an auditor may stand in a public queue, and neither of them remarked on it.

"The corridor said you came in by the square," she said.

"Yes."

"That is new." She looked at the board with the band line on it, and at the figure under it, and at the margin. "Three grains."

"Three grains."

"That is a figure I can read from here and you cannot read from there," she said, "and I have been sitting with a sheet of four sides of paper on my own table for eleven days and I have not shown it to anybody, and I am telling you where it is at half past ten in the morning in a queue of two hundred people, and if that is cowardice then it is cowardice with a date on it."

"It is not cowardice."

"No. It is arithmetic." She was quiet for a moment. "Ding put her own name on a clinic intake sheet two days ago. For a chest. She has been on my rota for four years and I have never once put her in front of a doctor, because I said something to her in April about not seeing anybody else about her, and she took it at the time to mean that, and she has gone on taking it to mean that for four months, and she has gone to a clinic on her own and signed her own name in her own hand."

Lin Hao went completely still.

"You said her name."

"I have said her name to two thousand auditors and to one classification clerk and I have never said it in a queue." Ines looked at him, and her face was doing the thing it did when she had decided something and was not going to undo it.

"She is alive, Lin Hao. She has two hundred and sixty grains and she carries a bag up nine steps every morning, and she is not in your ledger because I never let her near you and I have been wrong about that for four years."

She stopped.

And Lin Hao did the thing he had wanted to do for two minutes.

He did not ask a single question.

That was the entire discipline. He stood in a queue at the Exchange at eleven in the morning with a line in his ledger that had never had a deed on it and a name that had just been put into his head by the one person in Ostara who could not un-say it, and he stood there and did not ask her a single question, because every true sentence available to him went through a person and this one had come to him for free and the price of the asking would have been the whole of it.

"Ding," she said once, and that was all she said.

Then she told him that the Reading Office had asked her, formally, four days ago, for the source of the line she had not filed, and that she had sent back a one-line answer which was a question, and that this was not going to be sustainable and she had known that on the sixteenth when she wrote it.

"What did you send."

"I sent: the source of that line is the only source it could have."

"And they asked you again."

"They will ask me again on the seventh." She put her folder under her arm. "I did not tell you so that you could do something. I told you because it is half yours now and because you have just done the hardest thing I have ever watched anybody do in a queue, and nobody has ever watched me not ask a question before."

She went back into the queue and stood four feet away again, and the queue took about forty minutes to move.

[Ledger. One hundred and three lines. Eight turned.]

He walked home by the long road at half past one with the cloth bag in his hand and three grains on a card and two dates in a coat pocket and a name in his head, and the figure moved wherever he walked, all the way, because a hundred and eighty people in a queue and two hundred in a square hold doors for each other about nine thousand times a morning and there was not one of those doors that anybody was ever going to write down.

In a room two floors under the Exchange, a man with a rubber stamp wrote three dates on a sheet in a column, and signed under them, and did not think about them again.

On the twenty-first of next month, in this building, somebody would come and stamp a piece of paper and say his name, and on the twenty-fifth, in a district of two million people, three hundred women he had never met would walk into rooms and be better than their own charts had any right to expect, and an instrument would read a line on a map and a woman who had been counting a mass for six weeks would have already been counting it for four days, quietly, on her own, without a single signature.

Four days.

Sun Meiling had kept a sentence back for eleven days and would not give it to him until he had stood still long enough to earn it, and standing still was the thing that was doing it, and he had four days' worth of margin and a woman with four inches of stippling in her own forearm and a rack of eighteen boards in a room with a lamp on a cord.

He had the week. He had the band, held, on an instrument, with a black six on a white tile that nine streets away a woman and a child were standing in front of this very afternoon.

He had held it for one more week without spending a grain on anybody, without surrendering a name, and without being described by a single instrument in the entire city.

It had cost him a calendar, and the calendar now had five dates on it, and four of them belonged to a clerk in a sub-level who had told him, correctly and without malice, that the only thing standing between him and a room with no window was the thing he had spent himself to buy in a square in the sixth week: the willingness to come back and say no.

# The Held Band

Second floor, twenty past six, and the copy slip had been out of his coat twice and back in twice, because there was nowhere in a washroom to put a document that was the Bureau's business and his own brother's handwriting at one and the same time.

He cleaned the last row of basins while the building filled around him. The soap wanted cutting. The lino had dried overnight to a pale grey at its edges and wanted water and then wanted the water gone again.

On the corridor wall the branch board stood empty except for the date of the Review at the top in the clerk's hand, and under it somebody had put a second line in different ink in the night, at an hour when the office was shut, by a person who had come in early to look at a wall that used to carry ninety-one names.

The second line said: band line, Review candidate, held — plate to be read this morning at the office socket.

He read it twice and mopped around it.

By half past seven the second floor was full and nothing was said, which is its own kind of sentence. A porter he had walked past four years of mornings held a door for him and met his eye doing it.

A clerk from the sub-levels said good morning out loud in a corridor where good morning had never once been said. Tobias Renn came down with a box on his shoulder, saw him on the stair, stopped with the box balanced on his thigh, and then went on up without delivering whatever he had come down to deliver.

At the plate office the socket had the queue backed out into the corridor, which was ordinary, and then had it backed out further into the stairwell, which was not, and the door propped with a wedge, and Faber standing beside the machine instead of behind it.

Faber had been at that counter twenty-nine years and had never once stood beside his own machine.

"Read it yourself," he said. "I have read it four times this morning and I would like it read once by a person who is not me."

Lin Hao put the plate in.

The machine did the ordinary thing for four seconds and then stopped being ordinary. It printed the standing on the first line, which was correct, and the band on the second line with its floor beneath it, which was correct, and then it printed a third line it had never printed for anybody in that office in twenty-nine years.

Faber read it out loud, because the corridor had thirty people in it and the machine had a bell.

"Standing, eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," he said. "Band six, floor eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Carried on —" he stopped, and looked at the paper again as though it might have changed. "Carried on a Review ruling. Number one hundred and twelve."

Not one person in the corridor said a word.

"That is what it says," Faber said. "The band is not on the figure. The figure is a three-grain miss under its own floor and the band is sitting on a decision nine chairs made in a room."

A porter at the back of the queue said, "So he is six."

"He is six," Faber said. "He is also three grains under the floor of a five, and both of those are printed on the same slip, and from this morning anybody with a plate can walk up to that socket and read the same two sentences I have just read to you. That is not a fault in the machine. That machine does what it was told. It is the most honest thing in this building."

Somebody laughed, once, and did not enjoy it.

The queue moved. He stood where he was for a moment longer than the queue needed, with the printed strip still in his hand, and read the third line four times, because he had seen the number one hundred and twelve before. He had seen it in red ink.

On the Review day, while nine chairs and sixty witnesses watched, a clerk worked with both hands at a side desk and marked his line on a sheet — the thirty-first line on a sheet she had been marking all morning — with the number of the ruling beside it in the margin, in red, because the ruling and the entry were two different things and the sheet would have to be read again by somebody in another office.

She had done it in front of him. He had been in the room.

The relief he had walked in with came apart in the corridor and something else took its place, and it took its place without asking. Until that morning the gap between eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and twenty thousand had been a fact four people knew and could do arithmetic on. It had been, in the most literal sense available to him, a private humiliation.

It was on a printed strip now, with a bell on the machine that read it out.

He went up and cleaned the second floor properly, because his hands needed the work, and then he walked the long road down to the Exchange with his coat buttoned against a wind that had come up in the night, forty-one minutes instead of eleven, because a man on the Ferrier line at seven in the morning sits among a hundred and forty other people and the figure moves wherever he stands, and he was not yet ready to know what it did when a hundred and forty strangers decided to stand closer to him than they needed to.

They did decide that. He noticed it at the junction by the Reading and again at the crossing where the trams come in, and the third time he looked up and found a woman in a laundered coat holding her place in the queue two feet nearer to him than the queue required, and looking at the side of his face with an expression of frank and unembarrassed curiosity.

They had all read the fifth figure. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, his exact grains, in the largest type in Ostara, on a white tile, with a black six on it that no living person could explain.

[Ledger. Ninety-seven lines. Eight turned.]

The sixth of the ninety-seven was a woman at the crossing with a child's hand in hers, and the seventh was a man on the platform who had moved along the rail to stand at his shoulder and had no reason to be there at all.

He let them come. That was the arithmetic now. A man who spends on people keeps needing people who have fed him, and a man whose name is in the largest type in the city cannot choose which of them he stands near, and the account does not distinguish between the ones he chose and the ones that arrived.

The branch knew by noon. He did not go back to the reading pavement to be looked at, which was cowardice, and he named it as cowardice on the walk, which was the most he could do with it.

At the plate office at twenty past one the runner came in.

She was not in Bureau dress. That was the first thing he noticed, and it turned out later to matter more than anything else she did. A grey wool coat, a sealed square in her hand, no badge, and the flat unhurried walk of somebody who carries paper for an office that does not put its staff on the street.

She went past the queue rather than through it. She put the square on the counter beside the socket, squared to the edge, and stepped back two paces and stood there.

Faber did not touch it.

"Classification Wing," she said. "Intake. For Lin Hao, attendant, second floor, personally."

"The Wing doesn't send a runner," said a voice from the queue, and it was a man with a Delta badge who had been there an hour and had opinions.

"No," the runner agreed. "It doesn't."

The square was buff and unsealed on the outside, which meant the notice itself was not an instrument, which meant it did not need to be. Inside, four lines, one of them a date, one of them a time, one of them a line that asked for two things to be brought and one of them was a signature space with nothing written in it.

Attend. Bring the plate. Bring the ruling.

Faber read it over her shoulder because he could not help it, and then he read it again with his finger under the date, and then he looked at the runner and said the thing that told Lin Hao, standing six feet away with wet sleeves, that this was not going to be a morning.

"What is this for?"

"Attendance," said the runner. "I carry them. I don't open them."

Four boxes on the buff square. Three of them had a mark beside them and the fourth was empty, and it was not empty because it had been forgotten. It was empty because the office that had written it had had a fourth thing in mind and had found, when it came to writing it down, that the fourth thing could not be written without giving him something.

She stayed until he had read it twice, which she did by watching him read it, and then she said, "Two days' notice is the notice for an attendance. You have got it. If you are not there on the day, the sheet marks a lapse, and a lapse on a held band is a finding against the band and not against the standing, and I am telling you that because the woman at the counter will tell you the other thing and the other thing is wrong."

Then she took the buff square back off the counter, folded it once, put it inside her coat, and went out into the street, and the queue at the plate office stood and watched a man in grey wool leave without a name and without an office on the outside of it.

Faber put his hand flat on the counter where it had been.

"That was not an instrument," he said. "There was no seal on it. She told you that and she told you the date and she told you what happens if you are not there, and that is three things a person with paper over her heart does not usually say to a man she is looking at."

"She did not say what box I am in."

"She did not say what box you are in." Faber took his hand off the counter. "Twenty-nine years at that counter, and I will tell you the one thing I know about this office. It does not send anybody anywhere for anything. It files things. It has never once needed to hunt a person, because every person it ever wanted has walked in under their own name through a public door and signed for it, and if you do not walk in it will send the paper after you at your employer's address and there is a clerk at the Fourth Branch whose entire job is to receive employer post."

That was Tobias Renn, and both of them knew it was Tobias Renn, and neither of them said the name.

Lin Hao folded the notice into his coat beside the copy slip and the four counterfoils and the finding, and went back up to the second floor to finish the row, because the row was not finished, and because there were twenty-nine basins on it and every one of them asked nothing of him at all.

The plate was still in the socket. He had not taken it out. He went and took it out and put it in his pocket, and as he did it the machine gave the small mechanical clunk it gave when a plate settled into the reader, and the strip from the morning was still on the counter where he had left it, with the third line printed on it.

Carried on a Review ruling. Number one hundred and twelve.

Somewhere in another building, in a different ink, on a different sheet, with a clerk's hand and no badge at all, the same number was sitting in a margin beside the thirty-first line, and the office that had written the fourth box had run out of paper about two weeks before he started asking.

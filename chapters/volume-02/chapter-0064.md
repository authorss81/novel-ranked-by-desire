# The Address Book

Lantern Row at seven in the evening and there was a plate of cold something on the table with a cloth over it, which meant Ines Duarte had cooked and had not eaten, and he had known her long enough to know those were two different events.

"Sit down," she said. "I am going to read you the sheet."

"You do not have to—"

"I have known for six days that I was going to have to read you the sheet or stop having it, and I have been not having it for six days, and this morning an office in my own building told me the day after tomorrow is the seventh and asked me again, and I am not going into that room tomorrow with a piece of paper I have never said out loud in my own rooms."

She put it on the table. It was four sides of good paper, which cost more than she earned in a week, and the first three sides were full and the fourth had eleven lines on it and a great deal of white.

"Sit down," she said again. "I am reading it and you are not reading it, because if you read it you will correct the arithmetic, and you are not going to correct the arithmetic in my rooms."

"Side one," she said. "The line. One entry, undated as to deed, dated as to arrival, in a hand that is mine and not yours, reading: one line reading himself. That is the whole of side one and it is four lines long and it has taken me three months and it is the only thing in the city that I cannot hand to anybody."

She turned the sheet over without looking at it, which told him she had the pages in the right order by feel.

"Side two. The arithmetic. Ninety-one lines, of which eight turned, of which one of the eight is himself, arrived on the thirteenth in a room with nine chairs in it, in front of sixty people, and the composition of the standing has never once included one grain of it." She read it in the flat voice.

"That side has a heading: why it is there and what it cost. It cost three people a cold minute each and it cost you the option of the Review floor being a room you could stand in and be looked through in.

I have written both halves and I have not written down whose names, and if you ever ask me for the names on side two I am going to tell you that they are the same names you would not give Silas Drant, and we will both find out at the same time whether you have kept your mouth shut for reasons or out of habit."

"Go on."

"Side three is new and it was written on the third of the month after you came down those stairs looking like a man who has signed something he did not read, and I am going to tell you exactly what I heard and exactly where, because you are going to start inventing the rest of it otherwise.

I heard it from two auditors in a queue at the Exchange and one of them is a fool. That is the provenance of the whole of side three and it is the whole of what I am entitled to tell you about it."

She turned to the third side.

"It has a heading and the heading is: places I have watched you stand in. Eleven of them. The fourth-floor landing of the branch. The Exchange counter. The Reading Square at nine. The Vestry arcade. The tram at the junction by the Reading, where the crowd is worst. The last row of the second floor at half past six. Nine more, with times, because I am an auditor and I am not going to write a list of places without times on it."

He looked at it and did not touch it.

"That is eleven places I have watched you stand in," said Ines, "and not one of them has ever been in the middle of anything. I have been checking them against what is in the sheet you will not give me, and I cannot, because I do not have it, and that is the correct arrangement and I am not going to try to improve it."

"Side three is useless."

"Side three is two-thirds useless and one-third lethal, and it took eleven days to work out which, and the proportions are correct." She put a finger on the bottom of the third side.

"The proportions are correct because a register in that office keeps addresses and no faces, and your ledger keeps faces and no addresses, and neither of those is a person, and the two of them together is one, and there is a third ruled line on a buff square with your name on it and it is not faces and it is not names."

"Addresses."

"Addresses." She said it the way she said a figure she did not like. "And I have given you no addresses, and if I am honest with myself in my own rooms at seven in the evening, the reason I have given you no addresses is not integrity. It is that I have one and it is not mine to give."

He did not ask what it was, and it cost him something not to, and he understood that it cost her something to have written eleven places that were all the wrong kind.

"Side four," she said, and her voice changed by about a degree, which was how she changed. "Side four is the question. It was one question when I drew it on the seventeenth and it is two now, and I am going to read both and you are not going to answer either tonight, and if you try I will stop."

"The Office's question: what does an Office do with a relief that had no relief in it."

"Mine: what does an Office do with a man it cannot put an address to."

She put the sheet face down on the table.

"I have been an auditor for eleven years. In eleven years I have never needed the second question.

In eleven years every person who has walked into that building has had a thing on paper that says where they live. The paper is not there because the Bureau put it there. The paper is there because a landlord, an employer, a school or a plate does it."

"There have been a handful in eleven years where none of the four did it, and I have never once had to work out what to do with the case. You write the words: address of record not established. And then you write the four alternatives. And then you hand it up."

She squared the sheet.

"There are no alternatives for you. There is no address of record. There has not been one since you were a boy working somewhere else."

"I have known that about you for four months and I have been running it as a thing about a form, and it is not a thing about a form."

She stopped for a moment and picked up her glass and put it down again without drinking from it.

"You are the only man in Ostara who cannot be found," she said, "and I have been treating that as an administrative condition my profession has a phrase for, and it is not that. It is the answer. Every person in this city can be found by an office and you cannot, and an account that cannot be found cannot be claimed by an office, and that is not a loophole. That is the entire reason it landed on you."

The room was very quiet and the cloth over the plate had gone cold and neither of them was going to eat it.

"So that is what is on four sides of paper," said Ines Duarte, "and it is nine days old, and I am going to do something tomorrow that I have never done in eleven years and I am telling you at seven in the evening on the sixth of the month so that you cannot say afterwards that you did not know what you were agreeing to."

"What are you going to do."

"I am going to hand this sheet in."

Lin Hao put his hand flat on the table.

"Ines—"

"Not file it. Hand it in. It goes in on the seventh, on my own hand, to the office that has asked me twice, and it goes in as a four-sided sheet with no attachments and no names and no addresses, and I sign it, and it gets a number." She did not raise her voice and she did not speed up. "And then it exists.

It will have a number and a date and my name on the front of it and it will be a document in this city about a man with no address, and there is no office in Ostara that can produce a document of that kind because there is no rule for one, and a document with a number cannot be shut and cannot be forgotten and cannot be described away by the next person who gets the file."

"That is not handing it in. That is putting it on a record where the Office can find it."

"Yes." She looked at him steadily. "That is exactly what it is, and I have known that since the fourth, and I have had two days to be frightened about it and I have been, and I am doing it anyway, and here is the reason and it is one sentence and it is the only reason I have."

"Go on."

"Because they will keep a standing inquiry open against you until they can say what you are, and they cannot say what you are, and the only document in Ostara that says what you are is four sides of paper that I wrote in my own rooms." She pushed the sheet two inches towards him across the table. "The Office will never produce it.

An auditor in rank two has produced it, in her own hand, with a number on it, and it is the only description of you in the city that is not a guess. And a guess can be redone by anybody with a better office and a worse week. A document with a number on it cannot."

She picked up her glass and this time she drank from it.

"I am not doing it for you," she said. "I want that on the table with everything else.

If the Office keeps a standing inquiry open against you for two years you will be a man who is six and under his own band and under a calendar for two years, and a man like that does not get to rank nine, and you will not open a file, and you will finish this at a washroom counter on a night shift.

I have thought about your want for four months and I have come to the conclusion that it is not possible for you while you are the only thing in this city nobody can describe. And I have decided that I would rather there be one true document."

There was a knock at the door and it went again and neither of them moved.

"Side four has got something else on it," Ines said, "and I put it there at about eleven at night two days ago and I did not put it in front of you because I am a coward about documents."

She turned the sheet over to the fourth side and turned it round so that it faced him.

Under the two questions there were eleven lines in her hand, with the times against them, and then there was a twelfth line, and the twelfth line was empty, and it had a word printed above it in ink.

RECIPIENT.

"There is a heading on a blank line," said Ines Duarte, "and I have not filled it in, and I have not crossed it out either. I want you to understand that a heading with nothing under it is a document inviting a question."

"I wrote it on purpose. On the seventh I am going to have to say where this sheet has been since the seventeenth, and I am going to have to say the true answer, and the true answer is: in the rooms of an auditor, in a drawer, and also in the pocket of the man it is about since this evening at seven."

She put the sheet in front of him.

"Take it."

"If I lose it—"

"Then I have lost four months and a good hand's worth of paper and I will be considerably worse at my job than I was on the seventeenth, and I will not blame you, and I am not being generous, I am being an auditor, and an auditor who blames the last holder of a document is an auditor who has no procedure." She squared the empty space where the sheet had been.

"If you are taken, it will be on your person, and it will be four sides, and it will be in your hand and not mine, and I will be asked whether I put it there, and I will say what I said to you tonight, which is that an auditor does not put a sheet in a man's pocket, a man asked her to take it."

"No," Lin Hao said. "You put it in my pocket."

"I am going to say that to an office on the seventh." She almost smiled and did not quite get there.

"And I am going to say it in those words, and there will be a minute. The minute will say: attendee volunteered receipt of document at auditor's request, full text not read aloud, contents not disputed. And it will be the first thing you have ever handed that office that was not a plate."

She stood up and took the plate of cold something away and put the cloth back over it and did it badly.

"One more thing and then I am going to sit down, because I have been standing up for most of an hour and I have not eaten."

"Say it."

"You told me on the seventeenth that this was half mine." She was at the sink with her back to him. "It is not half any more and it was not half on the fourth when I wrote the heading.

From the seventh this is a document I am producing in a proceeding, and a document you are producing is a document you have to keep. I am sorry about that and I am not going to soften it."

"There is a version of tonight where I keep it and there is a version where I hand it in on my own and you never see it. I went back and forth between those two for about six hours and I want you to know which one I chose and why."

"Why."

"Because you would have let me," said Ines.

She sat down and pushed the plate towards him.

"Eat that. It is not good and it is cold and it has been through a worse evening than either of us."

He put the sheet in the inside pocket of his coat, over the plate, and it was four sides of good paper with a word printed on the bottom of it and nothing under the word.

[One hundred and nine lines. Eight turned.]

On the seventh of the month a woman was going to say a true sentence into a room and an office was going to be able to write down that she had said it, and for the first time in three years there would be a piece of paper in Ostara that did not describe a figure, a band, a plate, a register line or a ruling.

It described a man with no address, and it had a heading at the bottom of the fourth side with nothing under it, and a heading is the only thing about a document that cannot be unasked.

And somewhere in a building off a third court, a woman who could not see one thing clearly was putting two hundred sheets of paper back on a wall and pinning them up in the order she always put them up in, and she had stopped asking about the data.

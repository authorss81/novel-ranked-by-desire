# What September Still Holds

The figure was the same number for the eighth week and the queue at the Exchange worked out what that meant while Garrick Neve was still reading it.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," Neve said. "Band six, held, floor eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, three grains of margin, carried on a Review ruling, number one hundred and twelve." He wrote it on the board and drew the line under it, and put the card back in the machine, and the queue moved four feet.

Lin Hao stood in the marked square and said nothing, because there was nothing to say and because the eight-week figure had started doing something the six-week one had not.

A man on the left of the rail said it out loud.

"Flat."

He said it to the man next to him and the man next to him turned round and looked at the board and then at Lin Hao and then back at the board, and within about eleven seconds four people in that queue had said the word or a version of it, and the queue in Ostara does not need to be told anything twice.

Neve did not correct it.

Lin Hao saw him decide not to correct it, which took about a second and a half, and he understood it too, and he was grateful for it in a way that made him feel slightly sick.

Because the man was wrong, and being wrong in the queue was cheap, and the wrong thing was going to be said out loud at the counters and in the canteens all morning, and the wrong thing was that a standing which does not rise is a standing with nothing going into it.

He had told Halvard Rees in the fourth week of the season that a thing that was wrong and simple was worse than a thing that was right and complicated, because a man would build the wrong one out of whatever was to hand.

On the sixth floor of a building in Cordwain a woman with a notebook and no badge was asking operators for their counts, and a woman with a dark coat and no paper at all had been getting answers out of frightened men at counters for eleven days, and now the largest queue in the district had begun to invent an explanation that would be finished and complete and entirely wrong inside a fortnight.

His strip was on a spike in his own corridor with a bell on the machine.

That was how. He had stood in a room two floors under the Exchange on the third of the month and signed a piece of paper that gave them a figure every Thursday, and the figure was on nine strips on a spike where ninety people walked past it with wet hands, and a flat figure on a wall is a thing the whole city can read.

He had known what he was buying. He had not worked out what he was selling.

The rooms above the chop-house on Ferrier Street had four chairs and a table with a candle on it and the window open because the room was warm with four people in it, and Nadia Ferrous was sitting on the sill with her boots off, which was how Lin Hao knew she had done four flights of stairs rather than taken the lift.

"You have been up here since the day before yesterday."

"I have been up here once. It was four flights. I have been thinking about it since the day before yesterday and I have decided that I am going to be extremely unpleasant about it and then never again." She put her boots side by side under the sill with the heels lined up. "It is two floors of the Vestry and I cannot take the lift anywhere.

I have not been in a room I did not have to go into since I was a girl in this quarter and my mother used to put a chair by the stair and say she would carry me down and I would not let her and that was the whole of it. Four flights.

I have got a blister and I have not mentioned it because you would have made it into a thing about me."

"Then I am not going to make it into a thing about you."

"I know, that is why I did it." She grinned at him with her chin on her knees.

Marguerite Vale came up at eight with two bottles and did not stay long, and put one of them on the table and sat down in the chair that had stayed empty all summer.

"How is the strip," she said.

"How is the strip," said Nadia, "is what a broker says when she has already run it four times."

"I have run it once and I have run it well." Marguerite put her gloves on the table, which she never did. "A weekly strip is the cheapest instrument in this city and it is the cheapest for both sides.

It costs that office a clerk's minute and a wire, and it costs you one grain a month on a plate with three grains on it, and in exchange it makes your figure legible at a rate of once a week. In four months a man can learn a person from four months of a figure.

In a year he can learn a person from a year of a figure and a strip is the only one of the two that keeps giving."

"I signed it in a room."

"You signed it in a room and you had been awake for twenty hours and there was a calendar on the table and the calendar was the expensive thing in Ostara and it was free." She looked at him steadily. "I could make that strip three things and I would do it in a fortnight and you would never know which."

"Say the three."

"Invisible, meaningless, and valuable." She put one gloved finger on the table for each. "Invisible is a week where your figure reads and nothing comes back off the machine and the clerk at the branch does not get a strip, because there is a man on the plate office rota who owes me a favour and has never once been asked to use it.

Meaningful is a strip that says something — a figure that moves when a thing happens in that corridor, which I can arrange in four days with a private deed and a witness, and it would be a real deed and it would really score, and it would be the first time in a year that anything you did was in your own ledger in any way that a machine could see.

And valuable is a strip that costs somebody who is not you a grain to produce, and sells for four times what it costs, which is what I do, and it is what I would do."

The room had gone very quiet.

"No," Lin Hao said.

"I know. I told you I was going to tell you anyway." She took the gloves off the table and put them in her lap. "The reason, and it is not virtue and I want it understood that it is not virtue.

The terms are still down on that table and they stay down until you sign them, and a thing I have touched is a thing I have a standing to call in, and I am not going to have a standing in you before there is a document that says so, because you have never once in four months let me have anything that was not written, and I am not going to be the woman who taught you that people can be taken on a nod."

"Then say the other reason."

"The other reason is that I have had two houses and four months and a set of books that do not agree with each other, and I have not got you." She said it without any drama. "Four months. Two houses.

Eight women at my table who have all been extremely kind to you and none of whom has any idea what you are, and not one useful word about it.

And now an office has arranged to look at you once a week for the rest of a standing for the price of a stamp, and if that does not produce a description of you inside a year then nothing will, and I am telling you that I would rather be the woman who never knew than the woman who made him knowable on an ordinary afternoon."

Nadia said, from the sill: "That is the first thing you have said in four months that I have believed without asking a question about."

"I have earned the belief of a woman with a blister," said Marguerite, "and I will take it."

She put her gloves back on and stood up.

"Nadia. Your car."

"The day after tomorrow," said Nadia. "Nobody is asking me to give anything up and nobody has asked me to be a person about it."

"I am going to the Vestry at half past ten and I am taking a woman down two floors who has not been down in a fortnight, and I am not being paid and I do not want to be. And if anybody comes and asks me a question about that I will tell them the truth, which I have decided is a policy."

Marguerite Vale stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"What truth," she said.

"The truth." Nadia did not move off the sill. "Earlier in the week a woman came and sat in my car with a notebook and no badge and she had booked a seat, which you do not do on my line unless you have a reason. She asked all of us whether we keep counts and whether we feel them. And I said yes and I said the number, and then she asked if we could feel when it went, and I said we could."

The room was very still.

"And I told her the truth, which is that I have felt one cold minute in my life. It was in this lift, with the doors shut between floors, on an afternoon in the spring before you could read a plate."

"And I have told about four hundred people since I was a girl in this quarter, because I am loud and because it is funny, and because the only person it ever happened to was the man who came up to me in a stopped car with a figure in his pocket." Nadia put her chin back on her knees.

"I told her his name. She wrote it down. She did not ask me twice."

The room said nothing at all for about nine seconds.

"I know," said Nadia. "I have had all afternoon and a blister to think about it and I want it on the record that I worked it out before I said it and that I said it in a room with three people in it on purpose, because I am not going to be the one who gets found out later and says she did not know.

And I am not going to pretend I mind, because it is the only thing I have ever been in any record about, and there is not one single thing about me in the whole of this that anybody wanted written down."

"She has got a notebook and four operators and a name from a signalman," Lin Hao said, "and now she has got you."

"She has got me because I am not frightened of a notebook and you are." Nadia Ferrous said it without any edge in it at all.

"And because four hundred people already know, and a woman with a notebook is going to find out what four hundred people know before she has got off the tram, and the only question was whether she got it off me in a car or off Nadia Ferrous in a canteen. I gave her the polite version in a lift.

It is the same information and mine cost a fortnight."

Lin Hao looked at the two of them.

He had spent ten weeks refusing to give an office sixty-one names, and had said in a room, with his hand flat on a table, that the faces were not instruments and that an office which wanted a name was describing him and he would not be described by one.

A woman with a blister had handed the same office his name in a lift in exchange for nothing whatever, and had told four hundred strangers first, and had done it on purpose, and would not be talked out of it.

"Sorry," said Nadia. "That was not fair. I have had the advantage of you on this since June."

"Four hundred people know."

"About nine hundred now, and it stopped being four hundred about the time you got a band." She put her boots back on under the sill. "That is what I wanted to say about it and I have not said it properly and I am going to say it now and then we are done.

You have spent ten weeks treating the ledger like a thing you can hold shut with your hands. It is not a thing anybody can hold shut. It is a thing that happened in front of strangers, and strangers talk, and that is not a failure of yours.

That is the only good news I have got, and I have been sitting on it since the sixth, because it is not mine to hand over and I did not know whose it was."

They sat in it for a while.

Then Marguerite said, from the stairs, not coming back in: "Lin Hao. One thing before I go down."

"Say it."

"The twenty-fifth." She said it from the top of the stairs with the bottle in her hand. "Somebody in this city has given you a date and it has been written on the back of your hand in pencil and you have washed it off twice and put it back on.

I have not asked and I am not asking, and I am telling you that a date you have washed off twice is the only asset you have got that is not in your own hands, and that a broker's whole trade is knowing which dates are real."

"Who told you about the pencil."

"Not one porter." She went down two steps. "A man in a dark coat has been asking my porters and two of them are women who talk. You are not the only one being counted and you have not been for eleven days."

She went down the stairs, and the two of them sat with the candle and the two bottles and the window open over a street where a chop-house had been trading since before the Veil and where the whole unmeasured half of that city's kindness walked past twice a day.

Nadia put her boots back on and stood up off the sill and looked at him for a moment.

"Nobody in this room is going to say the word September," she said.

"There is a date in this household that three of us know about and one of us is not allowed to have and I am not going to have it either, and if you say the word in this room I will take the car down and I will not come back up for a month.

So you can have it or you can have the room and you are not getting both and I have made the rule and the rule is not about you."

"Nadia—"

"Go to bed, Lin Hao." She went to the door and put her hand on it and stopped. "Nine hundred people know. Nine hundred. Whatever it is, it stopped being yours about four months before I got in your lift, and there is not one single thing you can do about it, and the only thing left that is yours is who you say it to."

She went down four flights.

The strip was on a spike in his own corridor. A woman with a notebook was at the gate of a yard in Cordwain. A broker's porters were being asked questions by a woman with no paper at all. Nine hundred strangers had been talking for four months.

And on the ninth of September a signal operator had a date she had never told anybody, and four women in a room above a chop-house had all found out about it by now, apparently, and not one of them was going to say the word out loud.

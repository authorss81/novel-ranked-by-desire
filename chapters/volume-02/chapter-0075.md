# The Turned Room

The room on the third floor of the Reading had no chairs in it on the twenty-sixth of the month, and the nine of them were stacked in the corridor outside in two rows with the legs up, and a woman in a grey armband was putting a tally sheet on a chair by the door.

Lin Hao stood in the doorway and looked at the floor.

It was a plain room. There was a rail round three sides of the open square in the middle of it, and the panel had been taken off its table and stood against the far wall on its side, and there were four marks on the boards where the legs of the table had been for eleven years.

"You have come to see the chairs," said Ruta Vesely.

She was sitting on the window ledge in her coat with the map rolled up under her arm, and she had clearly been there some time, and she did not look like a woman who had come to work.

"I have come because there is an arithmetic I want to do out loud in the place where the dot is."

"Then come and do it." She got off the ledge. "And before we start I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to ask you one, and the three are going to cost me and I have decided to spend them, so do not thank me for them.

One: this room has been attended four times on the standing, on the third and the tenth and the seventeenth and the twenty-fourth, and I have been the one attending it, and this is the fourth, and there is nothing in it.

Two: I have stopped it as of this morning. The event has no distribution, my own line says so in my own hand, and I have entered it as insufficient, and from today this office is not sending anybody to this floor and it is not sending anybody to any of the nine people who work in this building.

Three: I told the woman at the counter by the door before I left, and she is the one who puts this room up and takes it down, and she has put it up for eleven years and not one person has ever told her why."

"That is three," Lin Hao said. "What is the one you are going to ask me."

"I am going to ask you one in about ten minutes and it is going to be the only question I have left." She turned the roll over in her hands. "Do the arithmetic."

He walked to the middle of the room and stood on the open square where a chair had asked a question on the thirteenth of last month with sixty people behind him.

"Three episodes," he said. "Nell Ardery, on a landing in Cordwain. Garrick Neve, at a counter. The man at a yard gate, who has never given anybody an address and whose square has a gate written on it where a name goes.

Three buildings. Three streets apart. Four seconds."

"That is what is on my sheet."

"That is what is on your sheet and it is what is on three separate people's hands in three separate offices and it is not in dispute and nobody in that building has ever gone to look at it." He put his hands in his pockets because the room was cold and the floor was bare. "So do the sum and there are three ways to read it.

The first reading is that three ordinary things happened to three ordinary people in three ordinary places in four seconds. That is not a thing that happens and I would like anybody in this building to write down how often it happens, because it does not.

The second reading is that one thing left this room and arrived in three buildings three streets apart in four seconds." He looked at her. "I cannot do that and it is not a matter of not wanting to. I have one pair of hands.

In four seconds my hands are in this building and in no other building, and there is no instrument in Ostara that puts a thing in a man's hand and takes it out of him somewhere else, and the man who would know that best has been telling me for a month that nothing about me moves and that I should keep it that way."

"And the third reading."

"The third reading is that nothing left this room." He let it stand for a second before he said the rest, because he had known it for three days and he had not yet said it to a person. "Relief does not travel. It has never travelled in four years. Not one person has ever felt it twice, and not one person has ever felt it in two places at one spend, and the reason is that it does not go out of me.

It goes in, from whoever is nearest, out of whoever it came from."

He took his hands out of his pockets.

"So the three buildings emptied at once, and this room is not connected to any of them, and a thing emptied three buildings is not a thing that left a man in a fourth.

Which means there is no point. Which means the point on your map is not a person."

Ruta Vesely put the roll down on the window ledge and did not pick it up again.

"Say the last part again."

"A person is where his hands are. A person cannot be in three buildings. So the dot is the room in which I was standing and the room in which I was standing is not where anything happened, and your ring has been round the correct room for four weeks for a reason that has nothing to do with me."

The tally woman by the door had stopped writing.

"It is Sabine Rooke," Ruta said, without turning round. "She has set this room up for eleven years and she was asked twice about a Thursday and she did nothing wrong. I have known that since the twelfth and I have been carrying it about and I have just told a room that is not mine.

And that is what I spent, and I would like it noted that I did not spend it for him, I spent it because I had it and he did not."

Then she took the acknowledgement out of her coat.

She had it rolled inside a sheet of wax paper, which was not how a document that had gone up on the twentieth should have been, and when she unfolded it she put it on the end of the table that was still on its side against the wall, and squared it.

"You signed this on the seventeenth in a room with a cloth on the table and a clerk with a rubber stamp."

"Drant wrote the heading and I signed under it."

"Drant wrote the heading. You signed it." She put her finger on the third line. "The three episodes recorded at the addresses attached to the analyst's sheet were caused at his hand, and that they are his cost and are not evidence against the persons in which they occurred.

Your office has been reading that sentence for nine days as a statement about this room. It is the reason three buff squares went to three addresses with a nineteenth on them and it is the reason the fourth box exists at all, and it is the only document in that building in which the word addresses is joined to a man by his own hand."

"Go on."

"It is a statement about a hand." Ruta Vesely looked at him. "A hand cannot be in three places. So the sentence is true and the sentence is not evidence of what this office has been treating it as evidence of, and the falsification is on a piece of paper this office wrote, dated, minuted and registered, and it is in your handwriting at the bottom."

The room was very quiet and the nine chairs stood in the corridor with their legs up.

"So that is the proof," she said, "and it is proof on the Office's own paper, and it is being given to the Office by a man who has spent a month refusing to give that office a single true sentence about himself.

What is it going to cost."

"Everything they have spent on the square," he said. "The four attendances. The nine clerks. Sabine Rooke, who is out there on a chair with a tally sheet and does not know why she is a subject of anything. That is what it costs and I would like it paid today and in full and I would like it written down that I asked for it and that it was not argued."

Ruta Vesely wrote for about nine seconds, which was how long it took her to write everything she had ever written for him, and then she turned the sheet round so he could read the line.

Finding: a distribution of one event is not a distribution. Office attendance at the Review room is discharged. No further attendance at any address on this map. Cause not established and not pursued.

"That closes my file," she said. "It also makes me a woman who signed that nothing in this building can be found in a room a man stood in, and I have put my name to it, and there is a woman at the head of this office who has been getting the sixth heading of her fourth book wrong for two attempts and will get it right on the third."

She rolled her sleeve back down.

"Now the question." She put the acknowledgement away inside the wax paper. "There are five headings on a page in a book in this building, and they are yours, and they are attendances, plate, ruling, figure and margin, in that order, every time, for every entry that came to this floor and could not go in the register.

And under the fifth of them there is a ruled space, and it has been ruled since the eighth of last month, and it is empty, and the head of this office has written what goes in it twice and got it wrong twice, and she will write it a third time and she will get it right, because a form describes a thing that has already been described and she can do the arithmetic and cannot do the sentence.

What goes in the sixth heading, Lin Hao."

"That is my question to write and not yours to ask."

"It is nobody's question but yours and I am asking it in an empty room because a woman with a tally sheet is four feet away and I have had four weeks of asking it in rooms with a clerk in them." She did not raise her voice.

"And I am going to tell you what I think it is going to be. I have read your acknowledgement and I have read your plate and I have stood in nine different offices in this city and worked out what the finest ones have in common.

It is that a man with a plate that does not rise is a man who is not being given the whole of what he earns. That is the finest sentence in this city and it is wrong about you, and you are the only person alive who can write the true one."

Lin Hao looked at the bare boards with the four marks where a table's legs had been for eleven years.

"The number it goes under is not a figure of mine," he said.

"I did not ask for a figure."

"You asked what goes in it, and I am telling you what it has to say first, and then I am going to tell you the number, and the number is the last thing I have." He turned round. "There is an account. It is not in my name and it was never in my name and it has been in this city for three years.

Everything the account is, everything it has ever been, was sent to an address, and the address is a man on the fourth floor of a branch office, and the address was chosen on purpose by somebody who had more than two hundred thousand grains to give away and did not choose a reader or a clerk or a woman with a standing."

"Who sent it."

"That is the question and I am not going to answer it in this building and I am not going to answer it in yours." He held her eye. "But the file it went into has a heading, and the heading is a number, and it is a single digit, and I have had that number in my head since the fifteenth of the month before last and I have never said it out loud in a room where it could be written down."

The room got quieter in a way it had not been quiet before.

Ruta Vesely did not move.

"Drant told me a thing on the twenty-first," she said slowly, "about a consult that left this building on the twentieth, and about the account heading that every consult carries at the top so that a person can tell which of four hundred things they are about. And he said that in nineteen years he has seen that heading about forty times on his desk, and that every one of them was two digits. And he said this one is a single digit, and then he said no."

"He said it in a room with me in it."

"He said it in a room with me in it." She put her hand flat on the rolled map. "Lin Hao. A single-digit heading on a departmental consult is not a rare thing. It is the bottom of a list. One is the first account this office can be handed and still hold, and it is the lowest file in Ostara, and it has been the lowest file in Ostara for as long as the Wing has kept a list."

"I know what One is."

"Then say the sentence, because I have written a finding today and I am going to have to carry it up three floors and somebody at the top is going to ask me where it came from, and I would like to be able to say that the man in the room said it."

Lin Hao stood on an open square with the marks of a table's legs on the boards in front of him.

"The account in me is held in file One," he said. "There is no Zero. It is the lowest address in this city that will hold two hundred and fourteen thousand grains, and somebody with a seal put it there three years ago, and it has been filling itself from strangers ever since, and I am the address and I have never been the source, and I have never once been the source."

Ruta Vesely took about four seconds.

Then she sat down on the window ledge with her coat still on and put her hands in her lap, which he had never once seen her do in a room with him in it.

"Right," she said. "Thank you. I am not going to pretend that was not worth the afternoon."

"You have stopped it happening in this room."

"I have stopped it happening in this room and I have opened a document that this office cannot now close without putting a man in it, because a single-digit account heading spoken aloud in a room with an analyst in it is a description, and you have given me one." She looked up. "Do you understand what you have just done to me as well as to yourself."

"I gave you a finding that was true."

"You gave me a finding that is true and I have carried it up three floors and it is going to be the reason a paper leaves this building that I cannot read and cannot stop, and a paper that leaves this building goes into a thin book with a receiving house printed on it, and that house has answered the last consult about this account, and the same man signed for it." Ruta Vesely did not raise her voice at all.

"And you knew that on the twenty-first and you came to this room on the twenty-sixth and said a number out loud anyway."

There was nothing to put in front of that.

"That is true."

"I know it is true." She stood up and picked the roll off the ledge. "I am going to go up three floors and enter a finding that I have closed a room over, and I am going to write it in my own hand, and it will be the only document I have ever filed in this building that I am not able to defend on paper.

And I want you to understand what you have handed me, which is a word with a number under it. That is the only shape a description can take and I have spent a month with a man whose shape has none, and I would have gone up with a sheet full of marks and no meaning, and now I have one word and a digit, and I am going to be asked which word, and I am going to have to say that it is not written down."

She held the roll under her arm.

"Write it. That is all I am going to say about it and I am going to say it once. Write the heading before somebody in that building with better arithmetic than me writes a worse one."

She went out past Sabine Rooke, who was still on the chair with the tally sheet, and who did not look up, and who had been setting up and taking down a room for eleven years for nobody.

Lin Hao stood in an empty room with the panel on its side against the wall, and four marks on the boards where a table's legs had been, and a grey place at his left temple on the fourth day of it, and the word he had been carrying since the eighth of last month came up out of him in the shape of two words, and he said them once, out loud, to nobody, in an empty room, so that he would have said them.

Not earned.

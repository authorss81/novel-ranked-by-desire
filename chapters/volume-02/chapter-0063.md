# What Was Sent

The wall had been cleared.

That was the first thing and it was the whole of the first thirty seconds. Sun Meiling stood in the middle of the side room in the iron-stair corridor with a step stool folded under her arm and pins in her apron pocket, and every sheet was off the wall, and the wall behind them was old plaster with the picture hooks still in it and the paper a very slightly different colour where eleven years of paper had hung.

"Sit down," she said. "You have four minutes before I decide I have made a mistake."

He sat down on the bench. There was a jug of water on it and nothing else. No kettle.

"You took them down."

"I took them down on the last day of the month and I have not put them up since, and I want you to understand that they are not in another room, they are in a drawer nine feet from where we are standing, and I did that on purpose and I have not slept properly since the last day of the month." She set the stool down.

"I have been looking at pictures of you for three years, Lin Hao. Not of you. At the shape your account makes in other people's data. And a picture of a shape is the safest thing a woman with my trouble can put on a wall."

She stood in the middle of the cleared wall with her hands at her sides.

"Now I am going to say the thing I have been holding since the eighth of last month and you are not going to be allowed to have it in instalments, and afterwards you are going to be angry with me, and I would like to get the anger out of the way before I ask you for anything, because what I am going to ask for is worse than what I am about to tell you."

"Say it."

"You asked me to go on the stair in September and I did not, and in October I said it was because you had not stood still long enough. That was half of it.

The other half is that I was not protecting you, I was protecting me, because saying it to you puts you at the middle of it and I have known since the eighth that you are not at the middle of anything." She sat down on the stool, which was a thing she had never once done with him in this room.

"So here it is, all of it, and then you may be angry."

He waited.

"What is in you was sent."

The room was very quiet. Down the corridor a door closed and eight other doors stayed shut and there was no light on in any of them.

"A thing that is sent has a sender." She said it the way she said everything, as arithmetic.

"I have been in this building three years and I have asked four heads of house whether there is a man at the middle of the set, and there are four of them in this city who would know, and not one of them has ever put a man in the middle of anything. I have looked.

I looked at the oldest shape in my own house and the oldest shape in this one and they are the same shape and neither of them has a person in it, and I did the arithmetic myself and I got the same answer twice, which is the only time in three years I have got the same answer twice."

She put her hand flat on her own knee.

"It was sent to you. Somebody with a seal and more than two hundred thousand grains to give away chose this address out of a city of two million, and he did not choose a reader and he did not choose a clerk and he did not choose a woman with a house and a standing. He chose the lowest address that would hold it."

"How do you know it is the lowest."

"Because the file it went into is One." She said the number the way she said every number, as a fact about paper. "The consult came up to this house three years ago. I have the sheet. I wrote the answer, four lines, and I initialled it, and the answer went back through the house and out to the office that asked, and the sheet I have is my draft and it is not what went back."

"Who put the question."

"Departmental. It never carries a name on a consult. That is the entire purpose of it." She looked up. "But the consult is headed. Every consult is headed with the file it is about, because somebody has to be able to tell you which of four hundred things you are about to answer. And the heading on that sheet, at the top, under the date, is the account the transfer went into. It is a number and the number is One."

"And there is no Zero."

"There is no Zero." She said it quietly. "One is the bottom of the Wing's list and there is nothing under it and there has never been anything under it, because a file is numbered by what went into it, and one is what you get when what went in was the smallest thing the Wing can be handed and still hold."

The picture hooks were in the wall behind her and the plaster was a slightly different colour where eleven years of paper had been, and Lin Hao sat on a bench in an iron-stair building and understood four things in a row, each of them separately, the way you hear four bells.

It was not his.

It had never been his.

Somebody had given away two hundred and fourteen thousand grains on purpose.

A Bureau that could not describe the giving had had to take the person who did it, and the thing that had been sent had been sitting in the lowest file in Ostara for three years, and it had been filling itself from strangers.

It had been coming to him because a man who cleans toilets is the only person in this city who is not looked at hard enough for the thing to be a scandal.

And his brother had stood in a public hall three years ago in front of a hundred and forty people and said the number was wrong.

His brother had been right.

Not approximately. Not in the way a man is right about a figure. Right about the whole of it, in the specific sense that a man is right when he has seen the thing and says it out loud and is gone by morning, and Lin Hao had been carrying the objection around inside his own chest for three years believing it was a question, and it was not a question, it was a receipt.

He put his hands flat on his knees.

He had been saying for a week that he was the address and not the source, in a side room, to a woman, as though that were the discovery.

He had known that since the Review. He had simply not known that it had a person behind it.

Sun Meiling let him have it. She did not touch him, and she did not say anything at all for about two minutes, and Lin Hao understood while the two minutes went by that she had done that on purpose, because she had watched two people try to hold this and she had said so herself, in this room, in a coat with a sentence behind it.

"You have a face on," she said. "Say the thing on it."

"My brother said so."

"Your brother said so in a hall in front of a hundred and forty people."

"Yes."

"Then you have been carrying his sentence for three years and you thought it was a question about a figure." She said it without any softening at all, which was the only way she could have said it. "It was not.

He was right, and being right is not a thing the Wing can hold, and I have spent three years building a picture of the shape of his being right on a wall, and there is a woman in this city who put her son in a queue in the year it took, and I am not going to ask you to feel better about that tonight."

"Who is she."

"Alma Quayle." Sun Meiling said the name without weight, the way a clerk says a heading. "She is not in this building and she does not come up the iron stair, and I have met her twice and both times it was in a corridor, and both times she was extremely pleasant and both times she asked me a question she had already written down."

"She is keeping a book."

"She is keeping a book on you and she has been keeping it since the eighth of last month and it is not a register." Sun Meiling stood up off the stool. "And that is the last of the sentence.

You have it all now and I am not going to say one word more about where it came from or who signed it, because I do not know and I have spent three years trying to find out and the only thing I have ever been given is a heading with a number on it."

She put the pins out of her apron pocket onto the bench, in a line, and counted them under her thumb.

"Now the part I want to ask for, and I am going to be very bad at asking for it, because I have watched three women in your life manage this well and I have watched you manage it badly every time, and I have had three years and a wall of sheets to work out what I actually want, and what I actually want is not what you are afraid of."

"What am I afraid of."

"You are afraid of somebody reading you for a reason." She said it flatly. "In every one of those cases, somebody in a corridor or at a counter or under the Exchange has put a form on you and gone through the same five headings and you have stood there and refused. A woman in a room is not a form. And I am not going to pretend that is the whole of what I am telling you, so I am going to go further than that."

She came and stood in front of him where he was sitting on the bench.

"I want to read you," she said. "I want to do it out loud, in this room, for as long as you will let me, with my hands on you and my voice describing what is under my hands, and I am not going to be kind about it and you are not going to be able to stop it by being still."

"I can stop it."

"You can stop it by saying so and I will stop and I will not be angry and I will not ask you why, because the whole point of it is that you can, and if you cannot then it is not what I am asking for." She crouched down so that they were level with each other, which she had never done. "Lin Hao.

I have never in my life been able to look at one man and know what he is. That is the price. Everything I see is a set and it comes in groups and there is no middle and there is no single thing anywhere in it. Do you understand what it would be worth to me to have one?"

"A room with a man in it."

"A room with a man in it, for an hour, where I do not have to guess." She put her hands on his knees. "I have had that for four years and I have never asked and I am asking you now and you may say no and you may say no for four more months and I will come up this corridor on the day I said I would and I will ask you a wrong question instead."

"Four true things," he said.

"That is what Ines says and she is right about it and I have heard her say it through you." She did not smile. "Mine first, and they are longer. One: you tell me to stop, in any words you like, including one word, and I stop inside the second and I do not ask what it was.

Two: if I say something is wrong, I am right, and you will believe me about it and not argue, and I will say it plainly and you will not have to guess. Three: I am not going to ask you for anything.

Not your plate, not your file, not the face of any person who has ever been cold in your hand, and not the numbers, and if you start giving me those things to be polite I will stop, because that is a different afternoon."

"And the fourth."

"Four: I am not going to pretend I do not want this." She said it with her face about eight inches from his. "I want it. I have wanted it since you sat down on that floor in September and did not try to be worth it.

I have a house and a standing and eleven years of work and I am going to be perfectly all right, and I am telling you that in advance so that nothing that happens in the next hour can be mistaken for me being hard up. Say yours."

"You stop when I say."

"I stop when you say."

"If you say a thing is wrong, I believe you and I do not argue."

"Yes."

"If you want me somewhere, you ask me first."

"There is nowhere," said Sun Meiling. "It is a room with a bench in it and a wall with nothing on it."

"If I do not know what I am doing, I say so instead of guessing."

"Say that one now," she said, "because you have not said it in the last twenty minutes and you have said it every other time you have been in this building."

"I do not know what I am doing," Lin Hao said.

"Good." She stood up. "That is four and it was the true one and I have never once been glad to hear it before."

She took her coat off and hung it on the back of the door, and rolled her left sleeve to the elbow without unpinning it, and set the jug of water where he could reach it, and then stood in front of the bare wall with her hands at her sides and looked at it.

"Get up," she said. "You have been on that bench for an hour and this is a room with a floor in it."

He got up.

"Face the wall."

He faced the wall, and she came behind him and did not touch him for about a minute, and he stood there with eleven years of paper-shape left on the plaster in front of him and understood that he had not been looked at properly by anybody since he was a child and that the reason was not modesty.

It was that being looked at had never once turned out to be anything except an inventory.

Her hands came in flat on his shoulders, low, near the blades, and she put her thumbs in and went down the spine a vertebra at a time, slowly, and said what she was doing while she did it.

"Your shoulders are up here. Both of them. They have been up here since you walked into this corridor and they will be up here when you leave it, and they have been up here for a month while an office has been keeping a calendar for you."

Her mouth was close to his ear and he could feel it more than hear it. "Down. That is better. That is me telling you something is wrong and you believing me about it, and your body agreeing before you do."

They went on like that for a long time, and she talked the entire time, in the flat set-reading voice she used for a sheet of figures, and it turned out to be the most exposing thing anybody had ever done to him.

She told him the shape of him. Not the account — she was careful about that, and he understood the care, and she never used a number. She told him what her hands found and what she could see in it: the left forearm, the whole length of it, which was not the arm that hurt and had not hurt since June.

The breath that came in at the top and did not go all the way down. The exact place at the base of his skull where the day sits when he has been in a building full of strangers since six in the morning.

"You are not tense," she said. "You are braced. Those are different and a person can hold a brace for a month and call it being a person and it is not, it is a man holding a door shut from the inside." She put her thumb on it and pressed, not hard. "Let go of that."

"I cannot tell whether I have."

"Then I will tell you. You have."

She got his coat off and his shirt off and she did it without hurry and without any of the awkwardness of a woman doing something she has not done, and then she stood back and looked at him the way she had looked at a sheet, and said:

"Right. Say it back. What am I looking at."

"You are looking at a man who has been holding a door shut."

"More."

"A man who has been holding a door shut since the Review, and who does it because the last time he stood in a room with nine chairs in it he opened one, in front of sixty people, and three strangers took something out of the room and two of them have written it down."

She was quiet for a second.

"Yes," said Sun Meiling. "That is what is under my hands and you have never once said it out loud to anybody, including a woman who has been inside your chest for two months."

She put her mouth on the side of his neck and her fingers into the hair at the back of his head and pulled, gently, enough.

"Bed," she said.

"There is a bench in this room and it is a bad bench, and neither of us is going to spend an hour on a bad bench. You are going to tell me what you want and I am going to do it and you are going to say it in words, out loud."

"Because the single most interesting fact about you is that you have never once asked for anything in a room with a woman in it. And I want to hear how your voice does it."

"I do not know what I want."

"That is the fourth one, again, in the wrong place." She laughed, and it was not a polite noise, and she pushed him back onto the bench and got on top of him and put her weight on his chest. "Say it anyway. Say the nearest true thing."

"Stay on top of me."

"Say that again and slower and do not look at the ceiling."

"Stay on top of me." His hands were on her back and did not know what to do and then did. "Do not stop to look at anything. Not to write a figure down, not to check a shape, not to go and get the lamp. I want you to be a person and I want it for an hour and I have never had that off anybody and I want it off you."

"That," said Sun Meiling, "is the first thing you have ever asked me for, and you are going to get it, and you are not going to know what to do with your hands for about four minutes and then you are going to be extremely good at it."

She was right about the four minutes.

What he had not been ready for was the talking. She kept it up the whole way, in that flat unembarrassed voice, and it did not stop when it should have, and he understood about a third of the way through that it was not for him at all.

It was for her.

She was narrating. She was putting him into words while she had him, because she could not hold a single thing in her head and this was the only room in Ostara where a single thing could be held by another person long enough to be read aloud, and she was going to use him until she had finished, and she had told him so before she started, and he had said yes.

"There," she said, at one point, her mouth against his collarbone. "That. You have stopped talking."

"I have."

"You have not stopped listening. That is the difference and I want it on the record that I know the difference, because a man who stops listening has gone somewhere else and I do not want you there." She got a hand under the back of his head and turned his face up. "Say a thing."

"Any thing."

"A true one."

"You are the only person who has ever wanted the thing I cannot give anybody."

"Then say the next one, and do not look at the wall."

Afterwards she lay with her head on his chest and her hand flat on his sternum for a long time, and neither of them spoke, and the room was a room with a bare wall and a bad bench and the door shut and no sound in the corridor at all.

Then she got up, filled the jug, and washed him herself the way she had done it in September — unhurried, thorough, entirely unembarrassed — and sat on the floor with her back against the bench afterwards, in the position they had ended in before, and handed him a cup of water out of the jug.

"Right," she said. "Terms."

"Not sworn. Not housed. Not filed."

"Still, and I will tell you that you said all three in under a second again, and I have decided to stop remarking on it." She drank some water.

"What is new is that you have given an office a strip of your plate once a week for the rest of a standing, and you have not told me, and I am not asking you to tell me, because there was no reason on earth for you to tell a woman you met a fortnight ago and I want you to know that I noticed and did not ask and am not going to make it a thing."

"That was on the third."

"I know what day it was.

I have known what day it was since the fourth, because on the fourth a colleague of mine in this house came back from a filing run with a strip off a branch printer in her hand, and it had a number on it, and the number was a ruling number, and she did not know what she was holding and she brought it to me." Sun Meiling said it perfectly level.

"A filing clerk on my own floor took it off her in the corridor and looked at it and gave it back and said nothing to me about it, and there were four other people in that corridor and not one of them looked up, and I have known since the fourth, and it has been sitting on the table between us for six days like a third person."

She looked up at the bare wall.

"Now. The thing I said I would not say until you had stood still long enough, and you have stood still for twenty-two days, and it has not gone flat, and it has not stopped moving, and both of those are what I asked for. So I have kept my side of it and here is the rest."

"I am listening."

"There is a thing about you that I have never been able to see and it is the only thing in eleven years of sheets I have ever failed at, and it is not that I cannot see it. It is that it is not there." She turned her head against the bench so she could look up at him.

"Every shape in my three years has a place in it. Three bodies here, in a room, on an afternoon. A line of charts here, on a street, in a quarter. When I put a mark in the middle it means a room. That is what a middle is. It is a place where it happened."

"And mine."

"Yours has no middle that I can find, and there is not one thing in three years of sheets with your shape in it that has a place in it." She sat up. "That is why three people went cold in a room and the dot an analyst put on a printed map landed on that room and was wrong. It was not wrong about the room. There were three episodes in a room. It was wrong that the room is where you are."

"Where am I."

"You are in a corridor on the fourth floor of a branch office at seven in the morning and you are in a tram and you are on a landing in Cutler's Row on an afternoon, and in nine years of mine not one of those places has ever been in the middle of anything." She held her hand up flat. "You are the only thing in this city I have ever had to look at that has no address."

He sat on the bad bench and let that go all the way down.

It was the best thing anybody had ever told him and it was the end of the only thing he had been able to hide behind.

A map cannot hold a man with no address, which meant the dot could never become him, which meant the Office would keep attending rooms he was not in and keep interviewing the clerks who worked in them and keep going on about a square where nine chairs had been on one afternoon in the sixth week.

And a man with no address could not be cleared of a room either.

There was no map in Ostara on which he was not in that room, and also no map on which he was anywhere at all, and the second fact was the only thing that had ever been standing between him and a requisition, and the first fact was what would be standing there the day somebody decided to check.

"You have gone very pale," she said. "Say what it is."

"If I have no address, then nothing proves I was in the room."

"No," said Sun Meiling. "And nothing proves you were anywhere else. Do you see it? That is what unreadable does to you. It does not keep you free. It keeps you safe in exactly the same way that a fog does. It saves you from being followed and it means that when somebody comes with a paper, there is no map you can hand them that puts you four streets away."

She got up and went and got the pins out of her apron.

"So here is where I have got to after three years," she said, "and I am telling you it plainly and I am not dressing it up. I have three years of data, one sentence, and a shape with no place in it, and the sentence explains the shape. What moved it in eleven years was not the sentence. It was you walking out of my door in September and coming back in a fortnight."

She started putting the sheets up.

It took her a long time. There were two hundred of them and she put them in the same order she always put them in, and the picture hooks went into the holes they had been in, and the room filled up behind her, sheet by sheet, until a woman standing with pins in her mouth could not be seen from the doorway at all.

"And now I am going to say the last thing of the night," she said, coming out from in front of the wall, "and I have thought about it for four days and I have decided to say it anyway.

"For three years I have been asking four heads of house about a shape. There were five of them by April. There is no man at the middle of it and I have said so to every one of them and I will go on saying so and it costs me something every single time, and you are the fifth one to ask me and I would like it recorded that you did not ask me, you asked me something else and got the same answer for free."

"And the next one."

"I am going to stop asking about the data."

She put the pins back in her apron.

"I am going to start asking about you. In a fortnight. And it will not be a wrong question, and I am not going to warn you which one it is."

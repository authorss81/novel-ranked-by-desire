# A Name Before Her

He went to work at ten past five and cleaned six floors of washrooms and nothing happened all morning, which after ten weeks was the most alarming thing that could occur.

The figure moved. It moved the way it had moved for four years, because the shifts came through the building and strangers held doors for each other in corridors with not one of them looking at any of the others. He felt it go up by a hundred and forty across the second floor and up by ninety on the fourth and by a hundred and something in the sub-levels, and he let all of it come, and none of it touched anything that had a name.

The nine corridors were the same nine corridors. The work was the same work. Basins, taps, rims, lino, soap cut in the machine at the end of the third floor, the fourth-floor landing where the door had been forced twice with a bar and had been left alone since the requisition. He put a bucket across it anyway, which he had been doing since June, and which was the only religious observance he had.

The branch was different and there was no version of the day where it was not. Clerks nodded who had never nodded. A porter met his eye at a fire door and held it four seconds longer than the door needed. Tobias Renn came down at eleven with a box and did not stop this time and did not start, which was its own kind of conversation, because a man carrying a box and looking at another man is deciding something and has not decided it.

At noon on the second floor he found a sheet of squared paper taped to the inside of the washroom door, at eye level, in the exact place where anybody washing their hands would have to read it.

It was a transcript of the ruling. The nine signatures. The standing, the band, the floor, the margin.

Underneath, in Ola Bright's round hand, she had written the number one hundred and twelve twice and underlined it, and beside it, where a teacher writes a question she does not expect to be answered in class:

WHAT DOES A FINDING NUMBER DO ON A REGISTER LINE.

He read it with soap in his eyes and then went and found her.

She was clerking on the third-floor landing, which was a promotion nobody had announced, and she had the finding copied out onto a clean page in her own hand with the long words left long, and she was pleased with the page and did not want to talk about the page.

"I wrote it out," she said. "I wrote out every word of it and I checked it against the branch copy and they are the same, and do you know what has not been said to me about it once? Not one person. Sixty people read that finding off a wall and every single one of them came past me on this landing to ask me whether I had copied it right, which means they read it, and not one of them said the thing you are not going to say either."

"What thing."

"Nothing. It is the thing." She squared the page against the desk. "Anyway. The number."

"The number is the ruling's number. Nine chairs made a ruling and gave it a number and the number is how anything ever finds out that a ruling happened."

"Right. And the number is on the plate. I saw it at the socket, I was behind the woman with the basket, it printed a third line." Ola's pencil went through the page and she did not notice. "So the number is on the plate in your pocket and the number is on a sheet in a building across the city, and they are the same number, and one of those two is a thing anybody can walk in and ask about. Lin Hao. The number is the join."

He had not thought of it that way and it took him a moment.

"The plate office prints it because the machine has to say what the band is on," he said. "That is not the same as the plate carrying it."

"It is the same as somebody with a socket being able to read it out loud to a queue." She put the pencil down and gave him a look that had got considerably older over the summer.

"I wrote that question on the door for whoever walks past it. It is not for you. There is a Wing intake and they have an instruction that says bring the ruling, and you have been up there once, and if that office can send a woman out in a grey coat with no badge then it can send anybody it likes into this building and ask anybody it likes a question, and there is one question in this city they have never managed to ask anybody and you have just made it the only question anybody in this building is frightened of."

"Who is frightened, Ola."

"You are not listening." She looked past him down the landing. "Third floor. Listen."

The third floor was quiet in the way a floor is quiet when everybody on it has agreed to be.

He had worked with Colm Deveraux across four years and never spoken to him more than twice in a month, because Colm was a supply supervisor with twenty-two years of shelving and a bad right shoulder and no reason on earth to talk to an attendant, and it had always been the easiest silence Lin Hao had in the building.

It was not a silence any more.

The door of supply room three — the one whose key Colm had refused to surrender in a draw-down week and had been withdrawn from the rota for — was open two inches, which it had not been since the ninth of the season, and there was a light on inside that did not get turned off, because Colm slept in that room four nights in seven and had since the eighth of the season.

He stood in the doorway. He did not go in. He put his hand flat on the frame and he waited, and he waited a long time, and he was in no hurry because he had learned in June what a frightened man costs when you make him wait.

"Mr Deveraux."

Nothing.

Then a chair went back eight inches on a lino that had a hard spot in front of the shelving, and Colm Deveraux stood in the middle of his own supplies in the light of a desk lamp with a bad right shoulder held up against his chest, and he did not come forward and he did not ask Lin Hao in.

"You are not on this floor till Thursday," Colm said. "You have a band now and you clean second floor and I have a key that is not a key to anything anybody wants. Say it fast."

"I am not here about the key."

"Then say what you are here about faster." The bad shoulder came up against his collar. "There is a woman came round two days ago, not a runner, not grey either, a dark coat, and she asked me one question at the counter of my own floor, and she had no paper at all. Do you know what she asked me?"

Lin Hao stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame and felt the whole building go quiet around him in a way that had nothing to do with the fact that nobody was speaking.

"She asked me," said Colm Deveraux, "whether anybody had asked me for names."

The corridor at the end of the third floor was the length of nine washrooms and had a window at the far end with the Yard behind it, and for about four seconds the only sound in it was a door closing somewhere below.

"Names," Lin Hao said.

"Names. Plate numbers, streets, dates. She said it in an ordinary voice, the way you would ask a man for the date of the next delivery." His hand went up to his collar and came down. "And I said no. I said this floor does not keep a list and that goes for my own staff, which is true, and she wrote it down, and she said that was very useful, and then she asked me the second thing."

"What second thing."

"She asked me whether I had noticed anything odd about the arithmetic." Colm's voice did not go anywhere. "And I told her I had. And I have not told anybody in four months, not the deputy, not Runa, not you, and I have not told anybody because of the night I did not sleep and the stair I did not take and the nine hundredweight landing that came down in four seconds with my hand on it."

Lin Hao did not say a word.

"And you knew," said Colm Deveraux, "about the door. You were on that stair. I heard you on it at eleven and you were not on it and I have been telling myself since that you were under it, and I have been telling myself that for four months, and a woman from an office has now stood at the counter of my own floor and told me the arithmetic on my plate is odd."

He shut the door. It went to about an inch and then he put his boot under it and held it there from the floor.

"I did not give her a name," he said, through the gap. "I did not give her a name and there was nobody to give her because there is nobody in this building who I would send to a room in that part of the Exchange, and I have thought about that sentence for four hours and I am saying it to you and not to her, and that is the only thing I have got and I am aware that it is a very small thing."

"Mr Deveraux—"

"Do not." The boot pressed harder and the door came another inch and there was light down the gap and the shape of a man behind it.

"You will want to tell me you are sorry and I do not want to be told that by you tonight and I will tell you why. If I had a key of my own tonight I would give it up inside a minute and we both know it, and if you had come up here in June with a key we would have had this conversation in June instead and everything after it would have gone differently, and the reason it is happening now instead is that you are six and I am frightened and a woman in a dark coat asked me one question at the counter of my own floor."

He took the boot off. The door shut and went to its latch, which was how it had been since the ninth of the season.

Lin Hao stood at a shut door on the third floor for some time.

Then he went down and cleaned the second floor again, all of it, properly, from the far end back to the stair, because his hands needed to work and there was nothing else on offer.

The Vestry arcade was at half past three and full of the sound it is always full of.

Ola had the sheet with the ruling on it out on the landing rail and was reading it to people in instalments, slowly, stumbling on the long words, and Nadia corrected her without malice, and about twenty people were standing about in the way of anybody who wanted to use the arcade, and the way the Vestry always listened.

Nadia Ferrous had her car held at the platform with her hand on the brass even though she was four minutes early and could have let it go round twice.

"You are not going to be at the terminus tonight," she said.

"No."

"Because you have a thing now, and the thing has a date on it, and every time you have come to the terminus you have come at midnight and you have come to stand where I can see you and you have stood there until I went up one floor." She said it in the voice she used for announcements.

"I am not going to be spent to fix that. You hear me? Not tonight and not the next time either. If you put a minute of yourself through my hand on a night when you have to be somewhere across the city, I will know what it was for and I will not forgive it, and I do not say that for nothing. I am the one thing in this quarter that goes up and I am not going to be the thing that pays for your calendar."

"Nadia—"

"You did not ask." She rang the bell and let the car go. "That is the whole speech. You did not ask, and I have noticed that you have not asked for eleven weeks, and I am putting it on the record that I noticed and that you were not asked and that I chose."

The doors closed. She took it up one floor, which was as far as she went, and went out of sight, and the arcade went back to its noise.

He stood at the rail with Ola's sheet on it and the finding number underlined twice in pencil.

A woman in a dark coat with no paper at all had walked into his building and asked Colm Deveraux for names, and had then said that was very useful, and had asked him a second question about arithmetic, and had got a second answer he had been holding for four months because of a stair at eleven o'clock on a night in June.

He had refused sixty-one names in a room in the Exchange two days ago and thought it was the safe end of it. He had thought it was a line he was drawing between himself and the people the office could reach.

And a frightened supply supervisor with a bad shoulder and a key to nothing had walked in on the same day she did and given the office two answers for nothing at all, in an ordinary voice, at the counter of his own floor, because a woman had said that was very useful.

The refusal had cost him a calendar. It had cost him nothing else. It had protected nobody.

There was a phrase going round the Vestry that the Office asked, and it was not the phrase anyone had thought of, and it was not asked in a sub-level or on a Thursday in a queue. It was asked in corridors, at counters, in shops and canteens and stairwells, by women in dark coats with no paper at all, to people who were frightened and had something to be frightened with.

Ninety-one names. Nine turned. One reading himself.

He had refused to hand over the list. The list was going to be assembled anyway, out of fright, by other people's mouths, in rooms he would never be in, and the only difference between the two versions would be that his version would have been accurate.

Ola Bright's sheet was on the landing rail with a question on it in round handwriting that he had assumed was about arithmetic.

There was going to be a woman coming round the Vestry with a soft voice and a pen, and she was not going to have to break anything, because he had broken the only thing that could have stopped her, in a room, in the only way he knew how to refuse anything.

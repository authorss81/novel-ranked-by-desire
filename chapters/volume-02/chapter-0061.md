# The Ledger Quayle Keeps

The corridor board in the Fourth Branch had carried one line for ten weeks, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth it carried two, and the second one was in the clerk's hand and had his name at the end of it.

The first line was a date. The Review date, put up on the sixteenth and not taken down, the way an office leaves a thing on a wall when taking it down would mean writing a sentence.

The second line was a day of the week and a room and a word he had been careful never to say out loud in a corridor.

He read it from the bottom of the stair because the stair gave him the whole board at once, and then he went back up four steps and read it again from close to, and then he went down and cleaned the second floor, all of it, from the far end back, because his hands needed the work and there was nothing else on offer before ten.

The soap wanted cutting. The lino had gone grey at its edges overnight the way it does when the building is empty and the heating is off until six.

"ATTENDANCE, THURSDAY, INVENTORY ROOM — FROM THE THIRD — LIN HAO, ATTENDANT."

It had been typed. Not written. Somebody in an office who had a machine had sat down and typed a man's job into a corridor board of a branch office, and the word attendant was not a courtesy, it was the description on his employment card, which he had quoted back at a clerk on the eighteenth of the month in a room two floors under the Exchange.

Somebody in that building had read his employment card.

Faber was at the plate office at half past seven and had the queue out into the corridor, ordinary, and the strip from the morning before was still pinned to the end of the counter where it had been left, because strips do not get taken down.

"Read it to me," Lin Hao said.

"I am not the board." Faber did not look up. He had the machine going and four people waiting and a man in front of him who had asked him to be a noticeboard.

"You read the third line out to thirty people ten days ago. You will read this one too, and you will do it in the same voice, and I would rather you did it now while there are forty people here than on the next day, when there are four."

Faber stopped the queue.

"Attention," he said, to the rail. "Second line on the board. Attendance, Thursdays, inventory room, from the third of next month, Lin Hao, attendant." He put the card down. "That is an office instruction posted on a public board, and I have read it because it is on the wall I stand behind, and I am not going to say anything else about it, and neither is anybody else in this corridor."

"Was that necessary," said a man with a Delta badge two places down.

"No," said Faber. "It was fast."

The runner came in at ten past nine with the door propped and the grey wool coat and no badge, and she did not come through the queue this time either. She put the square on the counter, squared to the edge, and stepped back two paces.

Lin Hao turned it over before she had finished stepping back.

The first one, on the sixteenth, had been buff and unsealed on the outside, and the counter clerk had told him in the corridor that an unstamped notice is not an instrument, which meant it bound one man and left everybody else free.

This one had a seal on it. Small, dull, the office's own, pressed into the corner where a signature would go if there were a signature space, and there was not one.

"They have learned something," he said.

"They have learned that you came in person twice," said the runner. "I do not know what they learned it from and I am not going to guess at it on a counter."

He read it, because the corridor had gone quiet in the way a corridor goes quiet when the man at the socket is holding government paper.

ATTENDANCE STANDING, EVERY SEVEN DAYS, COMMENCING THE THIRD.
BRING THE PLATE.
THE INTERVAL OF NINE DAYS IS DISCHARGED.

And under those, four ruled lines with a printed heading and a space under each one, which was the part nobody had a box for, because a box can be left empty but four ruled lines are a thing somebody sat down and asked for.

DAYS ATTENDED. PERSONS SPOKEN WITH. ADDRESSES.

Three, not four. The third was the address.

"There is no date on it," he said.

"There is a day," said the runner. "The day is the third and then the tenth and then the seventeenth and then the twenty-fourth. It runs to the end of the sheet and there is more sheet than that."

"Nine days was the deal."

"Nine days was a desk. There is a woman at the head of this office who does not accept a desk's arithmetic, and I am a woman who carries paper and I am telling you the paper says what it says."

"Will you tell me who wants the addresses."

"I will tell you that it is not me and that it is not the man at the desk and that if I were you I would understand what a third ruled line is for, and I would not need a runner to explain it."

She took the square back and folded it once and put it inside her coat, and went out into the street, and a man in the queue said something to a man in the queue and neither of them said it loudly.

Lin Hao stood at the socket with the plate in his hand and the morning strip still pinned to the end of the counter.

The third line was the one about the addresses.

He had spent four years building a life on the distinction between a face and a name, and there was a third thing underneath both of them that he had never once had to think about, because in four years it had never once been asked of him, and there was no line in any of his working life where the question had come up.

The register at the head of the Office kept addresses and no names. Thirty-one rows in a ruled hand, plate numbers, streets where the accounts were, and not one face on it.

His own ledger kept faces and no addresses.

Somebody upstairs had put the two together on a form with three ruled lines, and the third line was the join, and it was the only join in Ostara that made a person out of the pair.

He had refused sixty-one names in a room on the eighteenth and had thought the refusal was a wall.

It was a wall with a door in it, and the door was not made of names, and he had been standing in front of the wrong panel for ten days.

The rest of the morning he cleaned six floors.

At the plate office at noon Faber read him the machine's opinion of the day, which he did not have to be asked to do, and the figure was where the figure had been for a fortnight, and the band was on the ruling, and the strip went on the spike with the others, and there were now nine of them on the spike and every one of them carried the third line.

"You know what you are looking at," Lin Hao said.

"I am looking at nine strips of a man being measured in his own corridor." Faber pulled one off the spike and held it up. "This office has had a plate socket for twenty-nine years and in twenty-nine years it has never printed a third line on anybody. It has printed them nine times on one man in three weeks and I have been the man who reads them out, and I have got to the point where I do not need to look at the paper."

"Then say the thing you actually think."

"I think the machine would have printed the third line on anybody whose band was sitting on a decision," said Faber. "There have not been any others. I have been here twenty-nine years. That is the whole of my opinion and it is not worth writing down, and I would like to go back to a queue."

He went to the Exchange at forty-one minutes rather than eleven, on the long road, and he did not look at the Board in the square, and he noticed himself not looking at it four times in nine streets.

The register corridor in the Classification Wing had a bench, a fire that had not been lit, and a wired light over the second door, and Anneke Roos came out of the register room at ten past four with her coat already on one arm, which she did not do until she was going.

"You cannot see the book," she said, before he had got to the door.

"I have not asked to see the book."

"You have the face of a man who has been to a counter this morning and has come up here afterwards to work out what the counter cost him." She pulled the register room door to behind her. "Ask me a question I can answer and go away, because I go at five and there is a runner on the stair who thinks I am still in there."

"Four books," Lin Hao said. "You said the other day that the register is the big one. How many books does that office keep."

Anneke Roos stopped, and looked at him, and then leaned against the door frame, which was the single unprofessional thing she had ever done in front of him.

"Who told you four."

"Nobody. I am counting. There is the register, which is thirty-one rows and nine years old. There is the attendance book, which is Drant's. There is whatever the Rule man at the brass counter keeps. And there is one more, because a form describes a thing that has already been described, and there is nothing on your form yet."

"That is not right."

"Then tell me the part of it that is."

She looked up the corridor at the light over the second door.

"There is a fourth," she said. "It came out of a drawer in the middle of the season, before you came to the desk the first time, and it is not the register and it does not have thirty-one rows in it. It has got a page for every entry that has ever come to this floor and could not be put in the register, and the register has one line about those and the fourth book has the line itself."

"How long has it got my page."

"Since the eighth."

"What is on it."

"Twenty days of dates." She said it with her coat over her arm and her eyes on the stair. "Attendances, plate, ruling, figure, margin. All of it in the same order every time, in the same five headings, which is what a book does when the person writing it has decided there is going to be a sixth heading and has not decided what it is."

"And when she finds out what it is."

"Then the first twenty days get copied onto the sixth heading and nothing before it is lost, and it is the only book in this building that works that way, and I have watched a man do that twice and both times he was wrong about the sixth heading and right about the arithmetic." She looked at him.

"I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go, and I am saying it because you have a face on, and because not one person in this building is going to say it to you, and because it is the only thing in this corridor that is mine and not the office's."

"Say it."

"She does not read it. She writes it." Anneke Roos said it as flatly as she said headings. "Every clerk in this building reads what goes up to her. She has been getting it for twenty days and I have watched her read it twice, both times in about four minutes, and both times she put it down and asked for the next one. She is not trying to find out what you are from those pages."

"Then what is she doing with them."

"Keeping them," said Anneke Roos. "There is a difference and it is the only difference I have got and I have had twenty days to look for it. And now go away, because I have said four things to a man at a desk and my supervisor signs my attendance and not hers."

She went down the stair and he stood in the corridor with a fire that had not been lit and a light over a door and the whole of it working exactly the way it was designed to work, which is to say slowly, patiently, without anybody hunting anybody, on a woman keeping a book.

He went down the long road and past the square.

At nine o'clock the branch emptied in four minutes and Ola Bright was still on the third-floor landing with a page she had copied out twice, because she had checked it against the branch copy twice, and she had it propped on the rail.

"The board," she said. "Lin Hao. The board."

"I saw it."

"You saw it." She put the pencil down.

"Since the start of the season you have not once told me not to write things on walls. I did not put that on the board."

"Whoever did it did it because there is a machine somewhere with your name typed into it. And a typed thing gets copied by people who cannot type, and then it gets read out by people who cannot help it, and by the fourth it will be in the canteen."

"You are sure of that."

"Not one finger of it. And you are sure of it too, and the fact that you are sure of it is why we are both standing in this corridor looking at a wall instead of talking about a canteen."

"No. Somebody printed it and somebody carried it and a clerk in your own corridor pinned it up." She was not looking at him. "And here is the part that has been sitting on me all morning. The board is a Bureau board. The board is where your building tells you what has happened in your building.

It has got the Review date on it and now it has got your Thursdays on it, and both of those are things that happened to you, and your building has agreed to publish both of them, and that is what a building does when it is given a line to print."

He stood in the corridor of the Fourth Branch office of the Bureau of Merit and looked at two lines of typing on a board that had held ninety-one names in his first week, and the second line had his job on it.

The woman had not said anything he could not work out for himself.

That was worse, in the way that a refusal is worse than an argument. She had simply put a piece of paper back into his hands and walked off down the stairs, and there was no room in the world where he could put it down.

On the twenty-eighth he learned three things, and the third one was the only one he would still be holding in a month.

The interval was seven days and not nine, which was two days back and had cost him nothing at all yet.

Somebody upstairs was keeping a book with his name on a page and no heading for the thing they had not worked out.

And the Office had stopped asking him to describe himself and started asking him to describe where he had been, and the place where that question was going to be answered was a corridor he had to walk through five days a week to get his wages.

He got his wages on the last of the month.

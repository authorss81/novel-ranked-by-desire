# The Fourth Box

The fourth of the week began with a runner at the door who took his name and wrote it down before she took him up, and the runner was not the one from the intake corridor and did not look up when she said it.

"Seventh entry of the standing is being kept," she said, from the sheet. "You are the seventh this morning and the eighth is behind you on the stair and the ninth is not written yet."

"The ninth is a Field officer," Lin Hao said.

"The ninth is mine," said Runa Sato.

She came up the last flight behind him with her hands behind her back and did not say anything in the corridor, which was the correct thing and cost him something to be grateful for.

The room was the same room. Drant had the minute sheet, and the plate in a cloth bag, and the wall map rolled up along its grain under a rubber band at the far end of the table with somebody's initials on the tie.

"Sit," he said. "There are four things on this morning and I am going to do them in the order they happen and then we will have the sheet, and one of them is going to cost you and I would rather you had it before the other three than after."

"Go on."

"There are two of you."

He put two sheets down side by side on the cloth, squared to the edges.

"The left is your standing. Seven-day interval, commencing the third, plate and ruling, discharge the three ruled lines against a weekly strip. That is the instrument you signed on the third and it is in force and there is a strip on it for every week since."

He put a finger on the other sheet.

"The right is the observation desk. Nine-day interval, first attendance kept on the eighteenth of last month and the second on the twenty-fourth of last month, and three more dates written in a column in front of you on the eighteenth, which are the third, the twelfth and the twenty-first."

"That was superseded."

"It was not. It was discharged, which is a different thing and the difference is going to cost you about nine days." Drant squared the two sheets so their edges matched. "A standing supersedes a schedule. A schedule does not supersede a standing.

I wrote the third, the twelfth and the twenty-first in that column on the eighteenth and I signed it, and when the interval changed nobody upstairs told me to cancel them, and I am not permitted to cancel my own instruments, and I would not if I could, because a man who has been told there is a line of dates coming should be able to see the line."

"So how many."

"Three after this one. The twenty-first on the schedule, and the twenty-fourth and the thirty-first on the standing. And the thirty-first is not a date you have ever been told about, because it is the fifth week of a strip and I wrote it this morning out of a habit, and I am telling you it is there so that you cannot say nobody told you."

Lin Hao looked at the two sheets.

Four days apart, then three, then seven, for two months, because an office had found a cheaper instrument and had not cancelled the expensive one.

"I am not going to complain about it," Drant said. "I want to be exact about why. Because if you complain about it I have to write down that you complained about it, and a man who complains about an interval is a man who has begun to keep his own calendar, and the moment you keep your own calendar this office stops being the thing that owns your time and becomes the thing you visit."

Runa Sato said, from the door: "I will say one thing and then I will sign it."

"Say it."

"Two attendances in eight days is a document about a man in a calendar, and a man in a calendar is a thing the Field can requisition, because the Field does not requisition people, it requisitions schedules." She did not come into the room. "I have spent five months watching this office run on the assumption that nobody keeps an independent record of its own subject.

That assumption is correct and it has just been tested by two sheets that do not agree and a man who has been told about both."

"I am not keeping one."

"You are keeping one," said Runa Sato, "in your coat, with three dates written on the back of your hand in pencil, and you washed it off twice."

Drant made a small noise that was not quite a laugh and put his hand flat on the right-hand sheet.

"Right," he said. "Second thing. I am going to tell you something I am not supposed to tell you, and I am telling you because Ruta Vesely is going to send that map up on the twentieth and once it goes up I will never be able to stand in this room and say it again."

The runner opened the door.

"Three."

She came in and put three buff squares on the table in a row, and squared them, and left.

They were the same paper as his. Unstamped. Three boxes marked on each and a fourth empty, and a line at the top with a word in it.

ATTENDANCE — PERSON NAMED ON AN ANALYST'S SHEET.

Nell Ardery. Garrick Neve. And a space for a name with a yard gate written on it instead of an address, because the checker had never given anybody one.

Each of them had a date on it. The nineteenth.

"That is the day after tomorrow," said Lin Hao.

"That is the day after tomorrow." Drant put his hand flat on the empty boxes. "I did not put them in that envelope.

I was not asked to and I do not know who did, and I have been sitting on this morning for twenty minutes deciding whether to put them on that table, and I have decided to put them on that table, and I want you to understand that in nineteen years I have taken three things out of that envelope before and I have put one of them back."

"What were the other two."

"Other people's." He did not look at her. "Go on, before I find a reason not to finish."

"The Office does not attend a person. It attends a register entry, and it attends a subject's standing, and it has never in nine years attended anybody off an analyst's sheet, because an analyst's sheet is not a procedure and a procedure is what this office is made of." He put both hands flat on the cloth.

"Three episodes in one room, in a ring, in a second colour, with a third line under it in the third colour that I have not read and will not read and have told Ruta Vesely I will not read."

"She said she would not send the third line without your initials."

"She said it would go up in her hand and it will, and I will read it when it arrives, because I am a clerk and it will be on a sheet that came to my desk." He squared the three squares.

"And when that sheet arrives, this office has a map with three names on it, and it will have to do something about three names, and doing something about three names is what this office does, and so somebody upstairs found the one procedure in the building that has never been used, which is attendance for a person named on an analyst's sheet, and they have used it, and they have used it on the three of them, and they have put it in for the nineteenth."

"Whose decision."

"Not mine." Drant said it flatly. "And I want to be careful here, because I am about to describe a room to you and I have described rooms to you before and one of those descriptions is the reason you said no on the eighteenth. That was mine. This one is not mine and I have had my name kept out of it deliberately and I am telling you that I have had my name kept out of it."

He picked up the three squares and squared them together and then put them down on the table with a kind of excessive care.

"The rules are these and they are four and they are short. One: the person attends. Two: the person is asked whether an episode is known to them and whether they have reported it, and the answer is written down and is whatever it is. Three: an episode reported by a person and not traced to a source is closed as unaccounted, and an unaccounted episode is closed by that person's own record and nothing else."

"And four."

"Four: an episode traced to a source is closed as accounted, and accounted does not require the source to be in the room." He put his finger on the fourth box. "Which is why there is a fourth box on those squares and it is empty, and it is empty because a source cannot be traced by this office without a source, and the only source in Ostara for three episodes in one room is the man sitting in that chair."

Lin Hao looked at the three squares for a while.

"So the fourth box is mine."

"The fourth box is an acknowledgement, and it is the first thing this office has ever asked anybody for in that form, and it has never been used, and it exists at all because there was a map." He took his hand off it. "I am going to read you the wording, because you have earned the wording and because I am not going to hand you a document to sign that you have not read in a room."

"Read it."

Drant took a clean sheet and wrote on it, and read it back in the flat clerk's voice.

"Attendee acknowledges that the three episodes recorded at the addresses attached to the analyst's sheet of the seventh were caused at his hand, and that they are his cost and are not evidence against the persons in whom they occurred."

He put the pen down.

"That is the whole of it. It is one sentence. It grants them nothing and it does not accuse them of anything, and it has never been used, and I have never seen it."

The room had a lamp on it and a rolled map with initials on the tie and three buff squares on a cloth.

"You understand what it is," Lin Hao said.

"I understand what it is and I am going to tell you what it is and then I am going to put the pen down and not pick it up again, and you are not going to get a speech out of me after this, because you have had three from me and I am not a man who has speeches."

"Say it."

"It is what your brother did."

Nobody said anything.

"Not the words. The other thing. He stood in a room and said out loud that the number was wrong, and a thing that is said out loud in a room is an instrument, and an institution that is handed an instrument has to do something with it." Drant did not raise his voice.

"You have spent a month refusing to describe yourself so that nothing you say can be used.

And if you sign that sentence then the first document in this building that says a man did a thing to three people on purpose is in your handwriting, in my tray, and it will be read by a woman at the head of this office, and it will be read by a Council bench in about two years, and it cannot be unsaid, and no office in this city can classify it, because it is not a score and not a figure and not a conduct finding.

It is an admission with no box, and the whole trouble you are in started with a shape that had no box."

"And if I do not sign it."

"Then on the day after tomorrow three people come to this desk and they are asked what they felt, in this room, in turn, and they are asked it by a man with a pen, and they are not told that anybody else was in the room." He said it without relish. "And I have watched that happen. Not here. In another district.

And what happens is that a woman who has had four of them in five days gets asked a fourth time about the fourth one, and there is no answer to give, and there has never been an answer to give, because there is nothing to explain it with, and so the answer she gives is a bad one, and the bad one is written down, and the bad one is the only version of that afternoon that exists in the city."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"Nell Ardery has four in five days and has never once asked you for anything. Mr Neve wrote his own up and put his name on it and said it cost him.

The man at the yard has written out his own attendance for this week and every week he has been on that gate on the back of a chart, because a man who has been standing where somebody stood is a man who should have to account for it.

All three of them have already done the thing you are being asked to do for them, and none of the three of them knows the other two are in this envelope."

Lin Hao picked up the pen.

"Write it."

Drant wrote it in his own hand as a heading and turned the sheet round, and Lin Hao signed beneath it, and the signature went on the table in a room with a cloth on it at half past ten in the morning.

"That is the first thing you have given this office that is not a plate," said Drant. "And I want you to have it said out loud in a room by the man who received it, because in four years somebody will read that tray and there will be no way on earth to know what it cost you."

He squared the sheets and put the three squares back in the envelope and the envelope inside the tray on the left.

"Attendance kept," he said. "Third of the standing, and no date of yours on the schedule at all, and your signature on an instrument that has never been used in nineteen years." He capped the pen. "The three of them are on the nineteenth and you are not, because you are not to be in the room, because you are the source and a source in the room is a coach, and I will not have that done to them."

"That is a rule you invented."

"That is a rule I am writing down in my own minutes on the twentieth because I have never had one, and if somebody upstairs objects to it then somebody upstairs can come down here and explain it to a man who cleans washrooms."

Runa Sato signed at the door, which she had not done at the previous two, and in the corridor she said the only sentence she said in an hour.

"You have made yourself a thing that has to be decided again. There is no version of that signature where the last person in Ostara to describe you is a clerk with a rubber stamp."

She went up the stairs at a steady pace and Lin Hao stood in a corridor two floors above a public counter where, on the seventeenth of the month, a man with a satchel and no badge had logged a discharge as an attendance kept and given him one for free.

That had cost the office eleven minutes and a piece of rubber.

This had cost him three names in a file that would not shut, and a sentence in his own handwriting that said he had done a thing to people on purpose, and the only part of it he was going to be able to give back was four days and a strip of plate paper.

He walked the long road to the branch at forty-one minutes, past the square, without looking at the Board.

And on the two flights of stone between the public counter and the corridor desk he worked out where all of it had come from.

The map goes up on the twentieth with Ruta Vesely's initials on the tie. A map with three names on it will be read by a woman at the head of that office inside three days, and that woman has been keeping a book of him since the eighth of last month, and wrote a sixth heading on it in her own hand on the twentieth.

Three names in a file. A second question behind them.

And a woman in a dark coat with no paper at all, who had walked into his building and asked a frightened man with a bad shoulder whether anybody had ever asked him for names, and who had said that was very useful, and who was at this moment being asked the same question by the same office that had just sent three buff squares to a landing in Cordwain.

# The Holding Ward

The Vestry had four rooms at the back that the ward called the quiet rooms and nobody else called anything, and Tessa Okonkwo opened two of them on the twenty-fifth of the month with her own key and walked him in without a word.

The first had a bed and a chair and a window. The second had two beds and no window and the light came off the corridor through the door and lay in a strip on the floor.

"That one," she said. "Come and stand where I am standing."

The woman in the second bed was sitting up with her feet on the floor and her shoes beside her, laced, ready to go somewhere.

"Her name is Ilse Korvin and she has been in that bed since the fifteenth of the month and she is going to be in it until the standing closes, and the standing closes when she turns up, and she is going to turn up on the twenty-ninth." Tessa did not raise her voice. "Mrs Korvin, this is the man I have told you about. You have met him twice."

"I have," said the woman.

"He is the reason I have a rack and I am not going to pretend in front of him that I do not think you know that."

"I know what the rack is." Ilse Korvin looked at Lin Hao without much in her face. "You are the one they are writing forms about."

"I am."

"Right." She put her hands on the rail and moved her feet back under the blanket, and it was not reluctance and it was not theatre, it was a woman putting her feet away because it was getting cold. "You will want to know why I am here and it is not interesting.

I had a berth on the ferry I paid for in March and I did not go, because my sister's boy was ill and then he was better and then it was the wrong day to go and then the berth had gone. And there is a standing open against me for not using a thing I paid for, and I have not been able to go and stand in a room four streets away and say I did not go."

"Would it help," Lin Hao said, "if somebody came with you."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because I would do it and then I would go home and I would not have gone." She said it evenly. "That is what this is. I am not here because I cannot get to the Exchange. I can get to the Exchange.

I have been up once, on the seventeenth, and I stood in the street for an hour and a half and I came back on the tram and I am here because a date is open and a date does not close itself and while it is open there is nowhere on paper for me to be discharged to."

Tessa Okonkwo stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it for ten days," she said. "There are four of these rooms and there have been two of them full since the spring and I have never once called this a ward, because a ward is a Bureau word and this is not a ward and nothing in this building is holding her. It is that I cannot stop paying for a bed that a date is sitting on."

"So it is holding her."

"It is holding her," she said, "and the difference is not in her and it is not in me, it is in a clerk's book in four streets, and the clerk has done nothing wrong, and if you stand in this doorway and look at that bed for long enough you will start wanting to go and be angry at somebody, and I have watched four men do it and two women, and none of them has ever been angry at anybody useful."

She shut the door on the room with Ilse Korvin in it and walked him back down a corridor past the cold store and into her own, which was one lamp on a cord, a rack of boards against a wall, a table with a cloth on it and a window that gave onto a yard.

"Sit down. And before you sit down I am going to say a date, and the date is today, and then I am going to say a thing about it that I have been carrying since the twenty-second, and you are going to want to go and find out what it means before you have heard the rest of it, and I am telling you not to."

She took her coat off.

"The twenty-fifth is the day the instrument can read you. It has been able to since about the twenty-first and it has been giving me a figure I have not looked at, and I have had it in a drawer in this room since the twenty-second, and I have not opened the drawer, and I am not going to open it today."

"Twenty-ninth."

"Twenty-ninth. Ask me for it on the twenty-ninth and I will give it to you." She hung her coat on the back of the door. "And now the other thing, which is why I have asked you to come at four in the afternoon on a day when there is no attendance and no strip and no form with my name anywhere on it."

"I have been waiting a month for a day like that."

"I know." She rolled her left sleeve to the elbow without unpinning it. "There is not one. That is the thing I am about to tell you and I have been telling it to myself in the wrong order for two months.

Sit down and take my arm."

He sat down and took her arm.

"Here," she said. "Wrist to the elbow, and you have seen it since June. Grey-black stippling from the wrist to mid-forearm, and it is eleven years old and it does not move, and past the joint it is a different colour, and pale blue is not a bruise and it is not a burn, and there is nothing in the medical books about it because nothing in the medical books has ever needed it." She said it as flatly as she said everything. "That is the arm.

Now the other one."

She gave him the other one.

The other one was worse past the shoulder and she showed him where, and then she made him stand up and she turned round and pulled the back of her neckline down over one shoulder blade and said, "There.

That is the fourth and fifth rib on the left, and there is a hand on it the size of a man's, and it is from the spring, and I do not know whose it was and I have never asked, because the rule is that you do not ask a person what they were carrying when you are the thing that took it."

She turned round again.

"Now you have seen the whole of it, and that is not a complaint and it is not a bid, and I want both of those understood before anything else happens in this room.

Eleven years. Two hundred and fifty-eight charts. Every one of them is a place where somebody else's damage went to stand in my frame while the body in front of me decided whether to live. And there is no number on that wall that is mine, and there has never been one, and I have been the woman who holds things and never the woman who is held."

"Sit down," she said. "No. Stand up. I have been working out how to say this for seven weeks and I have got it and I am going to say it in order.

One: you tell me to stop, in any words you like, including one word, and I stop inside the second and I do not ask what it was.

Two: if I say something is wrong, I am right, and you do not argue with me about it, and I will say it plainly and you will not have to guess.

Three: I am not going to ask you for anything tonight. Not your plate, not your file, not a face, and not a number, and if you start handing me those to be polite I will stop, because that is a different afternoon and it is a worse one.

Four, and it is the true one and I am not going to be coy about it in a room where I have just shown you my ribs: I have wanted this since the sixth of last month and I have wanted it in a way I am not going to describe to you now, because describing it will make it smaller, and I am going to describe it with my hands instead for the next hour and you are going to be able to tell the difference."

"Say yours."

"You stop when I say. If you say a thing is wrong, I believe you and I do not argue. If you want me somewhere, you ask me first and I will tell you no if it is no."

"And the fourth."

"I do not know what I am doing," said Lin Hao. "I have not known since the twenty-third and I am going to be worse at this than I was in September."

"Good." She put her hand flat on his chest and pushed him back onto the table, not hard, the way you move a thing you have decided where it goes. "That is four and it was the true one again and it is twice in a row, and I have never once been glad to hear it before and I am now, and I want that on the record in my own mouth before we start."

She took her sleeves down and turned the lamp so its cord was out of the way, and then she came and stood in front of him with her arms loose at her sides.

"Right," she said. "Say what you can see."

"The rack is behind me and the window is behind you and your arms are the thing I can see."

"That is a counter and not an answer and I will take it." She took his left hand and put it flat on her left forearm, low, over the stippling. "This is eleven years and it does not move. It is not going to move tonight either and I am not going to pretend it might.

Put your thumb where I put it and tell me what you feel, because I have never once had anybody tell me what they feel and I have wanted to be told for a very long time and I have not wanted it from a doctor."

He put his thumb where she put it.

"Cord," he said. "Under the skin. It is not bone, it is a rope."

"That is what is left of a hand." She held his hand there. "Say the next one."

She took his other hand and put it above her elbow, on the outside, where the second thing was.

"Flat," she said. "That one is flat and it has been flat since the spring and I cannot lift that arm above my head and I have stopped trying and I would have told you about it in October if you had asked me in October, and you did not ask me in October because you did not ask me anything until the twenty-second."

"I am asking you now."

"You are asking me with your hands, which is better." She sat down on the edge of the table beside his hip and turned her face away from him and pulled the neckline down again over the shoulder blade, and she did it slowly and she knew exactly what she was doing and she did not pretend otherwise. "There. The handprint.

Fourth and fifth rib, left side, somebody's whole hand, and it was a man in the spring and he was going for four days and I had it for nine and I do not know what it was and it is not going away in eleven months and I have begun to think it is load-bearing."

Lin Hao put his hand on it.

The skin was hot under his palm, not fevered, the heat of a thing that has been there a long time and has become part of the architecture.

"That is a hand," she said. "Say it."

"That is a hand."

"Five: there is nothing wrong with me and nothing in this room is going to be wrong with me, and I am not fragile and I am not careful, and I have held two hundred and fifty-eight bodies past the point they should have stopped and I have not lost one, and if you are frightened of what your hands are I would rather you knew tonight than in a corridor in six weeks."

"Say yours again. You said four."

"I said four and I meant five, and you are keeping count, and that is the most attractive thing about you and also the most expensive." She got up off the table and put her hand on the back of his neck.

"Now sit properly and stop being polite, because being polite in this room is going to get you nothing at all and I have a rack of twenty-seven and four hours and a thing I have been carrying since the sixth of last month, and I am extremely strong and I have not been held by anybody since I was a student, and you are the only man in this city who can do it, and I am not going to spend another month not asking you."

She kissed him the way she did everything, which was without any doubt about it at all, and he stopped being polite, and she made a small satisfied sound against his mouth and pushed him back and took his shirt off him over his head in one movement, the way a woman takes a bandage off, and then she stood there and looked at him the way she had looked at the rack.

"Eleven years," she said. "And this is the first week since March that I have not got a cold in my hands when I wake up, and I have not told a living soul, and I have been extremely rude to everybody in this building for twelve days and they have all deserved it."

"Six days."

"You have not spent in twelve days and it has gone out of my hands and I want to say that out loud to a man in this room before I take his clothes off him properly, because in eleven years that has happened once and it is the only clinical result I have ever had that I did not have to put on a chart."

She got him off the table and onto the bed that was not in the room, the one in her own quarters, which was small and had a lamp on a cord and twenty-seven boards in a column, and she took her clothes off standing up without hurrying and without one single moment of a woman's awkwardness.

She was pale and heavy-hipped and marked in four places and she watched him look at all four of them.

"Left," she said. "That is the fourth. That is the fifth. And that is a needle from the ninth of this month and that one will go, and I am telling you it will go so that you do not spend the whole night on it like a man apologising to a building."

Then she came down onto him and put her weight on his chest the way Sun Meiling had put hers on him on a bad bench three weeks before, except that Sun Meiling had asked him first and had said out loud that she wanted it, and Tessa Okonkwo did not ask and did not say it, because she had already said it in item four and she was not a woman who says a thing twice.

"Ask me for something," she said, about a minute later. "Not a plate. Not a name. Something you want in this room, out loud, in words, and do not be decent about it, because I have watched you be decent in front of a room full of people and it has cost this city two hundred and fifty-eight charts."

"I want your hands," he said. "I want them on me and I want to be able to feel where they are the whole time and I do not want to be let go of."

"Right," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That is the first thing you have ever said to me that was about you and I am going to take you at your word for the next hour and I will not be gentle and I will not be kind and if I hurt you I will tell you at the time and not afterwards."

She kept her word on all three.

Nothing was being taken out of him and nothing was being read off him, and twice she stopped entirely with her hand flat on his chest and made him say that he was still here. Once he said he did not know what he was doing and she said, "Then stop guessing and tell me where your hands are," and he told her, and she took them there.

Afterwards she lay with her head on his chest and counted his breaths for about a minute and then stopped counting them, which was the first thing all evening she had done that was not clinical.

"Nine minutes," she said. "That is the longest anybody has taken to do that. I am putting it on a chart and I am not putting your name on it."

"You have said you do not want anything from me."

"I have said I am not going to ask you for anything tonight, and it is half past six, and you are a man who takes a thing like that literally when you are tired." She got up, filled the jug, and washed him herself the way she had done it in the spring in a cold store, unhurried, thorough, entirely unembarrassed. "Put your shirt on. We have got about forty minutes and I am going to use them and I am going to be unkind about it."

She pulled the drawer out from under the table. It was not the drawer with the figure in it. It was a shallow one and it had a book in it, and she put the book on the table and squared it.

"Right. The part that is not tonight." She turned it round. It was a day book, cloth, columns ruled by hand, a year of dates down the left and a line across each one. "This is not the rack. The rack is what I hold.

This is what I am owed, and I have never once had a line in it, and I have been putting my own name in it for eleven years on the days I could not carry something and I have never charged anybody for it and I never will."

"Tessa—"

"Monthly." She put her finger on an empty line. "Standing. Your name, the date, and what it is, and nothing else and no number, because I do not want the office's number and I do not want mine and I want the day written down because a day is the only thing in this book that cannot be argued with afterwards.

And here is what it is for, and this is the part I would have put in a letter if I had trusted a letter. It is for the month my rack runs out. That is the whole of it.

There is going to be a month, and I am going to be the woman who held past the end of what she had, and when that month comes I am going to be asked by somebody in an office what I was holding and for whom, and I am going to have an answer that is a name and a date instead of a sentence I made up at two in the morning."

"That is a debt."

"That is a debt," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and it is the first debt anybody in this house has ever put in front of me that I did not have to ask for, and I am not going to be gracious about it, and I want you to be frightened of it, because a monthly thing that is written down is a monthly thing that gets called in and I intend to call it in whether you can pay it or not."

"I will."

"I know you will. That is not why I am telling you." She shut the book and pushed the drawer. "You have given a strip a week to a building four streets away since the third of the month and you have not given this house a single thing, and a household that gets nothing is a household that is standing outside a thing, and I have been outside one for eleven years and I have watched what it costs and it is not money."

She looked at the rack.

"So put your name in it," she said. "And do not put a number in it, and do not put a word in it that you would have to keep, and come on the first of every month and I will write the date down and you will sit on that table while I do it for four minutes and then we will not talk about it again until the next one."

He took the pencil from the lamp cord and wrote L HAO on the first empty line and put the pencil down.

"Twenty-ninth," he said. "I come on the twenty-ninth and I ask you for the figure and then I come on the first of every month and you write the date down."

"The twenty-ninth and the first is two days after it and I am aware of that and I am not going to move anything."

She put the book away and stood with her back to the drawer.

"Go home by the arcades and not by the Exchange," she said, "and eat something, and do not clean anything tomorrow morning before you have slept, and come on the twenty-ninth and ask me for it in the morning, not in the afternoon, because I want to watch you ask for it."

At the door he stopped and looked back at the rack.

"Twenty-seven on the wall."

"Twenty-seven on the wall," she said, "and it has been twenty-seven for eight days, and the eight days is the best clinical result of my working life, and it is yours.

I am not going to thank you for it, because you have not given me anything yet. You have given that woman in the second room a bed she cannot leave because a date is open, and I do not know what you are going to do about it, and I am not going to ask you until the twenty-ninth."

He went out into the Vestry arcade at half past six with two days of grey still on him and a name in a day book in a room off a corridor with four beds at the end of it, and a woman in one of the beds who had laced her shoes.

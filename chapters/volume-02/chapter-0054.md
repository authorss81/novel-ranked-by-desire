# The Inventory Summons

The Classification Wing's intake corridor is on the far side of the Exchange, and it has a bench, a fire that had not been lit, and a door with a wired light over it, and the light was on at a quarter to nine in the morning on a morning when the only person who came to it was the man it was lit for.

He had walked forty-one minutes to get there and had brought four things, and had checked the inside of his coat twice in the last hundred yards: the plate, the ruling with the nine signatures, the counterfoil from the file deposit with four thousand one hundred and twelve folios printed on it, and nothing else.

No notebook. No second shirt. He had put the copy slip back in the drawer in the room above the chop-house and had left the drawer open, which was not tidiness but a way of being able to say afterwards that he had gone and got it and chosen not to bring it.

The runner was right about one thing. After six a card goes in a tray. At a quarter to nine a person takes it up.

The clerk at the desk was called Silas Drant and he read the buff square and the ruling and the counterfoil in that order, and then he read the counterfoil a second time, which not one person had done since the Reading Office window.

"You petitioned a sealed file," he said.

"On the twentieth."

"Four thousand one hundred and twelve folios." Drant looked up at him for a second longer than the sentence needed. "You clean washrooms."

"That is the description on my employment card, yes."

Drant made a small sound that was almost a laugh and then stopped it, and put the buff square in a tray on his left, and put the ruling in a tray on his right, and left the counterfoil on the desk, face up, where it could be looked at.

"Sit down," he said. "Do not stand up. Standing men read as aggrieved and I have to write down whatever the room looks like, so I would rather it looked like a man answering questions."

There was one chair. He sat in it.

The desk had a machine on it that was not the plate-office kind. It had no key slot and no bell and there was a ruled card already in it, the height and width of a playing card, with thirty-one rows on it and a printed heading at the top that said ACCOUNT, and Drant put the plate in without asking, because attending was attending and the first thing they did at an attendance was the part the machine could do.

The machine ran for eleven seconds. It did not print. It made a sound Lin Hao had not heard before, three clicks and then a scrape, like a key that will not turn, and then it stopped.

"Four thousand," Drant said. "That is the fifth time this week that thing has made that noise at me, and I have been doing this nineteen years, and I am going to tell you what it means, because you are entitled to one true thing from a room you did not choose."

"Tell me."

"It means it read the band and it read the figure and it wanted the third thing and the third thing is the one a machine cannot get out of a plate." Drant wrote something on a slip the size of his hand.

"The band is real. The band is on your plate. The figure is under the floor of the band by three grains and I am going to come back to that, because three grains is the entire margin you have and I want you to know I can see it."

He put the plate down beside the counterfoil and squared them.

"Now we go through it," he said. "I am going to lay out the shapes this office has for a person who is not where his plate says he is. There are five. If you fit one, we stop. It takes about four minutes and you will not enjoy any of it and I am not going to apologise for it, because the reason I am not apologising is that the alternative is the fourth box, and the fourth box is what happens to the ones who do not fit."

He turned the machine's card around and drew five lines on a separate sheet with a nib pen, and wrote them out across the top, and turned the sheet so the man in the chair could read it.

"One. The band is struck from the figure and the figure cannot carry it. This is a machine artefact. It is dealt with at the Exchange in one afternoon and it is not here, because this office does not see Exchange artefacts."

He put a finger on the second line.

"Two. The band is carried by merit and the merit is on the reading and the reading supports it. Yours is not. Yours has no interval on it. There is nothing on yours for a merit to be carried by."

Third line.

"Three. The reading is unsettled and pending, and pending is a state that ends in one of two ways. It ends in a lapse at the quarterly, or it ends in a figure with an interval on it and a band struck off that figure by the Exchange." He looked up.

"Yours ended in neither. It ended in a ruling in a room. That is a third ending and it is not on this sheet and it is not on any sheet in this office, and it is why the machine in front of you just made that noise."

Fourth line.

"Four. The account is held. An account that is held has a holder, and the holder is a person, and a held account is described in one line and filed in one drawer and does not come back to this room." Drant wrote a word and underlined it once.

"This one is the door and it is not shut, and I am telling you it is not shut because in nineteen years I have never once seen a man prefer the drawer. They all think the drawer is where the trouble stops. The drawer is where the trouble stops being visible."

He did not write anything on the fifth line.

He put the nib down and turned the sheet a half inch so it was square on the desk.

"Five," he said, "is what you are, and there is no fifth line because the fifth line is the one the office upstairs has to write."

"So there are four."

"There are four and there is a sheet upstairs with four marks on it and one of them is blank, and the blank one is the whole of your file, and it has been blank since the eighth." Drant put his hands flat on the desk.

"I am a clerk at an attendance desk. I do not decide what you are. I decide what has happened so far that it is visible, and I put that down, and I have put down that the band is held on a ruling, that the figure is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, that the floor of the band is eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, and that the margin is three grains."

He wrote all four, one under the other, in the same nib pen.

"Read that back to me."

Lin Hao read it back to him. Drant checked it against the plate and against the ruling and nodded once, and the machine took the ruling and copied the number and the date into a column he had not seen, and then Drant did something he had clearly been deciding whether to do for four minutes, which was to put his hand flat on the little card the machine had printed nothing on.

"I am going to ask you the last question now," he said, "and it is the only question in this building I am not entitled to ask, and I am asking it because on the other side of it there is a sheet with a blank line on it, and I have never in nineteen years wanted that blank line filled more than I want it filled now, and I am going to be honest with you about that instead of pretending it is procedure."

He turned the card round.

There were eighty-one rows on it and every one of them had something on it: a plate number, a street, a date of an attendance, a state.

"This is what I have been building since Thursday," Drant said. "Eighty-one rows. Eighty-one accounts that came in through this door in nine years that somebody in this city has looked at and could not file, and every one of them is on this card with an address and a date and a state, because that is what we do when we cannot describe a thing. We keep its address. We do not keep its name."

Lin Hao did not move.

"And on the eighteenth of every month, for nine years, somebody has come to this desk and been asked for the names behind the rows." Drant's voice did not change at all and that was the worst of it.

"Not because we do not trust you. Because there is no other way to close a card, and a card that cannot be closed is a card that stays on the desk of the person who opened it, and that person is the head of this office, and she is a woman who has been doing this for thirty years and who does not like an open drawer any more than I do."

He put his finger on the last row of the card, the empty one, the one with a date already entered against it.

"There are eighty-one names on that card that somebody in this building has, and not one of them is on it in your row, because you have never given anybody one. I am asking you for them now. Tonight. With the figure on the table and the ruling on the table and the machine that cannot read you making its noise in the room." He looked at him steadily.

"Not to take anything from you. To let this office stop looking at you. That is all the ask is. Sixty-one names would close eighty-one rows and yours would be the last."

He sat in the chair and looked at the eighty-one rows and thought about it for longer than a man would think about it if he were thinking about a piece of paper.

It was not a piece of paper.

He understood that inside four seconds, the way he understood the figure drifting where he stood, and he understood it because of a man in a corridor in July saying that an analyst could put a dot on a wall map and the dot would be that room, and because of a woman at a counter three days ago saying that the register is a catch and not a census, about a hundred out there against thirty-one on the sheet, and you get on it the day somebody looks at you, and because of nineteen people and no twentieth and the whole of his working life being the arithmetic of not being looked at.

If he gave Drant sixty-one names, then the row closed, and the card went in a drawer, and there was no longer an open line with his plate number on it for any office in Ostara to pick up and pull. In a year there would be a drawer and a woman who had been right thirty years ago, and nobody would be asking.

And in exchange he would hand a clerk in a sub-level a list of every person who had ever been cold in his hand.

Nell Ardery. Garrick Neve. The checker at the yard gate, who had a docket under his arm and no address. Halvard Rees at number eleven, who had not turned and never would and whose shoulder had been off a gantry for four years. A woman on a platform with a child's hand in hers. A man on a rail who had moved along it for no reason. Ola Bright with her forty-one pages. Tobias Renn with his box on his shoulder. Runa Sato with her hands behind her back.

Sixty-one of them would go onto a card in a drawer in a building he had never been inside, in a column headed ACCOUNT, in a building where the whole business of the institution was turning a person into a line and putting the line in a place where it could be found later.

He looked at Drant's hand flat on the empty row.

"No," he said.

The word came out of him flat and small and it was the first time in his life he had said a no to an office and it did not feel like anything at all, which he understood afterwards to be the most frightening thing about it.

Drant did not move for a moment. Then he took his hand off the card, and took a pen, and wrote on the minute sheet, and Lin Hao watched him write it in the same nib pen and at the same speed as the four lines before it, and watched him read it back, because a clerk who reads a thing back is a clerk who cannot be accused of writing it.

"Attendee declined to supply names behind observation entries. Attendee stated the entries are not his to surrender. Declined at the desk, in person, in the room, on the eighteenth."

"Read it back."

"That's what it says."

"That is what it says." Drant signed it and put the minute sheet in the tray on his left with the buff square. "I want to say three things to you and then I am going to stamp your attendance and I am not going to be able to say anything kind about what happens next, because the kind thing is not true and you would rather have the true thing."

He held up one finger.

"One. The refusal is minuted, in a room, by a person, on a date. Not by paper. You came in person and that means the thing that is on this sheet today happened to a man who was standing in front of me, and a person cannot be argued out of that the way a letter can." Second finger.

"Two. A minute like that does not close anything. It moves it. Before this morning this was a transcription with a hold on it, and a hold is a shape on a sheet. It is now a refusal in a room, and a refusal in a room is an event, and an event is a thing that goes to a desk with a person who has to answer for it."

He lowered his hand.

"Three. You have just given me the only reason that office has to keep you. Do you understand that? A transcription is work. Work gets a tray. You could have sat in that chair for a month and given me nothing at all and I would have stamped you once a week for a year and nothing would have happened to you at all." He said it without relish, which made it worse.

"You have made yourself a matter that has to be decided. And the woman who has to decide it does not like being handed things she has to decide. I have been on this desk nineteen years and I have watched that happen four times."

Lin Hao sat in the chair and took it.

Drant drew the attendance sheet across and stamped it once, and the stamp made the small dead noise that stamps make, and then he drew it back and stamped it a second time in a different corner, and the second stamp was in a box that had been empty on the square in a grey coat's hand three days before.

"There," Drant said. "Your first attendance is kept. And your second."

He pushed the sheet round so the man in the chair could see it.

Two dates. The second was six days from the first.

"You may come on either day. Attendance is attendance and the office does not care which day you come, it counts the keeping and not the date." Drant capped the pen.

"A missed attendance is not a missed attendance. It is a lapse, and a lapse against a held band is a finding against the band, and a finding against your band removes the band and puts you back where your plate says you are, which is a five, three grains under a floor, and you may reapply. It is not a punishment. It is the machine doing the only thing it can do when a person does not turn up."

He put the plate in a small cloth bag and gave it back across the desk with the counterfoil on top of it.

"Take these. You will need the plate on the second attendance and the counterfoil because a held rank petitions on the band and the band is the thing that is in question, and I would like you to carry paper about the band in your pocket for the next six days so that anybody who asks you what you are has to wait while you find it."

At the door he stopped, because there was one more thing and he had decided at the desk that he was going to say it, and it was the only sentence in nineteen years he had said to an attendee that was not on any sheet in the building.

"There is a room," he said. "Third sub-level. No window. It is not a punishment and I will not describe it to you, and I am telling you that it exists and that there is paper prepared about it, because there is always paper prepared about it, because the paper gets prepared in advance and then it waits until somebody has done something that the paper will fit." He put his hand on the door frame.

"You have a running inquiry. If a running inquiry ends with somebody deciding what you are, that is the room, and I have never known the paper get prepared for a man who was still answering."

Lin Hao walked forty-one minutes back to the Fourth Branch with the plate in a cloth bag in one hand and a piece of paper with two dates on it in the other.

The 18th was stamped and kept. The 24th was stamped and waiting.

He had gone in to describe a plate and had come out owning two dates, and the second one was not a question any more.

It was the sound the office made when it could not get what it wanted and so it took the thing it could get, which was his time, and he would have to keep handing it over, over and over, with a band on the line at the end of it, until the woman at the head of it decided what he was or the clerk at the desk ran out of stamps.

Drant had told him the truth, which was the worst thing about all of it, and the truth was that a man who says nothing keeps a drawer shut for years, and a man who says no becomes a thing that must be decided, and he had six days and a corridor to think of a sentence that did not cost him a name.

He could not think of one.

Six days later, when it would be too late to be thinking of sentences at all, he would stand in a different room and dictate a different minute with his own hand in it, and it would be a stronger document than anything in this building, and it would not be enough either.

For now he had the plate and the paper and the two dates, and the second one was six days off, and there was nothing at all he could do about it before Thursday.

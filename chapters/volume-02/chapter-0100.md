# The Door At Rank One

On the twentieth, which was the last day of the hold, the machine on the fourth floor of the Fourth Branch had not run since the fourteenth and neither had the bell, and a fish woman three doors down put her head in and asked the same question she had asked on the seventeenth and was told the same answer.

The hold expired at four that afternoon and the next strip would be on the twenty-first, which was a Thursday, and Drant had entered the fourth floor as discharged on the eighteenth at ten past eleven in the morning, in his own hand, on his own floor, at his own instrument, so there would be a figure on the spike on the twenty-first and nowhere for it to go.

On the twenty-first it ran at ten and rang its bell at half past and the strip said eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight for the eighth Thursday running, and Drant entered it in the register as received and forwarded nothing, because there was no standing to forward it under.

Lin Hao had wanted the machine to print again more than he had wanted anything in the six days before it. When it did, he looked at the figure for a long time. It was the same figure it had been since the third of last month.

The grey at his temple was in its thirtieth day and had stopped being a thing that was going to come and go. It was a ridge above his ear now, and he knew from a woman at a table on Lantern Row that he had been counting it since the wrong end.

The paper on Anneke Roos's desk did not move that day. He went to the register at half past two and it was face up on the corner where she had left it on the nineteenth, and the five paragraphs were on it and the sixth space was ruled at the bottom of it, and the space was empty, and the line under it was the shape of the space in the fourth book.

"Two days," Anneke Roos said. "It has been here two days and it goes back at four and it is unsigned, and I am going to say the thing that has happened and then I am going to do my work."

"Say it."

"Three unsigned papers have gone back up three floors from that desk in two months." She did not look up from her page. "The first was a holding order on the thirty-first. The second was your four conditions yesterday, with a refusal on it, which is not the same thing at all. And the third is this.

"The difference is that the first two came to me as something that had happened to a clerk and then to a man. This one came to me as something that had already been decided by somebody three floors above me.

"It was on my desk at eight in the morning on the nineteenth with a date on it and nothing to be filled in. I have been reading it for two days and I am not going to tell you what is on it, because it is not mine and I am not permitted to want to know."

"What are you permitted to do with it."

"Carry it to four o'clock and put it in the tray." She squared it. "And I want to say one thing about the sixth space, because you have been looking at it since you came in, and then I am going to do my work.

"That space has been ruled in that book since before you came to this building. It was ruled when the head of that office wrote a heading herself on the twentieth of last month, in her own hand, and put it somewhere it did not belong, and every clerk on this floor has walked past it for a year and a half and not one of us has put anything in it. It is the only thing in this book that is empty on purpose.

"Whatever goes in that space is not a finding. It is a description of a thing in words, and once a description is in that book it does not come out and it does not get answered, and there is no appeal from it because there is nothing to appeal against. That is why it is ruled and empty. It is ruled so that a person can be shown where it would go."

Lin Hao looked at the space for a while.

"Who draws it," he said.

"The hand that writes the sixth heading draws it," said Anneke Roos, "and the hand that writes the sixth heading is a hand three floors above me that I have never seen and cannot describe, and I have been able to answer that question about four other things on this floor and I have never been able to answer it about this one, and that is not carelessness. It is the design."

At four o'clock she put it in the tray.

He went back down to the second sub-level at five, because the tray had gone down at four and there was no reason on earth for him to be standing on that stair at five on the twentieth, and he stood at the top of it and did not go down the first step.

Below him the corridor ran off in both directions with a door on each side and every door the same, and eight chairs stood against the near wall in two rows of four with their legs up, and one chair stood square to a door four feet out from the wall.

The door had a small plate on it that he had not been able to read from the top of the stair in five weeks, and he read it now, because the drawer in the cabinet was shut and the light off the goods lift was on and a man on a stair in a corridor is allowed to lean.

It said, in a plain engraved line, what four offices and a whole city had spent a month walking around.

It said what a classification is, and it said what happens to a person who goes through one, and it said the word rank and the word one, and it said that the holder is returned at rank one and that nothing else is returned with him.

There was no handle on that side of it.

He did not go down.

He felt something he had not felt in this building before, and it took him a moment to name it because it was so unlike the things he was used to feeling here. It was not fear and it was not the old cold. It was the flat satisfaction of a man being told the price of a thing by somebody who was not permitted to lie about it, and it went out of him as fast as it arrived, because there was nothing underneath it to stand on.

He stood on the stair for about two minutes, looking at a chair standing four feet away from a door he had spent a month learning the location of and had never entered, and he understood that a door was not a punishment and was not a threat, and was a piece of office furniture with a description on it.

The whole of the difference between the man standing on the stair and a man who came back out at rank one was about eleven minutes of not turning round a sheet of paper in a drawer on the eighth of this month.

Then he went back up in the cold that came out of the wall, and at the top there was nothing to go back up to, and he walked out of the building.

Behind him a drawer with four unsigned papers in it went down at four every working day, and would go down at four on the twenty-first and the twenty-fourth and the twenty-eighth and on every working day after that until somebody with a pen put a name in one of them. None of those papers had his name on them. Every one of them would take it.

In the room above the chop-house on Ferrier Street there was a visitors' book with a glass beside it on the table, and sixty-one entries in it had become seventy-eight, and a notebook lay open at the top of the table with a date ruled at the top of the page and nothing under it, and six women had spent six days not writing anything in it.

Nobody wrote it. On the twenty-first they wrote one line each, in turn, and Ola Bright copied all six into the pad, and the line was the same in six hands: that somebody was in a room on that day and wanted him to know.

"What does it change," he said.

"Nothing," said Nadia Ferrous. "It changes that six people know where you were and that not one of them has to ask. It is not protection. It is bookkeeping and I have decided to keep doing it and you are not allowed to be ashamed of it in this room."

"Then put it in the middle of the table and close it and we will leave it there."

"I am not going to do that either." Nadia did not move her hands off it. "You put your hand on the first page on the fifteenth and said we would hate it more than the writing. We do hate it. And you said you would sign it, and we are not going to let you, because the moment it is yours it is a document, and as long as it is ours it is six women being in rooms."

The sentence about the mass was in the house too, in Ola's pad, dated the eighteenth, in her own hand, four lines, and no woman in Bellhouse read it aloud, and Ines Duarte was not there that night and was not asked to come.

On the twenty-first Anneke Roos's book printed the receiving house on the refusal of the nineteenth, and Lin Hao walked past the corridor board on his way to six floors of washrooms and saw his own name under a word he had read on a sheet on the eleventh. The word was held. The six was still there. Nothing had been taken off him.

The plate stood at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Three grains of margin.

The band six still sat on the Review instrument and not on his plate, because six was seated there in the winter by a requisition of the nineteenth of last month and a finding with an open question in it, and neither of those had been touched by anything in the second half of this month. The man who had seated them there was still at his desk on the fourth floor with a bad shoulder, opening his office every morning including the mornings he did not want to.

Ruta Vesely's district table came down on the twenty-first with six lines of his on it and the finding number and the date it was assembled, quoted whole, and she did not tell him, and on the twenty-second she told him, on a stair, in about eleven seconds, because she had decided to and had thought about it for four days.

"The line is tabled," she said. "The seventh, with the finding number and the date it was assembled. Three things, and then I am going up to my own floor to be an analyst.

"One: it has not been copied by hand and it is not in this building. Two: there is a clerk in the Reading Office who rules that table twice a month and has done it for years and has never once been asked to justify a line in it. Her name is Saskia Verhoeven.

Three: a finding number is only attached to a line when somebody upstairs wanted one attached, which means somebody has wanted to be able to look you up by hand, and it will keep happening every time you are read."

"Verhoeven."

"Verhoeven." Ruta did not write it down. "Do not go and find her. Do not thank her. If you go and find her you turn a clerk into an event and this city will do that to a person inside a fortnight."

"She assembled a page with my figure on it."

"She assembled a page that was true," said Ruta Vesely. "And the second time she does it, somebody will ask her why the line is there, and she will have to have an answer, and the answer will be a person. That is how a reference becomes a finding and a finding becomes a name. Not this month.

But that is the road, and it starts with a clerk who cannot be got at because she has not done anything, and the whole of what has kept you alive for five weeks is that everybody who handles you has handled a machine."

"And Ines."

"Ines is a different animal and Ines knows it." Ruta took the pencil out of her coat and put it back again. "Your auditor is the only person in this city who can describe you. She has done it correctly, once, in private, to your face, and she has not filed it and is not going to. There are two people in Ostara who know what the one line in your ledger means. One of them has signed it.

The other one is you, and you have never seen it, and I would think very hard before you let anybody show you."

On the twenty-fourth the corridor board outside the brass counter carried his name on a typed line with a word under it that meant held, and a finding number under that, entered in the seventh week of a figure that had not moved since the third of last month.

Ottilie Prebble signed her return on the same day and wrote in her own hand that the cause was not at the counter and was referred, because an unsigned return is a lapse and a lapse against a held band is a finding against the band, and Lin Hao walked past her counter and said nothing to her and she did not look up.

On the twenty-eighth the sky over the Ferrier arc was the colour of a plate that has been held too long, and the four quiet rooms at the back of the Vestry were all empty for the first time since the spring.

Tessa Okonkwo cleaned the fourth room herself and did not put a sheet on the bed. The runner with the wrist carried twenty-two boards up two flights and would not let anybody take the second stack. And the rack on the wall went from twenty-seven to twenty-eight, which is the first time it has moved since the seventeenth of last month.

That means a person in this district was hurt somewhere in a way that a body had to hold. He did not know who, and would not have been told if he had asked.

A strip came off a machine on the fourth floor of the branch that morning and said eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight for the ninth consecutive Thursday.

The mass had not moved and the plate had not moved, and both of those were true in the same morning in the same district, and no instrument in Ostara could hold the two facts side by side.

The horizon was the twenty-third of last month and it was thirty-six days behind them, and a flat figure is publishable, and a published figure is the only kind anybody can do anything about, and the office had had every part of that sentence since the third of last month except a reason to print it.

Nobody spent anything. Nobody turned anybody. The account sat where it had been sitting since the thirteenth of last month, a long way past the horizon, and it was still the shape of a thing that had stopped moving, which is the only shape in this world that an office can classify.

On the twenty-ninth at nine in the morning Tessa read his weight at the Vestry with two hands twice and wrote nothing down, which she told him afterwards was the result and not an omission, and she sat on the edge of the couch with the instrument sheet face down on her knee and did not say a word for about a minute and a half.

"I have run a reading thirty-one times since the third of last month," she said. "Thirty-one readings and thirty-one returns, and I have written them in a day book with his name on one line and no number, and I am not going to write what I think this means."

"Write it."

"No." She turned the sheet over so the figures were uppermost. "I am going to do something I have not done since the twenty-second of last month and I am going to do it because you sat down on a bench in my arcade on the thirteenth and told me you would come back and read your arm, and you came, on the twenty-ninth, twice.

"I am going to write one sentence in that book. It is going to say that a member's mass has not moved across thirty-one readings and that the reading is mine and not the machine's, and that the cause is not established and is not mine.

And then I am going to date it, and it is going to be in my handwriting on a line that has your name on it.

When the month of my rack runs out, the office is going to read that line, and it is going to be a name and a date and a woman's own finding, and not a sentence I made up at two in the morning. That is the whole of what I asked you for in June and I have not mentioned it since, because I did not want it."

She wrote it while he watched, in one line, and dated it, and closed the book.

"You have not earned anything by standing still," she said. "Do not let anybody tell you that, including me, and including a man on a machine who has been printing the same figure since the third of last month and does not know what it is.

What you have done is survive a month of an office measuring you and not giving them anything to write down, and that is not a rank and it is not a merit and it will never appear on a Board, and it is the only thing you have actually done this season."

Lin Hao sat on the edge of a couch in a corridor with four doors shut along it.

"It does not open anything," he said at last. "It is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. It is under the band by eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two. And not one person in this building can tell me what it is, and neither can I, and thirty-one readings have told me it has stopped moving and I still do not know why a thing that takes things in would ever stop."

"No," said Tessa Okonkwo. "But in about nine weeks you are going to stand in a public hall in front of a man from the Correction Office who is honest about his profession and does not care in the slightest who anybody is, and something that has been measured privately since the third of last month is going to be measured in the open, and it will not be a plate and it will not be a strip.

"And when that happens, it will not be you it describes. That is what I have understood since the thirteenth of this month on a stone bench and I have not written it down because it is not a clinical finding and it is not mine."

None of that had happened yet.

What had happened was a corridor on the fourth floor of a branch office, a number on a strip that came off a machine on a Thursday, and a plate standing at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

A band of six sitting on an instrument and not on the plate, three grains of margin against a floor that had not moved either. The account kept. The inquiry open. And a classification paper that had gone back three floors unsigned and would go back three floors again unsigned on the twenty-first and the twenty-fourth and every working day after that, until somebody with a pen decided they were the one holding it.

He kept the account. He kept it by refusing a spend he had already made once, in a corridor, to a woman with a card, on the record, in front of a clerk, knowing it would cost him the one thing he still had, which was the shape of a man who had never been described.

Keeping it had cost him a household that could be looked up. A door on a second sub-level with a plate on it that said rank one. A mass in a woman's own hand on a line in a day book. A line in a field minute that put a household into a file as available material. A refusal inside an institute in a building with a window on a yard.

Three women in three rooms inside one day, none of them by his asking. And one line in a ledger that had been wrong since the sixth of last month, read back to his face by one woman and filed by nobody.

And he had been looked at. That was the price and he had not chosen it and it was not going to be refunded and it was not going to be possible to pay twice.

He had spent a month of Thursdays building the one arrangement in Ostara that did not exist, which was a man exactly legible in the respect that cost nothing and invisible in every other, and it had worked, and then a woman with a card had said you will not present in a corridor, and everything after that had been arithmetic.

So there was no door out of anything.

There was a Thursday, and a bell at half past, and a strip with a number on it that a hundred and forty thousand people in the district could walk to a wall and read.

There was a household that was legible and an account that was kept. There was an inquiry that stayed open into the next month and the month after that.

There was a held six that nobody could take off him, because the thing it was seated on was a requisition paper and not a paper about him. And there was an auditor in two rooms on Lantern Row who had the only true description of what he was, and who had chosen not to give it to anybody on a schedule she did not set.

The seven things it had cost sat in four rooms and one book and one field minute and one office file, and the account was whole, and the plate was three grains above its floor, and he was going to be read in public in about two months by a man whose entire profession was making invisible things stand in a room, and there was nothing at all he could do about it, and it would not be a description of him.

It would be a description of everyone who ever fed him.

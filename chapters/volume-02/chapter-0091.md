# The Fourth Summons

On the eleventh of the month Drant put a notice on the table face up and took his hand off it, and Lin Hao felt the cold he had felt on the thirty-first of last month when a different sheet came up three floors in the same shape.

He wanted Thursday's machine to print and he wanted to be standing in his own corridor when it did. Both of those had a date on them now, and the date was three days away, and it was the last Thursday before a window somebody else had drawn.

"Who is it from," he said.

"Read the second paragraph."

He read it. The first paragraph named the office, the inquiry and the standing. The second named a period, the fifteenth to the twentieth, six days, and said that inside it the member's plate was not to be presented at the place of score, and that no figure would be published for the member for the term of the period.

The third paragraph was four lines long and he read it twice.

An absent reading at a place of score is an absence of input. An absence of input requires a check-in by an officer of this office. The check-in is not made at the place of score.

"They are not holding your plate," Drant said. "They are taking your reading off your floor."

For Lin Hao, who had cleaned six floors of washrooms through a gate that read him every morning for four years, losing the reading meant losing the only part of his life that happened in front of ninety people.

"How many of these have there been."

"Four," said Drant. "You have the attendance notice, the stamped one that cut the interval, the plate presentation, and this. It is dated the ninth. It has been on my desk since the ninth face up and I have not known what to do with it, and I will tell you what I did with it, which is that I carried it back and forth for two days and then put it in front of you."

"Two days."

"Two days," Drant said, "because on the tenth a man telephoned a ward desk about a lift, and I want it on the record that I read this on the ninth, and that paper and that telephone had nothing to do with each other and I have thought about it for a day and a half and it has not made me feel better."

He squared the notice against the counter.

"Now let me tell you what it costs, because you have been in this building five weeks and you always ask me that before you decide anything, and I have got four things and one of them is mine."

"Go on."

"One. The machine on the fourth floor makes a noise on a Thursday at ten and a bell at half past, and every week for a month that noise has been a figure, and a figure you could stand next to. There is no Thursday inside those six days. That is the part I want written down.

It starts on the fifteenth and the next one is the twenty-first, so there are six days in a corridor where ninety people walk past a machine that has rung every week for four years and has nothing in it, and then it rings on the twenty-first, a week late, and everybody who has timed their week by that bell will know exactly what a week late means."

"That is the whole of it," Lin Hao said. "That is what a hold is."

"That is what a hold is," Drant said. "Two. I am the man who stops it.

The plate office belongs to this floor and the forwarding belongs to me and I signed the forwarding in a room in March, and on the fifteenth a piece of paper comes down three floors telling me not to run a machine that is under my own hand, and I am going to do it, and I am going to do it in a way that is correct, and there is a clerk in the Vestry corridor who will see me do it."

Lin Hao looked at him. "They could have put it in a drawer."

"They could have put it in a drawer and they have not, because a drawer would have needed somebody's signature, and this has needed a direction and a date and nothing else. That is cheaper for them and it is more expensive for me, and I have been in this building nineteen years and I have never once minded doing an expensive thing for them."

"Three."

"Three is not mine and it is the worst one on the paper." Drant put his finger on the bottom of the second paragraph. "No figure published, for six days. A junior clerk at the Exchange has signed a return every week for a month that is built out of your flatness, and her name on it is Ottilie Prebble and she has three years and four months at that counter.

On the twentieth she has a week and no figure in it, and the sentence that office has already written on you is that a standing which does not rise indicates an absence of input at the place of score. Absence of input is what they have just ordered. She is going to sign their own order back at them with her name at the bottom."

Drant took his hand off the paper.

"Four, and then I have finished, and four is not this building. They have had a look at that sentence since the twenty-fourth of last month and they know what it triggers. A place-of-score correction and an audit of the branch aggregate, both at once, both lawful, both signed by a man called Faber who has stood at that counter twenty-nine years and has never once been asked to account for a machine.

He was called into the front office on the twenty-third of last month for something else and came back and cleaned the fourth floor himself. That was the twenty-third of last month. This one has his name in it and he does not know it yet."

Lin Hao felt it arrive, the way it always arrived: not in him. Somewhere behind him and below his own weight a branch of nine thousand people was about to be audited because an office had found a way to measure him somewhere else.

"Tell him," he said.

"I will tell him before the fifteenth and not today, because a man with four days to think about it goes and asks somebody, and he is a better clerk than I am on the days he has had sleep." Drant capped the pen. "And I have thought about that for a day and a half as well and I would like it noticed that I decided who hears it and when."

He put the notice into the tray on the left, which was the one tray, which was for everything.

"You have asked me nothing about the six days," he said. "Everybody asks me about the six days."

"What would you tell me."

"I would tell you that six days without a figure is not six days of nothing. Your last movement was the thirteenth of last month and the horizon was the twenty-third, and today you are nineteen days past it and by the twentieth you will be twenty-eight." Drant squared the tray against the counter. "And I would tell you the thing I have been carrying since the eighth and have not said in this building, which is that a hold does not stop the mass. It only stops the printing.

Whatever is sitting on your plate has been sitting there since before the horizon and the only thing your machine has been doing is confirming it, and on the fifteenth it goes quiet, and from the fifteenth to the twenty-first you and I will both be guessing."

That was the sentence the whole notice was built out of, and neither of them said it again.

"I am going to want to be in this corridor on Thursday morning," Lin Hao said.

"You will be in this corridor on Thursday morning and I will have the seventh strip on the spike by twenty past ten, and it will say eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

"And on the fifteenth a piece of paper comes down three floors telling me not to run the machine, and I am going to read it twice and then go and stand in front of the machine for about four minutes doing nothing. If the last time that machine runs in my hands is on a Thursday, I would like somebody in this building to have seen me do it."

Drant did not answer that. He put the tray square and wrote the day on the spike pad and did not look up for the rest of the minute, and the corridor outside the brass counter had a strip on it with a figure that had not moved in a month and had three days of public life left in it.

"Date on it," Lin Hao said.

"The ninth."

"The ninth," he said. "The holding order came up on the twenty-fourth with a number on it that I did not say out loud until the twenty-sixth. This one was drafted on the ninth, which is a day after a woman with a berth card you cannot give back was put in your fourth room, and a week after a corridor got turned into a map, and I have now seen this twice."

"Twice is not a habit," said Drant. "Three is a habit, and you will know it is a habit on the day it happens a third time, and by then it will have been happening for some time without either of us counting."

Lin Hao took the long road to the branch at forty-one minutes and did not look at the Board.

Four o'clock that afternoon there was a tray on the second sub-level with four unsigned papers in it going down stamped, because a sheet with no name takes whichever name arrives first, and there was a notice on a counter three floors below that had cost a clerk with a bad shoulder a night's sleep, and a junior clerk her signature, and a man who had cleaned the fourth floor himself an audit of the whole of his branch. None of it had been written about him at all.

It was about where he was measured.

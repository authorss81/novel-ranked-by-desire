# What Settling Means

The strip came off the machine at twenty to seven and went on the spike and he took it off the spike at ten past and put it in the tray without an envelope, and it was the fifth Thursday, and the figure on it was eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and it had been eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight on five consecutive strips and the margin printed beneath it was three and the ruling was one hundred and twelve.

On the third of the month he had given that machine away.

He was in the register corridor at ten past four on the twenty-seventh with a week of grey still at his left temple and the ridge above his ear, and Anneke Roos came out of the register room with her coat already on one arm.

"You have not come up here for three weeks."

"I have come up here for three weeks."

"You have not come up here to ask me anything for three weeks, which is different, and I have noticed, and I am not going to comment on it, and you are here." She leaned against the door frame, which was the single unprofessional thing she had ever done in front of him. "What is it."

"Two words."

She stopped.

"That was quick," she said. "I had four weeks built into my morning."

She unlocked the register room and let him in, which she had never done, and shut the door, and the room was a room with a desk and a press and a smell of paper and the big book on the desk with the spine gone.

The fourth book was not on the desk. It was in the press, under a weight, and she took it out and put it on the desk square, and it had no spine and no title on the front and the pages had gone soft at the corners.

"Twenty days since the eighth," she said, "and then the rest of that month and the whole of this one, and there has been nothing since the twenty-fourth except a strip number in the corner and I have not entered it because I did not know what to enter it under."

"I know what to enter it under."

"Then say it slowly and let me write it as you say it, because I am going to have to write it, and if I write it in my own hand on the twentieth of a month it is going to be in this book for as long as this book is in this building."

"Not earned," said Lin Hao.

Anneke Roos did not move for about four seconds. Then she picked up the pen and put the point on the ruled line under the fifth heading and held it there without marking the paper.

"Where did you get that."

"It is what is on the page. Not what is on your plate. Every figure on that plate is in there because it is what the machine printed, and the machine prints standing, band, floor and margin, and none of those four things is a thing that has been earned by the man who holds it, and the only line on that strip that describes what happened is the one nobody has looked at."

"That is a heading for a form."

"It is the heading for that page. It is the only sentence in the five that is true of every entry, and the four above it are all about the shape and this one is about the cause." He put his hands on the edge of the desk. "She has written it twice and got it wrong twice. I am not interested in what she wrote.

I am telling you what goes under the five headings and the space is ruled for it and it is two words long and it is those."

"Write it," said Anneke Roos.

He did not move.

"I am not writing it in this book," she said, "and I want you to understand that I am not refusing.

There is a woman at the head of this office who has had that book for three weeks and has not read a line of it, and she keeps it. And if a word in the sixth space is in a clerk's hand at four in the afternoon on the day the paper goes, then the next woman to open that book will know a clerk wrote it.

That clerk is me, and I am junior to her, and I have a supervisor who signs my attendance and not hers."

"So you want it in mine."

"I want it in the hand of the person it is about, and I am aware that that is not how books work, and I am aware that I am asking you for something improper on an afternoon like this because it is the only way I have thought of in three weeks." She put the pen down. "Write it on a sheet.

And I will enter it as received, and it will go into the book as a thing that arrived and not as a thing a junior clerk decided, and that is the whole of my three weeks and I have been waiting for somebody to make it worth something."

He took the pen from her and wrote two words on a clean sheet, on a line, in the middle, the way you write a thing on a label.

Then Anneke Roos read her own book from the eighth of last month to the twenty-fourth of this month, in one go, standing up, without sitting down, and he watched her do it and she did not hurry and she did not stop, and when she got to the end of the twenty days she squared the book and put her thumb under the empty ruled space and did not write in it.

"It still has to be copied across," she said. "When a heading is settled the first twenty days get copied under it. That is what this book does and it is the only book in this building that does that. And I am not doing it today, and I will tell you why."

"Tell me."

"Because the moment those twenty days go under it, the twenty days become a claim. Not a figure. A claim." She closed the book. "At the moment they are entries. In six weeks they are a body of evidence with a heading on it, and this office has never had one, and it will have to be answered by somebody, and the somebody will not be me."

"Then who is going to answer it."

"Somebody who has to describe a thing that has already been described," said Anneke Roos, "and there is exactly one person in Ostara who can, and you told me so yourself at the end of that corridor three weeks ago and neither of us wrote it down."

The room was cold and the light in it was the light in the corridor.

"What does settling mean," he said.

She sat down on the edge of the desk, which put her above him for the first time in four weeks.

"It is the Bureau's word for merit arriving anywhere at all," she said. "Every figure in this city is called a reading and a reading is a rate. A rate goes up and down and it has an interval. If you have no interval then the figure is not a rate, and a figure that is not a rate has to have come from somewhere, and the somewhere the Office has a word for is arrival.

Settling is what the books call an arrival that has stopped moving. That is the whole definition and there is no second half of it."

"Stopped moving."

"Stopped moving, and gone flat, and gone quiet. And then, because a thing that has stopped moving has a shape that does not change, it can be described. And a thing that can be described can be held up to a form." Anneke Roos put her hands on her knees. "The Office does not say classifiable. The Office says settled, and a settled account is one that has been asked for and can be given."

He stood very still.

"And the count of days."

"Forty." She said it without hesitation. "Forty days without a movement. That is the number the Office has used since before I came here and it is not on my form and it is in every head in this building. Forty days and the figure is settled, and a settled figure is publishable, and a published figure is the only kind of figure that anybody in this city has ever been able to do anything about."

"Forty days from what."

"From the last time it moved." She looked at him. "Not from the last time it was spent. From the last time it moved, which is a different thing, and nobody in this building knows the difference because there has never been a figure here that moves when nothing is spent."

She got up and went to the press and took out the sheet with two words on it and looked at it a long time without touching it.

"You wrote it in the middle of the line."

"There was nowhere else to put it."

"There is nowhere else to put it," she agreed, "and it is going out of this building at five o'clock with the rest of today's paper, and everything that leaves this floor goes into a thin book at the desk, and the thin book prints the house it went to so that a reply does not have to be looked for."

"I know what the thin book says. I read it on the twenty-first."

"Then you know the name." She turned the sheet over, wrote the time on the back of it in the register's hand, and squared it. VEIL-RESEARCH. And under it, printed on the form and not written on it, a name that was not initials.

PERRIN SOWE, RESPONSIBLE.

He did not look at it for longer than a second and she looked at him for longer than a second.

"You knew that before I turned it over."

"On the twentieth of last month two things left this office and both of them were about me and neither of them came to the desk I sit at," he said. "I was told about it in a room with a lamp on it.

I was told it on the twenty-first and I have carried it for six days and I have not done anything about it, because there is nothing in that building I can do anything about, and because the man whose name is on that form has not come down a corridor in three years where a woman who has been waiting three years for somebody to ask her is two floors below him."

Anneke Roos put the sheet into a tray and did not close the tray.

"I am going to say a thing and then I am going to go home," she said. "I have read twenty days of your plate in my head since the eighth of last month and I never once read them as a man. I read them as attendances and plates and rulings and figures and margins.

And this afternoon I have written a heading on a sheet that says none of it is yours, and I am a clerk who has done her job for three weeks and has just put my name near the only interesting thing that has come up this floor since I came here, and I would like to go home now."

She closed the tray.

"Go down the long road," she said. "It is the only one that is not four streets past the Exchange and I have noticed that you have started going that way and I have decided not to ask why."

He went down it.

And on the third flight, with the stair going down and the light going green at the bottom, the thing he had been carrying for four years came up out of him in a shape he could not put back.

Nothing in him had ever come from him.

That was the part. He had spent four years believing that what was in him was a thing he did — that he gathered it, in corridors, by being where unrecorded good happened, and that the ledger was a record of a man's attention, and the arithmetic was a man's arithmetic, and when he put his hand on a person it was him going across.

It was not him going across.

It had arrived, for three years, from strangers holding doors for each other four times a day, wherever he stood. It had gone in. It had never once come out of him.

And on the thirteenth of last month, in a room with nine chairs in it, he had opened himself in front of sixty people and the account had used him, because he was the only person in Ostara that nothing looks at hard enough to be a scandal. It had gone out of three buildings at once through the only door in the city that nobody was watching.

He had not spent anything that afternoon. He had been the mouth.

And the only line in the whole of his ledger that could never have been relief was the line that read himself, because relief goes to the person it came from, and nothing in him had ever come from him, and that was the only reason the one spend that should have been impossible had worked.

The sentence he had signed on the seventeenth was true and had never been evidence of anything, and a woman at a counter four streets away had been signing an accurate account of a drain on Thursday, and he had been carrying two of the hundred and fourteen thousand grains of his brother's objection around inside his chest for three years believing it was a question about a figure.

It was a delivery note.

It said: this has been sent. It has been sent to the lowest address that will hold it. And the address has been filling itself with strangers for three years, and a man who cleans toilets is the only person in this city who is not looked at hard enough, and he is not going to close the gap by earning any more of it, because none of it was ever his to earn.

He got to the bottom of the stair and stood in the street in the green light with a week of grey at his temple. Upstairs there was a sheet in a tray with two words on it in his own hand, going out of a building at five o'clock to a house off a third court.

A man with a window on the yard had answered a departmental question three years ago about a transfer that carried no name, and had filed the answer, because filing is what an institution does with a thing it cannot describe.

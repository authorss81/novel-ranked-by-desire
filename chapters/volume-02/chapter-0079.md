# The Yard Gate Watch

Beatrix Halm put the tram at the stop outside the chop-house at ten past six on the morning of the thirtieth and did not say a word about it, and he got on, and she rang the bell with her knee.

They went out along the arc with the morning crowd and she worked the pole and did not speak for four stops.

"You have not slept properly since the twenty-third."

"I have had some."

"You have had seven nights of it and I have been driving past this stop at the end of a shift for six weeks and looking up at that window," said Beatrix, "and I have been off your list since the thirteenth and neither of us has said a word about it, and that has been correct of both of us, and I am not going to start it in a cab at six in the morning. I am going to tell you about a woman in a notebook."

The tram came up slow at the terminus and there was a woman already sitting in the third seat with her bag on her knees.

"Third seat, every time, since the eighteenth of the month," Beatrix said, under the noise of the doors. "Booked. Has a season on it. Has not had a season on it at any other seat and the guard has checked it four times for me without being asked. She gets on at the Yard end and rides four turns and gets on again at the terminus and does it all day."

The woman had a notebook open on her bag and a pencil held the way somebody holds a pencil when they write standing up, and she looked at Lin Hao across the aisle and smiled at him and went back to the page.

"That is not a fare evasion," said Beatrix. "I have seen fare evasion and I have never once seen anybody ride four turns for eleven days with a notebook."

At the Yard end the woman got off in front of them and Lin Hao got off after her because there was nowhere else to get off, and Beatrix went on and turned round in the seat and watched him through the back window all the way to the far side of the gates.

The gate was open and the checker was on it, and there was a queue of forty going through with dockets, and at the far rail a woman in a dark coat with no paper at all was talking to a man in overalls who had a lamp in his hand and was enjoying it.

She had been there before the tram came in. He was certain of it, and he could not have said how.

"Lin," said the checker.

He had a docket and a whistle and a chart. The chart was the gate tallies and it had been started that morning and had his name in pencil in the corner, rubbed out and rewritten, which had been true for a month.

"How many times," Lin Hao said.

"Three." The checker held up three fingers and then put them down. "The nineteenth. I have told you what that afternoon was like and I am not going to say it again in a yard. He was fair with us, and he did not tell the other two what the other two said."

"And the boxes."

"Four boxes, and he told us out loud that the fourth was empty on purpose and that we were not to worry about it." The checker took the buff square out of his pocket and showed it with a thumb. It was not signed. "I have had three weeks to think about that and I still do not know whether it was kindness or whether he was saving it for somebody."

"That was kindness."

"Right." The checker put the square in his pocket. "Then here is what I have got for you and I wrote it out myself and it is not a document.

I have had the cold four times and I know where they are and when they are, and it is a bad place to be, the left hand, and it came the first time in the spring and it has not done anything since except show up. Nell Ardery has had four in five days and that one is in her shoulder where the old one is.

Garrick Neve has it in his wrist and he writes down the minute of it now, to the second, which is a thing he started doing on the twentieth."

"They have been at it a month and it has not stopped."

"It has not stopped and it is getting a little more specific each time and that is not nothing." The checker looked down the yard. "And that is where I wanted to say something to somebody and I have been waiting for you to come out, and I am going to say it and then I am going to go back on the gate.

The dark coat has been here twice. Once on the twenty-first and once this morning. She has got no paper and she has got a memory like a clerk and she asked me three questions and all three of them were the same three questions I have answered in four rooms in a fortnight, and she asked them in an ordinary voice at my own gate, and she did not tell me what the answers were for."

"And the other one."

"The other one got on my tram this morning." He nodded down the yard. "Booked seat. Notebook. Asked me for my count and she asked me if I could feel it when it went, and I said yes, and she wrote it down, and she asked me how many times, and I said four, and she asked me where, and I told her."

"She asked you where."

"I told her the yard gate, because I was standing at it." The checker took his whistle off. "And she asked me one more thing and that is the one I have been standing here thinking about. She asked me whether I had ever been asked to be anywhere about it. And nobody has, except on the nineteenth in a room, and I said no."

The yard gate was loud and cold and a man in overalls was laughing at the far rail because the woman in the dark coat had just made him laugh.

Lin Hao looked for the notebook woman and found her about forty feet away at the signal box with her back to the wall and her eyes shut, which was not resting. She was counting with her lips and moving one finger on the page.

He walked over.

She opened her eyes and did not move and did not put the notebook away.

"Mr Lin Hao," she said. "You do not have to answer anything. I want to be very clear about that before you decide whether to run, because two men have run and I have written both of them down and neither of them was running from me."

"You have my name."

"I have had your name for six days and I did not pay for it and I am going to tell you how, because it is the interesting part and because I would like to be a person you can talk to rather than a shape you have to get away from."

"Say it."

"There was a signalman at this end on the twenty-fourth who has been at this gate nineteen years and who told me your name in about eleven seconds. He did not do it for money and he did not do it because he thinks anything of you. He did it because I asked him which men in this city had a name that everybody in a corridor says out loud without knowing they are saying it, and he thought about it for about four seconds and then he said yours."

"And before that."

"Before that I asked nineteen tram operators for their counts and whether they could feel it when it went. Eighteen of them could not. One of them could, and she told me the building, and I went and stood in the Vestry lift on the tenth of the month for two days." The notebook woman closed it and put it under her arm.

"That is how I got it, and I did not get it from anybody in an office, and no office in this city has got it, and I say that as a fact and not as a threat."

"What are you doing."

"I am doing a survey." She said it lightly. "It is not an audit. I do not have a badge and I could not get one. I write down counts and places and I never write a name down and I have been doing it for two years and I have four hundred and eleven of them and I have never once been refused, and I have never once been asked to stop, and I have got a season on a seat."

"For what."

She looked at him properly for the first time.

"That is the first question anybody has asked me in two years that has been the right one," she said. "Everybody asks me who I am for. Nobody has asked me what it is for.

I do not know yet. I am not going to tell you I am a Bureau thing because I am not, and I am not going to tell you I am your friend because I have taken eleven days on this line and I have not asked you anything for anything and there is no arrangement here.

I am counting things that people feel and nobody writes down. That is all. And in two years I have got four hundred and eleven and every single one of them is a person who has been somewhere near a thing they could not explain, and I would like to know what the thing is, and I have been standing at signals and in lifts and at gates for two years because it is the only way to find out."

"Do not write it down."

"I told you I do not write names down."

"You have just told me your name twice and mine twice and the gate's," he said. "You do not write them down.

You keep them, which is a different thing and it is worse, and the woman you got the Vestry from gave it to you out loud in a lift and told you the building and told you her own floor, and she told me in a kitchen in Ferrier Street on the twenty-second that she gave it to you on purpose and had worked it out four seconds before you asked her."

The notebook woman's face did not change at all.

"She worked it out," she said. "Yes. She told me that. She said it in a lift and she said it like a woman putting a card on a table. And that is why you are still standing here."

She put the notebook back out and wrote something in it, standing up, with her eyes on him.

"I am going to write down that you spoke to me," she said. "I am not going to write down what you said. And the first of those is going in a book that eleven people in this city are going to read one day and the second one is going to be in here, and this is the whole arrangement and you can work out for yourself which of us is in the more trouble."

She got on the tram behind him and rode four turns and got on again at the terminus.

At the far rail the woman in the dark coat was still talking to a man with a lamp. She had a bad coat and no bag and no paper, and she had been asking the same three questions at that gate that a man with a pen had asked inside four rooms, in an ordinary voice, at a man's own work.

Lin Hao stood in the middle of the yard and understood that there were two women in this city working on him, and that the office which had spent a month on him had not found out about either of them.

The household put it together at nine that night.

Ola Bright had the notebook woman on a landing rail in her own hand and the dark coat in her own hand in two places, and she had both of them in the same colour of pencil because she had not thought to use a second one, and Beatrix Halm put her hand flat on the rail.

"She gets on at the Yard end," Beatrix said. "Third seat. Booked. Four turns. Since the eighteenth of the month and she has been in my cab every working day of it and I have asked the guard twice and she has a season."

"How many people have asked the guard."

"Two that I know of." Beatrix did not take her hand off the rail. "The other one has no season and no paper and no badge and she has been on my tram twice and she got off at the same stop both times and went and stood at a gate.

And she has been into my yard at four in the morning and asked a man with a lamp what he could feel and when he could feel it, and that man has an excellent memory and thinks she is a joke."

"And the branch," said Nadia Ferrous. She had been standing since she came in. "I gave the notebook one my name and my building and my floor on the tenth, in a lift, and she has had the Vestry for twenty days. And Marguerite's porter on the twenty-first and again on the twenty-fourth, and he is not a man who talks, and she did not make him talk, she asked him something and he answered it."

Nothing was said for a moment.

"So there are two of them," said Beatrix Halm, "and one of them has my tram and a booked seat and four hundred and eleven counts and has been doing it for two years, and one of them has no seat and no paper and has been on my yard at four in the morning asking gate men questions in a voice that makes them laugh.

And one of them has been working this city for forty days and the other one since the eighteenth, and the one who has been here longer has not found him, and the one who found him in six days has never once been near an office."

Tessa Okonkwo put her mug down.

"It is the second of them that matters and I would like somebody to say why, because nobody in this room has."

"Because the Office knows about her." Beatrix said it flatly. "On the seventeenth a man with a pen was in a room four streets away asking the same three questions this office has been asking for a month.

She is being asked them by both, and she has answered both, and she has not once told either of them about the other, and that means she does not know there are two. A woman who does not know there are two is going to find out, and when she finds out one of us is going to be holding something for nothing."

Lin Hao put his hands flat on the table.

"No," he said. "One of them is faster than the Office and one of them is slower than a clerk at a counter, and they are not the same kind of animal at all, and I have been treating them as one thing for a month because a thing with no address is easier to carry as one thing."

"Say it slower."

"The one with the notebook got my name in eleven seconds off a man at a gate and she has got a season on a seat and four hundred and eleven counts and she has never once been refused and never once been asked to stop, and she has been doing it two years, and the Office has had me for a month and has spent the whole month asking me a question about an interval." He looked at his hands.

"And the other one has no paper and asks gate men what they feel at four in the morning, and the Office is asking her the same three questions I have been asked, which means she is four streets from being in the same file I am in.

It is not two collectors after all. It is one that reads and one that talks, and the one that talks is the one that is going to get put in a drawer."

"And the one that reads," said Nadia Ferrous, from the doorway, with her hands behind her back, "has my name because I put it there, and I would like it minuted, in this room, out loud, that I put it there, and that I did it on the tenth of the month on purpose after working it out in about four seconds.

And that if she uses it, then the first thing that happens is to me and not to him. I have been saying that for three weeks and nobody in this house has said it back to me once."

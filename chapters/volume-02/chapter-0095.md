# The Ninth Chair

On the fifteenth the machine on the fourth floor of the Fourth Branch did not run, and not one person noticed at ten, and by twenty past ten four people in that corridor had noticed that it had not rung.

Lin Hao heard it himself at half past ten, standing at the end of the long corridor with a cloth in his hand, because a machine that has rung for four years is a thing a body waits for and the body waited for it and there was nothing there.

Drant came up at eleven with his jacket off and his pen in his hand and no strip.

"Six days," he said. "Four minutes standing in front of it, like I said I would. A man from the plate office asked me twice whether I had gone round to the back to try it. I said no and I said it the same way twice, which is the only skill I have."

"Did you tell Faber."

"I told him at half past nine on the fourteenth like I said I would and he took his coat off and went up to the second floor and came back and cleaned the fourth floor himself for an hour, and then went and did the second floor as well, which no one had asked him to do." Drant sat down on the edge of the counter. "Lin Hao.

He has been cleaning floors since the twenty-third of last month and he has said nothing about it to one person, and I am telling you because you will otherwise hear it from a corridor."

"What is it."

"It is not a place," Drant said. "It is not a room, a number, a heading, a sheet, a finding, a desk, a stair, or a man. It is furniture, and it came up in a goods lift at about eleven at night on the twelfth, which was the night of the sitting, and eight chairs went past that runner and she counted them because she counts everything."

Lin Hao put the cloth down.

"Where."

"The second sub-level."

He stood in the corridor for a moment with the whole shape of the thing arriving before he had decided to be afraid of it.

"They have built it," he said.

"They have had a room built. It was built in September and it has been standing empty since September with a cloth on a table in it, and I have walked past that door every working morning for nineteen years and I can tell you exactly what it looked like in March, which was a cloth and nothing else. What is new is that it has furniture and that the furniture is ours." Drant turned his pen over. "Ours is the wrong word.

It came from the third floor of the Reading and it went up at eleven at night in a goods lift by two men who did not want the stairs, which means somebody wanted it there by the following morning, which means the sitting produced it."

"Do not go and look," he said, and then heard himself, and then did not take it back.

"I have not gone and looked and I am not going to, for a reason that is mine and not yours." Drant stood up. "That corridor is not on my floor. Everything in it belongs to a sub-level clerk and there is no instrument of mine that reaches it, and the last time I went down there I was nine years ago with a junior clerk and we opened nothing, and I told you that on the eighth and I have not been down since.

Go and look from the top if you want to. You have been on that stair twice now and you have not gone to the bottom of it either time and I would like to keep that true for as long as this lasts."

Lin Hao went down at two in the afternoon.

He got as far as the third step from the bottom, which was the step he had stood on on the eighth with a runner four steps above him asking whether he wanted it in the tray or in the book, and from there he could see the cabinet with the four drawers, and the drawer was shut, which was new, and he could see eleven feet of corridor to the left and eleven to the right with doors on both sides and every door the same.

Eight chairs stood against the wall on the near side, in two rows of four, with their legs up, exactly the way they had stood in the corridor outside the room on the third floor of the Reading for nine days after the twenty-sixth of last month.

And one chair stood on its own, square, facing a door, four feet out from the wall.

The door was not open. It was a door. Through the gap at the hinge there was a strip of light and the edge of a table, and the table had a cloth on it, and the cloth was the grey cloth that had been on the inventory table on the seventh of this month with a plate beside it and a finding number on the spike outside.

Lin Hao stood on the third step for about two minutes.

He could have gone four more steps and put his head through the gap and seen the whole of a room with a table and a cloth and a chair in it, and the door was not locked and there was nothing on the far side of it that he could not have seen, and there is no rule anywhere in any building in Ostara that says a man on a stair at the foot of a flight may not put his head through a door that is not shut.

He did not go four more steps.

What he felt on that stair was not fear and it was not the old cold. It was the specific nausea of a thing being made, slowly and lawfully and out of his own household's furniture, by people who had done nothing wrong and would sleep that night.

He went back up. Drant was at the top and said nothing about how long he had been down there, which told him everything.

"There were nine," Lin Hao said.

"There were nine."

"Eight are against the wall and one is square to the door."

"Yes."

"Where is the ninth."

The clerk looked at him for a second longer than he usually did.

"Still on the third floor," said Drant. "In the corridor. The grey armband has it. The one by the door that she was putting her tally sheet on. She did not give it up with the other eight and no one went back for it, and it has been in that corridor since the twenty-seventh of last month and it is the only piece of furniture in this business that somebody has chosen to keep."

He went up to the Reading after his shift and took the stairs to the third floor, because there is no lift on that side, and the corridor outside the shut room was empty and the boards were bare and the four marks where the table legs had stood for eleven years were still there.

The ninth chair was against the far wall with a folded card on the seat and a woman sitting in it with her hands on her knees.

"You have come about the chairs," she said.

"Eight of them went up in a goods lift on the night of the twelfth."

"That is correct." She did not get up. "I heard about it at four o'clock on the thirteenth from a runner who was annoyed about it. She was annoyed because two men carrying eight chairs up at eleven at night is not something anybody in this building is supposed to have to notice, and she noticed it and wrote it down, and I read it, because I read everything that goes past this door."

Her armband was grey and there was a card on the seat beside her with a number on the top corner.

"My name is Sabine Rooke," she said. "I set that room up. I took it down. I put it up again on the twenty-sixth of last month for a finding with no event in it and then I took it down again, and that is the last work I have had on that floor in eleven years, and the room is shut and it is staying shut, and that is not my decision and it is not my room any more."

"How many times have you been asked."

"Twice," Sabine Rooke said. "Both times about a Thursday. Both times by a woman with a card who was very polite and both times about a Thursday in a month that has gone.

The first time was the twelfth of last month and the second was the twenty-seventh, and both times I told the truth, which is that I set the room up on the same schedule every week for eleven years and there were nine people who worked there and I could not have told you which Thursday was which one without the sheet."

"You did not tell her about him."

"I did not tell her about anybody," said Sabine. "There was not one person to tell her about. I put nine chairs in a room and nine people in them and a man at the front with a sheet, and I have never known one of those men from another and I would have said so to a woman with a card as readily as to you."

She put her hand on the back of the chair she was sitting in.

"There are nine chairs in this corridor's history," she said, "and eight of them are in a room on the second sub-level of an office where nobody from this floor works, and this one is in a corridor, and I am sitting on it because a man took nine chairs out of a room and left me one and I would like him to have to walk past it for the rest of his life."

She said it without heat, and then she said the thing that made the whole afternoon.

"They will ask me a third time," she said. "I know that the way I know the schedule of that room. And when they do, I am going to say the same true thing, and it is going to be the third, and three is a different animal from two. Two is a woman being thorough. Three is a file."

Lin Hao stood in a corridor on the third floor of the Reading with his hands in his pockets and understood that in two days he would be asked to describe something, and that this woman had just told him what it feels like from the other end of the same question.

"If they ask you a third time," he said, "come and find me first."

"You are the worst person in Ostara to say that to," said Sabine Rooke, "and I am going to do it anyway, and you are going to have to decide by the fourteenth of next month whether a ward clerk at the Vestry can be trusted with it, and I will tell you now that I have never in my life asked anybody for anything and I am not starting until then."

He went down the stairs and out into the square, and there was no figure on the Board for him that week because there was no figure for him anywhere, and eight of nine chairs were in a corridor four hundred yards away with a cloth on a table behind a door that was not locked, and the ninth was in a corridor on the third floor where a woman was sitting on it waiting for a Thursday she would never be asked about twice.

# The Grey At His Temple

The woman at the second-floor stair had a left index finger that had never healed straight after a gate and she read every plate off a stranger with the other hand, and she had been doing it for four years, and in four years she had never once said his name out loud.

That was the whole of what he knew about her. He knew her hands and her coat and the half-second she took over his plate before she gave it back, and he knew that on the twenty-third of the month he stood at the top of that stair and put his right hand over her right hand on the plate and pushed.

She looked up.

He was two thirds of the way through it before he understood what he had done, and what he had done was the exact thing he had refused to do three times in a fortnight, in a cold store and on a landing and in the queue at the Exchange.

The reason he had refused was a rule he could recite standing up: a person who has already felt it once and is given it a second time makes an episode that can be written down, and a third one in a season gets a file.

She had not felt it once. She was not in his ledger and she had never been in his ledger and he had known that as certainly as he knew his own plate.

He pushed anyway.

Nothing moved.

Nothing happened at all. There was no cold in her hand and no cold in his chest and no minute of anything anywhere, and he stood on the second-floor landing of his own branch at twenty past six in the morning with his hand on a stranger's hand and the whole apparatus of it — four years, a hundred and nine faces, a square with nine chairs in it — went over and produced nothing, like a machine being turned with the belt off.

The plate came back into her hand and she gave it to him and she said, "You're grey."

"I am all right."

"You're grey in the temple," she said, "and I've been reading plates at this stair for four years and I have watched that happen to two men and a woman on this floor and none of them were all right, and you should go and sit down."

And she looked at him the way a person looks at a colleague who has been unwell too long, and she did not ask him a single question, and that was the kindness of it, and it was worse than anything a question would have done.

He went down four floors and washed his hands for a long time.

At ten to seven that morning the strip had come off the machine with the same figure on it, and at the end of the day he took it off the spike and carried it up to the Wing himself and put it in the tray by the brass counter without an envelope, because an envelope in that building was a thing that got weighed.

A day early, and the standing wanted it on the Thursday, and he put it in when he could because a day a man puts in himself is a day nobody can take off him.

The cost arrived at about half past nine.

It started at the left temple, where it has always started, in the exact place it has always been, and he had time to be mildly irritated about that, because a man who has paid a price for six weeks at least expects the supplier to vary the location.

Then it stayed.

He had seen it come and go since the Review. It came on the thirteenth and was gone by the Thursday. It came when he spent and it went when he stopped, and every time it had gone inside a day and a half, and he had built a small private arithmetic on it, the way a man builds an arithmetic on everything: the grey costs what the spend costs, and the grey is the receipt.

It did not go.

It was there at the end of the shift and it was there when he got home, and it was there when he woke, and he went to work in it for two days because there was nothing else in the calendar to do, and on the second of those days he noticed that it had come forward along the ridge above his ear about the width of a finger. It was not pain. It was the specific absence of a thing that was supposed to be there.

He had not done anything on the twenty-third that was bigger than anything he had done before. That was the part he could not get round. In the Review square it had taken the whole of the account and three strangers and his own line in a ledger, and he had been grey for a day and a half and had gone to work in it with his coat buttoned.

He had put his hand on a woman who had never fed him and nothing had come, and that had cost him the temple and two nights' sleep and it was still there, and the proportion was not in the size of the thing at all.

Those nights he did not sleep. That was the other half of it and it was exactly what it had always been.

It started at one and put him out of the bed at half past and let him back in at two and a quarter and gave him eleven minutes of something at four. By the second morning he had understood that the eleven minutes were going to come every night for as long as the grey stayed, and that neither of them was going to tell him anything the other had not.

He did not tell anybody.

He told Tessa Okonkwo on the twenty-fifth because she was going to look at his face and she was the only person in the household whose looking was an instrument, and he did it in eleven words on the steps of the Vestry while she was holding a door, and he gave her the cause in the same sentence because she would not have accepted it without the cause.

"I put my hand on the woman at my own stair on the twenty-third and nothing came and it has cost me the temple and two nights."

"Right," said Tessa. "Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There is always a rest of it and you are about to find out what it is, so you may as well have it in your own mouth." She did not let go of the door. "You went to a person who had never fed you. On purpose. Knowing.

That is the whole of the mechanism and there is no version of it where the cost is different, and it is not proportional to how much you were trying to spend and it never has been, and every man who has ever thought that has been thinking about his own feelings instead of about his hand."

He stood on the step.

"Then what is it proportional to."

"It is proportional to whether it came." Her voice did not change at all. "The grey is not a punishment for the attempt. It is the account being asked to move and finding out that there is nothing on the other side of the person you touched, and it charges you for the asking. The more you reach, the more it looks. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it since the eighth week."

She let the door go and it closed on its hinge.

"And it is not new," she said. "You have known that rule for years and you have just decided this morning that you wanted to be the exception, and I want you to say that out loud once so that both of us have heard it."

"I wanted to give her something," he said.

"She has read your plate four years and has never once said your name."

"That is what I wanted to do about."

"Then you have paid a woman at a stair two days and two nights of sleep so that a woman at a stair would not have been annoyed with you," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I am the person who says that sentence to you because every other woman in this house would say it kindly, and kindly would cost you another week."

She went back inside. He stood on the steps in the sun with his hand out of his pocket.

And then, because he could not help it, because he was the sort of man who cannot leave a rule alone once he has been bitten by it, he worked out what he had actually proved.

He had proved the negative case.

For four years and a hundred and nine lines he had assumed — never examined, never once, because there had never been a reason to examine it — that the thing in him worked. That it was a thing he did. That when he put his hand on a person's hand, something went across, and the mechanism was him, and the ledger was his, and the arithmetic was a man's arithmetic and the man was him.

On the twenty-third of the month he had put his hand on a hand that was not in the book and the entire apparatus had produced nothing, and the reason it had produced nothing was not that he had been unlucky.

It stopped there, and he let it stop, because the negative case does not come with an answer fastened to it, and a man who finishes a sum like that on his own at seven in the morning has usually decided the answer first and is now collecting reasons for it.

He had not looked properly at a person in four years and it occurred to him somewhere underneath the arithmetic that this was the whole of the subject.

The branch corridor was full at seven and he stood at the bottom of the stair with a queue going up behind him and forty people in the building who had spent four years being looked through by him and had never once been looked at by him.

A woman on the third floor told him her son had come back from the yard and had asked after the man who cleaned the washrooms, and he had said he would come back on Thursday.

He had said he would come back on Thursday and he had no idea whether he meant it, and the only reason he had said it was that the sentence had come out of his mouth the way sentences do at seven in the morning in a corridor, without a man in it deciding anything.

He went up and cleaned six floors of washrooms and stood in the middle of the building with three grains of margin on a printed strip he had carried up to the brass counter himself and a hole in his head where his own colour had been for three days, and a woman on the second floor read a plate off a man and said nothing at all.

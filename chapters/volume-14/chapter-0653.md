# Two Books Four Feet Apart

Seven days had gone since a man at a desk under a window spent the one question he was entitled to on the wrong thing on purpose.

Two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven was the eighth of February and the fifteenth is day two thousand one hundred and seventy-four, and two thousand one hundred and seventy-four less two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.

The division building was warm at about ten in the morning after the cold of the street, with a counter on the ground level and a corridor behind it, and Beatrix Halm was at the counter with a pencil behind her ear and a book open in front of her, and she turned her good ear toward him when he came in, the way she always did, without making anything of it.

Lin Hao had come to get the ninety-one a quarter out of her before somebody else did, because she had carried it on her fingers four years and given it up at the end of September, and it was the only number in Ostara anybody had actually counted.

He wanted it on paper, and she knew he wanted it, and both of them knew what that did.

## The Counter On The Ground Level

“You want me to write it down,” she said.

She did not ask him to sit, because there was nowhere to sit at a public counter, and she kept her hand on the open book, and her fingers were ink-stained at the tips from four years of returns, and her hair was pulled back hard from her face and pinned, and her coat was buttoned to the throat against the draught from the door.

“I want you to write it down because you counted it,” said Lin Hao. “Not because I asked. Because you did.”

She looked at him a long moment, and what she felt was plain on her face, which was irritation and fondness together, the way people look at a man who has asked for the same thing three times and has been refused twice.

“You know what happens if I do,” she said. “A number on my fingers is a thing I can only keep. Put it on paper with a date against it and it is a claim, and a claim is a thing you can argue with, including with the person it is about, which is me, and including by the person who counted it, which is you.”

“I know,” he said.

“And you still want it.”

“I still want it,” he said, and his chest ached with the cold he had carried in from outside, low and spent, the way it had ached since the fourth year, and he did not hide it from her. “I want it because nobody else has counted anything, and because a landlord may put a figure with a date against it in front of a magistrate, and because you know that and I know that.”

She closed the book on the counter without marking the place, because she knew the place, and came round the front of the counter with her keys.

“Then come through,” she said. “Not here. Through the back. What I have to show you is not for a counter.”

## The Corridor Behind

The corridor behind was narrow and clean, with a shelf along one wall and two books on it four feet apart, a round book bound in dark cloth and a desk book bound in pale boards, and a stool under the shelf and a window at the far end with light coming through.

Every paper that came into that division was read twice, once at the round desk and once at the returns desk, and the two readings were kept in those two books and had never been laid side by side, not once in six years, because laying them side by side would have been a third reading and there was no standing for one.

“The round book says six hundred and ninety-one,” said Beatrix Halm, standing by the dark one without touching it. “The desk book says six hundred and seventy-four. The difference is seventeen. Thirteen of them can be named and four cannot.”

Lin Hao stood at the other end of the shelf with his hands in his pockets, because touching either book without standing would have been a presumption, and he looked from one to the other and back.

“Which one is true,” he said.

“Neither,” she said. “Both. One of them is true and nobody will ever know which, because a paper entered by two people is two entries, and that is the whole of the rule, and nobody here wrote the rule and nobody here can change it.”

He understood it perfectly, the way a man understands a rule that has just closed a door he meant to go through.

He also understood that she had brought him there to show him why she could not make a copy enterable, and why no office that read everything twice could ever take a piece of pencil paper from a house and call it anything.

“What you were given is a sheet and not a person,” she said. “A person is not a claim and a claim is the only thing on any form in that building you work in. Your clerk may only enter what she is given. She was given paper.”

She said it without triumph and without apology, and he felt respect come up in him, sharp and unwilling, because she was right and because being right had cost her four years of carrying a number nobody would take.

“I still want the ninety-one,” he said.

“I know you do,” she said. “And I want to give it to you. That is the trouble. I have had it on my fingers since the second year and I am tired of carrying it.”

## What She Writes Down

She took a sheet of her own from under the shelf, plain and unheaded, and laid it on the stool, and took the pencil from behind her ear, and stood a moment with the pencil over the paper without writing, and he saw her hand shake once, very slightly, and then steady.

“What will you do with it,” she said.

“Hold it,” he said. “Not enter it. There is no form for what I want to do with it, and a clerk who may only enter cannot take it, and I am the only person that office may ask, and asking is all I am allowed.”

She laughed, short and surprised.

“That is the poorest promise anybody ever made me in a corridor,” she said, “and I believe it, because you are the poorest man in Ostara at promising anything.”

She bent over the stool and wrote, in her own hand, clear and unhurried, the figure she had carried four years, with a date against it and her name under it, and the pencil moved across the paper with a sound he could hear in the quiet, and when she finished she put the date beside it again, because a figure with a date against it is a thing that stands.

She gave it to him with both hands, the way people give a thing they have made, and he took it with both hands, and their fingers touched on the paper and stayed a moment.

Her hands were warm from the counter and his were cold from the street, and the difference went through him like a draught, and he looked at her face and she was looking at his, and something changed in the air of that narrow place, plain and unmistakable, the way weather changes before rain.

“You never laid them side by side,” he said quietly, meaning the books, and she shook her head.

“Never,” she said. “Not once. Ask me why.”

“Why.”

“Because if I did I would have to decide which one of my own people was wrong,” she said, “and I have worked with both of them six years and I am not going to do that to either of them for the sake of seventeen.”

He folded the sheet once and put it in his coat, and she watched him do it, and neither of them looked away.

## The Door Shut

She shut the door of the corridor without locking it, because there was no lock on it, and leaned against it a moment with her shoulders back against the wood, breathing a little fast, and he stood by the shelf with the paper in his coat and his heart beating hard against it.

“You asked me for it,” she said. “Say so.”

“I asked you for it,” he said.

She came to him then, directly, without hesitation, and put her hands on his coat and pulled him toward her, and kissed him hard on the mouth, and he kissed her back, and the pencil was still in her hand and it pressed against his shoulder through the cloth.

She wanted him, and she had wanted him since before September, and saying yes was fast and it was enjoyable to her, and she did not hide either thing.

They went to the stool and she cleared it with one sweep of her arm, and the spare sheets went onto the boards, and he lifted her onto the edge of it and she pulled him between her knees, and her coat was open and his hands were under it, on her waist, on her ribs, feeling her breathe.

Her blouse was thin and her skin was warm through it, and he unbuttoned it with cold fingers and she helped him, impatient, laughing once under her breath, and her breasts were full and pale in the light from the far window, and when he bent and took one in his mouth she gasped and arched and held his head to her with the hand that still held the pencil.

He undid her skirt and pushed it up, and she wore nothing under it but herself, and she was wet already, and when he touched her with his fingers she moved against them openly, with pleasure that was real and specific, sighing and spreading for him, and said his name once, clearly, into the quiet.

“Now,” she said. “Please. Now.”

He freed himself, and she guided him with her hand, sure and unembarrassed, and he entered her standing between her knees with her back against the shelf and the two books four feet apart on either side of them, and she wrapped her legs around him and took him deep, rocking with him, her face open and unguarded.

They moved together hard and unhurried, the stool creaking under them, her breath coming fast against his neck, his hands on her hips holding her to him, and the pleasure built between them cleanly, without reluctance and without performance, until she stiffened and cried out, once, and held him tight through it, and he followed her, spending himself in her with his face buried in her hair.

Afterward they stood a while without moving apart, breathing together, her forehead against his, the pencil still in her hand against his back.

“That cost me the number,” she said at last, quietly, matter-of-fact, without regret. “It cannot be un-counted now. It is on paper with a date. A landlord may put it in front of a magistrate.”

“I know it,” he said.

“And I do not want it back,” she said. “That is the part you need to understand. I got what I wanted in there. Both of us did.”

She dressed herself neatly, buttoning her blouse with steady fingers, pinning her hair back up, and he folded himself away and buttoned his coat, and she picked up the spare sheets from the boards and squared them.

At the door she stopped and looked back at the shelf with the two books on it, four feet apart, unopened.

“The second reading of it will be made by somebody who has never met me,” she said. “That is how it works here. Remember that when you hold it.”

He nodded, because there was nothing else to do, and went out past the counter into the cold with her number in his coat and the warmth of her still on his hands, and tenderness and dread together in his chest, and the knowledge that what they had done in about forty minutes could not be undone.

He wanted her still, and he wanted the number, and he had both, and both had a cost, and the cost was hers more than his, and she had paid it willingly, which was the hardest part of all.

*Two thousand one hundred and seventy-four days. Less two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven, the eighth of February of year seven, is seven. Fifteen less eight is seven by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*Ten since the last such hour in that city, and seventy-nine since her own last one. Pieces of paper are counted as pieces of paper here and never as claims and never as people.*

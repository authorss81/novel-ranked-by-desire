# Two Books Four Feet Apart

Seven days had gone since a sheet that had lain out on a rail in a passage off Cordwain since a year nobody at the desk mentioned had come back from the counter it goes to every quarter with the foot of it copied by hand on the back in somebody else’s pencil.

Two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven was the eighth of February and the fifteenth is day two thousand one hundred and seventy-four, and two thousand one hundred and seventy-four less two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.

He went into the division building off a fourth court at about ten in the morning with nothing under his arm, for the fourth time since the eighth of September.

What he wanted was a figure somebody would check.

There is not one in this building and he had been told so four times, and on the seventh of July he had put four of his own sentences out on open ground to about eleven thousand people and had been believed by about four hundred of them.

## The Counter On The Ground Floor

She came in twenty minutes after him with a round bag and forty-one papers in it, and she turned the side of her that works away from him before she had got the bag off her shoulder, which she has done every time for nine years and has never once explained.

“I have not got the round on the desk and there are two divisions after me.”

“I have not come for the round.”

“Then you have come for about nine minutes and I am going to spend them not looking at you.”

He stood on the side of her that works.

“Ninety-one,” he said.

She put a paper down on the tray about a quarter of an inch further along than it needed to go.

“No.”

“You stopped counting on the eighth of September.”

“I stopped counting on the eighth of September,” said Beatrix Halm, “and I said the reason out loud to a man in this building on that morning, and the reason was that after the fifteenth of September a counter figure is the only kind of figure anybody in this city will accept, and that if I went on holding up ninety-one on my fingers in a lane then I would be the only person in Ostara holding it up, and I was tired.”

“It is not in your head any more.”

“No,” said Beatrix Halm. “It is not. It is not anywhere at all, and that is the first peace I have had since the second year and I am not going to give it back to you because you have come four times in five months.”

## What She Wanted Instead

“I am going to ask you for something and I am going to ask you for it once,” she said, “so hear the shape of it before you answer it.”

“All right.”

“There is a paper going round this city. I have had one off a woman at a counter in the fourth quarter and I have had another off a man who could not write his own name and had it written for him, and both of them had the whole of the bottom of your sheet copied out on them by hand, and both of them had something written in the two inches.”

“I know. One came through a front door in a month and there were about nine made by hand.”

“Then you know it is a claim,” said Beatrix Halm, “and you know what this building does with a claim, and you know what your building does with one, and I am telling you what I want.”

She turned the tray about ninety degrees.

“I want you to make a copy enterable.”

He did not answer straight away, and she watched him not answer, and gave him the time, which she had not been doing for five months.

“A clerk may only enter what she is given,” he said. “And what this building has been given is a piece of paper. It is not a person. A person is not a claim, and a claim is the only thing there is a column for in any office in this city, and there is not one column from here to that building for a piece of paper with a month and a lane on it.”

“Then get the person.”

“There is a person. I do not have their name and neither do you, and if I had their name by the end of the week then I would have a claim and it would go into a column, and the two inches under that striking have been unreadable by any instrument in this city for six years and you are asking me to fill one.”

“I am asking you to open one,” said Beatrix Halm.

“No,” he said. “There is no opening. There is only a column and a date and a name, and a name has to come from a person who says it out loud, and nobody who has written in those two inches is going to say it out loud to a man in a rank-one coat, and we both know that and you knew it before you asked me.”

She squared the tray again.

“All right,” she said. “That is the answer and it took you about nine seconds, and I would have given you the same one before I asked.”

## The Number On Her Fingers

He did not leave.

That was the part he has been asked about since, afterwards, by nobody. He did not leave at the end of the nine minutes and he did not leave at the end of the twenty, and the two divisions behind him came and went at the end of the counter and nobody said a word to either of them.

“Show me the counting,” he said.

Beatrix Halm put the tray down on the table and squared it to the edge.

“You want to know how I hold a figure,” she said. “I hold it the only way anybody who is not the machine holds one.”

She put her left hand on the edge of the tray and used the fingers of it, in order, the way a person does with a thing they have done a great many times.

“One round at a time at the end of a round, in a lane, out loud,” said Beatrix Halm. “The last one goes on top of the one before it. I never take anything back off, and I have never once said a figure in this building that I did not count with my hands in the open air, and that is the only reason any of it is worth anything, because there is nobody standing at four o’clock who could take a quarter of ninety-one off me.”

“Do it.”

“It takes about nine seconds.”

“Do it anyway.”

She counted it on her fingers with her eyes shut, out loud, in the order she had always counted it in, and the bad ear was turned towards the window and the good ear was turned towards him, and she said the first one as a whole round and the second one eleven minutes after it and the third one nine days after that, and he wrote what she said and he did not interrupt her once.

He had the figure in his hand in about nine seconds.

A number on your fingers is a thing you can only keep. He understood that better standing there than he had understood it at a table in September, because she had just given it up in nine seconds and had not written it down and would go on not writing it down.

“Write it,” he said.

“No.”

“Beatrix. It is in my hand now and I am a man, and a man who has heard it in a corridor can say it anywhere he likes, and I would rather you had it in your own writing.”

“That,” said Beatrix Halm, “is exactly why I am not going to write it. It is the reason you have it and it is the reason I have not. If it is in my hand it is a thing in a division and it gets read.”

“It gets read.”

“Nothing stops at one set of hands,” said Beatrix Halm. “A woman in a district holds the sheet and puts what she makes of it in the volume at the end of that table, and then the sheet travels, and a second woman with a tray reads it cold and puts her reading beside the first one in a second volume, and nobody in between is permitted to see both volumes at once.”

She pointed past him with the pencil and did not touch anything.

“Both of them are right about the paper. One of them is right about the number and the other one is not, and there is nobody in this building who has ever held the two volumes open at the same time and asked.”

## The Second Reading

She went into the corridor behind the counter and he followed her, and the corridor is eleven feet long and it has two shelves on it four feet apart with a ledger on each, and the near one has a green cloth back and the far one does not.

“Six hundred and ninety-one,” said Beatrix Halm, off the near shelf.

“Six hundred and seventy-four,” he said, off the far one.

“Seventeen.”

“Seventeen.”

“Nineteen quarters out of the twenty I have come out of this building at seventeen,” said Beatrix Halm. “Six of them belong to a counter that is not mine and four of them are the same person twice in an afternoon, and three of them are one woman four times in a fortnight, and that is thirteen. The other four I have never been able to name and I stopped expecting to in the third year of counting them.”

She turned round.

“Now I am going to do the thing this division does to a piece of paper, and I am going to do it to my own figure, and I am going to do it twice, and you are going to hear both readings and you are not going to be able to tell me which one is the true one.”

“All right.”

## Read It Twice

The first time she did it standing up, in the corridor, with her bag still on her shoulder.

It was over before he had got the first word out of his mouth, and she did not enjoy it and neither did he, and it was plainly the thing two people do at the end of a counter on a day when neither of them has said the other thing yet.

Afterwards she leaned on the shelf with the green cloth back and got her breath and pushed herself off it.

“You have got a spent chest,” she said.

“I have.”

“I have watched you come up that stair at this counter four times since the eighth of September,” said Beatrix Halm, “and you do it in four, and you did it in three when you came in this morning, and you think nobody in this building counts landings.”

“Nobody in this building counts anything except you.”

“I count everything,” she said, “and it is not a gift.”

Then she took her coat off and hung it on the end of the tray handle, and she turned her own ear round to him and held the side of her head with two fingers for a second the way she does when she is making sure of a thing.

“Now the second reading,” she said, “and the second reading is at the same speed and nothing is skipped, and that is the only rule this division has that I have ever agreed with.”

She did not wait for an answer.

Her bag went down on the floor and the tray went off the end of the table onto the ledge with forty-one papers in it, and neither of them cared about the tray. She got his shirt open before he had worked out that she was going to, and she was not gentle about it and she was not careful either. She was thorough, in the way of a woman who reads a paper the second time because the rule says the second time.

She had him against the shelf with the green cloth back, which took his weight, and then she stopped and made him turn round.

“Face to the shelves,” she said. “I want the two of them in front of me while I am doing it, because you are going to look at them afterwards and I am not going to be able to tell you which one is the true one and you are going to spend the rest of the day trying.”

He turned round. Two ledgers, one on each shelf, four feet apart in an eleven-foot corridor, both open at the same page.

She got up behind him and got into him slowly and she made him watch the two books while she did it, and neither book ever went anywhere and neither of them moved at all.

There was nowhere in that corridor to put a hand. She tried the shelf and found the ledger on it and took her hand back, and put it on the edge of the table instead, and then came back to him when she had finished deciding.

“Stay there,” she said. “That is the second reading. Nothing skipped.”

She was not quiet about it. That was the thing he had not been ready for, because a woman who counts rounds in lanes and argues about a difference of seventeen at ten in the morning is a person he had built a version of in his head, and the version was wrong. She was loud in the way of somebody enjoying an argument she is winning, and she enjoyed this one, and she told him so twice.

She got a hand round the front of him and held him at the pace she wanted and not one beat faster, and she put her good ear against his jaw so that she could hear what he was doing without turning her head.

After a while she began counting under her breath. She did not notice herself doing it and he noticed, and he did not stop her, and it went on for a while and then it turned into something else that was not a number.

“Forty-one,” she said, into his neck, and then, about four seconds later, in a completely different voice: “Sorry.”

“Keep going.”

“I am counting the corridor and I have not counted the corridor since the third year,” she said, “and if you tell anybody I did that I will put you through the round book.”

“Seventeen,” she said, at his throat, at nothing in particular.

“Seventeen,” he said.

“If you count out loud I will stop.”

He did not count out loud.

He had it in about four seconds and she took him over afterwards with a hand and did not let him do any of it, and she said his name once and did not say it again because she had heard it.

He put his hands on her hips and held her there while she worked and she let him, and at one point she said *right* in the way a person says it when a line in a book has come out the way it was read.

She got there first and then made him finish, which she had not been asked to do and which he understood about a minute afterwards.

She put her forehead against his collarbone when it was done and kept her hands on him, and her hair was damp at the temple on the side that works and cold on the side that does not, and neither of them said anything for about as long as it takes to count a round in a lane.

“That,” said Beatrix Halm, “is what I have been wanting since the eighth of September and I did not know that was what it was.”

He had a spent chest and four landings in him and he had gone up a stair in three that morning, and none of that was in it.

“That,” said Beatrix Halm, “is both readings, and they are not the same, and both of them are correct, and nobody in this city is ever going to be able to tell you which of them was the paper.”

## What It Costs A Number

“There,” said Beatrix Halm. “That is both readings, and they are not the same, and both of them are correct, and nobody in this city is ever going to be able to tell you which of them was the paper.”

She got her bag off the floor and sat down on the end of the table in the corridor with her coat over her knees.

“Now give me a sheet.”

“There is no sheet in here.”

“There is one in your coat and you have had it in your coat since about the seventh of July,” said Beatrix Halm, “because that is what you do, and I am not going to be the one who says it out loud twice.”

He gave her the sheet.

She wrote on it in her own hand, and she wrote it the way she counts it, which is forwards, and she wrote ninety-one against the quarter and the quarter against the quarter, and she wrote the year on it and not the office and not the division.

Then she stopped with the pencil about an inch above it.

“If I put my name under that,” she said, “then it is a claim. And a claim is a thing you can argue with. And you can argue it with me and you can argue it with the person who counted it, and that person is you, and there is a man in this city who takes demands in front of a magistrate and has done since the seventh of July and will do again before the spring.”

“Yes.”

“A figure with a date against it is a thing that may be put in front of a magistrate,” said Beatrix Halm.

“It is the only thing I have ever counted that a person could put in front of one, and I have been the only person who knew it for four years, and I have never been able to be protected by it, because nobody could use it.”

“The second I write it down somebody can use it against me,” she said, “and it will be the first time in nine years that a figure of mine has been in the hands of somebody who wanted one.”

She wrote her name under it.

She dated it.

She put the pencil down on the tray and turned the sheet round so that it was the right way up for him and did not let go of the corner of it for about a second longer than she needed to.

“I do not want it back,” she said.

“All right.”

“I said I do not want it back, and you are going to hear it twice and I am saying it once.”

## Filed

She put the sheet in the division’s own tray, at the bottom, under forty-one papers that had come off a round that morning, and she did not take it out again.

“I am filing it in my own division and not in yours,” said Beatrix Halm. “That is on purpose and you know it is on purpose and you would not have done it any other way.”

“The tray goes to the desk at the end of the week and the desk will read it at the same speed and enter it in the far book. That is the second reading of a figure I counted on my fingers for four years, and the second reading is the one that stands.”

“That is how this building has always worked,” she said, “and it is not going to change for me.”

“Who reads it.”

“A woman called Eda who has been at that desk for two divisions and who has never met me and who thinks I am a person who argues about returns,” said Beatrix Halm. “And she will be right about the sheet and she will be wrong about it and neither of those two things can be shown to anybody, because we read everything twice and we have never once laid the two books side by side.”

She picked the tray up and went out into the counting hall with it, and she was gone about nine seconds and came back with it squared.

“That is your figure,” she said. “It is not a count of deeds and it is not on any page in this city and it is not in any column anywhere, and there is a sheet of paper in a tray in this building with a date on it and a name on it and no office behind it of any kind, and if a landlord puts it in front of a magistrate in the spring I would want it to be true and I have just made it arguable.”

*Two thousand one hundred and seventy-four days. Less two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven, the eighth of February of the seventh year, is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.*

*Two thousand one hundred and seventy-four less two thousand and eighty-four, the fifteenth of November of the sixth year, is ninety days, which is three months exactly. That is ten chapters since the last time anybody was read a page aloud in order, by the only person in this city entitled to read one.*

*Less the eleventh of June of the fifth year, one thousand five hundred and seventy, is six hundred and four days, which is twenty months with four days after them, and that is seventy-nine chapters since the last time this woman was in a room that was not about a return.*

*The round book says six hundred and ninety-one and the desk book says six hundred and seventy-four and the difference is seventeen, and the two books stand four feet apart on two shelves in a corridor eleven feet long and have never been laid side by side in nine years. This morning she laid a sheet of paper of her own at the bottom of a tray, and it is going to be read twice, and one of the two readings is the true one.*

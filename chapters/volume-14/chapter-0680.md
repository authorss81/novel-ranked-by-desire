# Four Hundred More

Seven days had gone since Emil Brack had put a chalk down on a ledge at a wall end and said, without being asked for anything, that the only thing he has ever wanted at ten to six is for one of his nine figures to be found wrong.

Lin Hao had not been able to get that out of his head in a week. Somebody has been at a wall end for four years waiting for a stranger to come and check him, and in July nobody came, and the reason nobody came is that there is no instrument in this city that can check a figure said out loud in a lane.

Two thousand three hundred and forty-eight was the ninth of August and the sixteenth is day two thousand three hundred and fifty-five, and two thousand three hundred and fifty-five less two thousand three hundred and forty-eight is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

Two thousand three hundred and fifty-five less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is two hundred and seventy-seven days, which is nine months with seven days after them.

Take instead the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen: two thousand three hundred and fifty-five less that is six hundred and thirty-seven days, which is twenty-one months and seven days.

Neither of those two is four hundred and eleven, and that figure has been printed twice in six years against two different anchors, and both times the figure was right and only the anchor was in doubt, which is the cheapest way for a figure to be wrong and the only way nobody catches.

## Ten In The Morning And Two Cardboard Boxes

There were two cardboard boxes under the third table of a laundry, and they had not been there in March.

He counted them himself while she was pressing, and it took him about nine minutes, and he counted them twice and got the same figure both times, and he told her the figure before she told it to him.

“Four hundred and nine,” said Perla Quist.

“Of pieces of paper.”

“Of pieces of paper that have come through that door since the ninth of January,” said Perla Quist. “That is not a count of claims. It is not a count of people. Two people have come through this door three times each since that morning and I have counted all three. About nine came in the last fortnight and I have not asked one of them where they were before.”

“Where did the other four hundred get to.”

“In pockets,” said Perla Quist. “In about four hundred pockets in about four hundred kitchens, which is where they have been since January, and there is no book in this city that says so except about three hundred and forty of these lines in a pencil, and I am going to tell you something about that book before you ask me for it.”

## What He Asked For

He asked for it in one sentence and in the form he was entitled to use, which is the form of an instruction, and he heard the form while he was saying it.

“I want the ledger.”

Perla Quist did not put down the bundle she was carrying and she did not take a step back and she did not stop what she was doing.

“No,” she said.

He had about eleven seconds in which he could have said it was an office matter and about four in which he could have said it was for the people in the boxes, and he used none of them, and afterwards he understood that that was the whole of what he had done.

“Say the reason,” he said, “and say all of it.”

“A name in a ledger is a different thing from a name in a claim,” said Perla Quist.

She put the bundle down on the second table and squared it and came and sat down at the third one, which she had not done since the seventeenth of June, when he had asked her for a book of her own and she had said no in about nine seconds.

“A name in my book is a person who came to a counter in this city and was refused and went home again with nothing. That is what I have been writing down since the thirtieth of September, nineteen years of it, and it is my own business and there is no office behind it of any kind.”

“And a name in a claim.”

“A name in a claim is a person something was done to,” said Perla Quist. “Put one of those in a drawer in a building nobody can walk into from a street, and in about four years somebody opens the drawer, and there is no form anywhere in this city that says which of the two they are, and the answer will be that they are all the second, and it will be assumed, and I will not be here to say it was the first.”

She looked at him for about two seconds.

“And I have been keeping mine in my own book since the end of September and I am not going to have it in a drawer. That is not politics. I have run this laundry nineteen years and nobody has ever been able to make me sign anything and I am not going to hand the only thing I have never signed over to a man who came in here with nothing in his hands.”

## Four Hundred And Nine Pieces Of Paper

Then she gave him the boxes.

She did it without any ceremony at all. She got up, put the first box on the third table, took eleven sheets off the top, squared that stack against the wood, and brought the second box over and set it beside the first.

“Four hundred and nine pieces of paper,” said Perla Quist. “Every one of them has the foot of your page copied out on it in a different hand and something written under the four sentences that is not yours and not mine. I have not read the two inches on any of them. It is not mine and you were not going to ask me to.”

“I am not going to read them either.”

“Then take them to whatever you have been keeping them in,” said Perla Quist, “and do not come back here and tell me what you did with them, because I have told you what I think of it.”

He stood and looked at the two boxes for about a minute.

“Where do you want me to put them.”

“Not in a drawer,” said Perla Quist. “Anywhere a person can walk in. Not that building. You knew why before I said it.”

He knew it before she said it. It took him about a minute there on the third table with his hands on a box of somebody else’s handwriting, because on the thirtieth of March he had asked a clerk to hold ninety pieces of pencil paper, and he had wanted it more than he had wanted anything in two years.

He got what he asked for on the thirtieth of March.

And he had been unable to read one word of it ever since, and that sheet had been describing the building he was standing in ever since, and about nine people a week read the whole of it to its last line.

## Two Streets And Four Floors And A Spent Chest

He had them up at about half past one.

It is four hundred and nine sheets of the kind of rag paper a laundry keeps, which is heavier than anything else sold by the sheet in this city, and two boxes of it is about as much as a man can get up four flights who has had a chest spent permanently since the fourth year.

He got them up four floors one at a time and sat down on the second and the third, and he was about nine minutes from being able to stop at either of them.

He had them on a table in the room off a third court at about two when Yusuf Adeyemi came in with his coat on, looked at them, did not ask, and said, “That is a lot of paper.”

“That is four hundred and nine pieces of it.”

“Then that is about four hundred and nine,” said Yusuf Adeyemi, “and it is a count of paper and not of anything else. The second column of that sheet stands at two lines and it will stand at two lines on the thirtieth of this month, and I am not going to be asked to do one thing more about any of this by anybody, including you.”

“That is correct.”

“I know it is correct,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “I have been the Registrar of a thing whose meaning I have never explained to anybody, and I am not going to start explaining it in the middle of an afternoon because there are two boxes on my table.”

## The Copy Of The Index Is Longer Than The Index

He got his coat off at about half past two because he had to sit down for a minute.

There was one more thing in the coat and he knew it was there before his hand found it. Nine ruled sheets in pencil, made on the twenty-fourth of June by a woman cutting her own paper with a linen blade. A hundred and ninety dates on them, and against every date a ruled space with nothing in it.

He put it on the table next to the boxes.

He did not put it in the drawer, and there was about four feet between the table and the drawer and he did not cross those four feet, and it was the only decision he made that afternoon that he was going to be able to live with.

Because she had told him at ten o’clock in the morning that she was not going to have it in a drawer in a building nobody can walk into from a street, and he could not put her book there, and he could not put a copy of her book there either without deciding for her that she had not meant what she said.

And then he understood the rest of it, and it took him about a minute, sitting down.

About nine copies of that index were in this city and every one of them had been made by somebody else out of hers, and about nine hundred people could read every one of them, and one of the copies in a yard off the Vestry was longer than the index by a page and a half.

Not because more had been added to it, but because nine people had written in the margin of it, in pencil, in public, over seven weeks, every one of them correcting a date or a place or the spelling of a lane, and none of them wrong about the corrections, and every one of them a person who has touched the only index in the city unasked.

His copy was the shortest thing about the index in the whole of Ostara.

*Two thousand three hundred and fifty-five days. Less two thousand three hundred and forty-eight, the ninth of August of the seventh year, is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is two hundred and seventy-seven days, which is nine months with seven days after them. A shutter at the far end of a yard off the Vestry has been bare for all of them and about two thousand people a day walk past a pale rectangle and cannot get by it.*

*Four hundred and nine pieces of pencil paper are in a room off a third court in two cardboard boxes that nobody in that building is obliged to open.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is six hundred and thirty-seven days, which is twenty-one months with seven days after them. Four sentences, his own hand out of one morning in the fifth year, and about nine hundred people in this city can read every one of them.*

*A woman off a street two streets wide has copied it out four hundred and nine times in her own hand, and has refused, in her own room, with the reason given out loud, to give the book it is all written in to anybody at all.*

## What It Closes And What It Costs

The option that closed was this.

For four months there had been an index in this city. One index. About three hundred and forty lines in pencil in a hand that could be got at, in a room off a street two streets wide, with a woman in it who said out loud in front of nine hundred people when she got it wrong.

And from this afternoon the office cannot hold it.

Not because the office refused it. Because a woman with a pencil said no to the only person in Ostara entitled to ask her, and gave a structural reason, and was right, and had been keeping that book in her own hands since the end of September for nineteen years of a habit that had nothing to do with him.

So the index is not in a building with no door onto a street, and it is not going to be, and the office is the only body in this city that could have held the one index in this city that anybody maintains, and it has been told, out loud, by the person who keeps it, that it may not.

That is what it costs.

The office is now handed four hundred and nine pieces of paper, which is the whole of the evidence there is, and not the one instrument in this city that could say what any of it meant or when it arrived or whether it arrived twice.

And every person whose name is in that book — nineteen years of them, a counter off Cordwain and a laundry off a street and every counter in between — has in about four years to find out that the only woman in Ostara who kept them straight would not hand the book to anybody, including him, including the office, including a drawer.

And he spent the one thing he is allowed to spend.

Nobody asked him to come to that laundry on the sixteenth of August. Nobody asked the office. He is the only person that office may ask, he went, and he asked a woman who cannot be asked, and she said no, and he is the only man in Ostara who could ever have asked her, and he spent him on that.

At about five he went down and along and up the stairs with his hands open, having carried two boxes up four floors and come down with nothing he had asked for.

In March he had asked an office to keep paper of that kind in a drawer, and he had got it, and it had been the best thing he had done in two years, and there is a man in this city who has not been able to look inside that drawer since.

And on the sixteenth of August he asked for a book and was refused by a woman who was right, and what he carried away was four hundred and nine pieces of paper and no index, and the four hundred and nine went into two boxes in a room nobody can walk into from a street, and the index went back out into about nine hands that have already made it longer than it is.

Same drawer. Same man. Second time.

# About Two Hundred At A Corner

Seven days had gone since a woman had stood four feet off him at a kerb with eleven sheets in her arms and asked him a question, and he had answered it correctly, and nothing about it had changed.

Two thousand four hundred and six was the seventh of October and the fourteenth is day two thousand four hundred and thirteen, and two thousand four hundred and thirteen less two thousand four hundred and six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## What A Place With Nothing On It Does

About two hundred people a day stop where that wall meets that kerb, and the ones who get down low enough to make out the two words cut into the bottom of it are counted in ones and twos a week and not in tens.

Seven days after the sheet went up at four on the thirtieth of September, about two hundred and eighty people came past that kerb between ten and one, and about ninety of them stood and read what was on the board, and nine knelt down at the foot of the wall, and not one of them did both.

The sentence going round the four hundred kitchens that week was not about the wall and it was not about the four inches.

It was the second of the four sentences, and it was wrong about forty times inside an hour, and every single wrong version got put right inside about four minutes, in daylight, out loud, by somebody standing there — which is what that corner has done since the middle of July and which this city did not build.

It was also completely useless, and he had been in a room off a third court with a woman with a pencil about four days when he worked out why.

They were all correcting each other about a page that most of them had never read.

## Nine People Being Right And Wrong At Once

He watched it from four feet off for about two hours.

A man said the office had never been given anything in its life. A woman beside him said no, they had started in the summer. A third person said the whole thing was a load of rubbish because the office takes things and gives you nothing back, which is different from both of those, and got an argument out of the first two about it.

And then about thirty people looked up at the board, because a boy with a paste brush was putting the month’s form up on it and the form was on the board and the board had the twenty-seventh sheet’s foot copied out on it in chalk from August, and somebody read it out loud from the bottom.

They got the second of the four right. They got the striking right. They got the six lines right and the two inches right.

Then somebody at the back said, well, that is what it says now, and the twenty-seventh one is different, and the twenty-seventh one is on a rail in Cordwain.

About nine people at that kerb believed him. About nine could not tell whether to. The other hundred and eighty did not care, and about forty said out loud that the man who writes sentences has been wrong before and can be wrong again.

Every one of those forty was arguing about a sheet and not one of them had a copy of one. About four hundred people in this city did, in four hundred kitchens, and none of them was standing at that kerb.

Lin Hao stood there with his hands in his pockets and understood the whole of it in about nine minutes, and what he understood was that the machine at that corner does one thing and one thing only, and the one thing it does is correct a sentence that everybody in the room can see.

And about four hundred pieces of paper in four hundred kitchens could not be seen by anybody.

## A Column With Nothing In It That He Can Fill In

He went down a street two streets wide at about half past two and Perla Quist was at the third table with a ruler in her hand and nine sheets of her own paper cut and squared on the second one.

She did not look up when she said it.

“There is going to be a fourth column.”

“There is not.”

“There is,” said Perla Quist. “I have ruled it at about two o’clock and it is empty and you may look at it for as long as you like.”

He looked at it for about nine seconds. It is ruled the same width as the others, it is the narrowest thing on the page, and there is no heading on it at all, which is not an oversight and is a decision.

“Why is it blank at the top.”

“Because I do not know what the heading is yet and I am not going to put one on it until I do,” said Perla Quist.

 “I have had nine sheets on this table since the seventeenth of June and about three hundred and forty lines on them and there is a date and a place and a number of months on nine of them and a ruled space for a name that is empty on every line, and every one of those three hundred and forty lines is a piece of paper somebody copied out of a printed sheet in their own hand.”

She put the ruler down.

“I have been keeping a list of when people wrote things down,” she said. “Not what they wrote them about. When. I have never once been asked and I never once wanted to, because the foot of that page had not moved since the ninth of November of the fifth year and there was nothing for a date to be out of date with.”

“The thirtieth of September.”

“The thirtieth of September,” said Perla Quist. “The page went up at four with three lines in the second column, and about four hundred copies in this city do not have the third line, and there is no instrument in Ostara that can go into a kitchen and find that out, and I cannot, and neither can you, and neither can the nine people who can read anything at all.”

She picked the ruler up and turned the top sheet round so it faced him.

“That column is for what the sheet said on the day the copy was made,” said Perla Quist, “and it is the first thing in this city that has ever been a record of a thing being true against anything.”

*Two thousand four hundred and thirteen days. Less two thousand three hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, is a hundred and four days, which is three months with fourteen days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is three hundred and thirty-five days, which is eleven months with five days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard off the Vestry has been bare for every one of them, and about two thousand people a day go past a pale rectangle at the end of a yard.*

*The corner on the hill is about two hundred a day. It is a different place and the two numbers are not the same number.*

## The Want He Cannot Get

He wanted to be able to correct it himself. That was the whole of the want and it took him until the middle of the afternoon to get it far enough into the open to look at it.

He had spent nine years being the only hand in a city that could put a sentence somewhere official, and there was about nine hundred people who could check him now, and he had spent the summer standing in the middle of that being praised for it in a yard of eleven thousand and had been ashamed of it every time.

And there were about four hundred pieces of pencil paper in about four hundred kitchens that were one line out of date, and he could go to about four of them himself because he knew where about four of them were, and he was not going to go to any of them, and there was not one thing in this city that would let him go to four hundred.

That is the shape of it and it had been the shape of it since the second of February and he had made it worse nine times in seven months and better once, in June, by standing nine feet off a wall with nothing in his hands, and about forty people in this city had said out loud that week that a sentence on that wall came from him.

He went down a street two streets wide at about five with nothing in his hands, which is the way he goes everywhere now, and he stopped at the kerb of the hill because he had not walked past it since the seventh and because he wanted to see whether about two hundred people a day really does come.

About two hundred and ten came past between five and six.

Ninety-odd stopped at the board and nine went down on a knee and nobody did either and both, and at the bottom of the new render there was a boy with a paste brush and about eighteen inches of band still to do and a jar on his shoulder, and he was not going to ask him anything either.

At the bottom of nine feet of new render there are four inches of dressed stone, and there have been for more than thirty years, and two words have been cut into them clean through the face of the stone, the left-hand one deeper than the right-hand one, and about two hundred people a day stop on a corner to look at them.

About nine of them a week get low enough to read them. There is no form anywhere in this city for the two inches of nothing at the foot of a printed page and there is now, for the first time in seven months, a column somewhere in this city that can say what the page said on the day a person wrote in it.

And the two words were cut into that block of stone before it was ever delivered to a wall, and a docket in a yard nine streets from the works department says so, and nobody in seven years has asked who cut them, and there is no column anywhere in this city for that either.

# The Yard Reads It Wrong

Seven days had gone since a man had refused, politely and permanently, to let a date off a docket go into a book, and since then he had been thinking about what about four hundred people do with a thing when there is nobody to check them.

Two thousand two hundred and sixty-eight was the nineteenth of May and the twenty-sixth is day two thousand two hundred and seventy-five, and two thousand two hundred and seventy-five less two thousand two hundred and sixty-eight is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

About four hundred pieces of paper are in about four hundred pockets in this city. That is a count of paper and not a count of claims and not a count of people, and it has been true since about the ninth of January and it was true about a fortnight before a woman on a step in a yard off the Vestry said a name out loud to four hundred strangers.

He went up the hill at about eleven to find out what happens to a sentence in a crowd.

## What A Yard Does With A Thing

The machine this city has got for knowing whether a thing is true is a division that goes over every paper twice, puts the first reading in one book and the second in another, and never sets the two books down next to each other. Seventeen in a quarter come apart, and thirteen of the seventeen can be named. It takes a thing, reads it, reads it again, and refuses to let the two readings meet.

About eleven thousand people in a yard have no such machine and are not going to be given one.

What they have instead is about nine hundred people who can read everything that has ever been on a board, standing in the same space, at the same time, in daylight.

He got to the flat space at the top of the path at about eleven and about nine hundred people were in it and the thing that had happened there had already finished and was being redone badly about forty times an hour by people who had not been in it when it happened.

## The Version That Went Round

The one that was going round was not his.

It was that a woman on the steps had said out loud that there is a list kept somewhere and that the office will not tell anybody what is on it and that people who were written down on it were not told.

Which was three true things, none of which was on the steps, joined together by about nine hundred people in a space the size of a hall.

He listened to it go past him about eleven times in about a quarter of an hour and he did not correct any of it, because he has no standing in that yard and has had none since a morning in the second year, and because about half of it is what he wrote and the other half is what a yard does to what he wrote.

The clerk would have entered it as given.

## Forty Times Inside An Hour

It got it wrong, and it got it wrong in the ordinary way.

A man near the rail had it that the list is a list of men who owed money. A woman about four feet along from him said no, it is a list of things that were done to people. A third man said it is a list of names of the dead.

And the first man said his brother-in-law heard it, and his brother-in-law is not a man who goes about this yard, and about eleven other people joined in, and it came out even inside about ninety seconds, out loud, in daylight, by people who could read.

Then somebody said it a different wrong way in the arcade and about forty people carried it out of the arcade.

He counted the errors he heard in about an hour and lost count twice and had to start again, and the number was about forty, and every one of the forty was caught inside about four minutes by somebody in the crowd.

Not one of the forty survived more than about a quarter of an hour.

That is the thing. He wrote that sentence in a room two years ago to stop a city believing a figure nobody could check, and it has been working exactly as he built it for every person in this city who can read, and about four hundred pieces of that foot in pencil went into that yard in about four hundred pockets, and about nine hundred people in it could correct anybody and about eleven thousand could not.

A sentence can be argued with. A figure can only be counted. In that yard, in daylight, for nothing, about nine hundred people argued with his sentences in public and got most of it right, and not one of them had any way of finding out whether any of it was so.

## What He Wanted There

He wanted to be in it.

Not to speak. He has been about nine streets away from any corner he does not own for six years and he wanted nothing on a step. He wanted to be one of the about nine hundred who can stop a wrong version in four minutes, because that is the position he has wanted since the seventh of December, and because being in that position would mean that about forty things said wrong in a yard in a month were stopped by a man who could do something about them.

There is no such man. There is an office with a column headed carried by and two lines in it, and the older of those two lines predates everybody who works behind that counter.

He stood in the flat space at the top of that path for about an hour and got it wrong about nine times himself before he understood that he was doing it, and what he was doing it with was four sentences at the foot of a printed sheet, and those four sentences do not say what nine hundred people in that yard now think they say.

## Ten To Six At The Wall End

Nine or ten people were at that wall end when he arrived at ten to six and there were about five times that many by six, because that end of that lane is where a city goes to stand next to strangers.

Brack had his slate propped on its end with the twelve books squared beside it. He has always used slate, and the reason is that there is no way to take a figure back off slate once it is on, and the whole of his method depends on there being no way back.

He read nine figures and said the reason for each one out loud before he wrote the first, and the date and the weather went against every line and a reason went in the margin against every one he ringed.

“You were in the yard this morning,” he said.

“I was.”

“I was in it too. I go where people stand.” He put the chalk down. “There is no need for you to say anything about it and I am going to say the thing that is useful and then I am going to stop, and I am not going to ask you for anything, and I am telling you that now so that you cannot say afterwards that I offered.”

“All right.”

“About nine hundred of them can read,” said Emil Brack. “That is the yard, not the city. And about four hundred of them got it wrong inside an hour and every single wrong one was caught inside about four minutes by a bystander and not one of the wrong ones lived past a quarter of an hour.”

He squared the books.

“That is the best thing anybody has ever built in this city and nobody arranged it and it took two sentences and four hundred pieces of paper and about three months.”

“Then what is the thing that is useful.”

“You have built me a bigger version of me,” said Emil Brack.

He said it without heat and he went on reading.

“I have stood at this end of this wall four years saying nine about two thousand times and I have never once been able to check whether any of the nine is true, and that is the whole of my standing, and there is no page anywhere in this city that would let me check one, and I would not use a page if there were.”

He looked up for about a second.

“This morning about nine hundred people said a thing out loud in a yard and every one of them could check whether the person before them had said it true, and not one of them can check whether it is true,” said Emil Brack. “Nine hundred readers and no instrument, and about forty errors an hour going round and being caught, and that is me with a crowd instead of with a wall, except that I never had a crowd and nobody ever handed me a piece of paper.”

He put the chalk down and did not pick it up again.

“Find out what it is called. Not the list. The other thing.”

“The other thing.”

“The thing where nine hundred people correct each other out loud and nobody can check any of it,” said Emil Brack.
“I have been that man on my own for four years at ten to six and I have got nine figures a morning and I have never once been able to make anybody come. And you have made nine hundred people do it in one morning by writing something at the foot of a sheet and I do not know what to call that and I have not got a slate for it.”

Nobody says thank you to him. That has been the shape of it for four years and he set it that way himself and would not have it otherwise.

## What He Is Going To Do About It

He walked back up the hill at about seven with nothing in his hands.

About nine hundred people in this city can read everything that has ever been on a board, and about four hundred have a piece of pencil paper with his own sentences at the top of it and something they wrote themselves underneath, and this morning in a yard they corrected each other out loud about forty times an hour and were right about eleven times an hour and neither figure is a figure.

A man in a delivery coat at the end of a wall has been doing for four years what about nine hundred people did in that yard this morning, alone, on the same principle, with no crowd and no page, and he has told about forty people about it six days a week and not one of them has ever come.

And the only person in Ostara who can check any of it is a woman auditing a branch who certified the sheet and refused to certify a copy, in a room with a shut door in it, three weeks ago.

There is no instrument for this. There is a drawer with about ninety pieces of pencil paper in it and no entry against any of them and no form for a holding.

There is one thing he could do about it and it would take about four inches of stone and a morning, and he did not do it, and he thought about it on the way down and again at the bottom of his own stairs, and he did not do it that night either.

*Two thousand two hundred and seventy-five days. Less two thousand two hundred and sixty-eight, the nineteenth of May of the seventh year, is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and ninety-seven days, which is six months with seventeen days after them. A shutter at the far end of a yard has had nothing at the foot of it for all of them, and about nine hundred people in that yard this morning corrected each other about a sentence he wrote, correctly, forty times an hour, and not one of the forty lasted a quarter of an hour.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and fifty-seven days, which is eighteen months with seventeen days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and a man in a delivery coat has said nine out loud about two thousand times in four years and has never once been able to check whether any of the nine is true, and this morning nine hundred people did the thing he does, in a yard, for nothing, and he says that is a bigger version of him and he wants to know what it is called.*

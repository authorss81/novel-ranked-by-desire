# The Page Is In The Demand

Seven days had gone since a keeper of real books struck an entry out of her own book with a pen because he had read it aloud to her, and in all of them he had not managed to put down what it was he could not stop thinking about: a man who takes a thing off a counter in the ordinary way and does an ordinary thing with it.

Two thousand three hundred and sixteen was the seventh of July and the thirteenth is day two thousand three hundred and twenty-two, and two thousand three hundred and twenty-two less two thousand three hundred and sixteen is six, and thirteen less seven is six by the month count.

## Served On The Sixth Of July

The man with the badge walked it back through the front door at about eleven on the sixth, which is the same door and the same man as on the third of June, and he was not in a hurry this time.

“This one I am serving,” he said. “I want you to have that said in your own book if you keep one. Last time I said the opposite and I have thought about it since and I was right to say it, and this time I am not allowed to say it.”

He laid it on the counter beside the door, took hold of one corner, and made the edges true.

It was a demand. A tenancy, a sum, a period of about nine months, and the sum was not large.

And at the top of it, above the landlord’s own heading, there was a sheet of paper.

The whole of it, nothing cut off and nothing written on. A figure at the top that has not moved in nine quarters. Under it a long column of accounts, every one of them dated and timed and carrying no sum at all. Then a narrow column with two entries in it. Then four lines of sentences at the bottom, and under those six more with the middle one struck clean out, a name written under the stroke, and a band of blank paper about the depth of two fingers.

Nobody had cut a strip off it and pasted it in. Nobody had copied it out in pencil. It was the sheet itself, and it was at the top of a demand in front of a magistrate, and it was there because the address at the head of every line the office prints is a building off a third court.

## What The Office Can Do About It

Nothing.

Yusuf Adeyemi put an end to that in about nine seconds, at about half past eleven, and nobody in the room argued a word of it.

A clerk can only write down what somebody hands her, word for word; she cannot tidy it, and nobody has the right to rush her. There was no entry to make, because there was nothing on the face of the paper that the office could put its hand to. A sheet of pencil paper is not a deed. A demand is not a return. And there is no form on which an office explains itself to a court.

“You can send a man,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “You are the only person this office may send and you may go and stand at the back of the room and say nothing at all for as long as you like, and it will be the truth, and it will not help.”

“Can I say anything.”

“No,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “Anything you say in there is the opinion of a man in a rank-one coat with nothing behind it, and that is worse than nothing, because it will be written down.”

So he went, at about half past one, in his ordinary coat, and he was permitted into a room with a table in it off a court because he was named at the head of a piece of paper, and he stood at the back.

## Eleven Times

The tenant was there with a woman beside her.

The woman was Perla Quist and she was there because the tenant had asked her to come, and she had a pencil and about nine sheets of her own ruled paper and no form of any kind, and that was exactly why she was there.

And Lin Hao understood, standing at the back of that room, that a woman with a pencil was now a thing this city was bringing to hearings, and that nobody had arranged it and nobody had asked and no office in Ostara had sanctioned it.

The landlord’s man was not a young man and he had the paper in front of him and he had it in order, and the order of it was the first thing Lin Hao noticed and it was the thing he could not get past for the whole of the afternoon.

He went through the lease. He went through the nine months. He went through the sum and the dates on which it had been offered and the dates on which it had not been taken, and every one of those was right, and he said “my client does not dispute this” about each one before he moved to the next, in the ordinary voice of a man doing a job he has done about nine times.

Then he came to the water.

“It is on and off eleven times between the second of January and the twelfth of June,” he said. “My client has the dates. My client cannot read and cannot write her own name and she has had a date and an hour written down for every one of them by an office off a court, and there is no figure anywhere on any of it, and no man has ever come.”

Nobody in that room said anything.

“I am not defending the water,” said the landlord’s man. “It is not my debt and it is not my client’s fault and I am not going to stand here and win on it.”

Then he turned the sheet round on the table so that the foot of it faced up.

## The Sentence At The Foot

“This is the office’s own paper,” he said. “I took it off a rail in a passage off Cordwain on the thirtieth of June, at about five, after the building emptied, and I have taken it because a public paper may be produced by anybody and I have not cut anything off it and I have not written on it.”

“You are entitled to it,” said the clerk.

“I am,” said the landlord’s man. “And I want it read.”

He put his finger on the second of the four sentences at the foot of it, and he did not read it. He said what it means, in his own words, because he had understood it and because a man who could read it was not required to.

He put it, in his own words, to the effect that an empty Register means nobody ever brought anybody anything.

And he said: that is why my client is where she is. She has been handed nothing, on eleven dates, by the body whose paper that is, and the body has not sent anybody, and there is nothing on that page that she could take to anybody, because each line on it is timed and dated and carries no sum at all, and she cannot read one word of it in any case.

He said all of that in about ninety seconds and it was correct in every part of it.

Then he said the thing that took Lin Hao’s legs out from under him about four feet from the back wall of the room.

“I am not producing that sheet to help my client,” said the landlord’s man. “I am putting it in because it is the paper of the office that was told eleven times and did nothing, and if this court is minded to record anything about my client’s water I would like it recorded that the reason nobody has come is that nobody can be asked.”

## Nine Words From The Floor

The clerk asked whether anybody wished to be heard, and did not ask it in a way that expected an answer, because it is a formality and it goes the way formality goes.

The tenant stood up and held on to the back of the chair.

She said nine words.

She said that she had asked for the water on and the office wrote down the day and the hour, and she had not been told what the office did with the paper, and the landlord had come up nine times about the rent and nobody had ever come about the water, and that was the whole of what she had.

Then she sat down again, because somebody touched the chair.

Nobody asked her a question. She could not have read one.

Perla Quist had written all nine of them down in a hand nobody in that room had ever seen on a piece of paper before, and had written them in the order they were said, which is the only rule she has.

“You have not asked her a single question,” the landlord’s man said to nobody in particular, and he was not wrong. “You have asked her whether she wishes to be heard. She has told you in nine words that this office knows and has not come and that you are the people who should have come, and nobody in this room is going to ask her how she knows the dates were written down.”

He looked at Lin Hao at the back of the room for about a second, and then he did not look at him again for the rest of the afternoon.

The clerk wrote for about eleven minutes and asked nothing.

He asked nothing at all, and at the end of it he said, out loud, to nobody in particular, that he had one question and had not used it and would use it at another time, and that he was telling the room that so that nobody in it thought he had forgotten.

Then the matter went away without anything happening to it, in the way that about nine matters a month go away from a room with a table in it without anything happening to them, and the room emptied.

Nobody thanked anybody. The landlord’s man gathered his paper. Perla Quist put her nine ruled sheets in order and squared them and put the pencil on top of them, and the woman she had come with was holding the landlord’s paper in both hands and had been holding it in both hands for the whole of it without being asked to give it back.

*Two thousand three hundred and twenty-two days. Less two thousand three hundred and sixteen, the seventh of July of the seventh year, is six, and thirteen less seven is six by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, two thousand three hundred and nine, is thirteen days, which is no month at all with thirteen days after them, and a sheet that went up on a rail at four that afternoon was carried off it at five by a man who is entitled to it, and put at the top of a demand in a room with a table in it.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is six hundred and four days, which is twenty months with four days after them. It is the second of four sentences at the foot of that sheet, and a man read it out loud in a room in the wrong words and told a court that it was the reason his landlord had never had to come and look.*

## The Fact That Is Left In The Room

The landlord’s man came out last and he came out into the corridor and he did not hurry past him.

“You have a plate on you,” he said. “I saw it when I came in and I have read what it says and I know what your office is.”

“Yes.”

“How many times have you been in those two rooms.”

There was a corridor and a door at the end of it and about nine people in it going out, and a man who had been right about every single fact in a room for about half an hour and had said so himself at the top of it, and the question he had asked was the only question in the afternoon that Lin Hao could not get away from.

“Never,” he said.

“Never,” said the landlord’s man.

He nodded, once, as though Lin Hao had confirmed something he had already put down.

“Then you are the second person in this city who has written something down about my client’s water and has never come and looked at it,” he said. “The first one is an office with a printer.”

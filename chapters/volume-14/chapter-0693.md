# She Reads A Copy And Stops

He carried four hundred and nine sheets of rag paper up two flights in two cardboard boxes to a room with a shut door and a bolt on the inside of it, and the whole of what he wanted to know was whether one of them could be read.

Two thousand four hundred and thirty-four was the fifth of November and the twelfth is day two thousand four hundred and forty-one, and two thousand four hundred and forty-one less two thousand four hundred and thirty-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

He had been carrying the instruction in his other coat since the twenty-first of October and it was in this one too, because he had not been able to make himself leave it at the bottom of a stair anywhere, and it had come up two flights with him.

## A Room With Nothing On The Walls

It is at the end of a corridor on the fourth floor of a division building and there is no window in it and one table and one chair and a bulb in the ceiling on a cord, and it has been locked on the inside of every working day for eleven years and is not locked on any other day because nobody locks it on any other day.

He had the boxes open on the floor before she came in and there was nowhere to put them, and that was the first thing about the room that was not the room at the bottom of her own stair, and he noticed it and did not know why.

There is no shelf in it. One table does not take four hundred and nine sheets and he was not going to stack them on the table, because a table is a thing a page gets read on, and he had worked that out in a corridor without knowing he had worked it out.

So they went on the floor in nine heaps.

She came in at about half past two in the coat she had come in, and she looked at the nine heaps and then at the table and then at him, and she did not take the coat off and she did not look at it once.

“On the floor,” said Ines Duarte.

“There is nowhere else.”

“There is a table,” she said, “and you have not put them on it on purpose, and I have been in this room about nine times in eleven years and about four of those nine times there was a page on that table.”

“I know.”

“You do not, because you have never asked, and I am going to tell you and then I am going to read one,” said Ines Duarte. “You have left that table empty because a copy is not a page. You did not know that you knew it. Nobody in this city has ever taught you that and there is no form anywhere that says it.”

She took the first sheet off the top of the nearest heap, squared it against the side of her own hand, and held it up at the height of her eyes.

“Stand where you are,” she said. “If you stand in front of me I will stop.”

## The Order She Reads In

She read it.

The date first, because whoever made that copy put the date at the top of it.

Then the hour with the date, because about nine of them in every hundred carry an hour and about four hundred do not. Then the four sentences in the order he had set them in nine seconds on a week day afternoon in an earlier year, then the six lines under them with the fourth of the six struck through from the first letter to the last, and then the name under the striking.

She read all of that in order without hurrying and without improving one word of it, and she gave the striking in her own mouth because it is his, and she gave the name in her own mouth for the same reason, and she said nothing at all while she was doing it.

Then she got to the two inches and stopped.

She did not slow down and she did not speed up. She arrived at the bottom of the name and her eye went to the two inches below it and her voice stopped the way a hand stops on a doorframe.

“Go on,” he said.

“No,” said Lin Hao.

“There are two inches of nothing on that sheet with something written in them in pencil.”

“There are,” said Ines Duarte, “and I am not going to read them.”

## Where A Page Ends And A Copy Does Not

She put the sheet down on the top of the heap she had taken it off, which is not the table, and put it down square.

“A page is read in order,” she said. “The date first, the hour with it, nothing skipped. That is eleven years and it came with the job and it is three parts and I have broken half of it once in six years and I am not going to break it twice in a month for a man who has come with four hundred sheets.”

“It is the order.”

“It is the order,” she said, “and I will read what is in order and I will not read what is not, and those are two different things and only one of them is my job.”

“I will read anything on that sheet in order and I will read it to one person and I will not stop to be spoken to while it is open. That is the whole of what I am for. And I am going to read the date, and the hour if there is one, and the four sentences, and the six lines, and the striking, and the name. And then the sheet is finished.”

“It is not finished. There are two inches of it.”

“There are two inches of writing on a piece of paper that a person made on a table in a room with a door on a court,” said Ines Duarte, “and I have read you the whole of what that piece of paper says about itself, and everything under the name belongs to the person who made it, and it is not in order with the rest, because the rest of it is a page and this is not a page.”

She turned the sheet over. There was nothing on the back of it, because a copy is the size a person copied it and no bigger.

“This is what I want you to understand and I am going to say it once,” she said.

“A page has a bottom. The foot of it is the foot of it, and under the foot there is paper, and then the paper stops, and the stopping is a fact about the page that anybody may rely on. A copy has no bottom.”

“It has whatever the person who made it put there, and then they stopped, and where they stopped is about them and not about me, and there is nothing in this city that can tell the difference between a person who stopped because the page ended and a person who stopped because they had had enough.”

“Which is that one.”

“I cannot tell you,” said Ines Duarte. “And I have been the only thing in Ostara that could have told you for nine years, and I told you, and it took about four seconds, and you did not ask me for it, and that is not a fault of yours and I am not going to let you make it into one.”

## What She Says In Her Own Mouth

“You have come up two flights with four hundred and nine of them,” she said, “and you want to know whether I will put my name against them, and I am going to tell you why I will not, and then you are going to go away and I am going to get on with something.”

“I am listening.”

“Anything a person in this city puts on a piece of paper in pencil is a thing that may have been invented,” said Ines Duarte.

“And nothing in this city can be done about that, and it cannot be done about it because the sheet that says so is printed by the office you work in, and there is a man’s name at the foot of that sheet, and the name is not yours, and he reads it out loud at the end of a wall every morning of the week and says the reason for every figure before he writes the first one.”

He did not say anything.

“You wrote that in nine seconds,” she said. “You wrote it for one woman, and she is in number four and she cannot read her own lease and she adds up her money on her fingers and knows she is short before anybody puts a hand to her door. You did it well.”

“It cost her the only thing she had left, which is the right to stand in a room and say that somebody took four figures off her and meant them. You have known that since the twenty-third of November and you struck the fourth line yourself and put your own name under the striking.”

“You have said that to me before.”

“I have said the first half of it before,” said Ines Duarte, “in a room with a shut door in it, in the seventeenth month of a year I am not going to name, and you brought me one copy that day and I gave you an hour for it, and it is the only hour I have ever given anybody, and you have not mentioned it since and neither have I.”

She turned round and looked at the nine heaps on the floor.

“Now bring me four hundred and nine of them,” she said. “Any four hundred and nine. I will read every one of them in order, date first, hour with it, four sentences, six lines, striking, name, and I will not skip one of them and I will not stop to be spoken to while any of them is open, and you may sit down.”

He did not sit down.

“And then I will put the first of them down and the second of them down and the four hundred and ninth of them down, and every one of them will be in order, and every one of them will stop in the same place, and there will be about four hundred answers and they will all be the same answer.”

## The Four

“The four questions,” he said.

“Three of them went down in a corridor in the first year with a sheet open on a table in front of me, and the fourth was put to you at a door on the fourteenth of September of the fifth,” said Ines Duarte.

“Three of them. And the fourth at a door on the fourteenth of September of the fifth year. None of the four has been answered and none of the four is going to be answered in this room, and I am naming them standing so that you cannot go down two flights and tell anybody that I spent them this afternoon. I have not spent one of them.”

“I will not spend one of them on a man who has come up with four hundred sheets of pencil paper and a question he already knows the answer to.”

He stood in the middle of a floor with nine heaps on it and let that stand where she put it.

“I will certify the sheet,” she said. “I have certified it about four hundred times and it is the only record in this city of a street dug in the cold that anybody can put two things side by side. Take all four hundred and nine of them home.”

“None of them has a bottom and I will not put my name against one of them, and if you ask me why, it is that a name of mine on a piece of paper in a drawer would make it the best-looking false thing in Ostara, and I have spent eleven years making sure that the instrument is not me.”

## What She Did After She Stopped

He got down to lift the first box.

“Do not,” said Ines Duarte.

He stopped with his hand on the card.

“You have been standing on my floor for about forty minutes and you have not sat down and you have not touched anything. You came up two flights with four hundred and nine sheets of other people’s handwriting to find out whether one of them was real. I have told you that not one of them is.”

“You are going to go down those stairs and lose an argument in a room with a clerk in it and a man with four things to say at the end of a table.”

“I am going to try to keep it.”

“You are going to lose it and I am going to be right and it is going to cost you the only argument you have got left in this city,” said Ines Duarte.

“I have thought about that for about nine minutes while you were standing there and I have thought about it for about nine minutes while you were standing there, and I will not dress it up as anything else. And I am not going to let you carry it down two flights into a room where there is nobody in it but me in the mouth of a body I do not run.”

She pulled the cord and the light came over to one side of the floor and left the table in the dark.

“You did not ask me for this,” she said.

“I want that said out loud while it is happening and not afterwards.”

“I did not want a figure off you and I did not want a question answered and I have not wanted anything off you for about two years, and you have been careful with me for about two years and you have been careful with me in the wrong place, because being careful with me is a thing you do in a corridor with a sheet open in front of me.”

She came off the chair and down onto the floor among the nine heaps with her hands down in the rag paper on either side of her knees, and there was a heap of about ninety of them against her hip and she did not move it.

“Come here and be slow,” said Ines Duarte. “I have told you in the last three years that you are about a fortieth down on the spring and I have looked for a way to put it back and there is not one person in this city who could, and looking is the whole of my trade. So do not hurry, and do not perform, and do not tell me afterwards what it was like.”

She got her cuffs undone and her sleeves back about four inches off her wrists and put her palms down on the floor on either side of her knees, and the floor was covered in other people’s handwriting, and she did not appear to think that was remarkable.

“Nine heaps,” she said. “You carried them up two flights and you put them on the floor and not on the table, and I am going to be honest with you, that is the single thing about this afternoon that I have not been able to stop turning over.”

He got down on the floor with her among them.

She was quicker than she had any business being for a woman who has spent eleven years at a desk.

Her fingers were cold and they were quick at the same time, which had never been true of them in his experience.

She got his shirt open at the front with both hands at once and off his shoulders, without any of the ceremony of a woman who is enjoying herself pretending she is not. Then she got her mouth on his cock about four inches down it and held him there with a hand round the back of his head, the way she holds a page.

“Do not move,” she said, through it, and he did not move.

That was the first time in about four months that anybody had told him what to do in a room and he had obeyed it, and the reason was not obedience.

She worked at him without hurrying and looked up at him twice and said nothing at all on either occasion, and about three minutes in she came off him and got up onto her knees over his hips and got her own clothes open with one hand and pushed them down and off, and she was naked on a floor covered in four hundred and nine pieces of paper and she did not put one hand out to keep the cold off and neither did he.

“There is nobody on this floor,” she said. “Not one person. Not in this building. Not anywhere in this city. That is the whole of what I have and I have not had it in about three years and I am not going to be told about a room at half past two in the afternoon by a man who cannot open a drawer.”

She set him against her and went down onto him in one slow movement, all the way, and her cunt closed on him and she made a noise about it that was not a small one and did not appear to have been planned.

“Slower than the yard,” she said, “because I am telling you to and not because you cannot do it.”

He did it slower.

She rode him with one hand on his hip and the other braced on a heap of about ninety other people’s sheets, and she decided the pace twice and watched what it did to his face both times, and when his hand came up onto her spine she put hers over it and held it there.

“Say something,” she said.

“No.”

“Say one thing.”

“There is nothing on this floor,” he said.

“There is about four hundred sheets of paper on this floor,” said Ines Duarte, “and not one of them has a bottom, and I have spent about nine minutes on the back of them and about forty minutes deciding whether to put my name against one, and I have decided no, and I am going to remember that I decided no while I am doing this, and if you say one word about a drawer I will stop.”

He said nothing about a drawer.

She took him deeper than the position allowed and put her face down into his neck and ground the heel of her hand against herself where the two of them were joined, and she came with her mouth open and gave the room the whole of it, and the room took it and gave a little of it back, and she shook all the way through with her fingers dug into rag paper on either side of him.

He held her through it and loved how much of her there was and could not have put the size of it into a sentence afterward if he had tried for a week.

Then she took it down to nothing and held him there on her own time until he was finished, one hand still on his hip, and when he was she stayed exactly where she was and breathed hard for about a minute without saying one word, which for her is a great deal.

Nobody in that corridor came to that door.

She got off him and sat back on her heels on a heap of about ninety sheets of other people’s pencil and put her cuffs together again and squared them against each other, and did not hurry.

“You may ask me the practical thing now,” she said, “and I will answer it, and then you will take your four hundred and nine home and I will not see the inside of that room again until you have something I cannot do at a table with a page on it.”

## The Thing He Took Down Two Flights

He took the boxes down himself, a flight at a time, and stood on every landing, and a woman coming up the second one asked if he was all right and he said yes.

He had lost. That was finished on the floor at about four o’clock in the afternoon and it was not going to change on the third of December or on any day after it, and the argument was going to be lost in a room off a third court in front of a woman who enters what she is given and a man who has four things to say at the end of a table.

And the reason he had lost it was nine seconds long and about eleven hundred and twenty days old, and it was this. A figure nobody can check is the strongest thing in Ostara. A page that cannot be checked is not worth the paper it is printed on.

He wrote both of those on the back of his left hand in pencil on the top landing, because there is nowhere else in this city he is permitted to put a thing down.

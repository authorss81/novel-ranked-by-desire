# A Woman With A Pencil

Seven days had gone since he stood nine feet off a wall on a hill, empty-handed, and said the foot of his own printed page out loud in his own words to about two hundred people. Since then about nine hundred people in this city had gone on saying it back at each other, and not one of them had come to tell him anything he could check.

Two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine was the tenth of June and the seventeenth is day two thousand two hundred and ninety-six, and two thousand two hundred and ninety-six less two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine is seven, and the month between them says seventeen less ten.

He went down a street that was two streets wide at a little after ten, and he was carrying nothing, because in a month he has learned that the difference between a man who is asking and a man who is collecting is entirely in his hands.

The door stood open, which a laundry does, and the heat came out under it across about four feet of floor.

There were three tables down the middle. Clean folded things on the first. Clean folded things on the second, and a better table underneath them. On the third there was a ledger and there was one pencil beside the ledger and there was no ink anywhere in the building, and there had been no ink in that building in March either.

There were about a hundred and forty sheets of paper on the floor under the third table, in a stack that had been squared at some point in the spring and had not been squared since.

A woman came out from behind a press with a bundle in both arms and put it on the second table.

“You have not been in this room since the fourth month,” said Perla Quist. “Take the third one. It is out of the wet and it is the only one I would have you take.”

“You knew my name in March and you have used it seven times since and you have not said yours.”

“My name is Perla Quist,” she said, “and I am telling you it now, before I ask you for something, because I would like a name against the asking and not only against the refusing. I have been keeping a thing in this room for about five months that has no name on it anywhere in this city, and I am tired of that.”

## Who Was Keeping The Dates

What he wanted to know was who was keeping the dates.

Not who had made the copies. That had been answered on the second of February by a girl on a second counter who does not say nine about anything that is not nine, and it had been answered again in a yard of about eleven thousand people, where about nine hundred of them could correct anybody out loud and not one of them could correct anybody at all.

What he wanted was the provenance. Which piece of paper came out of which kitchen on which day, because there are about four hundred of them in this city and every one of them is the foot of a printed sheet written out by a different hand, and there is no instrument in Ostara that can say whether two of them came from the same table or from two tables four streets apart.

That is not a small want. It is the only want he had left that anybody could do anything about, and he had not got up at four in the morning for it, which meant that some part of him had known on the walk down that nobody was going to give it to him.

She had the ledger open before he had finished standing up.

Thirty names in it.

Two ruled columns and a third narrow one, and every name had a date against it, and the dates went back to the thirtieth of September and there were about a hundred and forty of them.

“The ruled columns are the laundry,” said Perla Quist. “What a house brought and what it owed. That is what a ledger is for and it is what it has been for nineteen years.”

“And the margin.”

“The margin is the ninth of January.”

She turned the ledger round so that he could see it, and he could see it, and for about four seconds he could not see anything else.

There were two ruled columns and a third narrow one and thirty names, and against the dates there was nothing at all in the ruled part of the page, and down the outside of the page, in the margin, in her own hand, in the same pencil, there were about a hundred and forty entries. A date. Then a place, most of them a lane or a stair or a back. And on about nine of them, a number of months.

“There is no column for this in a laundry book,” she said. “I looked for one for about four months and there is not one, and I have been in this trade nineteen years and I have never once wanted a column for anything before. I put them in the margin because the margin is the only part of a ruled page nobody has ever agreed anything about.”

## What She Wanted Him For

He told her about the drawer.

About ninety pieces of pencil paper had come in since the thirtieth of March through a door that opens on a court, and a clerk had entered every one of them as nothing, because nothing in this city has a form for holding paper and holding is not entering.

“Then they are lost twice,” said Perla Quist.

He waited for her to say the rest of it, and she took her time about it, and she was not a woman who took her time to be impressive.

“A drawer with nothing entered against it is a drawer of nothing,” she said. “That is the first time it is lost. And then in four years or so a man is going to stand at a counter and ask what that office ever did with a sheet of paper, and he is going to be told that it did nothing, and he will be told true, because there is not one thing in that drawer to be anything else. That is the second of the two.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair, it is arithmetic,” said Perla Quist. “You have been in this city nine years and you have not once said that word.”

He had not. He had said true, and correct, and that is not what happens. He stood at the third table with his hands at his sides and found that he had nothing to put against a woman with a pencil who had thought about it for five months from the other side of her own table.

“And what do you want,” he said.

“The same thing you want,” said Perla Quist. “Somebody to have them.”

“I cannot ask the office to have them.”

“You can. You are the only person that office may ask, and there is not another one in this city, and I have known that since about the ninth of January and I have been sitting here for five months with about a hundred and forty pieces of paper under a table waiting for you to come back into this room for a reason.”

“I came back into this room for the sheets.”

“You came back into this room for the number of sheets and I gave you the number of sheets,” said Perla Quist. “I am not going to argue with a man about what he came for twice.”

## What She Is Not Going To Do

He asked her for a book.

Not a ledger. A book of her own, with a column, and a date against every line, and a place, and a line for however many months a thing went on, and about four hundred lines in it by the middle of the month.

She said no before he had finished, and then she told him why, which he had not expected and which was the whole of the morning.

“No,” said Perla Quist. “And I am not going to do it this morning, and here is the reason. And after the reason I am going to tell you that on the twenty-fourth I make you something, and I am telling you both at once so that you cannot say afterwards that I did only one.”

“Why.”

“An instrument in this city is a thing with a name at the foot of it,” she said. “I have run this laundry nineteen years and not one person in this city could make me sign anything, and I am not going to start in June.”

“And I am not going to be a thing two people can check each other about either,” she said. “About nine hundred of you can check each other about the foot of a page. That is not a register. A register has somebody in it who can be got at.”

“That is not what a yard has.”

“No,” she said. “It has nobody in it who can be got at.”

She put the pencil down on the ledger and drew it up flush against the wood, the way a woman does with a pencil when she has finished saying a thing.

“That is what I am refusing,” she said, “and I would like you to notice that I am refusing it in a margin, in pencil, where the first person who comes in here can rub it out.”

## The Third Of February

*Two thousand two hundred and ninety-six days. Less two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, the tenth of June of the seventh year, is seven, and the month between them says seventeen less ten.*

*Less the thirtieth of September of the sixth year, two thousand and thirty-nine, is two hundred and fifty-seven days, which is eight months with seventeen days after them, and a ledger in a back room off a street has had dates put in the margin of it in pencil for all of them, because there is no column in a laundry book for a date.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and seventy-eight days, which is nineteen months with eight days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and a woman with a pencil has copied it out about four hundred times in this city in her own hand and nobody put it there.*

## The Woman Who Came Eleven Times

He got as far as the door and then she said a thing that made him turn round.

“There is a woman who comes to this table.”

He knew the voice she used for it. She had exactly the same voice for the third table and for this, and he understood before she had finished the sentence that it was a different kind of fact.

The woman had been coming since the ninth of January. That was the part he had not had before.

“She cannot read and she cannot write her own name,” said Perla Quist, “and she came to this table before eleven in the morning on the ninth of January and she has been back eleven times. On the last four of them she did not bring me anything to write.”

“What did she bring you.”

“The same piece of paper,” said Perla Quist. “Every time. Four sentences at the top and six lines under them and one of the six gone through, and a name under the striking, and a band of blank paper at the foot of it, about two fingers deep, with her own pencil marks in it, bought out of the money for soap. And she puts it down on this table and she says, is that right.”

He could not answer her. That was the thing he understood in the four seconds it took him to get back across that floor, and he understood it standing at the door of a laundry with the heat coming under it.

He had spent nine years learning how to be the only hand in a room that could put a sentence somewhere official, and he was that hand, and he had a plate with no figure cut into it, and a woman who cannot read had been carrying a piece of paper up and down a street for five months and eight days waiting for somebody to say four words to her.

He had come down that street for a column in a ledger and there was one thing in the room he wanted more than the column.

“I cannot tell her either,” said Perla Quist. “I have looked at it about four times. It is four sentences and a striking and a name and then about two inches of nothing with her own pencil in it, and there is nobody in this city entitled to say whether that is right, and I have about a hundred and forty dates in a margin and about four hundred pieces of paper under this table, and not one line of any of it has ever been anybody’s to check.”

He went up the street with nothing in his hands.

Under the floor of a building off a court there was a drawer with ninety-odd sheets of pencil paper in it and nothing entered against one of them. In a back room off a street two streets wide there was a margin with a hundred and forty dates in it in a pencil that cost money.

And in this city there was a woman who had been carrying the same piece of paper to the same table on eleven mornings since the ninth of January, to find out whether four words were true.

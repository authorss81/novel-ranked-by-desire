# The Whole Of His Position

He wrote the whole thing on the back of his left hand on the top landing of a stair at four o’clock on the twelfth of November and he read it back to himself going down, and by the bottom of it there were three arguments on his hand and two of them were dead and he knew which two before he got to the second landing.

Two thousand four hundred and forty-one was the twelfth of November and the nineteenth is day two thousand four hundred and forty-eight, and two thousand four hundred and forty-eight less two thousand four hundred and forty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Argument One, Which Was Dead Before He Got Down The Stair

The first one was: a copy has a date on it, and a date is the only thing in this city anybody has ever been able to put a name against.

He had got as far as the top of the second landing before he understood that it was dead, because an auditor had already read nine hundred of them out loud in order, date first, hour with it, nothing skipped, on the twelfth of November, in a room with about four hundred and nine sheets on the floor of it.

The dates were fine. She had not stopped at a date. She had stopped two inches below a name at the bottom of a piece of paper a person had made on a table, and she had stopped because there is nothing under a copy.

## Argument Two, Which Was The Good One

The second one was the good one and he had it in his mouth by the second landing.

Not the claim. He had never wanted the claim. A woman who was turned off a street in the cold is not going to be helped by anybody at the bottom of a page saying she was right.

He wanted the writing. Not that what she wrote was true. That she wrote it. That on a day in the second month of this year, in a kitchen, a person took a pencil and put a lane and a number of months into two inches of nothing under a name.

Say that. Certifying that is not certifying a claim. Certifying that is saying that on that day somebody sat at a table and wrote, and about four hundred of those can be read by about nine hundred people, and about nine hundred people in a yard correct each other out loud forty times an hour and get it right eleven times an hour and it is the fastest correction machine in this city.

Say that and the four hundred pieces of paper stop being a rumour and start being a record, and the two inches stop being a space a person says something into and start being a place where something happened.

He had it in his mouth by the second landing and it was the best thing he had thought of in eleven months.

## He Took It Down Two Flights And Made It An Instruction

He went in at about eleven the next morning and put the back of his hand on the table face up in front of Wenna Raut, which he had done twice before and which is not a thing anybody in this city is supposed to do twice.

“Read that,” he said. “And enter what it says, and do not improve it, and do not ask me whether I want it in the column, because you told me in February that asking you that is asking you a question in a room where you do not take them.”

Wenna Raut read it where it lay, the way she reads everything, and then read it a second time. It took her about nine seconds and he watched every one of them and understood about four of what was happening on her face.

“No,” she said.

“You have not finished reading it.”

“I have finished reading it,” said Wenna Raut. “I finished it at about the third line and I have spent the other six seconds being polite about it, and I am going to tell you what the rest of it is, because you have been in this room eleven years and I have been in it nine and one of us is going to have to say it out loud.”

She put the pen down on the book.

“You have brought me a sentence again,” she said.

“There was a sentence in this room in the eighth month and I wrote down the exchange and went back to my column, and I told you on the twenty-third of August that you would come back on the last day it could be done and give me something that is not a sentence. You did that. It is the nineteenth of the ninth month and there is a second one.”

“It is not a sentence. It is three things with commas in them.”

“It is not three things with commas in them either,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am going to tell you what it is, and it is worse than a sentence, because a sentence is at least a thing a person means. It is an instruction. And an instruction is a thing that goes into this room whole or does not go into it at all.”

“Read the fourth field.”

“The fourth field is the person it was done to, or a person who was there, or a person who was given it,” said Wenna Raut.

“And you have got four hundred and nine hands and not one name, and you have come up two flights and put a thing on my table that says somebody wrote something, and I cannot put that in the field for a person, because a hand is a person, and the moment one hand of four hundred and nine goes in that column then this office is holding four hundred and nine claims about four hundred and nine people who did not know they were the subject of anything.”

She squared the book.

“The field goes in empty or the line does not go in at all,” she said, “and an empty field in a form in this city is not a silence, it is a finding, and you have had one of those put about this office in July and you did not enjoy it.”

## Four Things At The End Of The Table

Yusuf Adeyemi had his coat on and had been at the end of that table since about half past ten and had read the back of Lin Hao’s hand upside down without moving his head, which he can do.

“I am going to say one thing,” he said, “and then I am going to go and look at two rooms I have, and it is going to be the fourth thing I have said at the end of that table about a thing you brought in here and it is going to be the shortest.”

“All right.”

“You have lost an argument,” said Yusuf Adeyemi, “and you lost it yesterday afternoon on the fourth floor of a division building to the only person in this city entitled to lose one to, and she was right, and I am not going to discuss it with you because I have not read it and I have not been asked to and I do not want it.”

“That is not the worst of it.”

“No,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “The worst of it is that you have come down here this morning to find out whether a clerk can be got round, and I am going to let you have the whole of the answer before you have asked any of it, because I have been at the end of this table for eleven months and you have never once come in here the morning after.”

He turned the pen over once.

“You may not. That is the answer and there is nothing else in this room.”

“What an auditor can and cannot certify is hers and not mine, and what this office may hold about a piece of paper is Wenna Raut’s and not mine, and I hold a title in this city that I have never once set out for anybody who has not asked, and I am not beginning in the ninth month of a year that does not get named in this room.”

“And if it went in with the field empty.”

“Then it goes in with the field empty and it goes in as a statement about this office having been handed about four hundred and nine pieces of paper in nine different hands, which is a statement about four hundred and nine people in a register that is in a room with no door onto a street,” said Yusuf Adeyemi.

“And I have already got one of those in the second column with a man’s name under it, and I told you in the summer that you would want to put a name on something one day and that there is no form for that either, and you have done it, and now you have come back for a second one inside three weeks and I would like you to hear yourself.”

He put the pen down.

“Do not come in here on the last day of this quarter,” he said. “Bring me the list. Not the sentence. Bring me the list, and I will not ask you a question about it, because a man who has already said a thing in a room with a clerk in it does not need to say it twice in a room with me in it.”

## The Thing He Cannot Say Out Loud

He went down to the second landing and stood on it for about four minutes.

The thing he could not say out loud was about nine words long and he had it by the third of November and he had been carrying it since the seventeenth month of a year he does not name, when she had told him in a room with a shut door in it what his sentence had cost a woman he has spoken to twice.

*One claim she was allowed to make is worth more than four hundred claims nobody made.*

That was the sentence that would have won the argument. Say it and it is finished: the copies are evidence because the woman they were made for is standing there to say that she could have made one herself and did not, and a person who could have claimed and did not is the standing proof that a claim cannot be written down.

And it is the exact sentence the third of the four was written to stop.

Four figures on a wall, a room number, a date, and a woman who cannot read a lease.

Every figure a person puts on paper may be a figure that person invented, and so a landlord’s four figures are four figures a landlord invented, and the woman in number four can add up her money on her fingers at the end of every month and knows she is short before anybody puts a hand to her door, and needs to prove nothing at all.

That sentence freed her.

And it cost her the only thing she had, which was the right to stand in a room and say that somebody took four figures off her and meant them.

So he could not say the nine words, and he could not say the other nine words either, and the reason was that both of them were true and he had only ever permitted himself to build on one of them.

## The Whole Of His Position, Written Out Plain

He wrote it out on the top landing at about six in the evening with a pencil, because that is the only place in this city where a man of his standing is allowed to put anything.

*A figure in this city cannot be checked. That is what the third of the four says and that is why four hundred pieces of paper are worth nothing.*

*If the third of the four is not true then four hundred pieces of paper are the best record this city has had since the second column was ruled.*

*He wrote the first sentence in nine seconds to free a woman in number four. He cannot now say the second one, because saying it makes the first one a lie, and the first one is not a lie, it is the only reason a woman who cannot read is not standing in a room being robbed with a piece of paper in her hand.*

*He has one sentence. He cannot have two. About four hundred people have spent seven months building the second one out of pencil in their own kitchens and every one of them has been right to, and they are standing at the foot of the first one and neither of them is going anywhere.*

He read it twice and it did not improve and it did not get shorter, and it was the whole of his position, and it had been the whole of his position since about four o’clock on the twelfth of November.

He did not go up the hill. He went down a street two streets wide and there was nobody at the third table and about nine sheets of pencil paper under it, which is about a fifth of what had been there in June, and he stood in the steam for about nine minutes and then went home and got nothing for dinner and did not want anything.

*Two thousand four hundred and forty-eight days. Less two thousand and ninety-two, the twenty-third of November of the sixth year, is three hundred and fifty-six days, which is eleven months with twenty-six days after them, and six lines have stood under the four sentences for all of them with the fourth of the six struck through from the first letter to the last and a name under the striking and two inches of nothing.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is forty-nine days, which is one month with nineteen days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them with three lines in the second column of it and eight hundred and fifty-two at the head.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is three hundred and seventy days, which is twelve months with ten days after them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is seven hundred and thirty days, which is twenty-four months with ten days after them, and he has one sentence and cannot have two, and about four hundred people in this city have built the other one out of pencil without ever being asked to.*

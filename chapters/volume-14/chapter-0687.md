# The Twenty-Seventh Sheet

A sheet that goes up on a rail at four cannot come down, and he had known that since June and had used it twice, and on the thirtieth of September he put the thing he had done into a piece of paper and let somebody else carry it down a stair.

Two thousand three hundred and ninety-three was the twenty-fourth of September and the thirtieth is day two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, and two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine less two thousand three hundred and ninety-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## Half Past Two, And The Third Quarter

It came out of the drawer at half past two and it was squared against the wood inside about four seconds, which is faster than it used to be and which nobody in that room remarked on.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it, and it has stood at that since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and it is unmoved across eleven sheets.

About four hundred and nineteen accounts under the head, every one of them somebody’s account of a street dug in the cold, with a day against each and an hour against the day and no figure and no name on any of them.

Three lines in the second column, headed carried by, and one of the three has been in there since the twelfth of September and two of them are older than this office.

Four sentences at the foot. Two of them older than this office. Two of them set on a morning in November by a man with a delivery coat’s name under one of them and not his own.

And under the four, since the twenty-third of November: six lines, the fourth of the six struck through from the first letter to the last, a name under the striking, and two inches of nothing.

“Twenty-seventh,” said Wenna Raut.

“Twenty-seventh,” said Lin Hao, who had said it twenty-six times before.

“Head has not moved and is not going to,” she said. “There is no permission and there is nobody in this city entitled to give one. I say that once a quarter in this room and I am saying it with a clerk’s hand on a page that is about to be folded.”

She signed for it. She folded it into eight and carried it herself down two floors and along to the counter with the window on it, because that is what happens to it every quarter and nobody makes a ceremony of it, and at four it went face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain, and there was nothing else anybody in that building could do with it.

## The Copies

About four hundred pieces of paper were in four hundred kitchens in this city on the thirtieth of September.

Every one of them was the foot of a sheet written out longhand by somebody who could not afford a press. Four sentences, six lines, a striking, a name, two inches of nothing with a pencil line or two in it, and in a few hundred of them a number of months, because a number of months is the one thing a page in this city has no column for anywhere.

Not one of them had been wrong.

That was the thing. For seven months, from the ninth of January to the thirtieth of September, four hundred copies of the foot of the most widely read page in Ostara had been the foot of it, and about nine hundred people could read every line of any of them, and not one of them had been out of date, because the foot of that page had not changed since the ninth of November of the fifth year.

On the afternoon of the thirtieth of September, three lines in the second column went up on a rail at four in a passage, and about four hundred copies of that page were one line short.

There was no instrument in the city that could tell a kitchen.

He stood at the kerb outside the passage at about ten past four and worked it out in about nine minutes, and then he got a piece of paper out of his coat and wrote the whole of it on the back of his left hand in pencil, because there is nowhere else in this city he is permitted to put a thing down.

*Four hundred copies of a page are one line out of date and there is nothing in this city that can go and tell a kitchen.*

## Eleven At The Rail

The man at the desk under the window had it face up already and had his own copy face up on the counter, and he had been about nine years at that desk and had a hand on every one of twenty-seven sheets.

“Twenty-seventh,” he said.

“Twenty-seventh.”

“Go down,” said the man at the desk, “there are eleven of them behind you and I have never had more than four and I have had this desk nine years.”

He had. It was the number that had moved.

He read them out himself. The man at the desk has always read the whole of the foot to anybody who stands there and asks, and about nine people a week ask, and it is not in his contract and it is not paid for and there is nobody he could name who has thanked him for it.

There were about nine hundred people who could read what was on that sheet. About four of them in a month stood in that passage and got to the bottom of it, and every one of those four had done it because the sheet was in the way and they had their hand on the corner of it.

On the thirtieth of September there were eleven, and eight of the eleven got to the second of the four sentences and said something out loud about it, and the thing they said was *so they have started.*

“That is correct,” said the man at the desk. “I have had a copy on this counter since the second of February and I take mine off the rail in the morning and put it back before I go, and I have never had anybody tell me that it was out of date and I found out about it at about ten past four today because there were eleven of you instead of four.”

He put the pencil in the drawer and shut it.

“Do you know what the worst of it is,” he said. “It is that in about four hundred kitchens in this city there is a person with a copy of that foot in their own handwriting and they have not got my news and they are not going to get it. I read it out to about nine people a week for six years and every single one of them took it away in their head and wrote it out again later in their own time.”

“That is the whole trade and nobody has ever asked me to say it.”

## The Ninth Of The Eleven

The ninth one was a man with a copy of his own in his inside pocket, and he had it out and had it open before the man at the desk had finished, which had not happened in nine years.

He had it open at the two inches.

“I have been coming here since the second month,” he said, “and I have never once got to the second of the four. What I want to know is whether they have started.”

“They have started,” said the man at the desk.

“What is it that they give you.”

“They do not give you anything. There is nothing anybody can bring there and nothing they can do with it, and that is not me being careful with you, that is the form.”

“That is what I have,” said the man, and put it away, and said thank you to a man behind a counter who has never been thanked by anybody who came to read a rail.

Lin Hao stood about nine feet off for the whole of it.

The ninth of the eleven wanted a thing that had never once been on offer in that building, and he had written the foot of that page out in his own hand and carried it inside his coat to a passage off Cordwain on the last day of a quarter, and he had come to a counter under a window because there is nowhere else in Ostara you can walk to and be told the truth by a stranger.

That is what the page had been doing for nine years without knowing it, and that is the thing it was for, and it was not the thing the third of the four sentences says it is for.

And not one of the four hundred kitchens knew.

*Two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine days. Less two thousand three hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, is ninety days, which is three months exactly. The twenty-sixth sheet went up on that rail at four and the twenty-seventh went up at four this afternoon, and the head of it has stood at eight hundred and fifty-two across eleven of them.*

*Less the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, two thousand two hundred and nineteen, is a hundred and eighty days, which is six months exactly, and there have been two sheets on that rail in a year and neither of them moved the head.*

## What A Copy Is Dated Against

He walked the two streets to her at about half past four, and Perla Quist had her hand on a ruler and was cutting a sheet of her own paper, and she listened to the whole of it standing up, and she did not interrupt him once in nine minutes.

Then she put the ruler down.

“Every one of those four hundred is in my book,” she said. “Three hundred and forty lines with a date and a place and in some of them a number of months, and every one of them is a copy of that foot in somebody’s hand. I have been keeping a list of copies. That is what it is.”

“I have been calling it an index and it is not an index and it has never been an index, it is a list of four hundred pieces of paper.”

“Yes.”

“So I can tell you that a copy was made on the fourteenth of July,” said Perla Quist. “I cannot tell you what the page said on the fourteenth of July. There is no column for it anywhere in this city and there never has been and there was never going to be, and that is not a thing anybody forgot to write down. It is a thing where the writing down stops.”

She picked the ruler up again and put it down.

“The copies are only worth what the page was when they were made,” she said, “and nobody has ever had to think about that before because the page had not moved since November and there was nothing to be out of date with.”

The heat came out under the door across about four feet of floor the way it did every morning of the year.

“I am not going to tell you what I am going to do about it,” said Perla Quist, “because I have not worked it out, and I have never once in nineteen years told a man in a rank-one coat what I was going to do before I had done it.”

She cut the sheet in half and looked at the two halves.

“But I will tell you what is wrong with mine,” she said. “It is dated. Every line in it is dated and there is not one line in it that says what anything was true against.”

“I have been keeping a list of when people wrote things down and I have not been keeping a record of what they were writing down about, and that is the first wrong thing I have done in nine years and it took a man who writes on the back of his hand in pencil to point at it.”

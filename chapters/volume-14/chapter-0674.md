# Once A Year Is A Record

Six days had gone since a woman had come down off the first of April, said no to him in her own room in about nine sentences, opened a door that had been shut for thirty years and done something on a landing with nine people going up and down it, and told him afterwards that there is no page for any part of it, and since then it had not been possible to put her sentence down anywhere.

Two thousand three hundred and nine was the thirtieth of June and the seventh of July is day two thousand three hundred and sixteen, and two thousand three hundred and sixteen less two thousand three hundred and nine is seven. A month stops on its thirtieth. Nothing is carried past it, and seven days on from that thirtieth of June is the seventh day of July.

Two thousand three hundred and sixteen less two thousand two hundred and nineteen is ninety-seven, which is three months with seven days after them.

That is the whole of the summer so far. The twenty-fifth sheet went out at four on the thirtieth of March and the twenty-sixth at four on the thirtieth of June, and in ninety-seven days about nine people in this city have got to the last two inches of the first of them.

## The Thing He Wanted Before Eleven

He wanted to be able to say which of the two things this city had was the real one.

He had spent about four months believing that this office was the only real thing in it, and then he had stood on a hill on the tenth of June with nothing in his hands and read the foot of it out loud to about two hundred people, and since then he had watched about nine hundred people in a yard of about eleven thousand correct each other out loud about it forty times an hour and get it right about eleven times an hour.

And now he had the answer from the one woman in Ostara who could not be argued with, and the answer was that it was not a question, and that had been the answer since the middle of June and he had spent three weeks unable to use it.

A record is a thing with a day on it. You can meet it. You can bring the same page back in a year and there is an earlier one to set it beside and about four hundred and nineteen lines on it that somebody else wrote down first.

A season is a thing that happens. It runs. It has no day anybody can name in advance, and it does not correct itself against anything, and about nine people a week in this city were on the seasonal one and about four hundred people a day were on the other.

Both of those were correct. He could not make them cancel and he had gone up a stair off Cordwain at about eleven to find out whether anybody could.

## One Floor Above A Shop

The counting house sat over a shop, up a stair with a rail on one side and nothing on the other, and he took it four at a time.

Solveig Rask had a book open in front of her and it was her own book and not the house book, and the house book was chained in a room off a landing and had not been in that room for twenty-six years.

She shut it when he came in, which she had not done the last time.

“Sit down or do not.”

“I have brought you a sheet of paper,” he said.

She did not take it.

“Tell me what it is before I touch it, because if I touch it first then it is a thing that has been handed to a keeper and everything that happens to it afterwards is on this house and not on you.”

“It is a copy of the foot of the page this office prints, written out by hand in pencil, and there are about two inches of nothing at the bottom of it with something written in them in the same pencil by the person who copied it.”

“How many of them.”

“About four hundred in this city and about four hundred more coming.”

“Then you have come to the wrong floor,” said Solveig Rask, “and you have come to it about six weeks late, and both of those are my fault and not yours.”

## The One Rule Of The Trade

“What would it take,” he said. “To put about four hundred of them into a real book.”

Solveig Rask looked at him for about four seconds.

“Come here and read this.”

He came round the table.

She had turned a fresh page and there was a date at the head of it in her own hand and against the date, in the same hand, the address of a room off a third court and the words *held, not entered* and a line for a figure that she had not filled in.

“That is an entry and it is in my book and it is in my hand and it is yours,” she said. “Read it out to me.”

“Why.”

“Because you have never once asked me what a real book is,” said Solveig Rask. “You have asked me what paper costs and you have asked me whether a real book can hold a claim, and both of those are trades. This is the book. Read it out loud, in this room, to another person.”

He read it out.

She took the pen and drew a line through the whole of it from end to end, and then she turned the page and wrote the date again at the head of the next one, and left that blank.

“What have you just done.”

“I have voided it,” said Solveig Rask. “It is the only rule in this trade that anybody ever breaks, and it is not about honesty at all. It is about what a book is for. Nobody may say their own line out loud in a room. Do it and the line is struck out, and the whole of it has to go in again, and there is no second go-in before tomorrow.”

“It is the same four words twice.”

“It is not even four words,” said Solveig Rask. “It is a fact about the order of a hand and a voice. The hand writes it, and the voice lifts it out of the hands of the only person who could find out about it by asking. If a person is going to say hers aloud in a room she is not carrying anything. She is performing. There is no debt in a performance.”

She put the pen down.

“And I told you that on the seventeenth of March,” she said, “and you wrote it down, and you came back up this stair on the seventeenth of June asking me whether a real book can hold a claim. It can hold a claim. It cannot hold a season, and that is now the most useful thing anybody has said to me in twenty-six years and I did not say it, you said it, in my room, without knowing what you were saying.”

## Forty Errors An Hour

He sat down, because he had been on four nights of the rota and two stairs in a morning.

“There are about nine hundred people in a yard off the Vestry who can read everything,” he said. “They have about four hundred of these between them and they read them out loud to each other, all day, in daylight, and they get them wrong about forty times an hour and every one of the forty is caught inside about four minutes by somebody standing next to whoever said it.”

Solveig Rask did not move for about two seconds.

“You have just described the thing that voids an entry,” she said. “You have described it at about forty to the hour and about nine hundred times a day, and every one of them is a person in this city standing in front of another person and reading out what a hand wrote, and it is the one thing that cannot go in a book and still be a book.”

“They are not entries.”

“They are not entries yet,” said Solveig Rask, “and in about four years a fraction of them will be, and by then there will be about four hundred pieces of paper in this city that have been read out loud in a yard forty times a night, and any keeper in this city who took them would have to decide what a hand means after its owner has said it eleven times in public.”

She shut the book.

“And I have not had one of those in twenty-six years and I am not going to start, and I am not going to tell you that that is the whole of my reason, because the other half of it is that I like it.”

“You like it.”

“I like that about nine hundred people in a yard can stop a lie in four minutes without anybody in an office,” said Solveig Rask. “I have kept real books for twenty-six years and nothing ever handed to me has been stopped by anybody at all.”

“You have built the best correction in this city out of four hundred pieces of pencil paper in four hundred hands,” she said, “and not one of them can be carried into a book, and I am not going to choose between those two facts for you.”

*Two thousand three hundred and sixteen days. Less two thousand three hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, is seven. A month runs thirty days and stops, so a day seven after the thirtieth of June is the seventh of July, and there is no thirty-first to carry it.*

*Less the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, two thousand two hundred and nineteen, is ninety-seven days, which is three months with seven days after them, and about nine people a week in this city get to the last two inches of a sheet that went up on a rail at four on that morning.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and ninety-eight days, which is nineteen months with twenty-eight days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and about nine hundred people in a yard are at this moment reading out loud, to each other, what a hand wrote, and a keeper one floor above a shop has just told him that this is the single thing that cannot be carried into a book.*

## The Landing With No Light Under The Door

He went down at about half past one because he had not been able to stop walking since half past twelve.

The two flights under the sales floor are cold at every hour and the door of the counting room has been shut since a woman opened it once in June.

There was no light under it and no sound in it and there was a lock in it with a key in it that only one key in this city fits, and on the landing there was the sound of a floor of people coming up and going down, and about nine of them went past him while he stood there.

He had asked her for the book on the thirtieth of June and she had said no in nine sentences, and he had understood every one of them, and about four days ago a woman who keeps real books had taken a pen and struck out a real entry because he had read it out loud in a room.

Two instruments.

One of them came out four times a year, could be met by anybody on the day, and had never in six years been argued with by a soul. The other one ran every day in about four hundred kitchens, in about four hundred different hands, and had nobody in it who could be got at, and was the only thing in this city that had ever been corrected inside four minutes by a person who knew nothing whatever about it.

And the two of them could not be put in the same room.

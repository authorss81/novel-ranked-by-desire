# Thirty Names In A Ledger

It was the same desk, the same window on the wall behind it and the same rail along the front of the counter, and the same sheet lying face up on that rail where it has lain since a March that nobody behind that counter mentions.

Two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven was the eighteenth of September and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand three hundred and ninety-three, and two thousand three hundred and ninety-three less two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven is six, and twenty-four less eighteen is six by the month count.

## A Man With A Pencil And A Cloth

He had been in that passage twice since the twelfth of May and had not asked the man behind that counter a single question, and the man had known he was arranging his mornings so that it would not come to that, and had said as much.

He came at about half past ten and he stood at the counter for about four seconds and the man put the pencil down before he said anything.

“No,” the man at the desk said, before he had been asked anything at all.

“I have not said anything.”

“You were standing there deciding whether to say something and you decided not to and I have watched about nine people do it at this counter in nine years,” said the man at the desk. “Say whatever it is.”

“I am not going to ask you anything.”

“All right,” said the man at the desk. “Then you have come for the book and you are not going to ask me for it, and that is the correct shape of the visit and I would have been annoyed by a question.”

He took the drawer out from under the counter with his foot and put a cloth-wrapped thing on the wood and unwrapped it.

It was a ledger with two ruled columns and a third narrow one, and about thirty names in it, and a date against every name, and the dates went back to the first of March of a year nobody behind that counter mentions.

“All of it.”

“All nine years of it,” said the man at the desk. “There is no second book. I told a man in May that the second of the second month of a year I do not name is the first line in it and that is the first of March of the second year, and that line has never been in a drawer and has never been in anybody else’s hands, and I have never told anybody which woman it is.”

He squared the ledger against the counter.

“I am asking for nothing and I am telling you that first so that neither of us has to arrange it later,” he said. “Not for it to be in the office. Not for my name on anything. Not for a receipt, because there is no receipt in this city and there never has been one and I have told nine people that this month.”

“I am giving you a book that has been sitting under a counter being correct and useless for nine years because I could not think of anybody who could do one single thing with it.”

## Why A Woman With A Ruler And Not An Office

“You cannot give it to the office,” he said.

“No,” said the man at the desk.

“She would refuse it and she would be right, and she refused me about four hundred pieces of paper in August and gave me a structural reason in her own room and I have not been able to put it down since.”

“She will,” said the man at the desk. “Everything in that book is a person who came to this counter and was turned away and went home with nothing, which is the first thing and not the second, and put in a drawer in a building with no door onto a street it stops being the first thing, and there is no form anywhere that would say afterwards which of them a name was.”

“I have been writing the first of those things down for nine years without knowing that is what I was writing.”

He put his thumb on the cover of the book.

“A pencil ledger with thirty names and no form on it is the only instrument in this city that can date a thing and say whose hand wrote it down on the day,” he said. “There is one other and she is not going to get either of these from me.”

“That is the woman who can read a page in order.”

“That is the woman who can read a page in order and put a second document beside the first and say which of them came first,” said the man at the desk. “And you are not going to take either of these to her, and I know that without being told, because the third of the four sentences at the foot of your own page is the only reason in this city that a copy is a thing nobody can stand behind.”

“You did not write it. Emil Brack did, on a stair, in the third year. And it is the reason that a woman with a pencil is the keeper of this and not you and not me.”

He put the cloth back over the ledger and folded the corners in.

“Take it down the street,” he said. “There is a woman who keeps dates in the margin of a laundry book because there is no column for a date, and I have read nine of her names out loud to a woman off a street two streets wide in June and she wrote them down on her own paper, and about nine of my thirty have been in her room, and neither of us could find out why until the third of August.”

## Thirty And Three Hundred And Forty And One Figure

He got down a street two streets wide at about half past eleven with a cloth-wrapped ledger against his ribs and it was heavier than four hundred and nine sheets of rag paper had been.

Perla Quist had the third table and about nine sheets of pencil paper under it and she looked at the cloth before he had untied it.

She did not say anything for about four seconds, which was the first time since June.

“Nine years,” she said.

“Nine years and a bit more.”

She unwrapped it on the second table and did not open it, and stood with both hands on the wood on either side of it for about a minute and a half.

“I have wanted this since the twenty-second of June,” said Perla Quist, “and I have wanted a column for a name in a book of my own since the seventeenth of June, and those two things are not the same thing and I have not been able to explain the difference to anybody in this city including you.”

“There are two things this city could not give anybody in nine years,” he said, “and one of them is in a room off a third court and the other one is a woman with a pencil who will not have a name against anything, and neither of them is going to be in a building.”

“No,” said Perla Quist.

She opened it. Nine years and about thirty names in two ruled columns and a third narrow one, and she went down it with a finger the way she goes down her own margin, and about nine of them she stopped on, and at two of them she stopped for longer than the others.

“Your second of the second month,” she said. “The woman with a bundle who wanted a receipt for having left it.”

“Yes.”

“What word did you write against her.”

He had asked that question in May and been told he was not going to get an answer he could use. He had not asked it since.

“Told,” he said.

*Two thousand three hundred and ninety-three days. Less the first of March of the second year, three hundred and ninety, is two thousand and three days, which is sixty-six months with twenty-three days after them. A sheet has lain face up on a counter under a window in a passage off Cordwain for all of them, and about a hundred people a month put a palm on the corner of it and go round.*

*Less the thirtieth of September of the sixth year, two thousand and thirty-nine, is three hundred and fifty-four days, which is eleven months with twenty-four days after them, and a margin in a book in a laundry has had dates written against it in pencil for every one of them, because there is no column in a book of that trade for a date.*

## A Word He Put In And Never Found Out

“It is not a strange thing to find out,” said Perla Quist, “and you have been carrying it since May as though it were.”

“No.”

“It is *told*,” she said. “She came off that counter somebody had sent to her. That is the whole of the word.”

“It is not a note about her. It is a note about a man who had sent a woman to a passage off Cordwain with a bundle, and there is not one form in this city on which the fact that somebody was told to go anywhere ever gets written down, and you wrote it in a pencil ledger in about four seconds in the second year and you have had it under a counter ever since and nobody has ever been able to tell you what it means.”

He looked at the third table and did not say anything for about nine seconds.

“I have never read the two inches on any of the four hundred and nine,” said Perla Quist. “I am not going to.”

“But I know that word, and I know it because about nine of the three hundred and forty dates in my margin are dates a woman came to this counter on because somebody had told her to, and about nine people a week in this city are standing in a place they were sent to by somebody who has not been asked to say so.”

She closed the book and squared it against the edge of the second table.

“There are three things in this city with a figure in them that nobody has ever corrected,” she said. “Mine has about three hundred and forty dates in the margin and no figure anywhere in it at all. Yours had about thirty names and now it is in this room.”

“And there is a figure against a course of dressed stone in a book in a yard nine streets from here with no name against it that has not moved in seven years and that nobody has ever opened the page of, and a counter figure can be added to and cannot be taken from.”

“I am not going to be thanked and neither is he.”

“He is not going to be thanked and neither is he,” said Perla Quist, “and you have come down here with it under your arm and you are going to say thank you, and I am going to say thank you, and we are both going to do it at the same time and not once to his face.”

Neither of them said thank you, and neither of them said anything else, and that was all of it.

She put the cloth back over the ledger and folded it in and put it under the third table on top of about nine sheets of pencil paper, and squared the corner of the cloth with her thumbnail, and that was the whole of it and it took about four seconds and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

“He gave it to you in a passage with nobody in it,” she said, at the door. “He has never given anybody anything in a room with a person in it in nine years. That is his standing and you have just spent it on me and there is no getting it back and I did not ask you to.”

# The Form Goes On A Board

Seven days had gone since about four hundred people had stood four feet off four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of a wall, and since then two men had put render back on about nine feet of it and the words had gone behind a new joint and come out the other side exactly where they had been.

Two thousand two hundred and forty was the twenty-first of April and the twenty-eighth is day two thousand two hundred and forty-seven, and two thousand two hundred and forty-seven less two thousand two hundred and forty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

He had a piece of paper in the inside of his coat that he had carried for about a hundred and twenty days and there was nowhere in this city to put it, and that was the whole of the morning.

## The Piece Of Paper That Has Nowhere To Go

It was the size of a lease and it had been upside down in his coat for nine weeks before he ever read it, and it had an address at the top of it and four words under the address and a small ruled square at the bottom.

*Take down to course and make good.*

A man at a counter nine streets from here took it in on the twenty-seventh of December and put it on a spike under a number the size of a thumbnail, and it sat on that spike through the winter until the seventh of April, when the men who were taking that wall down came to the works and pulled about nine hundred sheets off it.

That is what happens to a piece of paper when a job starts. It comes off the spike and goes onto the job, and the job has got a foreman with a folding rule and about nine men and no drawer, and it goes in a box in a hut, and at the end of it somebody writes a date in a small ruled square and it goes back on a rail and gets filed against the wall, and the wall is not there any more.

“I asked for it back,” said Lin Hao. “I went to that counter on the eighth of April and asked the man behind it whether a form had come off a spike.”

“And he said.”

“He said it had come off a spike on the seventh along with about nine hundred others and that they go in a box and the box is in a hut and the hut is at the far end of the site and the site is closed to the public.”

“Did he say you could have it.”

“He said that when a job is done the paper goes back on the rail and anybody can read it there,” said Lin Hao. “He said that before he asked me why I wanted it, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said nine times, and he did ask me why I wanted it afterwards, and I did not have an answer that was worth the asking.”

## Nine In The Morning By A Kerb

The board by the kerb had the sixty-third month’s form on it, and it had been there since the twenty-eighth of February, and it had two ruled squares at the foot of it and both of them were empty and they were going to be empty at the end of the month as well.

The boy was there at about nine with a paste brush and a jar of paste and a piece of rag, and he had been there every morning of the last about nine months at the same time, and nobody had ever asked him to be.

“You have had that other one in your coat since December,” he said.

“I have.”

“Then it is going in a hut and the hut is going to be knocked down and the paper is going to be gone.” The boy wiped the brush. “You cannot keep a thing like that in a coat for four months. There is no place to keep it. Where have you been keeping it.”

“Against my ribs.”

“That is not a place,” said the boy. “That is a coat.”

## A Board That Is Not A Wall

He gave it to him at about ten past nine and the boy turned it over and looked at the front of it and turned it back and did not read it.

“This is a job.”

“It is a job for that wall.”

“It is a job for a wall that is not there,” said the boy. “There is a hoarding in front of it and nine feet of new render on it and it is a wall now, and it is a different wall, and this piece of paper is about a job that has been done, and I am going to put it up on that board with the other one because there is nowhere else for it and you have run out of coats.”

“There is a corridor nine streets away with nine hundred lines on a wall in it and anybody may stand and read it.”

“You cannot paste anything on a schedule in a public corridor and there is not a person in this city with the authority to let you,” said the boy. “A board by a kerb is not a board on a wall and it is not correct about anything and it is the only one there is, and I have been using one for six years and I have never once been asked whether it was the right one.”

He mixed the paste thinner than a man mixing paste for a hoarding would mix it, because paste for hoarding board is not the same as paste for a form, and he did that without being told.

He put it up level and it took about four minutes and he used his own weight against it the way he always does, and the sixty-fourth would go over the top of this one next month and the sixty-fourth would be on the board by the ninth month.

“There are two boxes at the foot of that one as well,” said Lin Hao. “There is a small square at the bottom of it for the day the work was finished.”

“There is.”

“You could put a date in it.”

“No,” said the boy.

He said it the way he says everything, which is once, and then he stopped, and he went and put the jar of paste on the kerb and came back.

“You asked me in the autumn what I was supposed to put in those two squares and you told me yourself that the left-hand one wants a number and the right-hand one wants whatever the address stands as, and I have gone on thinking about it at about four hundred corners since,” said the boy.

“And the left one wants a number and there is no number, because it is a wall. I am not making one up and getting a man in another building in trouble over something I invented on the end of my own brush. And the right one wants a standing, and a wall on a street does not stand for anything, it stands up.”

He wiped the brush on his coat.

“Six years. Empty both. Going to be empty after I stop, and nobody is going to ask me, and you can stop looking at the bottom of it.”

He said all of that in about nine seconds and then he stopped, and it did not need a second hearing, and it was the same reason he had given about two months ago on a wet step.

## A Small Ruin And A Relief

The relief came first and it was not comfortable.

He had wanted since about the seventh of April to put a piece of paper somewhere in this city that said a wall is coming down and had two words on it, and there is no board in this city that will hold a sheet about a wall, and the only one that exists is a board by a kerb that a boy pastes a month’s form on about once a month with paste mixed thinner than hoarding paste.

That was the whole of what had been available and he had wanted it since the twenty-eighth of February and had not known that was what he wanted until the boy mixed the paste.

And then the ruin, about ten minutes later, standing four feet off it.

Two hundred a day stop at that corner and about two of them in a week get to the foot of it. On that board there were now two sheets of paper and both of them were about a wall, and not one word of either of them said that there were two words cut into the bottom four inches of it.

The one on the left was a job for a wall that had been taken down and made good and would be knocked down with its hut.

The one on the right was this month’s form for an address, with two empty squares at the foot of it, and it would be taken down at the end of the month and another one would go up in its place, and that had happened sixty-three times.

Nine feet of new render is at the bottom of that wall now and the four inches are under it with a new joint over them, and paint will go over the six inches above the joint and stop dead at the top of it, because paint will not hold on dressed stone, and that is still the only reason anything is still down there.

And a man standing in front of that board is looking at a piece of paper about a wall.

*Two thousand two hundred and forty-seven days. Less two thousand two hundred and forty, the twenty-first of April of the seventh year, is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.*

*Less the twenty-seventh of December of the sixth year, two thousand one hundred and twenty-six, is one hundred and twenty-one days, which is four months with one day after them, and a form saying take down to course and make good was on a spike nine streets away for all of them, and it came off that spike on the seventh of April along with about nine hundred others and it is on a board by a kerb now and it is not carried forward.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and sixty-nine days, which is five months with nineteen days after them, and a board by a kerb is carrying sixty-four sheets about that wall, sixty-three of them one a month for six years and one of them a job that has been done, and not one of the two squares at the foot of any of them has ever had a word put in it.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and twenty-nine days, which is seventeen months with nineteen days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and it is why there are about four hundred pieces of pencil paper in this city with the foot of it copied out on the top of them.*

*Neither box at the foot of a form by a kerb is ever going to have a word in it.*

## What Is Four Inches Away

He said it out loud, which he had not planned to do, and he said it to the boy and not to the street.

“There are two words cut into the stone at the bottom of that wall about nine feet behind you.”

“I know there are,” said the boy. “There have been since before I was doing this and I have never once been asked and I am not going to be now.”

“I am not asking you to be asked anything.”

“You are asking me whether I am going to be asked something,” said the boy, “and the answer is no, and you have asked me that twice in about four months and I have answered it twice and it is going to be three times on a morning like this one.”

“All right.”

“And there is nothing on either of those sheets for it and nothing on either of the boxes, and there is no form in this city for a word on a stone.”

“If I wrote them down on a piece of paper at a kerb then somebody in about four years would come along and ask me who told me, and I would have to say a man in a white coat with no figure cut into his plate told me, and then they would go and ask you.”

“And you want me to write them down,” said the boy.

“That is not what I was going to say.”

“It is what you were going to say,” said the boy. “You were going to say that a hundred people have now read them and there is nowhere to put it. I have watched you get to the bottom of a thing nine times now and every time you get there it turns out to be a place with no box on it.”

He shouldered the jar of paste.

“The two words are four inches away and underneath nine feet of something new and I am painting around them on the next morning I paint, because paint will not hold there and the form does not tell me to try,” said the boy. “That is the whole of what is going to happen to them and it is not going to happen in a document.”

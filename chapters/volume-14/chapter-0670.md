# He Reads It On The Render

Seven days had gone since a sheet of paper had come back to a building with no door onto a street, with his own address at the top of it and a strip cut off the bottom of his own page pasted into the middle of it, and since then he had not slept properly twice.

Two thousand two hundred and eighty-two was the third of June and the tenth is day two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine, and two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine less two thousand two hundred and eighty-two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

The wall is nine feet of new render with a new joint at the bottom of it and four inches of dressed stone under the joint, and the first coat of paint on it since the joint went in reached the joint and stopped there, because there is nothing on a dressed face for paint to take hold of, which is the whole reason anything is still down there.

Two hundred a day stop at that corner, and two in a week get low enough to read four inches of stone.

## What He Wanted Before Ten

He wanted three things before ten in the morning and he only ever allows himself one at a time.

He wanted the strip of paper out of the folder. He wanted the name of the woman on the demand, and there was no name on it, and the man who brought it back had not read it and the clerk at the counter had not read it and nobody in this city is going to read it for him.

And he wanted a counter figure, a grain a head, a yard of eleven thousand people with something on their own sheet.

And he wanted to stop being a man whose sentences only exist where a page goes. A page goes out four times a year and lands on about two thousand counters, and of the people who walk past those counters, about nine in a week read the last two inches.

About four hundred people have got the bottom of it in pencil on about four hundred pieces of paper, and they are getting it wrong about forty times an hour and correcting each other about it in the open, in daylight, and none of them can check a single word of it. The man who could check all of it is auditing a branch in another city.

That was the want. He had it at about half past eight and he was still holding it at about ten.

He is rank one and he is on a branch night shift, four nights and three off, on a rota he wrote his own name against and asked for, and a man on a night shift has the morning to himself, which is the only reason there is a man on a hill at ten.

He had the reasons as well, and every one of them was good.

This is not his corner. The corner belongs to a boy with a paste brush and a board with two empty squares at the foot of it, and the boy has never once been asked what is at the foot of that wall and has said so, twice, and told him to stop looking at the bottom of it.

Nobody in this city has ever asked him to stand in a street and say anything.

A man who reads four sentences off a wall to about two hundred people a morning does not go back to being a man about whom a page exists. He becomes a man about whom a morning exists.

About four hundred people have been doing his sentences in about four hundred kitchens since the ninth of January, in pencil, at their own tables, and nobody put them there. And this morning at the top of that path he watched a version of it that was not his go past eleven times without anybody who could read stopping it.

He stood at the bottom of his own stairs for about four minutes with all of it in front of him.

At 638 he had stood nine feet off that corner and said nothing and let a yard decide for itself whether a sentence was worth anything. It had cost a woman a job and it had cost him the only useful thing he has ever done, which was staying out of it.

This was the same thing done once more. And this time he knew the price in advance, which he had not at 638, and he was going to pay it deliberately, on purpose, in daylight, on a morning he had chosen.

He went up the hill at about ten with nothing in his hands.

No sheet. No folder. No rail and no copy and no chalk and nothing he could put down afterwards and say a man in a white coat had handed him.

The boy was at the corner with the sixty-fourth month’s form going up on the board by the kerb, and the board had the works form on it as well with its four words and an empty small square, and about nine people were standing about four feet off the wall.

The boy looked at him for about four seconds and then went back to the paste.

## What He Said, In Other Words

He did not read it.

That is the first thing and it is the thing people who were not there will get wrong. He did not stand in front of that corner and read four sentences off a printed page in the order they are printed, and he did not give anybody the text, and not one person in this city can afterwards say he read out what is written at the foot of the standing sheet.

He said it in other words.

He said that the Register is a thing this office prints, and that anybody in this city may put down a number and say it happened, and that no instrument anywhere behind the standing sheet will ever stand up and say the number is true.

He said that this was put there on purpose by him and by a man in a delivery coat, and that if it stops a landlord taking four figures off a woman who cannot read a lease then it has done the work it was built for.

And then he said the other three in the same way, in his own words, in about four minutes, to about nine people who had come to look at four inches of stone at the bottom of a wall.

And when he came to the four lines with the fourth one struck through from the first letter to the last he stopped.

He said that there were four conditions and that he had struck one of them off in his own hand in front of a clerk and put his own name under the striking. And that he was not going to say what it said this morning and would not say it ever, because he has no instrument that could put a reading of a struck line back on a page and he is not going to make one up in the open.

Then he said that anybody who wanted the actual words could go to the rail in the passage off Cordwain at four on the last working day of a quarter and get to the bottom of it, and that about nine people a week do.

## About Two Hundred People A Day

About forty of them were there by eleven and about two hundred by about one and they stood in a loose line about nine feet off and most of them could read.

That is the part he had not allowed himself to think about. Of two hundred people standing in a street, about one in every thousand cannot read at all and about one in nine can read badly and slowly and will not do it in a street with strangers in it.

He said it twice, slowly, in the middle, in case. He said it a third time at about one when a man at the back asked him to say the second part again.

A woman near the rail asked him, without any preamble at all, whether it was true that the office could not tell anybody what was on the list.

He said that was right. She said that her brother had been in a list in a different building in a different city and nobody there had been able to tell her anything either. He said he was sorry for that.

She said she was not asking him to be sorry. She was asking him because she wanted to know if it was the same. It was not the same. She said all right.

Nobody thanked him. He had made sure of that in advance and it worked.

The boy came over at about one with paste on his hands and asked him one question, and it was not about the two boxes and it was not about the four conditions and it was not about the struck line.

He asked whether the man was going to be there tomorrow.

“He has not said.”

“Then I will tell you what happens tomorrow,” said the boy. “There will be a woman at this corner tomorrow who says what you said today and gets two words of it wrong and gets them right by about eleven o’clock, and there will be a man who says you were up there selling something, and about nine people will not come at all because they do not want to be counted in it.”

“I know.”

“You do not know,” said the boy. “You have just found out that you can say a thing here, and everybody who has ever said a thing out loud in this city has found out that on the day they say it, and then about nine days later they find out the other thing, and nobody in this city has an instrument for the other thing and neither do you.”

He wiped his hands on his coat.

“There was a number on a board on a hill when I was small and my father put it up,” he said. “The whole yard had worked out what it meant inside about eleven minutes and not one of them was wrong about the yard.” “And there were four words on a stone at the bottom of that wall before I was doing this, and nobody has ever asked me about either of them.”

“Now there is a man with nothing in his hands saying the same words again in a louder voice, and I am going to be here on the next morning I paint with a brush, and I am not going to be one of the nine hundred, and that is the only thing I have got to say about it.”

He went back to the board.

## What It Closes And What It Costs

That afternoon he sat in the room off a third court and worked out, properly, what he had done, because he had gone in knowing roughly and he wanted the exact figure by dark.

The option that closed was this.

For four months the copies had been the only version of those sentences in this city. They were in about four hundred hands and nobody had asked him for any of them, and about four hundred people were making them on their own initiative at their own tables with their own pencils.

He had spent four months believing — not hoping, believing, the way a man believes a thing he has not tested — that this was a thing that stood between four hundred kitchens and him.

It does not stand between anything. It never did. Those copies were not his defence. They were him, in about four hundred other people’s handwriting, with nothing after them and nobody to answer for them, and about nine hundred people in this city can correct each other out loud about a thing that neither of them wrote.

From this morning he is the source.

Everything anybody repeats after the tenth of June is his. Not the copies and not the pencil and not the yards. A man stood nine feet off a wall at about ten in the morning with nothing in his hands and said a thing in his own words to about two hundred people, and about nine hundred people can read it, and from about one o’clock this afternoon there is a mouth in this city where it came from and that mouth belongs to him.

He cannot un-say it. There is no form in this city for taking back a thing said out loud, and there is no clerk who may be given it, and if there were, a clerk may only enter what she is given and could not improve it and could not be asked to hurry.

That is what it cost.

He is the same man who hoarded an advantage for eight years and then gave everything away in one of them, and spent the claim in another, and stood nine feet off in another and let a yard decide. He has now stood nine feet off twice, and the second time he knew the price, and he paid it.

The head of the standing sheet is eight hundred and fifty-two and it did not move this morning and it is not going to. The second column has two lines in it and it is going to have two lines in it on the thirtieth of this month. Both squares at the foot of the boy’s form are empty and they are going to be empty on the next morning he paints, when he goes round four inches he cannot paint.

Two inches of nothing is still exactly two inches of nothing under a name, and about nine hundred people have just heard a man read the top of that page out loud in a street and the bottom of it is still blank.


*Two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine days. Less two thousand two hundred and eighty-two, the third of June of the seventh year, is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.*

*Add one hundred and eighteen to two thousand two hundred and eighty-nine and it is two thousand four hundred and seven, and put the first morning back on the end of it and it is two thousand four hundred and eight mornings that a man has stood at the end of that wall in this lane with a slate and about nine figures, and he has never once been able to check whether any of the nine is true.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is two hundred and eleven days, which is seven months with one day after them, and this morning a man stood nine feet off four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of a wall with nothing in his hands and said the foot of a printed sheet out loud in his own words to about two hundred people, and he has not been asked for anything since.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and seventy-one days, which is nineteen months with one day after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet. A man read that foot out loud in a street this morning in other words and did not read the line he struck and will not read it again.*

*About nine hundred people in this city can read every word of what he said and are repeating it. None of what they repeat was in the four sentences, and all of it is his.*

## The Lane At Ten To Six

By six there were about five times as many at that end of the lane as there had been at ten to six, and Brack was on his feet at the slate with the twelve books squared and the first nine of them done and a reason spoken out loud in front of every one before it went on.

He put the chalk down when he was told, which he does not do.

“You stood on a hill this morning and said a thing out loud to about two hundred people,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Then that is what I asked you for a week ago and I have thought about it for seven days and I have got a name for it now and it is not a good name,” said Emil Brack.

“It is that you have become the mouth of it. I have been the mouth of nine figures a morning for four years and there is not one person in this city who could tell me what I said, and from this morning there is a man on a hill who everybody can point at.”

“I have been that man in a lane with about forty people and I could not do it because there was no paper.”

He squared the books and did not look up.

“Did you do it because you wanted to be safe.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I did it because about four hundred people in this city have been saying my sentences in pencil for five months and about nine hundred of them can correct each other about them out loud and not one of them can check one word, and a man in a delivery coat asked me for the name of that thing and I did not have it, and I have now got one and it is on a wall and I cannot take it down.”

Emil Brack did not say that was the right reason and he did not say it was the wrong one. He is right about every fact, he is not thanked, and nothing this morning takes a single one of his facts away from him.

A woman came up the lane at about a quarter past six with a piece of pencil paper in her hand and stopped at the edge of the nine and did not read it, because she cannot read, and she stood with her chin about four inches from the chalk.

She looked at the slate for a while and then she said, to nobody in particular, that a man was standing at a corner on a hill this morning saying things out loud, and that four people on her stair had come back and told her about it, and that she wanted to know whether that man was the one whose name was at the bottom of the board by the kerb where the boy paints the form.

Nobody at the wall end could tell her. Emil Brack, who has said nine out loud about two thousand times, put the chalk down and said he did not know, and the woman said that was a great help, and Emil Brack said it was not a help, it was the opposite of a help, and she said she knew that, and she had it written down.

# The List Is Wrong

Six days had gone since about a hundred and forty people had written a true thing about a street on a wall in chalk, all of them wrongly, and every one of them had been put right out loud by somebody standing next to them. He had been standing in a yard ever since trying to work out what a question that has one answer in it looks like when you cannot ask it.

Two thousand three hundred and thirty-five was the twenty-sixth of July and the third of August is day two thousand three hundred and forty-two, and two thousand three hundred and forty-two less two thousand three hundred and thirty-five is seven. A month stops on its thirtieth, and seven days on from the twenty-sixth of July is the third day of August.

## What He Came Up The Hill For

He came up to hear a thing said wrong by a person he had never met, and be the man who could put it right.

That was the want and it was a new one and it had taken him until the third of August to notice that he had it, and it had taken him until the third of August to notice that he had been refusing to do it in public for a very long time on principle.

In a yard off the Vestry about nine hundred people can check each other out loud. In a room off a third court there is one woman with a pencil and about a drawer. He had spent the summer being the only reader in the city and being extremely good at it and being nine streets away from every single thing that needed a reader, and that was not modesty and it was not discipline and it was the standing he had built at 638 and paid for at 670.

There was nothing in front of him this morning at all.

## The Flat Space At The Top Of The Path

About eleven thousand people live in that yard off the Vestry and on a working morning about nine hundred of them are standing in it.

He went up the hill at about eleven to hear his own sentences read by about nine hundred people who had never met him and never would, and he got about half way up before he understood that this was the fifth time he had done that in seven weeks and that doing it five times was a habit.

Perla Quist was in the flat space at the top of the path with nine ruled sheets of her own paper in her hand, and about forty people round her, and she had come on purpose and had told nobody she was coming except a woman in a yard who had asked her to come.

She had copied the index out twice since the twenty-fourth of June and there were about three hundred and ten lines on it now across eleven sheets, and the last seven days had been the busiest week the thing had ever had, and she said so without being asked and then said that she was not going to say anything else about how busy it had been because she was frightened of what the number was doing.

It was being read out.

A man with a good voice and no particular standing was reading the dates off about a hundred and thirty lines in about eleven minutes, and about nine hundred people stood in a loose half-circle and let him, and he read the dates and the places and not one of the numbers of months, because there were only nine of those and he had never once been told what they meant.

Then the man put the sheets down and a second man picked them up, and at that point a voice in the middle of the half-circle said, “There is a copy in this yard that is not in that.”

It was an old man with a cap on and a piece of paper in his hand that he had been holding against his chest the whole time.

“There is a copy in this yard that is not in that list,” he said, “and I know it is not in it because I have been in this yard every day for a fortnight and I have seen it every day, and it is the one the woman wrote for her sister in July and it has been in that list twice already and not once in between.”

“Twice.”

“Two lines for the nineteenth of July, here, here,” said the old man. “One copy. I know it is one copy because I have read it and it is the same twelve words on both lines and it is the same mistake on both lines and it is the same hand on both lines.”

The half-circle did about nine different things at once.

That was the thing about about nine hundred people in a yard and about nine hundred readers among them. In four minutes they would have got the shape of it, and a wrong version of it would have been halfway round the space and back, and every wrong one would have died inside a quarter of an hour.

Nobody could check it.

There was no list in that yard. There was a woman holding eleven sheets of ruled paper, and about four hundred people with pieces of pencil paper in pockets, and not one instrument in this city that could take two dates off a page and put them side by side and say which of them a person had actually gone to a laundry about.

“How do you know it is one copy.”

The old man took the piece of paper out from against his chest and held it up so that the people near him could see it.

“Because I have had it since the nineteenth of July and I have had it every day since,” he said. “It is in my coat. It has been in my coat in the rain and it has been in my coat at that wall end at ten to six for a fortnight and a man with a slate has looked at it twice and told me it was a good copy.”

“That is one man.”

“That is one man,” said the old man, “and he is the only person in this city I have met who has ever agreed with me about anything, and I am not going to stand in a yard of eleven thousand people and say a thing is missing because a man in a delivery coat was kind to me.”

He put it back against his chest.

“But it is not in that list twice for anything. I have looked at it. It is the same piece of paper two times and somebody counted it twice and that is not a small thing when there are about four hundred people making them.”

## Eleven Minutes

“You have about four minutes before this is wrong all over the space,” said Perla Quist to nobody. “I have a pencil. I have the book. Stay where you are.”

She did not go anywhere. She took the eleven sheets and turned them round on the flat of the thing she was standing on, which was a step, and she found the two lines, and she looked at them for about four seconds.

Then she said, before anybody had asked her anything at all, and in a voice that carried about thirty feet:

“I am wrong and I wrote it.”

The half-circle stopped.

“There are not two copies for the nineteenth of July,” said Perla Quist. “There is one, and it came in on the twentieth and it came back on the twenty-first, and the woman who brought it told me the nineteenth both times, and I wrote down what she told me and I did not look at the paper.”

She rubbed one line out with the side of her thumb in about two seconds, which is what a pencil can be rubbed out with when it is fresh.

“The copy is in this yard and it is not missing,” she said. “The copy is in that list twice. That is not the same fault and it is the same size.”

## A Sentence Said Out Loud In A Yard

It took about nine minutes all told, and it was the first time in four months that anything anybody had made in this city had been put right in front of about nine hundred people by anybody other than the person who had made it.

“The list is wrong and the woman says so first,” said a man about thirty feet back, into the half-circle, where about four hundred people heard it.

“It is not wrong now,” said Perla Quist. “It was wrong for two days and I have taken it out. Put that somewhere else, because it is not the same thing and I will not have my correction described as a mistake in a yard of eleven thousand people in about four months.”

Somebody laughed, which in that yard at that hour is about the loudest thing available.

“The list can be wrong,” she said. “And that is the only thing anybody has said in this yard this year about a piece of paper that was made by somebody else, and I have been waiting four months to be able to say it about my own.”

He stood four feet off at the back of the half-circle for about half an hour and listened to it go round, and it went round wrong about nine times, and every wrong one died inside a quarter of an hour, and about nine hundred people who had never met him and never would were saying out loud, in daylight, for nothing, that a list of dates kept in pencil by a woman with a ruler in a laundry could be wrong.

*Two thousand three hundred and forty-two days. Less two thousand three hundred and thirty-five, the twenty-sixth of July of the seventh year, is seven. A month stops on its thirtieth, and seven days on from the twenty-sixth of July comes out at the third day of August.*

*Less the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, two thousand three hundred and nine, is thirty-three days, which is one month with three days after them, and a list of about three hundred and ten dates in a pencil is being read out loud in a yard to about nine hundred people, and about nine of the versions that went round the space were wrong and not one of them lived a quarter of an hour.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is two hundred and sixty-four days, which is eight months with twenty-four days after them, and a shutter at the far end of that yard has been bare for all of them, and about two thousand people a day walk past a pale rectangle and cannot get by it.*

## What It Does To The Office

At half past twelve he got her to the side of the path, where about nine thousand people were not.

“Your index over-counts,” he said. “Not much. One line in about three hundred. But it over-counts, and every time it does that it makes this city look as though more people are doing this than are doing it, and in about four years somebody is going to count from it.”

“Yes,” said Perla Quist. “That is what happened today.”

“And nobody in this city could have found it except you, and you found it in about eleven minutes, in pencil, on a step, in front of nine hundred people.”

“That is not a triumph and you are not going to be able to make it one,” said Perla Quist, “because the reason nobody else could find it is that the whole thing is me. One index exists and it is in her hand and it is not in a building. If I am ill in August then it does not exist. That is not an instrument. That is a woman.”

He had heard it before and he had agreed with it in June and he was going to have to agree with it again.

“No,” he said. “It is not an instrument. It is the only thing in this city that can be corrected.”

“You are going to ask me for it,” said Perla Quist.

She said it while she was folding the eleven sheets into eight. She did not look up. She said it in the order she means it, which is the only order she says anything in.

“You will come here in about a fortnight and you will ask me for that book,” said Perla Quist, “and I am going to say no. I am telling you on the third of August so that you cannot stand in my back room on the sixteenth and say that I sprang it on you.”

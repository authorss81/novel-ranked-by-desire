# Four Hundred And Nine Pieces

Seven days had gone since he had carried two cardboard boxes up four flights and set them down on a table in a room off a third court, and in seven days nobody in that building had opened either of them.

Two thousand three hundred and fifty-five was the sixteenth of August and the twenty-third is day two thousand three hundred and sixty-two, and two thousand three hundred and sixty-two less two thousand three hundred and fifty-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## The Table Is For Entering

They were exactly where he had put them, one on the near side of the table and one at the end of it, and the rag paper had gone soft at the corners where the damp in that room gets into everything it stands on for more than a week.

Wenna Raut came in at about half past nine, looked at the boxes for about four seconds, and did not ask about them.

Then she moved them.

She did it without any ceremony at all, one box at a time, down off the table and onto the boards under the window, and she set them beside each other with the corners squared against the skirting the way she squares a sheet before she enters it.

“A table in this room is for entering things,” she said. “There is one table in this building and it is for entering things, and nothing has ever stood on it that was not going to be entered.”

“Nobody is entering these.”

“Then they were on that table for seven days and they should not have been,” said Wenna Raut. “They are on the floor now, and the floor is where a thing goes that this office has and cannot do one single thing with, and there is a great deal of that in this room and all of it is on the floor.”

He looked at the two boxes on the boards for a while. It was nine minutes before he admitted to himself that the difference between a table and a floor was the entire difference between two kinds of holding, and that he had been looking at a table for seven days and calling it a drawer.

## Eleven This Week

He had not known the rate.

That was the thing he carried out of the room with, and it took him most of the morning, because it had been sitting in the building since the thirtieth of March in a canvas bag with a piece of string round the neck of it and nothing at all on the outside.

“A bag comes to that front door every morning except the day the office is shut,” said Wenna Raut. “It has been coming since the end of March. I take the sheets out of it and I enter every one of them as nothing and they go in the drawer with the rest.”

“How many in the bag.”

“I have never counted them,” said Wenna Raut. “Nobody has ever asked me to count them and there is no place whatever to put the answer.”

She got the bag down out of the bottom of the cupboard where it lived and turned it out onto the table, which she now was allowed to use it for.

Eleven sheets, in about nine different hands, all of them the foot of a printed page with a pencil line or two in the two inches under a striking.

“Then it is eleven a week.”

“It is eleven this week,” said Wenna Raut. “At the end of March it was nine in a month. In the month of July it was most days. You have been counting what is in the drawer and nobody has been counting what is coming in, and those are two different figures, and both of them are right, and there is no form in this city that will hold the second one.”

## Half Past Two On The Thirtieth Of September

“There is one thing about the page that I am permitted to tell you and I have told you four times and you have written it down three of them.”

“The second column.”

“The second column can be added to until the sheet is squared at about half past two on the last working day of this quarter,” said Wenna Raut. “After half past two on the thirtieth of September nothing goes on that sheet until the thirtieth of December.”

“There is no form for going back to a sheet that has gone up on a rail, and I cannot be asked to hurry about it in September and I cannot be asked to hurry about it in December either.”

“How many lines are in it.”

“Two,” said Wenna Raut. “And the second of the two has been in that column since before I came into this room and it is not going anywhere. If you have something for that column then you have until then, and there is nothing after then, and there is no room in this city to put a thing you have decided late.”

He looked at the window.

It was the twenty-third of August and the thirtieth of September was thirty-seven days away, and he had said a thing in that room in June that had gone out on a rail at four on the thirtieth and could never be taken off it, and the whole of his life had turned on the fact that he had got that right by four hours.

Thirty-eight days was not four hours.

## A Sentence He Could Not Phrase

He had it written out twice by eleven o’clock, on a sheet of nothing, and both of the versions were a sentence.

He knew what it was when he read it. It was a decision he had made, in the mouth of an office, addressed to a woman who could not improve one word of it, and he had done this once already in this room and had got exactly what he asked for.

That was the first of the four things he could not get past that morning.

“What can you enter,” he said.

“A date,” said Wenna Raut. “A place. An hour. A thing that was done. And the person it was done to, or a person who was there, or a person who was given it.”

“I have a date.”

“You have a date,” she agreed. “You have got the day it came in and the number of the day it came in on and you have got four hundred and nine pieces of paper, and not one of the five fields on that form is a field a piece of paper goes into.”

“I am not permitted to put a piece of paper into the field for a person, because a piece of paper is not a person, and because I have entered nine hundred and forty of these since I came to this room and I have not improved one of them and I am not going to improve the first one that is about paper.”

“If I gave you the instruction as a sentence.”

“It would be a sentence and I could not enter it, and you have stood in this room with a sentence on my table and I entered the exchange and went back to my column,” said Wenna Raut. “I am not going to do that twice with a better sentence.”

“I am not going to put a sentence on your table.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “You are going to put it in your coat and you are going to go away and think about it for about five weeks, and you are going to come back on the last day it can be done and either say nothing at all or say something that is not a sentence, and I have told you the date so that nobody in this building can be blamed afterwards for not giving you a chance.”

He put the sheet in his coat.

He had done that before in this room with a nine-word sentence on it, and doing nothing at all had been correct then, and he had written the nothing down afterwards as if it had been an action.

*Two thousand three hundred and sixty-two days. Less two thousand three hundred and fifty-five, the sixteenth of August of the seventh year, is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the twenty-third of November of the sixth year, two thousand and ninety-two, is two hundred and seventy days, which is nine months exactly. The fourth of six lines has had a stroke through it from the first letter to the last for every one of them, and about four hundred people have written in pencil in the two inches under it without ever being told that it is not to them.*

*Less the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, two thousand two hundred and nineteen, is a hundred and forty-three days, which is four months with twenty-three days after them. That sheet went up on a rail at four on that morning with everything on it exactly as it was, and this building has not sent a paper to any other building since.*

## The Ninth Of August

He was at a street two streets wide by about half past eleven with his hands in his pockets and nothing at all in them, and Perla Quist had the third table and a bundle on the second one and about nine sheets of pencil paper under the third, which was about a fifth of what had been there in June, and she said the number before he asked for it because she has been saying numbers before he asks for them since the seventeenth of June.

“There is a woman who comes to this table,” she said.

He waited.

“She has not been since the ninth of August,” said Perla Quist. “She did not come on the tenth and she did not come on the eleventh and she has not come since, and she did not send anything and she did not say she was coming back, and I have not sent anybody to her house because I do not know her house.”

“She has been eleven times.”

“She has been eleven times since the ninth of January and she came four times in the last two months of it, and the fourth of those four she did not bring me anything to write,” said Perla Quist. “That is all I know. A person can stop coming for a reason that has nothing to do with you and I have not got a reason and I am not going to invent one to keep the morning tidy.”

He put his hands in his pockets.

There were two drawers in this city now that he knew the contents of, in two buildings, and neither of them opened onto a street.

In one of them were four hundred and nine pieces of paper in about nine different hands, and a cloth bag with eleven more in it, and nothing whatever entered against any of them, and a woman with a pen who was permitted to enter what she was given and had been given nothing.

In the other one, under a second table in a laundry that had been running nineteen years, was a single sheet of rag paper with a pencil bought out of the money for soap across the bottom of it, put there on the ninth of August by a person who cannot read.

He could not do one single thing with either of them.

He had spent the only thing this office is permitted to spend, which is him, and there was not one form in this city that would let him spend it again on the same question.

“About nine people have stopped coming here too,” said Perla Quist, not looking up. “Since the middle of the month. They were not unkind about it and I did not ask and I am telling you because you will hear it from somebody else and I would rather you heard it from me while it is a number and not a story.”

“Any of them say why.”

“No,” said Perla Quist. “That is what I would like about it. In four years somebody is going to ask them and they are going to have to answer, and I will not be here to say that they told me nothing at the time.”

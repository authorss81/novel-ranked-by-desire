# About Nine Houses

Seven days had gone since a woman who had run a laundry for about nineteen years had asked him at a counter whether that was where such a thing was supposed to go, and he had said he did not know, standing up.

Two thousand one hundred and ninety-three was the fourth of March and the eleventh is day two thousand two hundred, and two thousand two hundred less two thousand one hundred and ninety-three is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.

He wanted to know whether about nine houses was nine houses.

That was the whole of the morning and it is a smaller want than it sounds, because about nine houses is a thing that has been said out loud three times in six weeks by two men and a woman, and none of the three of them has an instrument, and he was going to go in and see.

## Seven O’Clock At A Kitchen Table

The first house was a kitchen and the woman was at a table in it with a wash-line across four feet of the room and a cup on the table in a saucer upside down so that nothing could get into it.

She could not read. He found that out in about nine seconds and did not have to ask, because she had the copy face down on the table with one corner of it under the saucer so it would not blow away, which is how a person keeps a thing they cannot read.

“It is the four sentences,” she said. “My husband reads them out to me on a week-end morning and he does them wrong on purpose so that I will correct him, and I have been correcting him every one of those mornings since about the middle of January.”

“Does he do them wrong.”

“He puts the Register where the man goes,” said the woman. “That is the wrong way round and I have told him nine times and he still does it, and it is the only thing in this house I am better at than he is.”

He looked at the copy and it was the foot of the twenty-fourth sheet with eleven words in the two inches in pencil, and two of the eleven were a month and a lane and the other nine were a street and a number of a house.

“You wrote this yourself.”

“My husband wrote it out for me and I did the two inches with a pencil off the mantelpiece,” said the woman. “I cannot write my name. I can do a month and a street and I have been doing a month and a street for about two months and there is nobody I could give it to, so it is under the saucer.”

## A Barbers At Eleven

The second was a barbers and there was a man cutting at the far end with a client in the chair and nobody else in it.

The man at the counter had it folded in four in the breast of his coat and he took it out before Lin Hao had said anything at all.

“That is the bottom of a sheet,” he said. “I have had it since the ninth of December and I do not know who left it and it is not mine and I have not thrown it out because it is a paper and you do not throw out a paper.”

“You have read it.”

“I have had it in my coat for three months and I have not read it, because I cannot read it properly and I have been afraid to get into it in front of the client,” said the man. “That is the whole of my interest in it and I have never once asked anybody about it.”

A man who has carried a thing for three months and has not been asked about it is the ordinary condition of this city. It is not the condition that foot of paper is in.

The third house had two of them.

He got about four houses in six hours and he could not tell which of them he had already been in.

There was a fourth place he did not go into, because it was a barbers’ back room and there was a man asleep in it and he had not the heart, and he wrote it down as a thing he had not been into, which is the first honest entry in anything he has kept since the spring.

## Nine Is Not A Figure

Nine is not a figure.

Nine is what a man says when he has been in three rooms and heard the same thing in each of them, and he had been in three rooms, and about nine was the most he had.

If he had counted them he would have had an instrument, and counting them would have been an instrument, and the whole of what this city does to a number that came out of a building is done by the sentence at the foot of that building’s page. He had been on the other side of that sentence for six years. He was not going to cross it now to find out how many kitchens he had been in.

He went down to the corner at about three and the boy was not there and the board by the kerb had the sixty-third form on it and both of the boxes at the foot of it were empty.

He asked the boy for the sheet on a week day and the boy said no.

The boy gave him the reason, and it was the reason and not a new one, and it did not need hearing a second time: nine people a week pass a rail, and a corner is where people talk, and what is said out loud in a street cannot be called back afterwards by anybody at all.

“I know,” he said.

“You do not have it,” said the boy. “You want it. I have said the reason. That is all there is.”

## Brack And The Chalk

There were nine or ten people at the wall end in the lane off Cordwain when he got there at ten to six, and there were about forty of them by six, because a wall is where people stand when they want to be near other people and outside at the same time.

Emil Brack had the slate set up on end and twelve books stacked beside it, and a slate is a thing you cannot rub a mistake out of, which is why he uses one.

He reads about nine figures a morning. He says the reason for every one of them out loud before he writes the first one, and then he writes, and the date and the weather go against every line and a reason goes in the margin against every one he has ringed, and about nine hundred people in this city have got that habit because of him and most of the rest of the city has not.

“The rate has not changed,” said Emil Brack, before Lin Hao had got to the front of the nine.

“I did not ask you.”

“You came at ten to six on a week day and you have been in about nine houses today and you have not counted them, which means you want to know whether what I say is true,” said Emil Brack.

“It has not changed. It is nine. It is going to go on being nine and it is not going to be a figure, and it has been nine since the thirteenth of July and it was nine on the ninth of November before that, and it was nine in the spring before that.”

“How many times have you said it.”

“About two thousand,” said Emil Brack, “and I have never once been able to check whether any of the nine is true. That is the whole of my standing and I have not been able to raise it in about four years and I have stopped trying and it is the best thing I have.”

He put the chalk down and squared the books and did not look at Lin Hao.

“I want a page.”

“I know you do.”

“I have wanted a page since the ninth of November,” said Emil Brack. “I have not asked you for it. I am not going to ask you for it, and I am telling you that I am not going to before you offer it, because if I asked you for it you would be able to give me one, and that is the reason I am not going to.”

“You could have had one in November.”

“I could have had one in November and I would have had it at a wall end and about forty people would have seen me receive it off a man in a rank-one coat,” said Emil Brack, “and then every one of the nine would have been a thing a man in a coat gave me, and I have spent four years standing at the end of a wall on my own and I am not going to hand the last of it to anybody.”

He is not thanked. That has been the arrangement for about four years and he chose it, and he was not thanked at that wall end tonight either.

## Envy, And The Cold Thing Under It

He wanted it. That was the plain shape of the evening and he did not manage to hide it from himself until the walk home.

He wanted a slate and twelve books and a wall end and about forty people who turn up because he is standing there, and he wanted to be a man that somebody asks whether a figure is true.

He had found out that same afternoon that a woman with a charcoal in a laundry behind three tables was keeping nine sheets in nine kitchens correctly, and did it better than his office does, and had been doing it since the ninth of January.

## The Woman Who Came Back Up The Lane

She came back up the lane at about six with a copy in her hand and stopped at the edge of the nine.

She put it up against the slate and she did not read it, because she cannot read, and she stood there with her chin about four inches from the chalk.

“Is that one true,” she said.

Emil Brack looked at it and then looked at her.

“No,” he said. “I do not know whether it is true. I have never known whether any of the nine is true and I have been saying so at this end of this wall since before you were born.”

Everybody at the wall end heard it.

“That is the first person,” said the woman, “who has told me that.”

*Two thousand two hundred days. Less two thousand one hundred and ninety-three, the fourth of March of the seventh year, is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and twenty-two days, which is four months with two days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard has had nothing at the foot of it for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is four hundred and eighty-two days, which is sixteen months with two days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet and it has let a man in a delivery coat say nine about two thousand times without once being able to check whether any of the nine is true.*

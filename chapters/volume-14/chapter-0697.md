# The Only Thing He Said Yes To

He had said no to a man with a folder on the third of December at four minutes past ten and on the fourth he went up a stair off a third court and asked for the opposite thing, and it took him about eleven minutes to get down to four words and it is the only request he has made of that office in seven years that he got.

Two thousand four hundred and sixty-two was the third of December and the tenth is day two thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, and two thousand four hundred and sixty-nine less two thousand four hundred and sixty-two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## Not In The Second Column

“I am not asking you to put a line in the column.”

Wenna Raut had the column open in front of her and did not look up, because that sentence has been said to her about nine times in this room and on every one of the nine occasions it has been the start of something that ended with nothing going in it.

“Say the rest of it.”

“There is a woman who comes to a laundry off a street two streets wide and she has been eleven times since the ninth of January and she cannot read,” said Lin Hao.

“She wrote on a piece of paper with her own pencil and she paid for the pencil out of what she had and she asked a woman with a pencil whether it was right, and the woman with a pencil has never once been able to tell her, and she has not been back since the ninth of August and she did not say she was coming back.”

“I have that,” said Wenna Raut. “It has been in my morning since the middle of September and it is not mine and I cannot do one thing about it.”

“I want a piece of paper with a date on it in this office’s hand that says she gave it something.”

Wenna Raut put the pen down the way she puts it down when she has finished with it for the moment.

“That column is not taking it.”

“That is not what I asked for.”

“It is not going in the column and I am telling you why before you say it again, and then you are going to say the four words and I am going to write them and that will be the end of it,” said Wenna Raut.

“The second column has three lines in it and it will have three lines in it on the thirtieth of this month and it will have three lines in it on the thirtieth of March and that is the whole of the column. And the thing you have brought me is not carried by anybody. There is no person it is against and no person who was there and no person who was given it.”

“There is a person who wrote something and the writing is not a thing that can be put in any field on this form, and the field for a person goes in empty or the line does not go in, and I told you that on the nineteenth of November and it has not changed since I said it to you.”

“It is not going in the column.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “And there is no other place in this building for it. There is no form on which this office gives anything to anybody. Nobody has ever been given a receipt by this city, and I have said so to about nine people this month and I will say it to nine more.”

“Then what do I do.”

“You give me four words,” said Wenna Raut.

## She Gave One Sheet

He had them ready in about eleven seconds.

“You may not improve one word of it.”

“I am not going to improve one word of it and I am going to read it to you before I write it and you are going to tell me whether I have got it right and I am going to tell you no, because I never do.”

He said, “She gave one sheet.”

Wenna Raut looked at him for about four seconds.

“That is four words and it says she gave this office one sheet of paper and it does not say what was on it and it does not say what it was about and it does not say it was true,” she said. “Is that the whole of what you want said to a woman who has been coming to a table for eleven times in eleven months.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

He had been ready with the answer to that one for about nine days and it came out in about four seconds and it was the truest thing he said in that room all year.

“Because it is the first thing this office has ever been given that anybody in this city could be told about,” he said, “and because she gave it, and because there is nothing on it that says she was owed it or that she was refused it or that anything was done to her. If it says any of those things then in about four years somebody will read it out and say this office made an entry against her.”

“It will not have, and I will not be here to say it had not.”

Wenna Raut wrote it on the back of a sheet, in ink, with a nib that has been ground down twice this year, and put the date at the top of it in her own hand, and signed for it at the foot.

She did not read it back. That is not what she does with a thing she has been given, and she squared the sheet to the edge of the table and gave it to him face down.

“You will not get that from me in a drawer,” she said.

“I am not putting it in a drawer.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut, “and that is the second time somebody has stood in this room with something in his hand and not put it in a drawer, and I did not expect there to be a third.”

## The Third Table And A Pencil

She came back on the eighth of December, and Perla Quist knew about it before he got there because Perla Quist knows everything that happens at that table about nine minutes before everybody else.

She had a bundle on her arm and she took it off and put it on the second table and she stood at the third one for about a minute and a half with her hands on the wood, and she did not sit down, and Lin Hao stood four feet off with a piece of paper face down in his hand and did not give it to her.

“It is a hundred and twenty-one days,” said Perla Quist.

“I am aware of it.”

“Nine of the eleven stopped.”

“She was the one who stopped last and she is the only one of the nine who has come back,” said Perla Quist, “and I have thought about that for about nine weeks and I have got a worse answer than the one I had. They stopped because the page said they were given nothing.”

“She stopped because she could not read what she was given, and she is the only one of the nine who could not have known that.”

She turned round.

“Give it to me,” she said.

## The Question She Has Asked Eleven Times

She gave it to her, not him.

She put it into her hand with the writing side up and shut the woman’s fingers on it, which she has done about eleven times with a pencil and had never done once with a piece of paper.

The woman turned it over once and looked at it for about four seconds, and turned it back, and looked at it again, and her face did not do anything at all, because there is nothing on that sheet she is able to read and there has not been anything on any of the eleven she brought that she was able to read.

“Is it right,” she said.

That was it. That was the whole of what she had come back for and it was the same eleven words she had said at that table eleven times in eleven months, in a kitchen off a street with her own pencil across the bottom of a sheet.

Perla Quist did not answer for about four seconds, and Lin Hao did not answer at all, and in about four years this office is going to have about four hundred and nine claims in a drawer in a building with no door onto a street and every one of them will be a thing somebody else wrote and not one of them will be checked by anybody.

“I cannot tell you,” said Perla Quist.

“You made it.”

“I did not make it. It came out of a building off a third court at about half past eleven yesterday in a woman’s hand and I have not read it and I am not going to read it to you, and it is not mine and there is nobody in this city who may read it to you.”

“Then what is it.”

“It has a date on it,” said Perla Quist, “and it is about four lines long, and it came out of a place that has never in nine years given anybody anything, and that is all that I am able to tell you and I am telling you all of it.”

The woman put the paper in her coat.

She did not say thank you, because she does not know what it says and nobody asked her to, and nobody in that room asked her anything else, and nobody sent anybody to find out where she lives, and there is no form in this city on which this office may ask a person what she has done with a piece of paper.

Lin Hao stood four feet off with his hands in his pockets for the whole of it and was not thanked and did not expect to be and had seen to it, in the summer, up on the hill with nothing in his hands, that nobody was ever going to be in a position to thank him for this.

## Four Words

*The whole of what is on it, in four words: she gave one sheet.*

That is what is on it.

It is in a clerk’s hand in ink on the back of a sheet with the date at the top of it and a signature at the foot, and it is the first thing that has ever gone out of the front door of that building in nine years, and it says that a woman who cannot read handed something in, and it does not say what, and it does not say that anything was owed to her, and it does not say that anybody was refused.

About nine years, and in all of them a person who could not read has been able to say that she came, because she came, and that is the whole of what happened, and she has never once had a piece of paper that said so in anybody’s hand.

She has it now. She does not know what the four words say and she is not asked to.

And in about four years there will be a finding in a building on another side of this city with four lines in it and one word at the end of them, and the word says that this office has never asked anybody in this city a question about a thing anybody else wrote down.

And a woman who cannot read has been asked nothing at all, and has been handed one piece of paper, and those two things happened in two different rooms and neither of them is going to be announced.

*Two thousand four hundred and sixty-nine days. Less two thousand three hundred and forty-eight, the ninth of August of the seventh year, is a hundred and twenty-one days, which is four months and one day after them, and a woman who cannot read had not come to a table in a laundry off a street two streets wide for every one of them, and did not send anything, and did not say she was coming.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is seventy days, which is two months with ten days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them with three lines in the second column of it and eight hundred and fifty-two at the head.*

*Less two thousand two hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, is two hundred and fifty days, which is eight months with ten days after them, and about four hundred kitchens in this city have the foot of that sheet written out in a hand that is not the office’s.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is three hundred and ninety-one days, which is thirteen months with one day after them. Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is seven hundred and fifty-one days, which is twenty-five months with one day after them, and a man who said no to a man with a folder on the third of this month has had one piece of paper with four words on it made.*

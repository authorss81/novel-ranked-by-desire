# Two Squares And Nothing

He went up the hill at about ten on the twenty-fourth of December with a piece of paper folded once in the inside of his coat that had been in a box under a counter in a yard nine streets from a works department for thirty years until the seventeenth of this month, and it was still folded, and he had not opened it, and he had not looked at the date on it, and he had not asked anybody about it.

Two thousand four hundred and seventy-six was the seventeenth of December and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand four hundred and eighty-three, and two thousand four hundred and eighty-three less two thousand four hundred and seventy-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## About Two Hundred At A Kerb

The sixty-fourth month’s form had been on that board since the seventh of October and it was coming down at the end of the month and the sixty-fifth would go over the top of it, and there had been sixty-three before it, one a month, and every one of them had two ruled squares at the foot and nothing whatever in either of them.

About two hundred people a day stop where that wall meets that kerb. About nine of them a week get low enough to make out four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of nine feet of new render and two words cut clean through the face of it.

About ninety of the two hundred read the board.

The boy was there at about half past nine with the jar of paste on his shoulder and a piece of rag in his hand, and he had come at the same time he came every morning and nobody has ever asked him to.

He looked at the coat.

“You have got something in the inside of it,” he said, “and it is not a bundle, because a bundle goes under an arm, and it is not the works form, because you gave me the works form in the fourth month and it has been on that board ever since.”

## He Did Not Ask

That was the whole of it and it lasted about nine seconds and then neither of them said anything for about four minutes.

The rule he works under is not a rule anybody printed. It is that nobody in this city asks him anything about either square at the foot of a form, and there has not been one in about a year and a half, and in seven years of putting them up he has not been wrong about anything, and he has never once been asked whether a board by a kerb is the right thing to be doing.

Lin Hao stood four feet off with a folded piece of paper in the inside of his coat and about a hundred and forty words of a sentence he had brought up the hill, and he did not say any of them.

There is a board by that kerb that about ninety of the two hundred people a day can read from the other side of the street, and it is the only thing in this city he can put a piece of paper on that the public stands in front of.

And there is no form anywhere in this city for a word on a stone.

He did not ask the boy to put the docket up.

He stood there with it in his coat and about two hundred and ten people came past in the first hour and about ninety of them stopped at the board and about nine of them went down on a knee at the foot of the wall and not one of them did both.

## What The Boy Said In April, And What He Said Now

In the fourth month of this year the boy had told him, once, in about nine seconds, what the two squares at the foot of a form are for: a figure on one side and a standing on the other. There is no figure for a wall, and he was not going to invent one on the end of his own brush and get a man in another building in trouble over a number he had made up himself.

That was the first time in seven years anybody had told him what the two squares are for.

On the seventh of October the boy had not said it again and had told him to stop looking at the bottom of it. On the twenty-fourth of December he did not say it at all.

He was not refusing. Refusing is a thing that takes two people and he had decided in the spring, in about four seconds, that it was not going to take two.

He mixed his paste the way he has always mixed it, which is thinner than a man mixes it when he is sticking a hoarding board, and he did it without being told, and he ran a strip of paper along the top edge of the sixty-fourth where it had lifted at the corner, and he did not look at Lin Hao once while he did it.

“You are going to go down the hill at some point,” he said.

“I am.”

“You were going to ask me something when you came up,” said the boy, “and you have not asked it, and you are not going to, and I am not going to help you not ask it, and that is all that has happened here this morning.”

Then he shouldered the jar and went up the render with the brush and stopped dead at the bottom of it, at the new joint, the way he stops dead every morning, because paint will not hold on a face a mason has worked.

## About Nine Of Them A Week

At about half past eleven he came down the hill and about two hundred and thirty people had come past and about a hundred had read the board and about nine had gone down to the four inches.

On the way down he worked out what it would take, and it took him about four steps.

To put a piece of paper in a public place in this city you need four things and he could get three of them.

Somebody has to be allowed to put it there. The boy is the only person who puts anything on that board and the boy has never once been asked and has never once asked.

Something has to say what the paper is. A board by a kerb carries a month’s form for an address, and the form has two ruled squares at the foot for a figure and for a standing, and a docket is neither, and putting a docket on a board would say that a delivery had been made to that address and nobody in this city would be told what was in it.

Somebody has to keep it there. Everything on that board has come down at the end of a month for sixty-four months, and about ninety people a day read what is on it and about nine of them a week go further down, and a page that comes down on the thirtieth is a page nobody in this city can correct.

And the fourth thing he could not get at all, which was a reason for it to be there that did not depend on him.

So it stays in the inside of his coat, folded once, in the hand of a man who carried it and not the hand of a man who cut it, and he is carrying it down a hill past about nine people a week who could read it if it were on the board and could not do anything whatever with it if they did.

There are four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of that wall and they have been there for more than thirty years. They came out from under nine feet of render in the fourth month of this year together with sixty-two month’s forms, one a month, stacked inside the wall since the eighth of October of an earlier year. Two words were cut into that stone before the block ever went anywhere at all.

He has one piece of paper in his coat that says so. It is about the size of a sheet folded once and it is in the hand of a man who carried it and not the hand of a man who cut it, and there is a line on it with two words against it and a date at the head of it, and he has not read the date and he is not going to.

There is a drawer in a building off a third court that would take it and that he has instructed in writing is not to be opened, and there is no form anywhere on which a building off a third court may be handed a piece of paper by a man in a rank-one coat, and there is a bundle of twenty-five things going into that room on the thirtieth of December that nobody is obliged to open.

There is a woman with a pencil off a street two streets wide who has about nine sheets under a table and about three hundred and forty lines in a margin and who will not put a name against a thing, and she has been right about that twice and he has not argued with her either time.

And there is a board by a kerb that ninety people a day can read from the other side of a street, and a boy who will not be asked.

*Two thousand four hundred and eighty-three days. Less two thousand four hundred and fifty-five, the twenty-sixth of November of the seventh year, is twenty-eight days, and a man with a folder drew a finding on the fourth of December out of four lines and one word.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is eighty-four days, which is two months with twenty-four days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them with three lines in the second column of it and eight hundred and fifty-two at the head.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is four hundred and five days, which is thirteen months with fifteen days after them, and less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is seven hundred and sixty-five days, which is twenty-five months with fifteen days after them.*

*About two hundred people a day stand at a place where there is nothing to read except a form about a wall that no longer exists, and nothing to put anything on except a board, and four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of it with two words in the face of them cut by somebody nobody in this city has found and is never going to be told about.*

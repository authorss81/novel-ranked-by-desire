# The Man In The Delivery Coat

Six days had gone since three hundred and ten lines had been read out loud in a yard and one of them had been rubbed out in front of about nine hundred people by the woman who wrote it, before anybody asked her, and since then he had been trying to work out who in this city could be told a thing once and hold it.

Two thousand three hundred and forty-two was the third of August and the ninth is day two thousand three hundred and forty-eight, and two thousand three hundred and forty-eight less two thousand three hundred and forty-two is six, and the month between them says nine less three.

## Two Flights Down, For The Third Time In A Month

He went down at about half past three because a magistrate’s clerk had put something on a page on the twentieth of July and he had not been able to put it down anywhere else.

Two flights run over that room and it has neither a window nor a fire, and it has gone twenty-six years without them, and a lamp is the only thing anybody has ever put in it to do anything about that.

He did not knock. There is no arrangement in that building by which a man knocks on the door of a keeper, and the door was shut, and there was no light under it, and he stood on the landing for about two minutes and then said it to a shut door, which is a thing he has now done once in this city and did not enjoy.

“A magistrate’s clerk has written down that this office was asked one question and did not answer it,” he said. “And you have written six words under a figure for thirty years and nobody has ever been asked about those either, and I thought somebody in this city ought to know that both of those are the same fact.”

Nothing on the other side of the door. About nine people went up and down the stairs.

He was going to go away and then he thought about it, and what he thought about it was that he had come down two flights to tell a woman who has certified about nine hundred compositions of premises four times a year for thirty years that a man with a pen had written down, in another building, on another sheet, in the hand of another man entirely, that a body will not answer a question.

She did not open the door.

“If you are going to use me,” said Hanneke Ruis through about two inches of wood, “then be quick about it and do it in the room. I am not doing it on a landing.”

“I was not going to use you.”

“You came down two flights with a thing a stranger wrote about you in your mouth,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and you have said, out loud on a landing, that a keeper and an office are the same kind of fact. That is using me. You have been doing it since the sixth of July and you have not noticed.”

“I noticed on the third of June.”

“You noticed on the third of June,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and you have been carrying it about ever since in a place where I could not see it, and now you have put it outside a door.”

The stairs went on.

“I will not be a thing you have used twice,” she said. “There are about four hundred people in this city with a pencil and not one of them has ever been used by this office, and you should leave it there. Come down when you want a figure. Do not come down when you want a witness.”

## Ten To Six At The Wall End

There were about ten of them at that end of the lane when he came at ten to six and about five times that by six, because that is where a city stands beside people it does not know.

Emil Brack had the slate up on its end and the twelve books squared beside it, because nothing that has gone on slate has ever come off it again, and there is no getting a morning back once it is ringed.

He read nine. He said the reason out loud before he wrote the first of them, and every line took the date and the weather against it, and every line he rang took a reason into the margin beside it.

Nobody has ever said thank you to him, and for four years that has been the arrangement, and he arranged it, and he would not have it any other way.

“A clerk has written down that your office was asked a question and did not answer it.”

“I heard,” said Emil Brack. “It is on a board in a yard. Somebody read it out on the nineteenth and got the number wrong and about nine people said so inside four minutes, which is exactly your instrument, by the way, working.”

“It was not a number.”

“It was a thing about a building with about four hundred papers in it that it cannot do one thing with,” said Emil Brack. “Read correctly by about nine hundred people who cannot check any of it.”

“Four years at this end of this wall,” he said, “and about two thousand mornings on that slate, and there is not one morning of it I have ever been able to prove. That is my whole standing, and I have told you so before, and nobody thanked me for it then and nobody will now.”

“You have never been asked.”

“No.”

“You have never once put that to anybody.”

“No,” said Emil Brack, “and I have wanted to be asked on every one of those two thousand mornings, and I have never once asked anybody, and I am not going to start with you either, and you may write that down as a refusal.”

He put the chalk down.

“You know what has happened to your office in the last month.”

“Yes.”

“Then say it, because you have had four months of doing it for other people and it is your turn.”

Lin Hao stood at the end of that wall for about four seconds.

“A body in this city has said out loud, in a finding, in a man’s hand, that it will not answer a question about whether it has been given anything,” he said. “And a man in a delivery coat has been saying the true version of that sentence at a wall end for four years, about nine figures a morning, and he has never once had to print it, because nobody in this city has ever thought to ask him.”

“Right,” said Emil Brack.

And he was right about every part of it, and nothing that had happened in a room off a third court took a single one of his facts away from him, and nobody thanked him for it.

## The Thing He Wants And Does Not Ask For

“I could check one of them.”

Emil Brack did not look up.

“You could not,” he said. “There is nothing in this city that could check one of the nine. I have had that written on the edge of the slate in my own hand since about the second year and I have never rubbed it off, and you have been standing in front of me for about four months.”

“No. I could stand here at ten to six one morning and count the people who go past, and write nine down after it, and it would be a figure I could not check either.”

“That would be me with a friend,” said Emil Brack, “and it would be worth nothing to either of us.”

He squared the books and then he stood still for about four seconds, which he does not do.

“I will tell you what I want,” said Emil Brack. “You are not going to be asked for it, and I am putting that on the table now so that the offer cannot happen afterwards.”

“All right.”

“I want one of the nine to be wrong.”

The lane went on.

“In four years,” said Emil Brack, “not one of the nine has been found wrong by anybody, and that is not because it is right nine times. That is because nobody can get near it.”

“If one of them turned out to be wrong this afternoon then the other eight would be worth something, because it would mean the method can fail and can be caught. At the moment I have nine figures a morning that cannot fail and cannot be caught, and that is the same as having nothing at all.”

He put the chalk on the ledge.

“That is what I want, and I have never said it out loud at this end of this wall before tonight, and I am not asking you for it.”

He picked the chalk back up.

## What He Is Not Going To Ask For

“Take it,” said Lin Hao. “That. That is yours. You have been saying it for four years and somebody has written it down in a building with a door onto a street, and I am the man who put it there, in the only way available to me, which was by refusing a question.”

“No,” said Emil Brack.

“It is yours and you cannot un-say what you said and I cannot un-print what was printed.”

“No, and I know both of those,” said Emil Brack. “And I am not going to stand at this wall end tonight and read it out.”

“Why not.”

“Because it was not mine this morning and it is not mine tonight,” said Emil Brack.

“It came out of a building with no door onto a street because a man in a coat could not answer a question. If I say it out loud on this wall at ten to six then about forty people hear it, and every one of them goes home and says it came from him.”

He turned one of the twelve books over and squared it again, which he does about nine times an hour.

“I have been the mouth of nine figures for four years and I can put a name and a date and a reason in a margin against every one of them,” said Emil Brack, “and not one of the nine is anybody’s. That is the only thing I have and I am not giving it away for a sentence a man in a coat could not finish.”

He squared the twelve books.

“You have been the mouth of it since the tenth of June,” he said, “and you are not going to enjoy the next four months the way you think you are, and when it stops being a thing you say and becomes a thing people say about you, you will not be able to stop it, and there is nobody in this city who will come and ask you where you got it.”

“That is what I am frightened of.”

“Then be careful with it,” said Emil Brack, “and do not come down here and tell me about it, because I have nine figures to get through before six and none of the nine is yours.”

## Nine And A Reason

He read the ninth one at about twenty past six and said the reason for it out loud before he wrote it, and it was about a street that has no water on it.

He put the chalk in his pocket at six and said nothing else, and there were about fifty people at that end of the lane, and one of them said thank you to him, because about fifty people do not know what he is, and he said he would be obliged if that stopped.

*Two thousand three hundred and forty-eight days. Less two thousand three hundred and forty-two, the third of August of the seventh year, is six, and the month between them says nine less three.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is six hundred and thirty days, which is twenty-one months exactly. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and it is not the reason a man in a delivery coat is standing at the end of a wall tonight, and he has never once said that it was.*

*Less the thirtieth of September of the sixth year, two thousand and thirty-nine, is three hundred and nine days, which is ten months with nine days after them, and a ledger in a back room off a street two streets wide has had about three hundred and ten dates in the margin of it in pencil, and one line of them was rubbed out on the third of this month in front of about nine hundred people.*

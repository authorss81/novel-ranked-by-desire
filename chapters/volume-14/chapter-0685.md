# The Second Sentence

He had believed that a sentence nobody could argue with was the strongest thing in the city, and it had taken him seven months and a third line in a column to find out that it was also the thing four hundred people had been standing behind without knowing what it was standing behind.

Two thousand three hundred and eighty-one was the twelfth of September and the eighteenth is day two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven, and two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven less two thousand three hundred and eighty-one is six, and eighteen less twelve is six by the month count.

## The One That Was True

The second of the four sentences says, in other words, that where the Register has nothing in it then nobody has been given anything.

It has been at the foot of every sheet since the first of February of the first year. It was there before this office had a room, before it had a clerk, before it had a Registrar, and it was written in an office hand and it was not his and two of the others were not his either.

For six years it has been the one line on that page that nobody in Ostara has ever been able to argue with, and the reason was never cleverness. The reason was that there was nothing on the page to argue with. It was a statement about an absence. You cannot put a counter-example to a sentence about something that did not happen.

He had spent two years of his life making that true on purpose.

## What It Was Carried By

He stood in the passage off Cordwain at about eleven on the morning of the eighteenth and let it come past him.

The sheet was still the twenty-sixth, still face up on the rail at the height of a man’s chest, and about a hundred people a month put a palm on the corner of it to get past the counter, and the counter is in the way. That is how it works and it has worked since a March in a year nobody behind the counter mentions.

He stood there for about forty minutes and watched what four hundred people in this city do with a sentence.

The first eleven went past without getting to the bottom of it, which is the ordinary rate and about nine in a week get there and one of those nine is the man at the counter.

The twelfth was a woman with a bundle over her shoulder who stopped, and went to the bottom, and read the second of the four, and then read the line under it that is not a sentence and cannot be read as one.

Then she said something to the man she was with.

He was nine feet off and he heard all of it, because this is a narrow passage and people talk at the bottom of a page.

“That is what I went for,” she said.

“You did not go for anything, you went for a receipt.”

“There is no receipt. Everybody in this yard has been telling me for seven months that you go to that door and they take it.”

“They take it,” said the man she was with, “and they do not do one thing with it. That is what the line under the four sentences is. Read it.”

She read the line under the four sentences, which is a striking and a name and two inches of nothing, and she read it twice, and then she said:

“Two inches of nothing. They do not even write anything in it.”

They went on up the passage and he stood there for another half hour with the two of them gone round the corner and about forty more people going past, and he understood what had happened in about four minutes and then had to sit down on the step of a counter for nine minutes because of it.

## Nobody Was Standing In The Yard

The correction had not been refused. It had been absent.

In a yard off the Vestry about nine hundred people can read and about nine hundred people can be contradicted, and a sentence that goes wrong in that yard is dead inside a quarter of an hour. That had been true since the third of August and he had stood in the middle of it and watched it work, and about nine people corrected about nine hundred inside about four minutes, for nothing, in daylight.

A rail in a passage has about a hundred people a month in it and not one of them is standing there for that.

He went and found Perla Quist at half past two and she had already worked it out and had been working it out since the fifteenth.

“They have stopped coming,” she said.

“Nine of them.”

“Nine of them since the middle of the month and I have counted because I count,” said Perla Quist. “And one of them came on the fifteenth with eleven sheets in his hand and put them on this table and asked me for them again.”

“You did not have them.”

“I have never had them and I told him so and he did not believe me,” said Perla Quist, “and I said the name of the building and he went and got them and brought them back here and counted them out in front of me and put them in his coat and said thank you.”

She squared the bundle she was carrying.

“He said the page says they never give anybody anything, and that he went with eleven sheets and it was true, and that he would not come again and that nobody he knows would come either, and that the woman who told him they take it was owed better than that.” She looked up. “He was not angry. That is the part I cannot put down.”

“He was in the right mood entirely and he was kind about it, and I have had this table for nineteen years and nobody has ever been kind to me about anything.”

## The Sentence Doing Two Jobs

He went down a street two streets wide at about four with nothing in his hands and worked the whole of it out in about nine minutes and it took him the length of two streets.

The second sentence had been doing two jobs for six years and nobody had ever noticed because both of them were true.

It was about a person who went to a counter and was refused and went home again with nothing. That was the job it was written for and it is the whole reason it works, and about four hundred people in this city had read it and understood it and gone on their way and said nothing.

And since the thirtieth of August it had also been about this office, and since the twelfth of September about four hundred people could go and look up and see that it was no longer about this office, and what they did with that was the obvious thing.

A man said to a woman on the step of this office that the office had never been given anything in its life, and she had believed him, and eleven sheets went back into a coat.

He had spent nine years learning how to make a sentence that could not be answered, and he had done it twice — once with the second sentence and once with the third, and the third one was under a man’s name and not his own, and neither of them was a sentence anybody could put a counter-example to, and that was the whole of the method.

The method had no answer to a person who comes back and says, in the right mood, that the page told them so.

He got to the room off the third court at about half past five and Yusuf Adeyemi was there with his coat on and the second column open in front of him and a pen.

“You have worked it out,” said Yusuf Adeyemi.

“I have worked out that I have spent nine years building a sentence that a person could not argue with and I have used it on this office by accident.”

“You have not used it on this office by accident,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “In a month of a year that is not going to be named in this room you asked me the reason that sentence is true, and I told you that the reason it is true is that nobody is obliged to open the door of the room a thing goes into, and you wrote it down and you have been living on it ever since.”

“An office that is not given anything is the most powerful thing in this city and you have wanted to be able to say that in your own hand since before I came.”

He turned the pen over.

“The whole of what has happened since the thirtieth of August is that about four hundred people have begun to notice what the sentence is for,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “They have noticed because of the third line in a column, which is yours, and which was correct, and which I have not read one word of that I disagree with.”

*Two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven days. Less the first of February of the first year, day zero, is two thousand three hundred and eighty-seven, which is seventy-nine months with seventeen days after them. That is the whole of the life of this office and the whole of the life of the second of the four sentences at the foot of the page, and neither one has moved.*

*Less the sixteenth of August of the seventh year, two thousand three hundred and fifty-five, is thirty-two days, which is one month with two days after them. That is the day four hundred and nine pieces of pencil paper went up four flights in two cardboard boxes and were put on a table in a room and have not been entered against in any way at all.*

## The Woman Who Comes To The Table

He went down a street two streets wide at about half past five and there was one thing he had to ask, and he had been not asking it for nine days.

“She has not been,” said Perla Quist.

“No.”

“She has not been since the ninth of August and she has not sent anything and she has not said she is coming,” said Perla Quist, “and nobody has been sent to find out where that woman lives, because a woman who comes eleven times and then stops has been told something, and a woman who has been told something and wants to pass it on comes back.”

He stood in the steam for a while.

“Then what is it.”

“I think somebody told her the page says they do not give anybody anything,” said Perla Quist, “and I think she believed it, and I think she took her paper back off that table and put it in her coat and went home, and she cannot read it, and I do not know one single other thing about her and I have not got one single way of finding out.”

“That is the eleventh person.”

“That is the eleventh person in about eleven weeks,” said Perla Quist. “Nine of them told me nothing and one of them was kind about it and one of them cannot read.”

She picked the pencil up off the ledger and set it down again flush against the wood.

“There is a thing I have not said to you since the third of August and I am going to say it now because you have come down here twice this week for nothing and you are going to keep doing it,” she said. “Every one of those eleven carried a sheet of paper to a counter because of four sentences at the foot of a page. Not because of me and not because of a drawer.”

“Because a sentence told them the office had never been given anything, and they decided that a place which is given nothing is a place where a thing can be put down, and they were right, and they were right about it for seven months and they were right about it on the thirty-first of August as well.”

She turned the ledger round so the margin faced him.

“And on the first of September a man stood on that step and read the second of the four sentences out loud and it was true and it was about this building, and there was nothing else on the page to stop him saying it out loud, and he went home and told eleven people.”

Perla Quist took her hand off the page.

“You did the right thing on the twelfth,” she said. “I want that said in this room and I am saying it once. Nine lines in a column is not a scandal. Nine people not coming through my door in eleven weeks is a scandal, and it was done by a third line that says a true thing, and the true thing is the whole problem, and you knew it in February and I did not know it until the fifteenth.”

# A Chained Book Comes Down

Six days had gone since a woman with a pencil had cut nine ruled sheets on the long table of a laundry with a blade she used for linens, and had ruled a column for a name on every line and written nothing in any of them, and since then he had not been able to think about anything else in this city, and whatever he turned over turned out to be the same thing.

Two thousand three hundred and three was the twenty-fourth of June and the thirtieth is day two thousand three hundred and nine, and two thousand three hundred and nine less two thousand three hundred and three is six, and the two months say the same, which is thirty less twenty-four.

There is no thirty-first in this city, and the thirtieth is the last day the month and the quarter both have, and the twenty-sixth sheet was out of the drawer at half past two, squared against the wood, signed for, carried down a stair, and on a rail in a passage off Cordwain by four.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head, unmoved across ten sheets. Two lines in the second column, headed carried by. About four hundred and nineteen accounts under it, each with a day against it and an hour against the day, and nothing in the way of a figure on any one.

And about nine people in this city, in an ordinary week, get to the last two inches of it.

## Two Flights Down

There is one instrument in Ostara that has held a composition of a building once a year for thirty years, and it is not a book anybody can walk in and ask a question of, and the only person who may put anything into it is a woman who has never once in thirty years left her own name off the bottom of a page.

He went down the two flights at about ten past two in the afternoon because the sheet goes out at four and he wanted to be back up them before it did.

The counting room has no window in it and no fire in it and has had neither for twenty-six years, and there is one lamp on a stand in it, and there are two tables, and there is a shelf with a book on it.

The book had the strap across it and the chain through the keeper’s lock and it had been on that shelf since the first of April.

He stood in the middle of the floor with nothing in his hands, which is now the only way he enters any room in this city.

She came in from the corridor behind him and shut the door, which she has done every time for thirty years, and looked at the shelf, and then at him, and did not offer him a chair.

“I am not going to take it off,” said Hanneke Ruis.

## The Reason Given Out Loud

He had a sentence ready and it was the wrong one and he had brought it up two flights and he threw it away.

“Why not.”

She said it the way a keeper gives a composition: once, at the same speed, with her hands where they are and her coat still on.

“A thing that goes into a chained book comes out once a year,” said Hanneke Ruis. “The first of April and the first of July and the first of October and the first of January. Anybody in this city may come up two flights and look at it on the day and not on the day before.”

“That is the whole of what a chained book is.”

“That is the whole of what a chained book is,” said Hanneke Ruis. “It comes off the shelf on the first and it goes back on the first and it is not taken out again afterwards.”

“And you have about four hundred pieces of pencil paper.”

“I have about four hundred pieces of pencil paper in this city,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and about nine hundred people who can read them, and they are made every day. A thing that comes out four times a year is a record and it has a day on it that everybody in the city can meet. A thing that comes out every day of every month is not a record. A thing that comes out every day of every month is a season.”

She let him have that.

It was about nine seconds long and it was the oldest sentence anybody had said to him in a month, and it was going to be the thing he carried about this building for the rest of the quarter, and she was going to be right about it in about four years when the first of those four hundred people writes down something in the two inches under a printed page that is not true.

“A season is a thing a person arranges,” said Hanneke Ruis. “You can arrange a day once a year. Four hundred people with a pencil and about nine hundred readers and forty arguments an hour in a yard is a season, and nobody stands behind it who could be called on, and in four years one of those four hundred people will write a thing that is not so and there will be no day on it to set it against.”

“I know that.”

“You do not know that,” said Hanneke Ruis. “You have known it since about the ninth of January and you have not put it down anywhere, and you have spent five months wanting an instrument to put it down in, and that is what you have come up these stairs with, and it is the wrong want and I am not going to help you with it.”

“I am not asking you to certify them,” he said. “I am asking you to hold them.”

“You put it to a clerk of your own in March and she agreed to it at once, on the grounds that a clerk enters what she is handed and nothing else, and that this city has no form for a holding, and that holding is not an entry,” said Hanneke Ruis.

“And I have not got a form either. I have got a shelf and a chain and a strap and a lock with a single key in it, and about nine hundred compositions of premises in the book on the shelf.”

She turned round and looked at him.

“There is no line on any of them for what a person did to a tenant across about eleven months of a year,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and there is not going to be one added by me in a fit in June.”

“I know that too.”

“You know a great deal,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and you have come up two flights with nothing in your hands four times in nine years and you have not looked at me once in any of them, and I have counted all of them, and I have never told you that, and I am not going to tell you now either.”

She went across to the shelf and put two fingers on the strap, not on the chain, and looked at the book the way you look at a thing you have been looking at for thirty years and have never once been able to be wrong about.

“One more thing before you go,” she said, “and it is a thing I have not done since before you were out of your year. It is about four minutes off. I am telling you that early so that you have time to think about it, and nobody has the standing to stop me.”

## The Door And The Landing

“There is no window in this room and no fire in it and there has not been either for twenty-six years,” said Hanneke Ruis. “The door on it has been shut since before I came in here. There are two flights of stairs under that door and a sales floor at the bottom of them, and there are about forty people on that floor between now and the end of the afternoon.”

“You do not have to.”

“I have not asked you whether I have to,” said Hanneke Ruis. She crossed the room, which takes nine seconds in a room the size of this one, and took the strap off the book and hung it on its hook and left the chain through the lock, because a thing that is going back on the shelf does not have its chain taken off it. “Here is the reason. After it I am not going to say one further word on the subject.”

She put her palm against the door and did not open it.

“I have certified about nine hundred compositions of buildings four times a year for thirty years,” said Hanneke Ruis. “Every one of them has been in my hand and every one of them has been right, and not one of them has ever been argued with by anybody, because there is nobody in this room who can be argued with. That is what a keeper is.”

She took her hand off the door.

“And I have spent an hour of this afternoon telling a man that a thing nobody can be argued with is not a record,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and I am not going to go to bed on that.”

She opened the door.

The corridor was cold and it had a window at the end of it, which that room has not had for twenty-six years, and the sound of a floor of people came up the shaft and went past them and did not stop.

“There,” said Hanneke Ruis.

Her hands were cold, and they were warm after about nine seconds, and they were the hands of a woman who has spent thirty years writing down the composition of buildings and has never once been asked anything about herself.

She unbuttoned her own coat and put it into his hands without a word, and then worked her dress off one shoulder and the rest of it and stepped out of it, and stood on the step in what she had underneath with the cold of that landing all the way through the thin of it.

“I have wanted this since the second of January of the year before last,” she said, without a preamble, which is how she gives a composition. “Two years and about six months. I am not going to say that and then stop, because a thing said and stopped is a rule, and I have been keeping rules for thirty years.”

“Then say it.”

“No. I am going to say the whole of it and the rest of it is for later. Take your coat off. Sit down there on the bottom step with your back to the wall.”

He sat down.

She got over him on the step with her knees on the stone either side of him, one hand on his hip and the other nowhere, and she took him into her mouth and worked at it slowly and without hurrying him, and she took her time over the first part of it the way she takes her time over a figure she means to certify.

At about half past two a man came up that landing with a parcel under his arm, looked down the stair, and went past without stopping. There are about forty people on those two flights between two and four in an ordinary afternoon and there is a reason for every one of them.

Then she took her mouth off him and sat down beside him on the step and laughed about two seconds at nothing in particular, the way she laughs when a quarter is finished.

“Thirty years,” said Hanneke Ruis. “Thirty years of one lamp and one chain and two tables and a figure under my own name four times a year, and there has never once been anybody in that room but me and a man who came down to read a page.”

“You have had me in it twice.”

“You have been in it four times,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and you have not asked me one question in any of them, and that is all I have ever wanted out of you, and I have wanted it since about the ninth of July of a year I am not going to name. And you are not going to be given it, because it is a want and not a claim, and I have had thirty years to learn the difference between the two.”

She got up off the step and turned her back to the wall of the stairwell, where the cold came through the plaster, and pulled him up off the step and into her, all of him at once, with her heels off the stone and her hands hooked over his shoulders.

He was in her about two inches and stopped.

“Put your cock in me properly,” she said. “I have stood on my feet in cold rooms for thirty years and I have had nothing whatever, and I am not going to be fussed over by a man who is being careful.”

So he did, and she took it with her hips and not with her hands, and her shoulders went off the plaster when he did it and she let them.

She made no attempt at all to keep the noise down. That had been decided on the stair and not in the room, and it went out through a door standing four inches open onto a landing that nine people an hour walk past and that nobody in this city has ever once looked at.

“Again,” she said, with her mouth against his jaw. “And do not be kind about it. I have been certain about everything for thirty years and I have got quick at it, and I have no practice at all with a man who is enjoying himself, and I would like to find out about it before I am too old to be bad at it.”

He got a hand on her where she wanted it and she told him where that was, and he did it, and she said *cunt* into his ear the way a woman says the name of a thing she is ordering.

Then she turned round in his arms and went down onto the step with him under her, her back to the cold wall and her heels braced wide on the stone, and she rode him with her fingers dug into the back of his neck and her face about four inches above his own, and she did not shut her eyes once.

She came with her mouth open and her mouth still moving afterwards, and about four seconds after that she said a word into his ear that is hers, and it did not go on a page anywhere, because there is no page.

Then she took him in her mouth one more time, on her knees on the step with the door standing open behind her, and finished him there, and took all of it, and dragged the back of her wrist across her mouth, and looked up at him from the bottom step.

## What A Keeper Said On A Landing

He got his coat and she got hers and they sat down on the landing together with the stair going up and down past them and neither of them put anything back on quickly.

Then she said the thing, sitting on a step in a coat with her hands in her lap, which is how she gives a composition.

“There is one thing I did this afternoon and I would like it understood,” said Hanneke Ruis. “A keeper may say a thing in her own room and write it in ink in her own hand under a figure with her name at the bottom of it, and that is thirty years and it has never once been argued with by anybody, and nobody has ever been able to take one word of it off a page.”

“Yes.”

“This afternoon I have said a thing out loud in a corridor with the door open and about nine people going up and down it,” said Hanneke Ruis. “There is no page for it. There is no day on it.”

She looked at the door.

“In about four years one of those four hundred people with a pencil is going to write down that a keeper in a building two floors under a sales floor said something to a man on a landing,” said Hanneke Ruis, “and there will not be one instrument in this city that can put my hand against his or his against mine.”

She stood up and picked the strap up off the step.

“I did it anyway,” she said. “I am going to be a woman who said a thing out loud. And you will not get the book, and the season will go on running every day in about four hundred kitchens without a keeper in it, and that is what you came up two flights for, and the answer is no, and it is the same answer I would have given you in March and it is structural and it is not about you.”

*Two thousand three hundred and nine days. Less two thousand three hundred and three, the twenty-fourth of June of the seventh year, is six, and the two months say thirty less twenty-four.*

*Less the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, two thousand two hundred and nineteen, is ninety days, which is three months exactly. The twenty-fifth sheet went up on a rail at four on that morning and the twenty-sixth went up at four this afternoon, eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it unmoved across ten sheets, and about nine people a week get to the last two inches of either of them.*

*Less the second of January of the fifth year, one thousand four hundred and forty-one, is eight hundred and sixty-eight days, which is twenty-eight months with twenty-eight days after them. It is her own last one, and there have been three since that had no floor under a lamp in them, and this is the second time a man has been in that room with the door shut and the first time one has been in it with the door open.*

## The Shelf, At Four O’Clock

He went up the two flights behind her and she put the book back on the shelf with the strap across it and turned the key, which is a quarter of a turn and which she has not had to do for anybody in thirty years, and she did not thank him for any part of the afternoon and he did not expect to be thanked and had not arranged the afternoon so that anybody would be.

He was on a stair off Cordwain at about ten past four with the twenty-sixth sheet already up on a rail and about a hundred people a month going round the corner of it to get past.

The oldest instrument in this city had told him, to his face, that the thing about four hundred people were building in about four hundred kitchens is not a record, and she had given the reason out loud, and she had been right, and he had no way of arguing with a woman who is right about a thing she has been keeping for thirty years.

From this afternoon the city had two of everything and one keeper. A page that came out four times in the year at an hour everybody could meet it at, and that about nine hundred people in this city could turn up for, and about four hundred pieces of pencil paper that came out every day of every month and could not be met on any day at all, and no instrument anywhere in Ostara that could tell a man which of the two he was looking at.

And the second one had a keeper now, in a back room off a street two streets wide, with a pencil, and about a hundred and ninety dates in it on nine ruled sheets, and a ruled space against every date with nothing written in any of them.

# She Says It And Is Heard

Seven days had gone since about four hundred people had stood in front of four inches of dressed stone and found out that the two words on it were older than the paint, and since then nobody had asked anybody anything about where a block of stone comes from.

Two thousand two hundred and thirty-three was the fourteenth of April and the twenty-first is day two thousand two hundred and forty, and two thousand two hundred and forty less two thousand two hundred and thirty-three is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

He went up the hill to a yard off the Vestry at about two in the afternoon because the office has had nothing to say to anybody since the seventh of July and he wanted to hear what it sounded like when a thing was said out loud to people.

## Eleven Thousand People And A Pale Rectangle

About eleven thousand people live in that yard and on a working afternoon about nine hundred of them are standing in it, and on the twenty-first of April between two and four in the afternoon about four hundred came down that path, which is about nine times what goes down an ordinary path on an ordinary afternoon.

At the far end of it, at the height of a man’s chest, there was a shutter with nothing on it.

Three boards had come off it on the ninth of November in about nine hours, and nine hundred people in this city can read whatever has ever been put up on a board in it, and what they can read has not been on that shutter since. Whatever colour the last board faded to, the paint behind it faded to, and that pale rectangle has been sitting on that shutter ever since.

One hundred and sixty-two days.

Two thousand two hundred and forty less two thousand and seventy-eight is one hundred and sixty-two, and one hundred and sixty-two is five months with twelve days after them, and about two thousand people a day slow down at that rectangle and cannot get by it and go on.

He had watched it go empty. He had watched two men in coats with a stick come down that path in February and look at it and not ask. About two thousand people a day cannot get by a thing that is not there, and that is the ordinary condition of this city, which is that it walks past unanswered things all day long and keeps none of them.

## The Steps At The Top Of The Path

She was already on them when he got there and he heard her before he saw her.

There were about four hundred people in the flat space at the top of that path and they were standing in a loose half-circle with their backs to the arcade, and the half-circle had been made by twenty years of a building knowing exactly where a voice stops and where it does not, and it was not a thing anybody had laid out.

Agnete Lindqvist went up two steps, which is all the height she takes, and she put her hands on the rail at the top and she waited until the yard stopped shifting, and then she said it.

It took about two minutes.

She gave them a person.

A man who carried water in that yard for eleven years and whose name she said out loud, and the yard he had, and the month he had stopped coming, and the fact that there is no instrument in this city that says he was here.

And then she said the other thing, which is that she is going to say one of those once a month on those steps for as long as she stands on them, and that this office does not keep it and cannot keep it and does not have a book for it, and that the yard is the only place it has ever been said, and that it will be gone by dark.

Then she came down the two steps and went into the arcade and she did not answer anybody, and about four hundred people stood in a half-circle in the flat space at the top of that path and let her go.

## The Rule Nobody In That Yard Has Ever Been Given

He had known about the stand since a note with four lines on it in a year he does not name, and he knew it was a rule, and he had never once been in a room where anybody named it, and neither had she.

It is one thing a month on those steps and not a second. She may give back a person and she may say that nothing keeps it, and she may not answer, and she has not missed one in about sixteen months, and about two hundred of those months have been in front of a woman who heard none of them because there has never been anybody at the foot of that path to hear the first.

He worked out the sixteen months from the shutter and the two takings and got it, and then he found the other thing, which was the reason his chest went tight on a step in a yard.

Sixteen months is two hundred and forty days of days.

She started in the last month of a year before last. For the first six of those months and about half of one there were four lines on that shutter, put up by somebody in that yard out of his own hand, and on the seventh of July they came off in about eleven minutes and she was not there for it. For four months after that there were three lines and they came off on the ninth of November and she was not there for that either.

Since the ninth of November there has been nothing at the foot of that shutter at all, and she has stood on those steps five times in front of a pale rectangle, and not once has it made a difference, and about two thousand people a day slow down at it on their way past.

He got that out of the arithmetic on a step and then he went and found her.

## What She Says About Sixteen Months

She was in the arcade with her back to a pillar and her coat still on, drinking out of a tin cup somebody had left on the sill, and she saw him coming and did not move.

“You are going to tell me you have been counting.”

“I have been counting.”

“Then say the number and then tell me what it means.”

“Sixteen months is four hundred and eighty days by this city’s count,” said Lin Hao. “You have done it about sixteen times and about a hundred and sixty of those days had nothing in front of you at all and about eleven thousand people knew there was a chaplain on those steps and about four hundred of them were standing in that half-circle this afternoon because somebody told them there was a month.”

“Yes,” said Agnete Lindqvist.

“Why this month.”

“Because in the sixteenth month there is somebody in the half-circle who can read, and she has a copy of the bottom of a page in her apron, and about four hundred people in this yard have a thing in a pocket that says what a person in a white coat in a building off a court meant by it,” said Agnete Lindqvist.
“I have been saying a name into a yard for sixteen months and this is the first month anybody in it has had the thing I have been saying it near.”

She drank out of the cup.

“I told a man in a room in March of a year before last that I was going to do it once a month, and I did not do it for nine months, and I have never told anybody that and you are not going to be able to check it either, and I am telling you now because you have been standing in my yard twice in six years and it is a debt and not a courtesy.”

## The Side Of The Steps Where The Yard Is

“What are you going to do now,” she said.

“Nothing.”

“You will stand over there,” said Agnete Lindqvist, “at the back of the half-circle where I can find you without turning round, and you will keep your mouth shut through it, and then I am going to do the thing I have not done in about two years.”

“The last one of these was two years ago.”

“That room is not here and I am not going to it. I am going to do it on the side of these steps with the yard about nine feet away and four hundred people in it, and I want it where it can be used against me.”
“The last one of these was the ninth of March of a year before last in a room with a door in it that came off that yard, and you were in it, and about eleven thousand people were on the other side of two doors, and that is the only time in twenty years I have had it where nobody could use it,” said Agnete Lindqvist.

She said it the way a woman says a thing she has thought about for nine months.

“You understand what that is. That is not a thing said to a room. That is a thing said to a yard, and a yard keeps nothing and repeats everything, and from this afternoon there is no version of me that gets to be only true in a room, and I have decided that and I decided it this morning at about six and I have not slept.”

He understood it.

He also understood that she had more than forty years of a life behind her in which nobody had wanted her for about four years, and that a woman who has been holding a position for sixteen months in an empty yard is in more danger from four hundred people than she was from eleven thousand, and that she had worked that out and chosen it, and that she had chosen the danger on purpose and had not asked him and was not going to.

“Yes,” he said.

“Say it slower than that,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “I have watched you say yes to four things in two years and I have not yet heard you mean it once.”

“I am going to do whatever you tell me to do,” he said, “and I am not going to be careful with you, and there are about four hundred people nine feet away from this step and I am not going to pretend that there are not.”

Her face did something and she let it.

“Good,” she said. “That is the whole of what I came out here for.”

## On The Step

She took her coat off and dropped it on the stone and got the front of her skirt up over her hips one-handed, and did not ask him for it and did not turn round for it.

She is a big woman with her hair white at the front of it and wet at the part, and there has not been a month in twenty years that she has not spent standing up in a yard full of other people. She came at him the way a woman who has decided a thing comes, and her hands were not warm and they got warm in about the length of a sentence.

“Here,” she said. “Mouth. And do not be quick, because the last time I had a man I have had two years to think about the end of it and not about the middle.”

She got him into her mouth the way she said she would, unhurried, with her hand closed round the base of his cock and her tongue doing the work of about half of it and her jaw going slower than a woman twenty years her junior would have gone, because she had said she was going to and not because anything had slowed her.

She was listening to him the whole time and only giving him half of herself while she did it, which is more attention than she has given anything at all since the fourth year.

Then she lifted her head off him, sat back on her heels with him still in her hand, and laughed about two seconds at nothing in particular.

“You are rank one with a chest that has been spent for four years.”

“That is what a doctor told me in a room off a ward and she said she had looked.”

“Then she looked,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “And you are going to last longer than you did two years ago, and I am going to be here for all of it, and I am going to enjoy it. I am telling you that now so that you cannot spend the next half hour managing me.”

She put him back in her mouth and finished him there, and she did not hurry the end of it either.

Then she got him up onto the second step with his back against the rail and came down onto him on top of it, in daylight, with her knees either side of his ribs and her hands gripping the rail on either side of his head.

A woman came down the path with a child and looked over and did not stop, because about eleven thousand people use that path and two of them were on the steps and there is nothing in this city to look at.

“Now,” she said. “Be here.”

She got her hand between his legs and took hold of him and put him into her and it was the same hand that had been in his hair, and she took all of it in about four seconds, and then she stopped with all of it in and said his name once and waited for him.

She was not quiet about any of it. She had told him two years ago that she was not going to be, and she was not, and the yard was on the other side of nine feet of air.

She rode him on that step with her hands round the rail behind his head and her face about four inches above his.

She named a thing she wanted, plainly, in the plainest words she had, and he gave it to her, and then she named another one and he gave her that as well. Not once in the whole of it did either of them stop to ask the other whether they were sure, and she has never in twenty years been able to say why that is the part she remembers.

“Like that,” she said. “Keep doing that. I have been the one in the room deciding for four years and I have got very good at it and it is the loneliest thing I have ever been good at.”

Halfway through it she put two fingers on his chest and held him there, which she has done before and which means she has something to say and not that she is finished, and she was breathing hard and her mouth was open and she said, “Do you know what I have not had.”

“No.”

“I have not had anybody in twenty years who did not look careful at me first,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “Every single one of them looked careful at me first. That is all of it. That is the whole of what has happened to me in four years and it is in the face, and I have been reading faces since I was twenty-five.”

She pushed his hips back down with the heel of her hand and took him again, all of it, harder than the first time.

“Do that again,” she said. “Not careful. I am asking you for it and I am enjoying asking.”

He got his hands into her hair at the back and she pushed his head up and held it there, and she said *cunt* into his mouth and made him say it into hers.

Then she put him back in, got herself off her step and up onto him, and rode him standing in the daylight at the side of the steps with about four hundred people in the yard. Her mouth was open and her face was about four inches from his and nobody nine feet off was talking about water rates any more.

She came with her back arching and her hands closed on the rail on either side of his head, and she said a word into his ear that he is not going to write down because it is hers, and it went on about four seconds after she had finished.

## What A Position Costs Her

They sat on the step with the stone coming up cold through her coat and his shirt and neither of them put anything back on.

She drank out of the tin cup somebody had left on the sill and did not say anything for about a minute.

“Right,” she said. “Say the thing you have been holding since I came down those two steps.”

“They heard you.”

“They heard me and about four hundred of them had the bottom of a man’s page in a pocket before I started and not one of them can tell which of the two things they came for,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “That is what you have been holding and it is correct, and it is the whole of what a position is, and I want you to understand what I have done to myself this afternoon.”

She wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist.

“For sixteen months I said a name out loud into a yard and nobody caught it and I was keeping a rule. This afternoon about four hundred people caught it and about nine hundred can read what they caught, and from this afternoon a thing I said is a thing that can be held against me, and nobody has to do anything to hold it. They only have to repeat it, and about eleven thousand people in this yard repeat things.”

She got her skirt down over her knees and did it herself and she did not look away from him while she did it.

“And I wanted it,” she said. “I wanted it about nine days ago and I have not slept since, and I would do it again this afternoon, and you are not going to comfort me about it. You have never once been able to and I have got very good at not needing it.”

He got his coat and put it round her, and she let him.

“Two things,” she said, “and then you go out of that arcade and do not stop.”

“Go on.”

“One, I am not claiming the column and I am not claiming the two words at the foot of that wall, and I have said no three times in four years and I am saying it a fourth time this afternoon to a man who has not asked me.”

“I know.”

“Two, the next one is in about four weeks and it will not be that man and I will not tell you which one it is. If you are at the top of that path I will look for you, and if you are not there I will still say it, and I have never missed one and I am not going to start.”

## The Corner At Four In The Afternoon

He was at the corner at about four.

The four inches were behind painted boards with a slot under them, two hundred a day stopped at that corner over the afternoon and two or three in a week ever got down as far as the stone, and on that afternoon about nine hundred came down the hill past a shutter with nothing on it and then walked to a corner and stood about four feet off a slot in a hoarding.

Nobody could tell them what she had said. That was the thing. About four hundred people had heard a woman give back a man who carried water in that yard for eleven years, and about four hundred other people in this city had a piece of pencil paper in a pocket with two inches of somebody’s own writing under four sentences they could all read, and the two sets of people were in this city now and had no way of talking to each other.

Both things were his, in the way that everything in this city is his this month.

About nine hundred people can read what she said this afternoon, because they are the people who read everything, and he was standing at the bottom of a wall with nothing in his hands and he had not said one word out loud in about six years.

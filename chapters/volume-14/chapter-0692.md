# Four Desks That Ask For Nothing

He went looking for somebody to ask him something on the fifth of November and it took him the whole day, and he went to four places, and the answer was the same shape at every one of them, and by the last one he had stopped being surprised and started being frightened.

Two thousand four hundred and thirty-four days. Less two thousand four hundred and twenty-seven is seven, and twenty-eight days is five days and two days and the month runs out at its thirtieth, so five plus two is seven.

## The Returns Desk At Half Past Nine

Wren Kallo had a tray and a stamp pad and a book face up on the counter in front of her with three questions written in it, and she wrote three more before he had got to the end of the counter, and then she put the pen across the page.

“A desk takes one question of a thing,” she said, “and only one, and a return raises about three, and about nine returns a day come through there, and that is between twenty and thirty questions a day and there has never once in nine years been anywhere for one of them to go.”

“Where do they go now.”

“Nowhere,” said Wren Kallo. “They go in the book, and the book is on this counter, and nobody has ever asked me what is in it, and I have never once offered to tell anybody, because the day I offer is the day somebody sends me a form for it and the form will have a box in it and the box will have to be filled in.”

She squared the tray.

“Two nails,” she said, not looking up, “and eleven years. Everything in that book is a thing a person could not do at a counter and wanted to know why, and every single one of them is right, and there is no counter in this city that the answer could be carried to.”

## A Counter With A Stack Behind It

Beatrix Halm was at the end of a run of counters at a quarter to eleven with about four hundred and nineteen accounts in a stack behind her and a tray in front of her, and she had two hands and one pencil and the round goes down a lane.

“That is not the same four hundred and nineteen,” she said, before he had said anything at all. “Do not tell me it is the same. Mine is streets and mine has hours against them and mine has nothing against the hours, and if the woman in number four ever reads both of them she will find out that two offices in this city keep a stack of the same height.”

“You are asking me about the account.”

“I have asked you about the account four times since the eighth of September and I have not asked you about it this morning,” said Beatrix Halm. “You are the only person I can hand a figure to and every time I hand you one it becomes a thing that can be argued with and there is nobody I would rather have that argument with than you, so I have stopped.”

She put the tray down.

“Three questions a round, and I have asked three questions a round since the second year, and not one of them has ever been put in front of a person who could answer it. I have never sent one. Sending is a thing you do with a document and I have never had a document.”

## Four Names On A Piece Of Paper

He looked at the rota in the middle of the afternoon. It is about the size of a folded hand and it carries four names on it and the man who keeps it is nine streets away and holds no authority over anybody and cannot be compelled.

His own. Nadia Ferrous’s. Petra Ilyin’s. Isolde Marchetti’s.

Three of the nine sworn had sworn in one room and none of them had lived with him again afterwards and all three had come in on the rota, and the rota is a piece of paper about hours and not one word of it has ever been about a thing any of them wanted.

He looked at it for about four minutes and put it back in his coat, and the four names went on doing what they had been doing, which was eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off, in a branch office off a third court with three floors of washrooms.

## The Same Answer, And A Folder That Stayed Shut

Two flights under a sales floor, at about half past four, and the sixth time he had come down those two flights and the second time he had put that question to her.

“What are you doing at the far table.”

She was at the far table with her coat still on and the folder in front of her, and she did not open it.

That was the first thing he understood and he understood it about nine seconds later, and it went through him the way the things she says go through him, fast and in order.

The last time he had asked her that, she had opened the folder out on the table and squared the edges of it and put a finger on the first thing in it and taken about four minutes to say why a folder is taken in the order it was opened.

The order is the only account anybody has of what happened when, and every other rule in that building is about finding a thing again and that one is about a thing not being taken out of order afterwards.

He had asked her what she was doing and she had shown him.

“A folder is taken in the order it was opened,” said Sun Meiling. “Not in the order anybody wants it and not in the order it is useful. A thing goes in at the top at the moment it arrives, and a paper from the second month sits above a paper from the fifth month, and it stays where it sits for as long as it is in there.”

It was the same answer. Word for word, in the same order, in the same flat voice, and it took about four minutes and it was as true as it had been in the summer.

And then she stopped, which is the part he has not been able to put down.

She did not open it this time, and she said why, and she said it in about four seconds and then she went back to the figure she had been on.

“A folder is only an account of what happened when for as long as nothing goes into it out of order,” said Sun Meiling. “That was true in the summer and it is true now and it is true in exactly the same words, and it is a different answer, and I am not going to tell you which half of it changed.”

“There are about four hundred of these pieces of paper in this city and one of them went on a rail on the thirtieth of September and about four hundred of them have never seen a rail.”

“That is the answer,” said Sun Meiling. “I have just given you the same answer I gave you in the summer and it is about a city with four hundred folders in it and one shelf, and there is no order in any of them, and the only person in Ostara who keeps one is sitting on a stool at my table and he has not asked me to keep this one either.”

She did not look up.

“You will not get it from me and you were not going to get it,” she said. “Ask me something else while you are on that stool, because you have never once come down two flights for anything that was not going to be the same both times.”

## A Woman Who Writes Down What She Saw

Runa Sato was on the ground floor at about twenty past five with her hat still on and a bundle carried in front of her rather than under, and she stopped three feet inside the door, which is what she does.

“You have not served anything in nine months,” she said.

“No.”

“Neither have I,” said Runa Sato. “I have written down what I saw in this city every day for about nine years and I have served about nine papers and every one of them was because somebody came and knocked and told me there was a person at an address. There is no instrument for anything else.”

“There is no form that says go and look at a thing that is not a person at an address, and if there were one I would fill it in and I would not be here.”

She turned her head about nine degrees.

“You are the only person I could put this to who has been refused something by an instrument,” she said, “and I have wanted to ask you for about nine weeks, and I am not going to, because you have not asked me for anything since the fourth month and I am not going to be the second one of us to start.”

## The Want He Cannot Put On A Form

He went down two streets and stopped.

What he wanted, and it came to him standing outside a laundry at about ten to six, was to be asked a question.

Not a favour and not a document and not an instruction. A question, put to him by somebody who wanted the answer, in a room he did not have to go to, on a day he had not chosen.

He had had one at a kerb on the seventh of October from a woman with eleven sheets in her arms and it had been the best morning he had had in four months, and since then he had been to four desks in one day and not one of the four had wanted anything off him.

There are four places in this city where somebody sits all day in front of other people and could put a question into it, and about nine hundred people can read everything any of them has ever written, and about eleven thousand people in a yard can put a wrong sentence right in four minutes.

And not one of the four has anybody to ask.

*Two thousand four hundred and thirty-four days. Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is thirty-five days, which is one month with five days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them with three lines in the second column of it.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is three hundred and fifty-six days, which is eleven months with twenty-six days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard off the Vestry has been bare for every one of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is seven hundred and sixteen days, which is twenty-three months with twenty-six days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and four people who could put a question into this city have been asked nothing by anybody all day.*

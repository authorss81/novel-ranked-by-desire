# The Spike With Nine Hundred On It

Seven days had gone since a clerk told him that a sentence is not a claim and there is no form on which an office explains itself.

Two thousand one hundred and eighty was the twenty-first of February and the twenty-eighth is day two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven, and two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven less two thousand one hundred and eighty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

The foot of the ranks wall was cold at about ten in the morning, with frost still in the shadow of the render and the trams going under the arch, and Lin Hao stood nine feet off it looking at the bottom of it instead of at the form, because the form was what everybody looked at and the bottom was what nobody did.

About two hundred people in a day stopped at that corner, and about nine of them in a week got to the foot, and the sixty-third form was up on a board with paste still damp at the edges, and the two ruled squares at the foot of it were empty.

The boy stood by the board with a paste brush in his fist and watched him, wary and unsurprised, the way a person watches a man who has come nine times since the twelfth of October and has not once asked for anything.

## Sixty-One Days On A Spike

“You have come about the spring,” said the boy.

“I have come about the form,” said Lin Hao.

The boy shrugged, a small tight movement inside his coat.

“It is on a spike nine streets away with about nine hundred others,” he said. “A number the size of a thumb-nail against it. Nobody has moved it.”

Lin Hao nodded, because he knew it, and because knowing it and seeing the wall together was worse than either alone.

The works form had been on a spike for sixty-one days, in at a counter nine streets away on the twenty-seventh of December under a number nobody read, and the line at the bottom of it said take down to course and make good, and the painting was a Bureau contract in another building, and the two had not sent a paper to each other nine years and nothing made them.

He walked the nine streets after leaving the wall, through the cold noon, and went up to the corridor off a second level where about nine hundred lines of schedule were pinned up and anybody might look at them.

An address, a line of work, a box for the date the work was done, and not one column about what is on a wall.

He stood a long time in front of it, with his hands in his pockets and his breath showing, and a man came past with a bucket and did not look at him, and that was correct, because a schedule in a corridor is scenery and scenery is not discussed in front of the public.

About nine thousand people went under that wall in a day, because it was an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams went under it.

He wrote that down in his head the way Emil Brack wrote figures, with the reason against it, because a rate without a reason is a thing somebody put together on purpose and a rate with one is a thing a man can stand behind.

## What Is Bare At The Far End

On the way back he went round by the yard off the Vestry, because he could not stop himself, and stood at the mouth of it looking at the shutter at the far end.

There had been nothing on it since the ninth of November, when about nine hundred people took three lines off a board in about nine hours, and a hundred and nine days had gone since, and about two thousand people in a day slowed down at nothing there.

He felt dread with a date on it and about three months left on it, because he had read that wall every morning six years as a thing that survives, and it survived because a coat of paint would not hold on dressed stone, and that was an accident, and the accident had a schedule.

At the corner again the boy was still waiting, because Lin Hao had not dismissed him and the boy did not leave a corner unattended.

“What are the boxes for,” said Lin Hao, nodding at the two ruled squares.

The boy looked at him levelly.

“They have been empty since the eighth of October of the first year,” he said, once, plainly, because saying it twice would have made it an argument. “One wants the number of the thing at the address and the other wants the standing of it, and there has never been anything in either on any form anybody here can remember.”

Lin Hao accepted that, because there was nothing else to do with it, and asked nothing more, because the boy was not to be given a second conversation about the woman with the short-handled broom, who cleaned those four inches every morning and had never once said she had seen a person put anything on them, and who was not to be named or asked.

“What do you want,” said the boy at last, because Lin Hao had stood too long.

“I want this wall to hold,” said Lin Hao, and heard the want raw in his own voice, and was ashamed of it, because wanting a wall to hold is a want a man has when he has nothing else to want.

The boy did not mock him, which was kinder than mockery would have been.

“It will come down in the spring,” he said. “A man will come with a form and paint the new one the same colour as the old, and there will be four inches of stone at the foot of it with two words cut into it, and he will not touch them, because it is not in his form. About two hundred in a day will stand at a corner where there is nothing to read.”

Lin Hao walked home in the dark with that in his ears, and anger under the dread, cold and steady, at two buildings nine streets apart that had not sent a paper to each other nine years, and at himself for having no instrument that could reach either of them.

He wanted a wall to survive weather and workmen and schedules, and he had no standing to ask it of anybody, and that was the whole of his position at the end of February.

*Two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven days. Less two thousand one hundred and eighty, the twenty-first of February of year seven, is seven. Twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*Less two thousand one hundred and twenty-six, the twenty-seventh of December, is sixty-one days that form has been on a spike with about nine hundred others. Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of year six, is one hundred and nine days that shutter has stood bare.*

# The Sentence She Cites

Six days had gone since a man in a delivery coat had said at a wall end, before anybody offered it, that he wanted a sheet and was not going to ask for one.

Two thousand two hundred was the eleventh of March and the seventeenth is day two thousand and two hundred and six, and two thousand and two hundred and six less two thousand two hundred is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.

She had been auditing that branch office since the first of October of the third year and had said nothing about it to anybody, and he had known that for about two years and had never once used it.

## Two O’Clock At A Branch Office

He came in at about two with a copy of the foot of the twenty-fourth sheet in his hand, made by somebody else, in pencil, on the back of a sheet of heavy rag that a laundry keeps for folding on.

She had her cuffs fastened and her sleeves pulled down and the door shut behind her when he got to the table.

“A copy,” she said.

“A copy of the foot of it.”

“With writing in the two inches.”

“With a month and a lane in them, and a figure on one of them that I cannot check and a date on another that I cannot check, and neither of those two facts is the reason I am here.”

He put it down on the table.

“Is it evidence.”

That was the whole of what he had come with. He had carried it from a third court to a corridor to a room with a shut door in it on purpose, because she is the only instrument in this city that can check a slate, and if she said a copy was not evidence then everything that was going to happen over the spring was already decided, and he wanted it decided in a room with a door shut.

## Not In A Corridor

Ines Duarte did not pick it up.

“No,” she said.

“Why not.”

“Because a sentence said in a corridor at two in the afternoon is a sentence a corridor has heard,” said Ines Duarte. “You did not bring me here to be quick about it. You brought me here because this room has a shut door in it, and that was the right decision and I want to say so before I say the other thing, and you are not going to get the other thing for free.”

“I have not got anything to trade.”

“You have got one copy of a piece of paper and an hour of mine,” said Ines Duarte. “An hour of mine is worth more to you than the copy, and I know that, and I am going to ask you for it properly, which is that I am going to answer this once and I am going to answer it in this room and I am going to answer it as the woman who holds the instrument and not as the instrument.”

“Go on.”

“The instrument is not me,” she said. “I have been told that by about nine people this year and I have never once corrected any of them, and I am going to correct you now because you are the only one of the nine who would have gone away and done something about it. The thing that can check a slate is a slate. I stand next to it.”

## The Question She Is Carrying

She took her sleeves back about an inch off her wrists, which is not a thing she does for comfort.

“I have had a question since the seventh of March of a year I do not name,” she said. “It is about a man. It is not about a sentence and it is not about the sheet and it is not about you, and I have not asked it and I am not going to ask it in this room, and you may put that down as something I have given you for nothing, because you are going to try to use it.”

“I would not.”

“You would not,” said Ines Duarte, “and I have known that about you for six years and it is the reason the question is still in my pocket. If I asked it in a room with a shut door in it then it would be answered, and it has been standing in that pocket since the seventh of March, and it is the only thing I have carried for two years that I have not been able to put down.”

She put a hand on the edge of the table at each end of it, far apart, and not near the middle.

“Ask your question properly and I will answer it.”

## What She Says

“Ask it again.”

“Is a copy evidence.”

“A figure in Ostara may be a figure somebody made up on purpose. This standing sheet is printed by the office itself. Emil Brack.”

She said it at the ordinary speed.

Then she stopped and let it stand in the room, because an auditor who lets a thing stand is doing the thing she is for.

“That is at the foot of the sheet you copied,” she said. “It is the third of the four. It is in your hand. It has been in your hand at the foot of that sheet since the ninth of November of a year I am not going to name, and it has a man’s name under it and not yours, and that man reads it out loud at a wall end every morning and says the reason for every figure before he writes the first one.”

“Yes.”

“You have never once read it to yourself.”

“No.”

“You do not need to read it to yourself,” said Ines Duarte, “you wrote it, and you know it the way you know your own stairs. That is not the trouble. The trouble is what it does.”

## A Warning And A Shield

“It is a warning,” he said.

“It is a shield,” said Ines Duarte, “and it is a shield with your name on it, and you have spent four months believing it was a warning aimed at a city.”

She turned the copy round on the table so that the two inches were at the top and pointing at him.

“Read what is in that space.”

“You cannot read it. It is a month and a lane.”

“I cannot read anything, and neither can about four in every thousand people in this city, and that is not what I mean,” said Ines Duarte. “I mean that no instrument in this city can tell that space from a space nobody has written in, and that a person who copies the foot of that sheet copies the sentence as well, whether they can read it or not, because it is printed on the paper and printing does not ask.”

“That is what it is for.”

“That is what you wrote it for,” said Ines Duarte, “and it freed a woman in number four who cannot read a lease from having to prove a thing, and it cost her the ability to say that anybody had taken anything off her, and you knew that on the twenty-third of November and you struck the fourth line yourself and wrote your own name under the striking.”

“I did.”

“And about nine people in nine kitchens have had it in a pencil for about two months,” she said, “and every one of them is unthrowawayable, and every one of them is also unprovable, and you did that, and I am not going to let you have the version where you did it to protect them.”

He could not answer that.

He wrote it. He wrote it about a woman in a kitchen off a street in a cold month and she could not write her own name, and he wrote it to stop a landlord taking four figures off a wall and turning them into a count of claims. If the whole of what a person writes may be something they made up, then the woman with a claim in pencil on a laundry sheet is protected by him in a way that has no hole in it.

And there is no clause anywhere in this city that lets an instrument put a name against a piece of paper, and the sentence that would be needed does not exist, and the sentence that does exist is the one he wrote.

That was the whole of what he could not do with it and it took him about nine seconds and she watched all nine.

## What She Has Read

She put the copy back down on its own edge and squared it, which is a thing she has never once done to a document in his presence and did now.

“I am going to say the last part and then you may go,” said Ines Duarte.

“All right.”

“Since the ninth of November that sentence has been read out loud to two men in two rooms,” she said. “One of them read it to me in a room with a clerk in it and it was not in a street. The other one reads it to about forty people at a wall end six days a week and says he is not a warning.”

“And you.”

“I have read it about four hundred times,” said Ines Duarte. “Neither of them has ever read it. I am the only person in this city that has read that sentence properly, and I read it because of my job, and you wrote it in nine seconds on a week day afternoon in an earlier year.”

She opened the door and held it.

“Take the copy back to the room off a third court,” she said, “and put it on a counter with the address at the top of it, and the office will be named at the top of it in nine seconds, and every man who comes in to that counter will see that a copy of that sheet exists and that this office has seen one. And you will not be able to stop any of it, and you will have spent an hour of mine getting nothing.”

*Two thousand and two hundred and six days. Less two thousand two hundred, the eleventh of March of the seventh year, is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and twenty-eight days, which is four months with eight days after them. A shutter has been bare for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is four hundred and eighty-eight days, which is sixteen months with eight days after them, and that sentence is the third of the four at the foot of a sheet, and it goes out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter, and about nine people a month are making copies of that foot in pencil on the strength of it.*

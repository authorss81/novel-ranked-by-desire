# Nine Feet Of New Render

Six days had gone since he had told a magistrate’s clerk that his office could not do one single thing with ninety pieces of pencil paper, and since then the thing he had actually been thinking about was a wall on a hill with nothing on it.

Two thousand three hundred and twenty-nine was the twentieth of July and the twenty-sixth is day two thousand three hundred and thirty-five, and two thousand three hundred and thirty-five less two thousand three hundred and twenty-nine is six, and twenty-six less twenty is six by the month count.

There is a question about that corner that has been open since the seventh of April and nobody in this city has asked it out loud, and he had asked it out loud twice in his own head and got nowhere.

What happens to a place where there is nothing to put anything on.

## Ten In The Morning At A Kerb

About two hundred people a day halt at that corner. Two in a week get low enough to make out four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of nine feet of new render.

Two sheets are on the board by the kerb. One is a job for a wall that is down to the course and made good, whose hut is going to be knocked down with it. The other is this month’s form for an address.

The boy was there at about ten with a jar of paste and a brush and a piece of rag, and he was painting the render the same colour as the rest of it, in a band about eighteen inches wide, working up from the bottom and stopping dead where the new joint is.

He does not try the four inches. He cannot: a coat of paint will not stay on stone that has been dressed and dressed is what that face is, and that has been the entire mechanism for more than thirty years.

And the form does not tell him to try, so he does not try.

He had about four feet of wall left to do when the man with the chalk came round the corner.

## A Fact About A Place, In Chalk

He was not young. He had a piece of chalk in his hand and he did not have anything else with him, and he looked up at the nine feet of render for a while as though he were measuring it for something.

Then he wrote on it, about four feet up, in ordinary block capitals, as high as his hand would go.

*BANK STREET — NO WATER SINCE THE 14TH*

It took him about a minute and a half. It was about nine feet of writing in a line and it was the plainest sentence in this city that day.

Then he stood and looked at it and looked down the hill.

“Four hundred and something households get that page every quarter,” he said, to nobody in particular. “About nine of them a week get to the bottom of it. There is a form for the date a job finished and nothing on any of them for whether the water is on.”

He had come up the hill to write it because a woman in two rooms off Bank Street had asked him to, and he was about to say so and then he did not say it, because about two hundred people a day halt at that corner and he is one of them.

The boy came over with the jar of paste on his shoulder and read it from about six feet away, from the ground, upside down, which he can do.

He read it twice.

“That is not mine,” said the boy, “and it is not the wall’s, and I am not going to be the man who keeps it.”

He wiped the brush on his coat and went back to the eighteen inches he had left.

That was the whole of what he said about it. He did not say anything else and he did not have to, because everything else had been said on this corner in April by a man with a form and had produced a board.

## Eleven O’Clock, And Then The Rain

It came in off the west at about eleven, the way it does, and it was not heavy for about four minutes.

The chalk went first off the last four letters and then off the middle of the word water and then off all of it, and there was a grey ghost of the line left on the new render for about ten minutes, and the man with the chalk stood in it and watched it go.

He did not write it again.

That was the part Lin Hao could not get past for the rest of the morning, and it took him about twenty minutes to understand why.

A man who has come up a hill with a piece of chalk in his hand to say a true thing about a street has not failed when the rain takes it.

A man who has come up a hill with a piece of chalk in his hand to say a true thing about a street, in a city where about nine hundred people can read everything that has ever been put up on a board anywhere in it, has failed at the precise thing he came out to do. There had been about two hundred people at that corner between ten and eleven. Not one of them had a pencil.

Not one of them. A yard off the Vestry has about eleven thousand people in it and about nine hundred of them can read, and in four months they had built a machine in there that puts a wrong sentence right inside about four minutes, and none of it was here, because none of it was needed.

The people at that corner had come to look at four inches of stone, and they had been handed the whole of the thing for nothing.

## The Woman With A Piece Of Chalk

It began at about a quarter past one.

The man who had written it was still there, and he was not the only one. There were about nine of them, and they were writing, and they were writing the same sentence, and they were getting it wrong.

No water since the fourteenth. No water Bank Street. The water off Bank Street since the twelfth. Water off Bank St. Not the fourteenth, the thirteenth.

A woman who could read very well and had never written on a wall in her life got it wrong in the third version, and a man standing next to her put his thumb on the fourteenth and said the word fourteen, and she said it back to him, and then they wrote it together, one of them on top of the other.

“That is what it is for,” she said, to nobody in particular. “You get it wrong out loud and somebody puts a thumb on it. That is the whole of the trade.”

He went down the hill at about half past three and then he turned round and came back up it, because there was one thing left to ask and he had not asked it in four months and a boy had told him on this kerb that he was not going to be told anything again.

The man with the chalk was still there with the stub in his hand.

“Where does the water come from when it is on,” said Lin Hao.

The man looked at him for about four seconds.

“That is a good question,” he said, “and I am not the man who knows. I know the street and I know the day. There is a pit four streets along with a lid on it, and a man comes to that lid every eighth day or so, and I have never once seen him and I would not know his name if I did.”

“Do you know where the pit is.”

“I know which way it is,” said the man. “You want a water office, not a chalk. There is a counter off the Vestry that takes it and they have never once come.”

He put the chalk in his pocket.

“I would have written it again tomorrow,” he said, “but there is a woman up here of a certain age who has just written it in the right place, and that is better than me twice over, and I am going to go and find out what her name is.”

About four hundred people went past that corner between one and three and about a hundred and forty of them wrote on it and every single one of them wrote it down and every single one of them left the hill with nothing in their hands, which is the only way anybody in this city has ever carried a thing away from a wall.

And the wrong ones did not last.

The rain had stopped by two. The lower half of the writing was dry by half past two and about forty people were at the wall correcting what nine hundred people could not, out loud, in daylight, with a thumb, the whole afternoon, and by the time it was light the sentence was on that wall correct and about a foot long and about nine feet up, and nobody at all knew what would happen to it at the next rain.

## The Corner Is Not A Place To Put Anything On

He stood four feet off it with his hands in his pockets, which is where they go now.

Two hundred a day come to that place because of four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of it with two words cut clean through the face. Nine in a week get down low enough to make them out. One in a week of those nine is carrying a pencil.

A boy with a paste brush has spent six weeks telling him that there is nothing to be done at that corner. He was right about it for six weeks. He is not going to be told anything else about it.

A place with nothing to put anything on is not an empty place. It is a place where whatever goes up dies inside a day. And a place where whatever goes up dies inside a day fills up with people, because about two hundred a day will stand in front of it and say a true thing out loud about a street, be wrong in front of strangers, and be put right inside a quarter of an hour by somebody with a thumb and no instrument at all.

That is the second instrument, and this city did not build it, and nobody is going to be able to write it down afterwards because there is nobody in the transaction.

There is no chalked line in four hundred kitchens. There is a corner, and about two hundred people a day at it, and about nine hundred people in this city who can read anything at all, and the rain coming in off the west.

*Two thousand three hundred and thirty-five days. Less two thousand three hundred and twenty-nine, the twentieth of July of the seventh year, is six, and twenty-six less twenty is six by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of December of the sixth year, two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine, is two hundred and six days, which is six months with twenty-six days after them, and the twenty-fourth sheet went up on a rail at four that afternoon and about nine hundred people could read every word of it, and about four hundred of them have the foot of it in pencil in a pocket.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is six hundred and seventeen days, which is twenty months with seventeen days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and this afternoon a man about seventy wrote it on a wall in chalk, wrong, and a stranger put a thumb on it, and it is correct, and neither of them can read it twice.*

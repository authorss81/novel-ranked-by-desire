# Scaffolding On A Wall

Seven days had gone since a man behind a counter had told him that a form had been carried upside down in a coat for nine weeks, and since then the office had done nothing whatever about four inches of stone at the bottom of anything, which was the correct thing for it to do and was also the reason he was standing at a kerb at nine in the morning with his hands in his pockets.

Two thousand two hundred and nineteen was the thirtieth of March and the seventh of April is day two thousand two hundred and twenty-six, and two thousand two hundred and twenty-six less two thousand two hundred and nineteen is seven, and thirty less thirty is nothing by the month count and the seventh of the fourth month is the whole of it, because there is no thirty-first in this city.

He had spent six years reading a wall as a thing that survived.

He found out at about half past seven that morning that it had a schedule.

## The Fourth Week Of The Month

The works men went up at about half past seven with poles and boards and a length of rope that was better than any he had seen in this city, and they went up on the pavement side, which is the side everybody in this city walks past, and they did not ask anybody.

The form had said nothing about when. The form had an address and four words and a little square at the end of the line for the day the work was finished, and the little square was empty.

He had known since the twenty-eighth of February that the work was coming. He had not known it was this morning, and the reason he had not known it was that the schedule in the corridor nine streets away says when a wall is finished and has never once in the history of that building said when a thing begins.

“They go up in the fourth week,” said the boy. “They went up in the fourth week in the year before and the year before that and it is the fourth week every time and it has been the fourth week since before I was working here.”

The boy was where he is every morning, at the corner, with the brush, and the sixty-third of those forms was up on the board by the kerb and had been since the twenty-eighth of February, and both of the two empty squares were at the foot of it doing nothing.

“So the form is not the date.”

“The form is not the date,” said the boy. “The form is what somebody wrote down in a room in December and the date on the room is when he wrote it down.”

## Sixty Feet Of Hoarding

About sixty feet of it went up along the foot of the wall by about nine o’clock, and it was hoarding, and it was hoarding the way a hoarding is always put up: boards across the bottom, painted, and a gap under them of about four inches so that a person can see where they are putting their feet.

He stood at about nine feet off and looked along it.

For sixty feet of a wall he has read at close range about four hundred times in six years, the bottom four inches were now behind painted boards with a slot under them, and through the slot you could see nothing at all except the shadow of the kerb.

That was the whole of the morning’s damage and it took a man about four minutes to do it with boards.

He was not asked a question about it by anybody. He wanted one badly for about nine seconds, and the wanting went out of him the way it goes out of him, which is not all at once and leaves the whole shape of it behind.

## The Only Column That Has Ever Existed

He went in at about ten and up two flights in four and had to stop on the landing, and then went the rest of it, and that is how he goes up two flights now. There is nothing in this city that puts it back.

The schedule had come down on the first and gone up again on the last working day of the month, and it was up, and there were about nine hundred lines on it and a line printed under the last one saying that anybody might read it.

He found the ranks wall in about four minutes.

It said where, and it said four words and a full stop, and the small square at the end for finishing it was empty.

There is one column on that schedule about a place. There is one about a job. There is one about the day the job was done. About nine hundred lines and three columns and not one of them has ever in this city held a thing that was on a wall.

He stood in a corridor and read a wall, and then he read the same line again from the other end, and then he went down two flights in four and stood on the step at the bottom of them.

Nine hundred walls on one sheet of paper and no column for the bottom of any of them, and the reason was not a decision. It was that there is nowhere to write the answer down, and a man with nowhere to write an answer down does not ask.

## Nobody Comes Down That Street To Ask

The works foreman came out at about eleven with a folding rule in his hand and two men behind him and a boy with a bucket, and none of them went near the foot of the wall.

They started at the far end. They started at about forty feet along from the corner and about nine feet up, and the first thing that came off was not stone but paint, six years of it, in sheets about a foot square, and it came off whole, and underneath it the render was grey and older than the paint and looked at.

He watched two of them take four feet of it off in about nine minutes.

The foreman came down the hoarding at about half past eleven and stood at the kerb and looked at a board that had a form on it with two empty squares at the foot of it.

“That yours,” he said.

“It is not mine. It is painted about once a month and it has been on that board since the twenty-eighth of February.”

“The address is right,” said the foreman. “That is the address of the wall.”

“It is.”

“And there is nothing on that form about what we are doing to it.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “There is nothing on it about four inches of dressed stone at the foot of it, and there has never been one, and there is not one on nine hundred other lines on that sheet.”

“All right,” said the foreman, and went back up the ladder.

He did not say anything else for about four minutes and then he said one thing over his shoulder, without coming down, which was that if anybody came and asked him what was at the bottom of that wall he would tell them what was at the bottom of it and he had a rule that would not tell him and that was the end of it.

## A Wall Is The Only Thing Here That People Watch

Two hundred a day stop there. Out of those, two in a week get low enough to read what is at the bottom. He has had that figure for about four months, from a boy, who then had to explain where he had got it.

He stood on the far side of the road from about one until about four and he did not have an instrument.

That is the thing about a wall coming down. There is no form for it. There is no column anywhere in this city for how many people stopped to watch a thing be taken off a wall, and the only way to get the number is to stand on the opposite side of a street in the wind with a coat that does not close and be one of the two hundred, which is not being the two hundred.

He counted what he could count, which was not people. He counted the sheets of paint coming off, about nine to a foot of wall, and he counted how many men came and went, and he wrote the two numbers on the back of his hand in pencil because he had nothing else to write them on.

*Two thousand two hundred and twenty-six days. Less two thousand two hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of March of the seventh year, is seven, and thirty less thirty is nothing by the month count and the seventh is the whole of it.*

*Less the twenty-seventh of December of the sixth year, two thousand one hundred and twenty-six, is one hundred days, which is three months with ten days after them, and a form saying take down to course and make good has been on a spike nine streets away for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and forty-eight days, which is four months with twenty-eight days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard has had nothing at the foot of it for all of them.*

At about four he walked back down to the corner.

The boy was there with the brush and the board by the kerb with the sixty-third form on it and both of its boxes empty, exactly as it had been for a hundred and twenty days, and the wall behind it had sixty feet of hoarding on it and about nine feet of bare grey render where six years of paint had come off in sheets.

“You cannot paint that,” said Lin Hao.

“No.”

“It is the wall.”

“It is not the wall, it is a thing in front of the wall that goes up and comes down again,” said the boy. “Nobody in this city paints a hoarding. A hoarding is not the job. The job is the wall and the wall is behind it and I am going to paint the wall when the wall is there to be painted, and that is not this month.”

He wiped the brush on his coat and looked up at about nine feet of grey render.

“And they will put render back on it,” he said. “That is what make good means. It is not going to be stone and it is not going to be anything anybody can cut into, and there is going to be nine feet of new stuff on it and the two squares at the foot of that form are for the new wall and not for the old one, and I have not been asked about either and nobody is going to.”

He has been reading that wall at close range for six years. It survived because a coat of paint will not hold on dressed stone, and he had taken that for a fact about the stone.

It is an accident. It has a schedule and a crew and two forms and a foreman with a folding rule, and it arrived on the seventh of April in the fourth week of the month, and it did not send anybody a paper.

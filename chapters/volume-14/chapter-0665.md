# A Stone Is Sold By The Piece

Seven days had gone since a boy had put a piece of paper about a wall on a board by a kerb and told him there was no box in this city for a word on a stone, and since then he had walked the length of a street and worked out that there was one thing left to try.

Two thousand two hundred and forty-seven was the twenty-eighth of April and the fifth of May is day two thousand two hundred and fifty-four, and two thousand two hundred and fifty-four less two thousand two hundred and forty-seven is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two added to five is seven by the month count.

A stone is sold by the piece. A mason is paid by the piece. Nothing else in this city is traded that way, and there is no other trade where the person who does the work and the person who keeps the figure are two different people and always have been.

## Nine Streets Away At Eleven

The yard was down a street that had a rail on it on one side and about nine hundred feet of open ground on the other, and there was a sound coming out of it that he had not heard in this city before.

A mason makes a sound on stone. Not a noise. A short flat crack, then a grinding that goes on about nine seconds, then nothing, then another one, and about eleven men were doing it in a building with three walls and a roof on it, and the whole of the sound went out over the open ground and came back at him across about nine hundred feet and it was the only sound in this city anybody makes on purpose.

There were dressed blocks on trestles along the front. About four hundred of them, in sizes, each one with a number chalked on one end, and about a fifth of them were going out through the gate in barrows all morning.

A yard that has been cutting stone since before the office existed does not stop for anybody and does not know there is an office.

He had walked in out of the glare and nobody looked at him for about four minutes.

## The First Relief He Has Had In Four Months

That was the first part of it and it went exactly the way he had expected and for exactly as long as he had expected.

Nobody in that yard had heard of the Register. Nobody in that yard had ever seen the standing sheet. Nobody in that yard knew that about nine people a week in this city get to the bottom of a printed page and write something in two inches of nothing under a name, and there is no reason any of them should know it, and about nine hundred feet of open ground is the reason there is no reason.

For about a minute he was a man standing in a yard where nobody wanted anything from him.

Then it turned over and became the thing he was least able to bear in nine years.

Because a man in that yard had carried a figure against a course of dressed stone for seven years and had never once been asked to justify it and had never once been asked to check it.

He had done it for no reason at all, except that somebody thirty years ago told him to write it down when the block went out. And about ninety pieces of pencil paper were sitting in a drawer in a building with no door onto a street under his own instruction, and not one clerk in this city was permitted to enter a word of any of them.

He had come four months ago with a plate and a title and nobody had wanted anything from him in this yard and they never would.

## The Counter With A Book In It

There was a counter by the gate with a book open on it and a man behind it who had the particular stillness of a person who has sat in one chair for a very long time.

Sander Vole had been in that yard for thirty-one years and he had held that book for nineteen of them, and he wrote in it himself, which is the only reason he was difficult about it.

“You have come about a wall,” he said.

“I have.”

“You have not come to buy anything and there is nothing on that counter for sale.”

“No.”

“There is one question this counter asks about a block of stone and I ask it myself and I have never once asked it of anybody who came in,” said Sander Vole. “It is where it went. That is the whole of it. A block comes in and a block goes out and the only thing anybody in this yard has ever wanted to know about a block is whether it got there.”

“I am not asking about a block of yours.”

“You are asking about a foot of a wall,” said Sander Vole, “and there is no foot of a wall in the ledger on this counter, and I have had it open here for nineteen years, and there has never been a foot of a wall in it. I am telling you that before you ask me the next thing.”

He put his thumb in the page and turned about nine inches of it and turned it back.

“There are two hands in this yard and only one of them writes in this,” he said.
“Mine. The men cut and I carry the figure and it has been that way since before either of us. If you ask a mason where a block came from he will tell you it came off a barrow, and that is not a lie, it is what he was handed.”

“If you ask me I will tell you a figure and a place and I will not be able to tell you whose hand is on the word, and I have never been asked that and I do not think anybody in this city has the standing to ask it.”

## A Figure With No Name Against It

He asked the only question he had come with, which was whether anybody kept a record of where dressed stone came from.

Sander Vole looked at him for about four seconds and then he did something with his face that was not unfriendly and was not friendly either.

“There is a figure against a course of dressed stone,” he said.

“How much.”

“I am not going to tell you how much.”

“All right.”

“Everybody comes back to that. Here is why there is no number on it. It is a figure with no name against it.”

“There is a line in the ledger for a course of dressed stone and there is a figure against that line, and there has never been a name against the figure. Not once in nineteen years, and not in the year before that either, because when my man had it there was no name against it, and he got it from the man before him.”
“You are going to come back to that,” said Sander Vole.

“Whose course of dressed stone.”

“That,” said Sander Vole, “is exactly the question, and you can see now why it has never been answered, and I am not going to pretend I have thought about it for nineteen years, because I have thought about it for about nine minutes and you have just made me do it.”

He squared the book to the edge of the counter.
“That is the rule of the ledger and I have never written it down anywhere and I do not know where I got it. You cannot take a figure off a line. You can only add to it and go on, and if you have made a mistake then the mistake is in there for good and the only thing that happens to it is that it stops meaning anything to anybody and it is still on the page.”

“It has been sitting on that line for seven years and it has not moved and it is not going to, and nobody has ever opened the ledger at that page.”

“A figure can be added to and cannot be taken from,” he said.

## What He Was Not Refused

He was not refused anything.

That was the part that undid the morning. He stood at that counter for about another quarter of an hour and asked four more questions and got four answers, and not one of the four was a refusal and not one of the four was a gift, and every one of the four was a thing a yard is for.

A block leaves. A figure comes in. Nobody has ever asked the two apart and there is no arrangement in this city that would separate them, and if you separated them then about nine hundred blocks a week would want to know where they went and about forty people would have to keep track of them and there is no money in this yard to pay forty people.

He said that last part without any bitterness at all and he said it as a fact about a yard, which is what it is.

“Does anybody ever write a word on a block.”

“A word.”

“A sentence, or a name, or two words.”

“No,” said Sander Vole.

“Why not.”

“You cannot put a letter on this and it will still be a letter in two hundred years and you cannot paint over it and have it go, and a stone that has been dressed and put into a wall is not a thing anybody can get at again without taking the wall down,” said Sander Vole. “And nobody has ever wanted to write a word on a block in this city in my time, and I have never asked anybody why.”

He closed the book and it shut with the sound that a book in a yard shuts with, which is the sound of a thing that is not a document.

“A course of dressed stone does not care where it came from,” he said. “It came from a quarry and a mason and a barrow and it is going to a wall and it is going to be a wall for about a hundred years and then it is going to be a wall that comes down and gets made good, and I have written down what I am told to write down for nineteen years and I have not once been asked to be right.”

## The Last Hour Of It

He stood outside the gate for about a quarter of an hour with about nine hundred feet of open ground going away from him in every direction.

A man cannot ask a stone yard about a stone. The office does not send a paper to a stone yard.

The office cannot be compelled and holds about ninety pieces of pencil paper in a drawer that a clerk may enter but cannot improve and cannot be asked to hurry about, and every one of those ninety pieces has a date and a place in a hand that is not hers and nothing whatever against it.

A course of dressed stone goes into the bottom of a wall and stays there and holds a line of two words cut into it all the way through the face.

A figure sits on a line in a book in a yard nine streets away with no name against it, and it has not moved in seven years. And the two words were cut by somebody who did not write down which block, and there is no column in this city for a word on a stone and no form for it and no box for it and no clerk who may be given it.

The book has never been opened at that page.

*Two thousand two hundred and fifty-four days. Less two thousand two hundred and forty-seven, the twenty-eighth of April of the seventh year, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two added to five is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and seventy-six days, which is five months with twenty-six days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard has had nothing at the foot of it for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and thirty-six days, which is seventeen months with twenty-six days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and a yard nine streets from a works department has a figure against a course of dressed stone with no name against it, and a counter figure can be added to and cannot be taken from, and it has not moved in seven years, and nobody has ever opened that book at that page.*

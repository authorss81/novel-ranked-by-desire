# A Woman With A Charcoal

Six days had gone since he stood at a wall end and learned that a form on a spike had sixty-one days on it and a shutter had a hundred and nine.

Two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven was the twenty-eighth of February and the fourth of March is day two thousand one hundred and ninety-three, and two thousand one hundred and ninety-three less two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven is six, and four less twenty-eight is six by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.

The laundry was hot at about eleven in the morning, with steam on every window and three tables along the bench and a ledger on the middle one, closed, with a pencil across it, and Perla Quist stood at the bench with her sleeves pushed up and her hands red from the water, watching him come in without surprise, because he had been there before and she had known he would come again.

She was a woman of about fifty who could write, and that was all the description anybody needed, because what she did with a pencil was the whole of her, and she was not the woman of about fifty at the corner, who could not write her own name and was a different person entirely.

Lin Hao wanted to find out who had filled the two inches in, and not to frighten them, because the only person that office might ask was him and the person who wrote it had come through a front door and left.

## Eleven, Corrected To Nine

“I copied the foot of that sheet,” said Perla Quist, before he asked, because she had had a month to decide what to say and had decided it. “A person came into this laundry in January who could not write and wanted somebody to write down what had been done to her, and there was nowhere to write it down. So I wrote it where there was room.”

Lin Hao stood very still in the steam, with water dripping somewhere behind him, and felt shame come up in him, which he did not name and could not get at.

The office had a room, a table, two chairs, a drawer and a clerk, and it had carried a column headed carried by six years with two lines in it, and the first person who came with something real in two inches went to a laundry because a laundry was where you went.

“What did you write,” he said quietly.

She opened the ledger, not to show him, but to steady her hands, and then closed it again.

“A number of months,” she said. “Not a name. Not a figure. Not a deed. A number of months, because that is the one thing a sheet has no column for anywhere in Ostara. There is no box for a duration. There is no line of work for it. It is the thing a person who cannot read has and cannot hand over.”

He closed his eyes a moment.

A number of months. The first thing anybody wrote in those two inches, and it was the one thing no instrument in Ostara could hold, and he could not enter it and did not try to, because trying would have been a lie in a room built for truth.

“How many people have asked you to do it,” he said.

“Eleven,” she said, and then stopped, and corrected herself, plainly, without embarrassment. “Nine. Eleven came to the door. Nine asked. Two went away.”

The correction was the whole of her honesty, and he felt tenderness toward her come up under the shame, sudden and unwelcome, because she counted the way Emil Brack counted, out loud and corrected, and nobody in his building did.

She took a folded copy from under the bench and gave it to him with both hands, the months in it in charcoal, dark and smudged at the edges, and looked him in the face.

“Is that where such a thing is supposed to go,” she said.

He had never before said that sentence in that office, or out of it, and saying it hurt more than any refusal he had ever been given.

“I do not know,” said Lin Hao.

She accepted that, because she was a person who wrote things down and knew what not knowing looked like, and she did not press him, and he stood a while longer watching her work, folding and stacking, her hands quick and sure.

## Four In The Afternoon At The Counter

At about four he went to the room off a third court with the copy in his coat and laid it on the table in front of Wenna Raut without a word, and she looked at it and did not touch it, because touching it would have been an entry and there was nothing to enter.

“The second column is headed carried by and has two lines,” she said, “and nothing goes in it from this. A number of months is not a fact about a person that I may enter. It is a fact about time.”

“I know,” he said.

He walked home through the cold dusk with the charcoal copy against his ribs and Perla Quist’s correction in his ears, eleven, nine, and what he wanted, unspoken and unadorned, was for that laundry to be wrong about needing to do what it did, and he knew as he wanted it that it was not wrong, and that was the cold thing at the heart of March.

Pieces of paper were counted as pieces of paper and never as claims and never as people, and somebody had to start counting them, and it was not going to be him.

*Two thousand one hundred and ninety-three days. Less two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven, the twenty-eighth of February of year seven, is six. Four less twenty-eight is six by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month.*

*The second column is headed carried by and has two lines, and nothing goes in it from this copy.*

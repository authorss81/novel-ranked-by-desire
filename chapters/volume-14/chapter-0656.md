# A Woman With A Charcoal

Six days had gone since two men in coats had stood at the bottom of a wall and looked up at it with a mark on a stick and had gone away without asking anybody anything.

Two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven was the twenty-eighth of February and the fourth of March is day two thousand one hundred and ninety-three, and two thousand one hundred and ninety-three less two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven is six, and thirty less twenty-eight is two and two added to four is six by the month count.

He had got as far as a corner, two counters and a laundry front door in nine days, and the laundry was the third of them and the third one is where the copies were coming from, and he knew that by about the fourth of March because the third house he went into had two of them folded in a bundle by the boiler.

## Steam And Three Tables

The laundry was off a street two streets wide and there was steam coming under the door into about four feet of cold, and the door was open because a laundry is open.

There were three tables down the middle. The first had clean folded things on it. The second had clean folded things on it and a better table under them. The third had a ledger on it, and there was one pencil beside the ledger and no ink anywhere in the building.

A woman was taking a bundle out of a press with both arms and put it on the second table.

“If you are here about the page,” she said, “sit down at the third one and do not stand in the steam, because I cannot talk to a man who is wet and I am not going to ask you which laundry you want.”

“There are no pages on a rail here.”

“There are no pages on a rail here,” said the woman, “and there have been nine of them on that table since the second of January, and you did not come about the rail.”

## Why A Laundry

She came and sat down at the third table herself, on the other side, and squared the ledger so that the lines of it ran away from her.

“You want to know who came here in January,” she said. “I will tell you who came here in January and I am going to tell you in the order they came, because I am not going to be asked to improve it and I have a rule about the order.”

“Please.”

“A woman came in on the ninth of January at about eleven in the morning and she could not write,” said the woman. “That is the whole of what I know about her. She did not tell me her name and I did not ask her and she did not come to a counter, and she came to a laundry, and I have thought about that for about seven weeks and I still do not know why she came to a laundry.”

“She came because a laundry is open.”

“A laundry is open and a counter is not,” she said, “and she had a piece of paper with a thing printed at the bottom of it and she wanted somebody to write underneath the printing. And there is nowhere in this city to write underneath a printed thing. I asked her that. I asked her where she thought she was supposed to put it and she looked at me.”

“And you told her.”

“I told her that there was a piece of paper on my table and a pencil and a woman who can write,” said the woman, “and I wrote down what she said, and she went away with it, and about nine days later a second woman came in and asked me the same question.”

## A Number Of Months

She put her hand on the ledger and did not open it.

“Here is what she wanted written,” she said. “And I am going to say the words and I am going to say them in the order she said them, and then you are going to tell me that there is nothing to do with them.”

“All right.”

“She said: it was eleven months.”

He waited for the rest of it.

“She said it was eleven months and she said it three times and she did not say what it was eleven months of,” said the woman.

“I wrote *eleven months* on a sheet of paper and I copied the four sentences and the six lines off the sheet she had with her, all of them, including the one somebody has gone through with a pen. And I put her four words in the two inches underneath.”

“What four words.”

“*The fourth of March. Back lane.*”

The same four words as the sheet that had come through the front door of his building on the first of February, in the same hand, in the same order, and about a month earlier.

“There is no box for it,” said the woman. “That is what I have been sitting here with for seven weeks. There is no box on that paper for eleven months. There is no box in this city for how long a thing went on. You can put a date in a box. You can put an hour in a box. You cannot put a duration in one, and I have looked at about forty forms in my life and not one of them has a place for how long.”

“A duration.”

“It is the one thing a woman who cannot write has got,” said the woman. “She cannot read the demand and she cannot write her answer to it and she cannot put a figure on anything. She can count. She can say how many months. And there is nowhere in this city for it and that is why she came to a laundry and not to a counter, and I would like somebody to tell me what I was supposed to do with her.”

## Eleven, And Then Nine

“How many people have asked you to do it.”

He said it as a question and not as a figure, and he watched her face while he said it, and he got what he expected, which was that a woman who can write and has a pencil is the most dangerous person in this city to ask a counting question.

“Eleven,” said the woman.

She stopped.

It took her about four seconds. He counted them and did not take his eyes off her.

“Eleven of what.”

“Eleven people.”

“No,” said the woman. “Nine.”

“Say why.”

“Because two of them were the same woman twice,” said the woman, “and she came in on the ninth of January and she came in again on the second of February and she wanted the same eleven months written a second time because the first one had been in her apron pocket and come out folded, and she wanted it whole.”

“So there were nine people.”

“There were nine people and there are nine sheets of paper,” said the woman, “and they are in nine kitchens in this city and I have never been in eight of them and I have not asked where they go, and I am not going to, and you are going to ask me whether that is a mistake and it is not a mistake, it is the only arrangement I have that does not make them frightened.”

“All right.”

“And you are going to leave them where they are.”

“Yes.”

“You are not going to take them.”

“No.”

“Then say it slower,” said the woman, “because a man in a coat has taken things out of a room in this city before and I have a counter at four o’clock this afternoon and I can see from here that you have spent something.”

## The Counter Off A Third Court

He was at a counter off a third court at about four in the afternoon and Wenna Raut was folding the bundle for the last time before the thirtieth.

He put one sheet on the counter.

It was a sheet of heavy rag of the kind a laundry keeps and the four sentences at the top were in her hand and the six lines under them were in her hand and the fourth of the six had a stroke through it, and in the two inches under the striking there was one line in charcoal.

It said: *eleven months.*

Not a figure. Not a name. Not an hour and not a deed.

There is no column for eleven months. There is no column for a duration anywhere in this city between a counter off a fourth court and a works department nine streets away, and the second column of the standing sheet is headed carried by and has two lines in it and one of them has been there since the fifteenth of October of an earlier year.

He did not ask her to enter it. He did not ask her anything.

## The Sentence He Has Not Said In That Office

The clerk looked at it for about four seconds and then she put it down the way she puts down anything that is going into the bundle without being entered, and squared it to the edge of the table.

The woman at the third table had come in behind him with her coat on and she did not come all the way to the counter.

She stood about nine feet off, which is where everybody stands, and she said, “Is that where such a thing is supposed to go.”

He turned round.

He had a room off a third court with a table and two chairs and a drawer and a clerk who has entered everything given to her for six years without improving one item, and a column headed carried by, and a form that has been on a counter nine streets away since the twenty-seventh of December.

“I do not know,” he said.

She waited about four seconds to see whether he was going to say anything else.

“No,” said the woman. “And I have run this laundry about nineteen years and you are the first person in this city who has ever said those three words to me at a counter, and I would like you to notice that you said them standing up, and that I am the one asking.”

*Two thousand one hundred and ninety-three days. Less two thousand one hundred and eighty-seven, the twenty-eighth of February of the seventh year, is six, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two added to four is six by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and fifteen days, which is three months with twenty-five days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard has had nothing at the foot of it for all of them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is four hundred and seventy-five days, which is fifteen months with twenty-five days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, it lets any person in this city set down a number of their own invention, and it is the reason nine people in nine kitchens have a copy of that foot.*

*There is still no column in this city for a number of months. A date goes in a box and an hour goes in a box and a duration goes nowhere at all, and about nine people have found that out in about nine kitchens in about seven weeks and not one of them has said so to anybody who could do anything with it.*

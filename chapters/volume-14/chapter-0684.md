# One Line In The Second Column

He went in with the list on the back of his hand again, and this time he had been to a woman with a chart on the sixth of September and had been told about a line across the foot of every column of it, and it had taken the four hours between the top of the stair and that room to work out what it had to do with the second column.

Two thousand three hundred and seventy-five was the sixth of September and the twelfth is day two thousand three hundred and eighty-one, and two thousand three hundred and eighty-one less two thousand three hundred and seventy-five is six, and twelve less six is six by the month count.

## Eleven Days

He had eleven days left and he had thought about it for six of them and had not improved a word.

The date was the thirtieth of September at about half past two, and the twenty-seventh sheet would be squared to the edge of the table at that hour and signed for and folded and taken down a stair 

It would go up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain at four, and there was no form in this city for going back to a sheet once it has gone on a rail, and he had made that sentence himself in June and it was the truest thing anybody had ever said about a piece of paper.

“There is one difference from the thirtieth of August,” he said. “On that day you entered the same fact in the Register. Today it goes on the page.”

“That is correct.”

“It goes on the page on the thirtieth of September and every quarter after that for as long as this office exists,” he said, “and about nine people a week read down to that part of it.”

“Yes,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the part of it I have thought about for six weeks and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not. Enter the line. I will read it back and you will tell me if I have improved one word of it, and if you do not tell me then I have not.”

## What A Column Takes

A column headed carried by has five fields in it and he had gone into that room knowing what they were, because a clerk had read them out to him once in February without being asked.

A date. A place. An hour. A thing that was done. And a person.

He stood at the table for about a minute and worked out that he had four of the five and that the fifth one was the whole of the problem, and that the fifth one was the only one anybody could ever want.

“I am not giving you a person,” he said.

“You are not giving me a person and there is nobody in this building who may enter one without having it from the person it is about,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the rule and it is the only rule I have and it is in the first half of the first page of the bound register in front of me, and you have been in this room long enough to have read it.”

“Then it goes in empty.”

“The field goes in empty or it does not go in at all, and an empty field in a form in this city is not a silence, it is a finding, and about four years from now somebody will stand at a counter and read that field out loud.”

“Then it goes in empty and it goes in with the receipt on it and the receipt says what is not in it.”

Wenna Raut looked at him for about four seconds, which was as long as she ever looked at anybody.

“Say the last part again.”

“The receipt says what is not in it.”

“Write that down,” said Wenna Raut.

He put the list on the table face up, in her hand, in pencil, and she copied it into the column in ink with a nib that had been ground down twice this year, and she copied it in the order it was in.

## Three Lines

The second column had two lines in it when he came in on the twelfth of September and three lines in it when he went out, and the difference between those two states of the same column is the whole of what happened that morning.

The new line said that between given dates four hundred and nine pieces of paper were given to this office, each of them a copy of the foot of the twenty-fourth sheet and each of them with writing in the two inches, and that none of them is a deed, and that none of them carries an hour against it, and that none of them carries a name, and that the office has entered the receipt and not the claims.

That is what is in it.

The first of the two lines that were already there is about a bundle. The second of the two has been in that column since before this office was opened and is not going anywhere.

She squared the book and read all three back in the order they stood, and he heard his own handwriting come out of another woman’s mouth in a room he was standing in, and the only word he was listening for was the fourth one in the second field, and it was not there.

*None of them is a deed.*

“Then the head,” he said.

“The head stands at eight hundred and fifty-two and it is not moving today and it is not moving on the thirtieth,” said Wenna Raut. “It has not moved since the thirtieth of March of a year I was not here for, and there is no permission and there is no one in this city who can give one, and I am going to say that out loud once every quarter for as long as I am in this room, and today I have said it.”

## The Second Of Four, Read Back About The Office

Yusuf Adeyemi had not moved from the end of the table.

“I want to say the part nobody is going to enjoy,” he said, “and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go back to the two rooms I have.”

“The second of the four is the one about the Register, and it is the truest sentence on the sheet, and it has been at the foot of that page since before this office existed and about nine people a week read down to it.”

“Yes,” he said.

“It is now going out at four on the thirtieth of this month saying that, in the same hand, on the same page, under a line that says this office has been given four hundred and nine pieces of paper.” He turned his pen over once.

 “The sentence is true about every person in this city who has ever been refused at a counter and it is false about this office, and there is nobody in this city entitled to say which of those a person reading it is supposed to take it to mean, and that includes you and it includes the woman at the table.”

“You cannot print a correction.”

“There is no form anywhere in this city on which this office may say a thing about itself and I have looked and looking is not a permitted reason for refusing to print something,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “That is the fourth thing I told you in February and you have made it true in seven months without noticing. You cannot correct it.”

“You can only put one more line under it, and there is a limit to how many lines a page takes before it stops being a page.”

*Two thousand three hundred and eighty-one days. Less two thousand three hundred and seventy-five, the sixth of September of the seventh year, is six, and the days of the two months say twelve less six.*

*Less the thirtieth of June of the seventh year, two thousand three hundred and nine, is seventy-two days, which is two months with twelve days after them, and the twenty-sixth sheet is still face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain with about a hundred people a month putting a palm on the corner of it to get past.*

*Less the twenty-third of November of the sixth year, two thousand and ninety-two, is two hundred and eighty-nine days, which is nine months with nineteen days after them, and six lines have stood under the four sentences for all of them, and the fourth of the six has had a stroke through it from the first letter to the last, and a name under the striking, and two inches of nothing.*

## Two People At A Door On A Court

He went down at about one because he could not sit still in that room and could not think in it either.

There were two people on the step of that building and the door was shut and they were not knocking.

Both of them had pieces of paper in their hands. He saw the shape of them from about thirty feet off, which is the shape of about four hundred pieces of paper in this city, and he came down the last of the stair and out through the arch and one of them looked up and recognised him from the lane and the corner and did not say so.

“You are the one from the board,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It is not the thirtieth,” said the man.

“It is not the thirtieth,” he agreed.

They stood on the step of a building with a door onto a court and no door onto a street, and neither of them knocked, and about nine minutes passed and he understood what he was looking at.

They had worked out the date.

Not the date the sheet goes out at four, which is printed in no form anywhere in this city and has never been printed anywhere in this city. The date the column closes. Some woman with a pencil in a laundry had told them, or they had counted, or they had been in that room before him in a year neither of them would name and had heard the same thing said.

Two people at a door with sheets in their hands, waiting for a day that had not come, because a man they had never spoken to had spent five weeks thinking about what a third line in a column costs.

He could have said the line was entered. He could have said it was in the column and it would be on the page on the thirtieth, and there was nothing anybody could do with it before then.

He did not say either of those things, and he went down the stair and out through the arch and along a street with nothing in his hands.

He had been the only reader in this city for nine years and about four hundred people had started building a machine that did not need him, and the first thing the machine had done with him was stand on his step on the wrong day.

# An Index In Pencil

Seven days had gone since a woman with a pencil had refused to have her name against anything, and had then told him in the same breath that she would make him something on the twenty-fourth that would not be a book, and he had gone up a street two streets wide and not thought about it once, which was a lie and he knew which parts of it were.

Two thousand two hundred and ninety-six was the seventeenth of June and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand three hundred and three, and two thousand three hundred and three less two thousand two hundred and ninety-six is seven, and the days of the two months say twenty-four less seventeen.

He was at the third table at about ten with nothing in his hands, which was the only way to arrive anywhere now.

## What She Made

It was nine ruled sheets of the same paper as the copies, because she had bought them out of the laundry’s own stock on the twenty-second and had cut them herself on the long table with a blade she used for linens.

Down the left of each one there was a date. Then a place. Then a line ruled the width of the page for a number of months, which was empty on most of them and had a figure in it on about nine. And a column for a name that was empty on every single line.

There were about a hundred and ninety of them.

“There is no column for the name and I have ruled it anyway,” said Perla Quist. “That is the first thing I want you to look at and then I want you to say whether I should take it out.”

He looked at it.

“Take it out.”

“You are going to regret saying that and I am going to make you say it again in about a month.” She wrote the word *date* at the top of the first column and did not write anything at the top of the second. “There. Nine sheets. That is the whole of it, and it took a day and a night, and it is not a book.”

## Why A Pencil

“It is better than the office’s,” he said.

“No it is not.”

“It is better than the office’s and I would like you to hear me say the reason before you tell me I am wrong, because you have been right about everything since the fourth of March.”

She put both forearms on the third table, which she had never done.

“Say the reason,” said Perla Quist.

“It is in pencil,” he said, “and it is yours.”

He put his thumb against the edge of the top sheet and did not move it.

“And the sheet this office prints goes up on a rail at four, three times in a year and once in a fourth, and it cannot be got back. If a line on it is wrong then it is wrong for three months.”

“Three months.”

“Three months, and nobody in this city has ever been in a room in which anybody argued with one of its lines. I have printed twenty-five of them and not one has ever been argued with by anybody, and it is not because of the four sentences at the foot. It is because there is no pencil on it.”

Perla Quist did not say anything for about four seconds, and when she did, what she said was not what he had got ready for.

“A thing that can be corrected is a thing somebody has argued with,” she said. “That is all. That is the whole of it and you have had nine years to get to it and you have got there on the fourth of June on a hill in front of about two hundred people.”

He had not thought of it that way. He had thought of it as an advantage and she had thought of it as a thing with a history, and there is a difference, and the difference was about a hundred and forty people in a yard correcting each other out loud inside four minutes for no reason at all.

“You have made two of them,” he said.

“I have made two of them.”

“Why two.”

“One of them is a thing that can be lost,” said Perla Quist, “and I have known about that since about the ninth of January. I made the second one last night and I said nothing about it at the time. You would have spotted it inside about nine seconds. I would rather hand it over than be found out keeping it.”

## Nine Names

She asked one question on the twenty-second and it was about nine names, and the nine names were the ones that appeared in both places.

She had gone to him.

That was the part of it he had not expected at all. On the twenty-second, between cutting the sheets and ruling them, Perla Quist had walked to a passage off Cordwain and stood at a counter under a window and asked a man who had run it about nine years for his ledger.

“Did you give it to her.”

“No,” said Perla Quist. “He would not give it to me and I would not have taken it if he had. I said I wanted to copy the thirty names out of it onto my own paper and he turned it round to face me and read them out loud while I wrote, and then he took it back and shut the drawer, and then he stood there for about a minute and a half as though he had meant to say something and had not.”

“What was it.”

“I have no idea and I am not going to find out,” said Perla Quist. “I am going to ask him one question and I am going to ask him about nine names, and I am telling you the number before I tell you the question, because you have a habit of arriving at the answer first.”

Thirty in his ledger over nine years, and about a hundred and forty in a margin over five months, and nine of them in both.

A woman who came to a desk under a window and asked for something, and afterwards came to a laundry with a pencil put in her hand and about two fingers of blank paper to write in. A woman who had been on and off eleven times and had a date and an hour against every one of the eleven and no figure anywhere on any of it.

Seven others of the same kind.

She had nine names and about four hundred pieces of paper and no instrument anywhere in this city that could put the two lists side by side, and he had a drawer with about ninety pieces in it and no entry against any of them, and the only thing in the whole of Ostara that had ever put two documents side by side was a division that read every paper twice into two books four feet apart and had never once laid them together.

“I have been counting other people’s sheets for nine years and nobody has counted mine,” said Perla Quist, “and it turns out nine of the thirty have been in this room.”

“So the desk under the window has been feeding you.”

“No. The other way round, and I do not know which, and that is the part I cannot get past.” She squared the second sheet against the first.

“I have copies in this city, in one part of it or another, in about four hundred kitchens. Nine of those kitchens have also been to a counter off Cordwain and asked a man for something he did not ask them about.”

“That is a spine.”

“It is a spine and I did not make it and I cannot see the ends of it.” She put the pencil down on the second sheet. “And I am not going to write it down as a spine, because the day I write a thing like that down it is a claim and not a list.”

He wanted to be able to correct something.

That was the whole of the morning underneath everything else and he did not say it out loud, because he had learned in five months that the things he wants most are the ones that sound like an office asking for something.

He had wanted it since the fourth of March, when a woman had told him that eleven months was the one thing a person who cannot write could put on a page, and not one form in this city has a place for it, and he had gone into a room off a third court and asked the clerk to enter a sentence and could not.

He could not correct anything. The drawer had ninety pieces of pencil paper in it and no form for a holding and no entry. The twenty-fifth sheet went up on a rail at four on the thirtieth of March. The twenty-sixth goes up in six days. Neither of them will ever come down.

And about four hundred people in this city were about to be able to get something wrong in public and have a bystander put it right inside about four minutes, and he was going to be able to do none of it at all.

The plate in his coat carried his name and nothing cut into the metal, and the rank that went with it entitled him to the only instrument in the city, and the instrument was a press that came out four times a year.

“Take one,” he said.

Perla Quist put the second one down on the third table and turned it round to face him, and it was nine ruled sheets with a hundred and ninety dates on them and an empty column at the right-hand edge of every line.

“No,” he said. “Not in the drawer.”

“Then where.”

“Nowhere,” said Lin Hao. “There is a drawer in a room off a third court with about ninety pieces of pencil paper in it and not one entry against any of them.”

She waited.

“If a piece of paper with a hundred and ninety dates on it goes into that drawer, then in about four years somebody is going to stand at a counter and ask what this office does with paper. The answer will be that it does nothing and it will be true. You told me that on the seventeenth and it has not stopped going round since.”

## The Seventh Of July

*Two thousand three hundred and three days. Less two thousand two hundred and ninety-six, the seventeenth of June of the seventh year, is seven, and the days of the two months say twenty-four less seventeen.*

*Less the first of March of the second year, three hundred and ninety, is one thousand nine hundred and thirteen days, which is sixty-three months with twenty-three days after them, and a sheet has lain face up on a counter under a window in a passage off Cordwain for all of them, and about a hundred people a month put a hand on the corner of it and go round, and not one of them has got to the ninth line.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is five hundred and eighty-five days, which is nineteen months with fifteen days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and a woman off a street two streets wide has copied it out about a hundred and ninety times in pencil in nine sheets, and ruled a column for a name on every line and written nothing in any of them.*

## Somebody Is Going To Write To Me

She let him have it and he put it inside his coat against the place where he had kept a form for a hundred and twenty days.

He got to the street before she said it, and she said it through the steam.

“You have taken a piece of paper out of this room with nine sheets on it and there is not one entry against it anywhere and there is not one name on it anywhere and nobody in this city is obliged to do anything about it.”

“That is correct.”

“I know that it is correct,” said Perla Quist. “What I am going to say is that in about two weeks somebody is going to come through that door with a piece of paper and ask me whether it is the right one, and they are not going to ask you, because you are on a night shift and you are in a building with no door onto a street, and I am going to be here at six in the morning with a pencil.”

He stood in the street and listened to the press.

It was the first time in nine years that a thing in this city had come to somebody inside it rather than gone out of it.

About four hundred people in four hundred kitchens were about to find out about it at the same moment and none of them knew it yet. There was no instrument anywhere that could make it a record, because a record has somebody in it who can be got at, and she had told him that on the seventeenth and he had agreed with her and he had taken a copy anyway.

# Paper Costs Money

Seven days had gone since an auditor had said in a room with a shut door in it that a copy is a thing somebody made up on purpose, and had then told him she had read that sentence about four hundred times and neither of the two men who had said it out loud had ever read it.

Two thousand and two hundred and six was the seventeenth of March and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand and two hundred and thirteen, and two thousand and two hundred and thirteen less two thousand and two hundred and six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

He had never once in nine years paid for anything.

That is not a boast and it is not entirely true. He had bought a cup of coffee at four in the morning on a corner in the fourth year and had given a woman a share of himself that is not a purchase and cannot be taken back.

He had never once stood at a counter and been told what a thing cost.

## Two O’Clock Above A Street Off Cordwain

The counting house was one floor above a shop and the stairs had a handrail on one side only, and he went up them in four, and there was a counter with a bell on it and a corridor and a room at the end with a table in it.

Solveig Rask was at the table with a book open in front of her and her own book, which is not the house book, and the house book is chained in a room off a landing and cannot be in this room and has not been in this room in twenty-six years.

It has been open since the sixteenth of September.

“Twenty-six years,” she said. “Sit down or do not, and if you sit down then you are in a room with two books in it and one of them is mine, and I will tell you which one when you have been in here for nine minutes and not before.”

“Say it now.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Because there is a person coming up those stairs behind you and she carries a broker’s book and she could not reach the bell, and if I say the name of my own book in front of her then the next person to come through that door is a broker, and I have done this trade for twenty-six years and I have never once given a trade away for the sake of a conversation.”

The bell went about two minutes later.

## What A Real Book May Do

The girl went through with the book and did not look at either of them and did not stop.

“A real book is written in the hand of the person who carries the debt,” said Solveig Rask. “Not the clerk’s hand. The carrier’s. And the carrier may not read her own entry out to another person, out loud, in a room, and if she does then the entry is void and has to be written again and it cannot be written again that day.”

“So you cannot tell me a price.”

“I can tell you a price I have in my head that is not an entry in a book,” said Solveig Rask, “and there is not one price in that trade that is only in somebody’s head, and I have not been in this room twenty-six years to believe that a man in a rank-one coat cannot be shown an account.”

“Show me the account.”

“The account is not on your business,” she said. “You have not got a business. You have got a piece of paper you want made into an instrument, and that is a different trade and I have never run it and I do not know anybody who has.”

## The Price Of A Sheet Of Paper

“Forty-one sheets came through the front door of my building in a month,” he said, “and about twelve of them were the foot of a page copied out longhand, and there are now about nine pieces of paper in nine kitchens in this city and there will be more of them every week until somebody stops it.”

“And how many will there be.”

“I do not know. Neither does the man at the counter who put the number out. Neither does the woman with the ledger in the laundry, and she has the only index of them in this city that anybody has made.”

“Then you have no figure and you have come to a counting house.”

“I have come to a counting house because I have never once found out what a piece of paper costs,” he said, “and I am about to find out that it is not a price and that is going to be the whole of this morning.”

Solveig Rask put her own book open flat and turned it round so that he could see the page, and it was the page and there was nothing on it that he could read in the time allowed.

“Paper is bought by weight,” she said. “Not by the sheet. Nine houses in this city sell it and one of the nine is the man whose shop this counter is under and he sells about a hundredweight a week of nothing but it and he does not weigh it while you watch him.”

“Ninety sheets is nothing.”

“Ninety sheets is nothing at all, and it weighs nothing, and it is made in a shed nine streets from here out of rags off about four hundred houses, and that is the whole of what it costs, and you did not need to come up a stair for that.”

“No,” he said. “That is not what I came for.”

## Nine Wholesalers And Two Books Four Feet Apart

“I came for the number of people,” he said.

Solveig Rask stopped.

Not for long. Long enough.

“The number of people who bought paper in a given week,” she said slowly. “You want to know how many people in this city are copying the foot of that sheet.”

“I want to know whether stopping them is expensive.”

“Stopping them is free,” said Solveig Rask, “and the number of people who would stop is nobody, and the number of people who are already doing it is counted twice by a division in this city because a division reads every paper twice and keeps the two readings in two books four feet apart and has never once laid them side by side.”

“Seventeen,” he said.

“Seventeen in a quarter,” said Solveig Rask. “Thirteen of them can be named and four cannot, and they are seventeen apart the way they are apart about everything else, and there is no column anywhere in that building for a person who buys paper.”

He stood there and understood the whole of the morning in about nine seconds, and then he got angry, and it was the first time since September that he had got angry at a building rather than at a person, and the anger was correct and it did not help.

“Eleven hours a week,” he said. “Nine people at four hours a week each, copying out four sentences and six lines and then writing something in two inches underneath, with a pencil, at a kitchen table, and not one of them is being paid by anybody for it.”

“No.”

“Then the cost is the time.”

“The cost is the time and the time belongs to the people doing it and they did not ask you to count them,” said Solveig Rask. “You have come up a stair with a spent chest to find out what a thing costs and the thing costs thirty-six hours a week spread over nine women who cannot write and who have nobody to hand it to, and you are not paying any of it, and nobody else is either, and it will go on not being paid.”

## A Broker Above Her

There was a man come up the stairs while they were talking and he stood in the corridor and did not come in.

“There is a broker on the floor above this one,” said Solveig Rask, without turning round. “He will price anything you like. He has priced about six hundred things to about six hundred people in this city in nine years and he has never once done it for nothing and he has never once profited where a person lost, and that is a sentence I am entitled to say and he is entitled to hear it.”

“Have you used him.”

“I have used him twice,” said Solveig Rask, “and both times for a person and not for a house, and both times he told me the same thing, which is that he will tell me what a thing costs and he will not tell me what it is worth, and that there is no instrument in this city that turns one into the other, and he is right and I have never been able to argue it.”

She shut her own book.

“You should go up one floor,” she said, “because there is nothing left for me to give you and there is a man above me who will give you a number, and you are going to go up and he is going to give you a number, and I want to be able to say afterwards that I told you what the number was.”

## The Counter With A Figure And No Name

The broker had one room and a bench and a drawer with more sheets of paper in it than a man could write on in a year, and it took him about four minutes.

He gave Lin Hao a price for ninety sheets and a price for nine hundred and a price for the whole of what a rag is worth in this city on an ordinary morning, and all three of them were sensible, and none of them was the price of the thing.

Then he said, “And I will tell you the other figure, because you have come up a stair and you have not come about paper.”

“All right.”

“The counter in the basement under this building took paper in for about seven years,” said the broker, “and the figure against that counter has not moved in seven years and no name is against the figure, and it has been copied forward by whoever was holding the book, and nobody has ever opened it to find out whose counter it was.”

“Why would you tell me that.”

“Because a man who asks what a sheet costs is a man with a small number,” said the broker, “and in about four years you are going to be a man with a large one, and the two of you are going to stand in this city and argue, and I am telling you now which of us is standing next to which.”

Lin Hao took the stair down at a speed that was not careful and got to the bottom of it in three and stood in the street.

A counter figure can be added to and cannot be taken from.

He had been told that in a division building on a morning in September by a woman with a bad ear, and it was one of about nine true things said to him since September that he had put away and had not used, and it was the same sentence, word for word, that a man in a stone yard nine streets from this one has been saying to himself for seven years without knowing anybody in this city can use it.

He did not know that yet.

*Two thousand and two hundred and thirteen days. Less two thousand and two hundred and six, the seventeenth of March of the seventh year, is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the sixth year, two thousand and seventy-eight, is one hundred and thirty-five days, which is four months with fifteen days after them. A shutter has been bare for all of them, and about two thousand people a day slow down at nothing.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is four hundred and ninety-five days, which is sixteen months with fifteen days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet, and about nine people a month are making copies of that foot in pencil on the strength of it.*

*Paper is bought by weight from nine houses in this city. The number of people who bought any in a given week is read twice by a division into two books four feet apart and has never once been laid side by side.*

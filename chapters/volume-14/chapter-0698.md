# A Word On A Stone

He went back at two in the afternoon of the seventeenth of December and the light in that yard is different in the fourth month of the year than it is in the ninth, and a yard that has been cutting stone since before this office existed does not stop for it.

Two thousand four hundred and sixty-nine was the tenth of December and the seventeenth is day two thousand four hundred and seventy-six, and two thousand four hundred and seventy-six less two thousand four hundred and sixty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## A Book With A Figure In It And No Name Against It

Sander Vole had the book out on the bench behind the counter and had it open, which he has not had it open in nineteen years and did not open it for him.

“Nineteen inches of nothing and dust on it about nine hundred feet of open ground,” he said. “I have had it out to see if the page would come. It would not come.”

“It would not come.”

“It would not come and it has not come since the sixth year,” said Sander Vole. “I am going to tell you what is on that page because you have come about it twice and because you are the only person who has ever come about it, and then I am going to shut it, and I want you to hear the shape of the answer before I give you any of it.”

“There is a figure against a course of dressed stone.”

“There is a figure against a course of dressed stone,” said Sander Vole, “and it has no name against it, and I am not going to say the figure and you are not going to get it out of me. The reason is that I have never once been able to work out what a figure is for on a page that has got no name on it.”

“I have had that page in a ledger on a counter for seven years without opening it. A figure that a man cannot open a book to look at is not a figure.”

He shut it.

“The rest of the page is the same,” he said. “Two pages of things with no name against them, both of them about seven years back, both of them stone. That is the whole of what is in a ledger in this yard that has never once been read, and in nineteen years I have never had a man stand at that counter and ask me what either page says.”

## The Only Thing Anybody Here Can Do To It

“You can add to it,” said Lin Hao.

“You can add to it,” said Sander Vole. “That is the whole of the trade and it is the whole of everything. A counter figure goes up and it cannot be taken down, and there is no page in this yard where a figure comes off, and there is no man in this city who could come in here and strike a line through a figure in a book that has been shut in a drawer in a counter.”

“Then it has never been wrong.”

“It has never been found wrong,” said Sander Vole, “which is not the same and I have known that since the second year and I have said so out loud about nine hundred times since and I have said it out loud on more mornings than I have been able to count, and you have heard me say it on four of them, and it is the only thing about that ledger I have ever been sure of.”

He put the book away under the counter and it went in the way a book goes in that has been going in the same place for nineteen years.

“Now tell me what you came for,” he said, “because you have stood there twice in seven months and asked me about a figure both times and this time you have not asked me about a figure once.”

## What He Asked For

“I would like the docket,” said Lin Hao.

Sander Vole did not move for about four seconds, and then he took the box out from under the counter with his foot and set it on the wood.

It is about four thousand of them and they are in the order of the gate, which means the order things left this yard, and there is no other way of finding any of them and there never has been and there is no card index anywhere in this city for a stone yard.

“No,” he said.

“You have not said why.”

“I am going to say why and I am going to say it before I do it and not after, and then I am going to stop and you are going to let me stop, and that is the arrangement I have with about four hundred men who come through that gate in a year,” said Sander Vole.

“A docket says a thing left here and went somewhere. It is in the hand of the man who carried it and not the hand of the man who cut it. There is no figure on it, there never has been, and a figure goes in the book.”

“There is a line on it with two words against it.”

“There is a line on it with two words against it and a date at the head of it and the date is older than anything else in this yard including the yard,” said Sander Vole.

“And nobody has asked me for that paper in thirty years. Not the landlord who got it, not the builder who laid it, not the man who had the wall taken down in the spring and made good, and not you, and you have been the only person in seven years who has ever come near it and you came twice and asked me about a figure.”

“That is fair.”

“Now I am going to give you one thing and I am going to be exactly straight about it, and then you will not be able to put it anywhere and I will not be able to take it back, and both of us will be where we were on the inside of it.”

## Nine Inches From The Top

He went through about four inches of them and stopped and pulled one out and put it down on the bench.

A sheet of paper folded once. A place, a thing, a number of pieces, and a line running the width of the paper with two words against the end of it in an ordinary working pen under a heading in the same upright hand.

The two words were the two words at the foot of the wall.

And at the head of it, in the same upright hand, there was a date.

Lin Hao did not touch the paper and did not look at the date and did not ask for it to be read out, and Sander Vole watched him not do all three of those things and then said:

“You have had that date in front of you twice and you have walked away from it twice and you are not going to take it now.”

“No.”

“I am not going to make you,” said Sander Vole. “It is yours or it is not and I have carried that piece of paper for thirty years and I have never once been asked anything about it and I am not going to be the man who makes a clerk of somebody else.”

He picked it up off the bench and did not hand it over; he held it for about four seconds with the fold still in it, and then he squared it against the edge of the bench and put it into Lin Hao’s coat pocket himself, in the inside, where a man keeps a thing.

“Now listen to what that costs, because I have been thinking about it since the tenth of September and you are the first person I have been able to say it to.”

## What It Closes And What It Costs

“The box is in the order of the gate,” said Sander Vole.

“That is the only index this yard has and it has never been a card index and it never will be. A docket goes in about nine inches from the top on the day it comes back off a barrow and it goes in where it belongs in the order. There is no number on the front of one. There is no mark.”

“A man who wants a paper in this yard puts his hand in and feels along until he finds the month.”

He put his hand into the box and felt along it for about two seconds.

“There is a gap,” he said. “It is about two inches long and it is in the ninth year of a run and there is nothing on either side of it and in about four years nobody will be able to say what was in it unless somebody kept a list, and this yard has never kept a list of anything, because a yard is where stone goes and not where paper is remembered.”

“That is a counter figure,” said Lin Hao. “It can be added to. It cannot be taken from.”

“It is not a counter figure,” said Sander Vole. “It is a hole in a box of paper. And you have put it there, and I have put it there, and neither of us can put it back, and the figure on that page in the book does not move and will not move, and no book in this city will ever say that a piece of paper left this yard this afternoon.”

He shut the box and put it back under the counter.

“There is no office that sends a paper to a stone yard and there is not going to be one, and you have never once in seven months said the word office to me at that counter, and I have noticed that too.”

“All right.”

“I am not asking for anything and I am not being thanked and I am not going to be,” said Sander Vole.

“What I am going to say is the one thing I have had to say since the second year and this is the first time there has been anybody in the room to say it to. In thirty years there has not been one word cut on the face of a block that went out of this gate and came back with a word on it that said so, and that is not because nobody has done it.”

“It is because a man on a bench wrote it down on a bit of paper so that whoever got it would not send it back, and he was right and it worked, and the only person in this city who ever read it was me.”

He looked at the inside of Lin Hao’s coat for about a second.

“Do not come back for the rest of it,” he said.

“You have not asked me who and you have not asked me the date and I have noticed both of those and I am going to go on noticing them.”

“There is a wall in this city with four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of it and two words cut clean through the face of it, and I know where they came from and I have never said, and you have not asked me twice, and that is going to have to be good enough for both of us for the rest of the year.”

*Two thousand four hundred and seventy-six days. Less two thousand two hundred and sixty-eight, the nineteenth of May of the seventh year, is two hundred and eight days, which is six months with twenty-eight days after them, and a piece of paper about the size of a sheet folded once has been in a box under a counter in a yard nine streets from a works department for thirty years and nobody had ever asked for it until this afternoon.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is seventy-seven days, which is two months with seventeen days after them, and the twenty-seventh sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for all of them with three lines in the second column of it and eight hundred and fifty-two at the head.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year, is three hundred and ninety-eight days, which is thirteen months with eight days after them, and less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is seven hundred and fifty-eight days, which is twenty-five months with eight days after them.*

*The figure against that course of dressed stone has no name against it, has not moved in seven years, and was not printed this afternoon, and nobody in this city can take anything off it. There is now a hole in a box of about four thousand dockets about two inches long in the ninth year of a run, and it can be added to and it cannot be taken from, and there is no form anywhere for putting a word on a stone.*

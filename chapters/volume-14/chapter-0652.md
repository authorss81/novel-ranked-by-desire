# A Counter Under A Window

Seven days had gone since a copy of the foot of a sheet came through a front door with a date and a place in two inches of nothing.

Two thousand one hundred and sixty was the first of February and the eighth is day two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven, and two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven less two thousand one hundred and sixty is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

The passage off Cordwain was narrow at about eleven in the morning, with shoulders going past all day, and the desk under the window stood at the height of a man’s chest with a sheet lying out on it and a rail in front of it, and the corner of that sheet was rubbed round where hands went past.

Lin Hao stood a while before he went in, because he had never spoken to the man at that desk and did not know how to begin, and what he wanted was simple and could not be said simply, which was to find out who wanted a copy.

## The Man With The Pencil

The man at the desk had run it nine years.

He had a pencil and a ledger with about thirty names in it and no form about anything, and a sheet that had lain out since the first of March of the second year, and about a hundred people in a month put a hand on the corner of it and not one had got to the ninth line, and he had asked nothing about any of them, because asking what a sheet was for had never been his work.

“You wrote the date down,” said Lin Hao.

“I write every date down,” said the man. “A man came at the end of September and asked at the counter about a sheet, and I wrote the date instead of saying no, because saying no is a thing I do not do at a public desk.”

“What did he ask.”

“He asked whether he might have a copy of it,” said the man. “I told him that sheet is not mine to copy and there is no instrument here for reproducing anything, and he went away, and the next morning that sheet came back with a copy of the foot of it on the back in somebody else’s handwriting, and I did not make it and I do not know who did.”

Lin Hao felt the specific humiliation of having handed somebody the reason, low in his throat, because the sentence at the foot of that sheet was his own, put there with a man in a delivery coat’s name under it, and it said in other words what he had written to stop a city believing a claim it could not check.

A thing made by a hand that is not the office’s hand is a thing somebody put together on purpose, and a copy is a thing you can only obey, and he had given Ostara the words for throwing away any figure it liked.

“You have been entitled to one question since the twenty-third of December,” said Lin Hao, because the counterman had told him so in September and he had remembered it the way he remembered every rule that bound another man.

“I have,” said the man. “One question and not two. I have not spent it.”

“Why not.”

“Because I have run a public counter nine years and I have never once asked anybody what a sheet was for,” said the man, “and a question spent is a question gone.”

He laid his hand on the ledger without opening it.

Lin Hao looked at the sheet on the desk, and at the rail, and at the rubbed corner, and he understood that about forty had come into that passage in the last month, and that the man had been counting things coming in for nine years without knowing what he was counting.

## The Question Spent Wrong

“What do you want to ask,” said Lin Hao.

The man smiled, tired and unembarrassed.

“I am going to spend it wrong,” he said. “On purpose. I have had six weeks to think about it and wrong is the only spending that leaves me where I am.”

“Say it.”

“How many of these are there,” said the man.

Lin Hao stared at him.

“Not who made the copy,” said the man. “Not why. How many. Because asking what a sheet is for is a different question from asking who made a copy of it, and I am entitled to one of them, and I am asking the one that lets me go on running this desk.”

Lin Hao told him what he knew, which was that about forty had come through in the last month and about nine of them were made by hand, and the man wrote nothing down, because writing down a number at a public desk would have made it a figure, and he did not deal in figures.

“A sentence is a thing you can argue with,” said the man, looking at the sheet. “A copy is a thing you can only obey. About nine people made one in a month and nobody ordered it.”

He was right, and Lin Hao felt anger come up under the humiliation, clean and cold, at himself and not at the man, because the man had done nothing but run a desk honestly nine years.

“I want a copy of my own,” said the man. “Not of the foot. Of the sheet. To keep under the desk. So that when somebody brings one back I know what they brought.”

Lin Hao gave him the sheet from under his arm, the twenty-fourth, folded once, and the man laid it beside the one on the desk without touching the rail, and thanked him by nodding, and neither of them said anything about thanks.

On the way out Lin Hao stopped at the mouth of the passage and looked back at the desk under the window, small and exact in the light, and what he wanted, fiercely and at once, was to take back the sentence he had written with his own hand, and he knew as he wanted it that a sentence gone out four times a year cannot be taken back.

He went home with his hands in his pockets and the cold on his face, and the want stayed with him all the way.

*Two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven days. Less two thousand one hundred and sixty, the first of February of year seven, is seven. Eight less one is seven by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*That sheet has lain out since the first of March of the second year at a desk under a window in a passage off Cordwain, and the man at it has run that desk nine years with a pencil and a ledger of about thirty names and no form about anything.*

# A Counter Under A Window

Seven days had gone since a sheet of paper had come through the front door of a building with no door onto a street, and a man in a delivery coat had carried it there from a desk under a window, and there had been a month and a back lane in pencil in the two inches under a striking.

Two thousand one hundred and sixty was the first of February and the eighth is day two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven, and two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven less two thousand one hundred and sixty is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

The desk was off Cordwain in a passage and it had a counter with a window on it and a rail on the counter, and a sheet had lain out on that rail since the first of March of a year nobody at the desk mentions.

About a hundred people a month put a hand on the corner of that sheet and go round it to get past the counter, because the counter is in the way and the sheet is at the height of a man’s chest.

Not one of them in about four years had got to the ninth line.

## The Man Who Runs It

He had run that desk about nine years and he had a pencil and a ledger with about thirty names in it and no form about anything whatsoever, and he had been asked exactly one question about a sheet on that rail since the twenty-third of December and he had not spent it.

“What do you want,” he said.

“I want to know who wants a copy.”

“You have come to a counter under a window to find out who wants a copy of a sheet that is lying out on my rail.”

“Yes.”

“Then you have come to the right counter,” said the man at the desk, “and I have a pencil and about thirty names in a ledger and no standing, and that is the whole of what I have got, and I am telling you the whole of it before you ask me anything so that you cannot say afterwards that I offered it.”

“I am not going to ask you for a copy.”

He said it before he knew he was going to say it, which was the second time in a week that something had come out of him ahead of the decision.

“No,” said the man at the desk. “You have not, and you were not going to, and you have come all this way to find out who does, which means you have already worked out that there are some.”

“About forty sheets came through that front door in a month,” said Lin Hao. “About nine of them were made by hand.”

“You know that.”

“A man at a desk keeps a pencil ledger and about thirty names. I am asking whether you have a column for it.”

“I do not have a column for it,” said the man at the desk. “I have thirty names and the dates people asked me things and about nine things I have written down since the thirtieth of September because I had a pencil and nothing else to do with it.”

## Why He Could Not Have Given One

He wanted to explain the refusal and he had to be careful about it for about nine seconds.

There is a sentence at the foot of that sheet, third of four, in the office’s hand, with a man in a delivery coat’s name under it and not his, and it says in other words that any number a person sets down in this city may be one they made up and there is no instrument behind it. He had written it. It had gone out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter since the ninth of November of a year he does not name in a street.

“If I copied that sheet,” he said, “anybody could take it and drop any line out of it, or put a line in, and nothing anywhere in this city could tell them apart. That is the reason I am not going to have it copied and it is the reason that sentence is at the foot of the sheet.”

“That is a good reason.”

“It is a warning.”

“It is not a warning,” said the man at the desk. “I read all four of them out loud to about nine people in a fortnight in the autumn and a woman asked me what a warning was for and I told her the same thing a man in a delivery coat told me, and I have never worked out whether he was right and I have had about two months to work out whether he was right.”

“What did he tell you.”

“That it stopped being a warning the first time somebody read it out loud to another person,” said the man at the desk, “and that is the only sentence I have got about any of it, and I have got about thirty names and it is the best of the lot.”

## The Question He Was Entitled To

“You have a question,” said Lin Hao.

“I have a question,” said the man at the desk. “Every man behind a public counter in this city has one, and he may ask it once, and then he has not got one any more for as long as he stands behind that counter, and I have not spent mine and I have had it since the twenty-third of December.”

“What is it.”

“That is the question.”

He put the pencil in the drawer and shut it, which is the thing a man at a counter does, and he does it about twice a day.

“I am not going to tell you what it is,” he said, “and I am not going to let you tell me what to ask either, because a question a man is given is not his question, it is the office’s, and this counter has nothing to do with any office in this city.”

“All right.”

“You can stay there or you can go,” said the man at the desk, “and if you stay there you will not get it out of me this morning, and you should know that before you decide.”

He decided.

Lin Hao said, “Then I am going to spend your question and I am going to spend it wrong, on purpose, and I am going to tell you that I am doing it before I do it.”

## How Many Of These Are There

The man at the desk turned the ledger round so that the top of it showed, and wrote four lines in it, and squared it, and left the pencil on top of the page.

“There,” he said. “That is the question as it is going to be asked, and I have written it out so that I ask it as it is given, because a counter takes what it is given and does not improve it.”

He read it out.

“How many of these are there.”

“That is the whole of it,” he said. “I have not spent my question on who wrote the one in your building and I have not spent it on what is in it, and I have spent it on a number, and I have never in nine years asked anybody a number about anything that came through that door.”

“You are going to regret that.”

“I have run this counter about nine years without asking one question at all,” said the man at the desk, “and I have got a ledger with thirty names in it, and if I had spent it on the man instead of the number I would have a person in here instead of a quantity, and I could not use either, and the quantity is the one I can use.”

## What Came Back On The Rail

The man answered it from about nine of the counters he has never been inside.

Forty-one sheets had gone into that front door in a month. Twenty-nine of them had a form on them and an address at the top and were going somewhere to be dealt with by somebody.

Twelve of them were the foot of that page, written out longhand, in pencil, in twelve different hands, and every one of them had something written in the two inches, and nine of the twelve had nothing in the two inches but a date and a place.

He asked how he knew.

“I do not know,” said the man at the desk. “I asked the girl on the second counter and the girl on the second counter said nine, and the girl on the second counter has been on that counter about six years and does not say nine about anything that is not nine.”

“Who made them.”

“Anybody who comes through that door with a pencil.”

Nobody ordered them. That was the part he came away with.

A sentence is a thing you can argue with, and a copy is a thing you can only obey, and he had gone six miles and had not found out who the obedient ones were.

## The Next Morning

He came back at about eleven on the ninth because there was nothing else to do with the morning.

The sheet that had lain out on that rail since the first of March of the second year had been taken up to a counter off a third court in the morning by a clerk who does that four times a month and put back on the rail on the same afternoon, and it came back with something on the back of it.

A hand had copied the whole of the foot onto the back. Four sentences. Six lines and a strike through one of them. A name under the striking. And in the two inches, in a different pencil, a different month and a different lane.

The man at the desk turned it round and looked at it and then turned it back.

“That was not me,” he said.

“I know it was not you.”

“That is the first time a person has known that about anything on that counter,” said the man at the desk, “and I would like it recorded that I have not got a pencil for that hand and no name for it and no way of stopping it, and that it is the best thing that has happened at that counter in nine years, and that I cannot say a single word of it to anybody who will do anything with it.”

A sentence is a thing you can argue with.

A copy is a thing you can only obey, and about nine people made one in a month, and nobody ordered it.

*Two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven days. Less two thousand one hundred and sixty, the first of February of the seventh year, is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is four hundred and forty-nine days, which is fourteen months with twenty-nine days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of a sheet and it is in the office’s hand with a name under it that is not the office’s.*

*It lets anybody in this city throw away any number they like, and about nine people in a month have used it to make a thing that cannot be thrown away.*

# The Wall Says It Again

Beatrix Halm read the plate at a quarter to six in the morning on the thirtieth of November in the dark, with the two words going blue behind her, and she wrote it down twice before she spoke.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one."

Thirteen under the floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-four. The lowest figure that had ever been under his name on a wall in this city, and seven below the number that had stood there all through October, and fifty-three below the green.

She wrote the number with the time against it, and then the two words under it, and then she shut the notebook and stood with her hands round the pencil.

"They have had two days with those words up," she said. "They have got bored of them. It is not news any more. It is a fact about a street and they have moved on to the weather and the lamps going on at four, and that is worse than when they said it out loud the first time. A word people say out loud is still a word about a man."

## Five Mornings On A Platform

"You said you would start on the twenty-fourth."

"I started on the twenty-fourth." She took the pencil out from behind her ear and looked at it instead of at him. "Five mornings. Eight women. I took them one at a time on the platform where I work and I did it in the ordinary way and I did not tell any of them what I was doing except the first, and the first was you, and you said you did nothing."

"What did you get."

"Nothing," said Beatrix Halm. "Not a flicker. Not one of the eight gives a grain to anybody in that building. There is not a well in this house. Petra lifts sixty pounds of flour four times a day and she gets nothing out of it and Nadia goes up and down in a cage all day and gets nothing, and our Nadia has never once felt a cold minute in her life because a lift does not do that to her."

"So there is no source in the building."

"There is no source in the building," she said, "and I have been wrong every morning for five days and I told you on the twenty-first that I would start and I have started and I have finished. Put it down that I finished. Put it down that I am not going to keep looking at the eight of them again."

"Beatrix."

"I am going to carry it," she said. "Not the hole. The hole is a shape and shapes are not mine to close. But for five days I was going to find one of my own and take it to a doctor, and I have not found her, and I am going to have that on me for the rest of my life, and it is a better thing to carry than nine years of your plate."

## The Count Again

At seven he said it out loud in the kitchen with the tea going round, because on the nineteenth of November a doctor had ruled eleven dates on a slip in a clinic corridor and read them back to him in a voice that had to be heard to be worth anything, and the rule for this household had been set before he came up the stairs that morning.

"The twenty-ninth," he said. "The twenty-eighth. The twenty-seventh. The twenty-sixth. The twenty-fifth. The twenty-fourth. The twenty-third. The twenty-second. The twenty-first. The twentieth. The nineteenth."

"Eleven," said Sun Meiling, who had been listening to him say dates out loud since the third of October. "And it ended at midnight last night."

The kitchen let it stand.

"I can spend again," he said. "That is what midnight last night bought. It has been eleven days since the last time I could have given anybody anything and there is no reason on the face of the earth why I should not walk into the Vestry yard this morning and finish it."

"I did not put a hand on one person in any of the eleven, and on the last of them I stood in a room for a dressing on a table with her leg open and I did not put a hand on her then either."

"Why are you not going to."

"Because the eleventh is not a rule that has ended," he said. "It was a number that a doctor had, and the number was about a body and not about me. The moment she is out of that bed she is a woman who has felt four cold minutes and written them on her hand, and if I put my hand on her now she writes a fifth, and there is a doctor in this city who knows about the other four."

"And in about four years somebody draws a line from a date on a hand to an address."

Nobody said anything.

Sun Meiling set the cup down.

"Then what good was the eleven days," she said.

"They were eleven days," he said. "They are counted and they are over and I did them. That is all any of this has ever been."

## A Drawer On Lantern Row

The description of premises 34-6-118 was open in a drawer in two rooms on Lantern Row, under an auditor's note in a small level hand that said four sheets, read on the morning of the twenty-eighth at nine.

No office in this city knew it was there. It was not a copy; it was the original, out of a wallet, out of a file, out of a sub-level, and it had come up sixty-one steps and then up two flights of a stair that smells of soap and heat, and a woman had put it in a drawer and shut it and written down what she had done, and that was the whole of the price and the whole of the payment.

He was not at rank seven this morning and had not been for a week, and there was no word on any wall in the square about him except two words at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one.

## Nine O'Clock

Wren Kallo came up the sixty-one at about twenty past nine with a leather folder under her arm and her hat in her hand, and not one of the steps made a sound under her, as never had.

She put the folder on the table and opened it and took out a single sheet.

"Eight," she said. "The composition of premises 34-6-118, at nine o'clock on the thirtieth of November, certified by me, in the presence of nobody, in my own division, at the address, which is the address, and which the sheet has said out loud in four words at the top of it."

She put it down flat. "It is the cleanest document in this city about this house. There is no dispute about the figure. There is no date in it that could be argued with."

"I have made a false statement of my own address in a public book this morning in order to make that true, and my division will find out about it in about four years, and the answer to why will be a line written in a kitchen in eight hands on the twenty-fourth of October, which is exactly the way I designed it in a kitchen on the twenty-eighth of October, and I have not changed the design since."

"Wren—"

"There is nothing about that address I am permitted to be asked," said Wren Kallo.

Then she took a second sheet out of the folder and put it beside the first.

"This is the one I told you about," she said. "The yard slip of the fifth with four witnesses on the back, written out a second time on the morning of the twenty-seventh, unsigned and not true, filed in the ordinary way in the standing papers for this morning's certification. I certified the composition of this address at nine o'clock with that sheet eight inches from my left hand."

The kitchen went completely still.

"I have had five days," said Wren Kallo. "I decided on the twenty-eighth that I would burn it at home in a grate, and I decided on the twenty-ninth that I would take it out of the file myself and destroy it in the office, and I have had a desk in a division of the Bureau of Merit for eleven years and there is a rule about destruction of papers that would have taken me nine months to answer honestly, and I have decided to leave it."

"You cannot leave it."

"I cannot leave it," she said, "and I cannot remove it and I cannot explain it and I cannot tell a single person in this building that I have decided to leave it."

"The reason I have decided to leave it is that if I destroy a paper I do not like, then a man who keeps things can be found out, and I have watched one man keep things for five years and it has cost eight women a list and a woman her keys and a woman her office and a woman her leg and I have decided, tonight, that the city is not entitled to one more destroyed document with his handwriting anywhere near it."

She squared the two sheets together.

"That is the whole reason and I am aware it is a stupid reason and I have made it mine."

She shut the folder and picked up her hat.

"You are not going to ask me a question about this address," she said, "so I am going to go, and the certificate is on your table, and the other thing is in my division's file, and on the first of December there is a valuation at ten to nine and a mark entered by a pen on a chain, and I have no standing in it and no business there and I am going to be at my desk by eight."

She went down the sixty-one and the stair took her weight without a sound.

## Eleven O'Clock In The Morning

He went down to the square at about eleven and stood at the north end of the wall in full daylight, in front of about nine people who were not waiting for anybody, and read the figure out loud once, slowly, the way he had been taught to read a wall at a quarter to six in the dark.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one."

He said it in daylight, and out loud, at the volume she had used in the dark, because a wall is read whole and he had been taught that at a quarter to six.

Then he said the two words.

"Out of band."

Nine people heard a stranger say out loud that a man under a number at premises 34-6-118 was not in a band, and not a soul there turned to look at the man under it, and that was the other thing that had changed since the fifteenth of November and would not change back.

He stood there for about four minutes with his hands in his pockets while the lamps of the afternoon came on in the wrong order, and then he went up the hill to Ferrier Street, where eight women were at a table with a certificate on it, and where on the first of December at ten to nine a pen on a chain was going to write something in a real book about him, and where nothing at all in the world was going to be any use.

# Eight Women On One Line Of Type

On the morning of the twenty-ninth of November the kitchen at Ferrier Street was laid for eight and the eleventh day of eleven was running, and he put both hands flat on the table and told them what a document in a sub-level in another part of the city said about the eight of them.

He said it in the plainest words he had. That there was a sheet. That it had a date on it and a figure and a reason. That it had been made on the twenty-seventh of October against him as the head of the premises, and not against the address, and that he had asked for it to be made against him, in front of all of them and a clerk, and had given the reason in four words about money.

"That is the whole of it," he said. "There is nothing in it about any of you except that you are on it. It does not say what any of you is. It says you keep an address here, in the order you keep it, with a date and a room against each line."

"It went out on the ordinary stream at four on the thirtieth of October and it cannot be taken back. It says eight and the priced line says seven and neither one of them corrects the other. And I did not want it done to a house and I had it done to me."

Not one of them spoke for about nine seconds, which in that kitchen was a long time.

## Eight Women And A Sheet

Sun Meiling took her glasses off.

"You have not told us what it costs," she said. "You said what it is. In four sentences on the evening of the twenty-sixth you told me you were going to be able to give a person nothing at all for eleven days, and not one of us asked you a question, and you have been grateful for that every day since, and you have not once told us where the eleven days were going."

"The cost is a doctor's frame," he said. "And a woman's leg. Both of them are named and neither of them is in this house."

"You did not tell us because you did not want us to vote on it."

"Yes."

"That is the correct answer and I would have given it myself," said Sun Meiling. "Put your glasses back on and eat something."

Runa Sato turned her cup round twice.

"The man in the hall writes in a book on any working day," she said, "and on the twelfth of this month I am going to be the woman who hands a Bureau officer a sheet with eight names on it and certifies the composition of this building."

"He asked me, in writing, with his name at the top of a column, and the price is his division, and he has not asked me one question about it in the whole of the time. Yesterday I wrote in a report that said the terms of the certification were proper and were observed, and I wrote the word *proper* at half past nine in the evening and I have not been able to get it out from under my hand."

"It is proper."

"It is proper," she said, "and I am going to certify a house I live in on the day after you finish eleven days of not spending to keep a bin woman alive, and both of those things are going in the same month of the same year, and no clerk will ever see that they belong together. I am the one who will see it. It is enough."

Petra Ilyin said nothing at all for the first half hour, and then she said something that moved the room.

"Lin Hao," she said. "I will write it."

"Write what."

"Anything you want on that wall." She had her forearms on the table and she had the flat unhurried voice of a woman who has enforced eleven years of other people's undertakings and has never once been asked to enforce her own. "There is a form and there are three columns and a woman at the basket counter writes down what a person tells her, and a yard clerk does the same with witnesses on the back."

"You do not have to have done anything. You have to have been standing somewhere when something finished, and every week for eleven years there has been something in this district finishing. I can write four slips before the month is out with my name at the foot of all four and I will not even have to lie much. I will write that I saw it. I saw it. I see you every day."

"No."

"You reached seven off three women who wrote down what they actually saw," said Petra Ilyin, "and you have been seven for a fortnight and you are under the floor now, and every one of those three is carrying your name in a book with a date on it and a woman of the laundry told you herself that she would do it again and that it had got her nothing."

"I am offering to put four more slips in the books of this district with a name on them that is the name of the woman who has been under a promise for eleven years and cannot remember who put it there. That is the best I have. It is the only thing anybody in this room has ever been able to give you that I have not already given somebody else."

The kitchen had gone very quiet.

"No," he said.

"You have refused a wall since the thirtieth of July," said Petra Ilyin. "You have refused it for five years on the grounds that every advantage feels like theft from somebody smaller. There are eight women in this room and not one of them is smaller than a slip with her name on it."

"I will not take four false slips off a woman I have known for eleven years. Not this month, not in December, not on the first, when the account gets called and I will need every name in this room to still be worth reading."

Petra Ilyin looked at him for a while, and then she pushed the three sheets of paper she had written in her apron across the table.

"Those are not signed," she said. "I wrote them before breakfast and I have not shown them to anybody. Do what you want with them."

## The Fire On The Dresser

Sun Meiling took them off the table, read all three the way she reads everything, folded them once, and put them into the fire on the dresser.

"That is a document being destroyed in a room with eight witnesses," said Wren Kallo, from the second floor side of the table, without looking up, "and I am a Bureau officer, and I have not said one word about it, and if any of you imagine that was a thought in my head, then I have been in this building for three years and you have not been listening."

"We are not listening, Wren," said Sun Meiling. She said it on purpose, which is what she meant.

"I am listening to the fire," said Wren Kallo.

Agnete Lindqvist pushed her chair back from the table for the first time in an hour.

"There is a room in this city where nobody can be compelled to testify," she said. "It has been true for eleven years and I have not been out of it."

"He has never asked me for it. I have offered him the use of a room in which any officer of any division could stand in the doorway and get nothing out of anybody inside at all, and he has never once asked, because he does not believe a thing he cannot pay for, and he is the only man I have met in eleven years who has had a free thing put in front of him and refused it on principle."

She turned to him.

"You have told us nothing and we have let you tell us nothing and that is what this table is for and you have never once been the man who asked us for more than we offered. When it goes wrong, which it will, and when a man comes up those sixty-one steps with a book, you bring him here first. That is all. Do not bring him here first, and this table is a list of eight lines."

Nadia Ferrous had not said anything for an hour and said it now, looking at him and not at anybody else.

"You have been eleven days without touching anybody," she said. "I have counted them. I counted the first one the night you told us and I have counted every night since and I have not missed one and I have not asked you for one. I would like that said at this table by somebody who is not me."

"Nadia," said Sun Meiling.

"No. He has not touched one of us for eleven days and there is a woman at the Vestry with her leg in plaster and none of us did that and all of us made him do it by saying nothing for eleven days. I am not being clever. I want it in the room."

He put his hand flat on the table, palm down, which is what he had been doing with his hands for eleven days in four hundred places, and put it away again before anybody could mistake it.

## What Wren Kallo Had Done

"Since you are all going to talk about what everybody did this morning," said Wren Kallo, "I am going to tell you what I did, because I cannot sit at a table with a thing in me and let it get to the nineteenth of December on its own."

She took a folded sheet out of her coat and put it on the table, and squared it, and did not open it.

"That is a copy," she said. "It is the yard slip from the fifth of November, with four witnesses on the back of it, and I wrote a second one out on the morning of the twenty-seventh, and it is not signed, and it is not true, and it is in my division's file under the standing papers for the certification of the composition of this address tomorrow morning."

The table did not move.

"I did it at about seven in the morning in the office, in my own hand, on my own time, and I filed it in the ordinary way, and there is no falsification of any column because every column in it is accurate," said Wren Kallo.

"The date is accurate. The premises are accurate. The deed is accurate. It is simply not a deed." She squared the sheet. "And nothing has to be done with it. It can sit in a file for four years and never once be looked at, and a paper nobody ever looks at is only a sheet of paper. If anybody ever looks at it, then it is a false attestation in the hand of a counter-forensics officer who sleeps at the address she certified."

"You said you would not ask me for a thing," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is a lie of about nine seconds old."

"I have not asked him for anything and I will not," said Wren Kallo. "I am not asking him for anything. I did it and I am telling the table and the table can do what it likes."

She looked at him.

"You are not permitted to ask me a question about this address and I am not waiting to be asked one," she said. "But I would like it observed that I did that at seven in the morning on the twenty-seventh and I decided tonight not to, and that is the whole of the difference between me and the woman at the basket counter, and I do not know yet which of us is right."

Eleventh day. Midday.

He stood at the table with eight women looking at a folded sheet, and worked out that nothing in this city could get that paper out of the file it was in, and that everything which could was his to use and belonged to somebody else.

# The Answer He Did Not Swear

He got to the district house at about nine on the nineteenth of October, and the young man at the premises counter was the same one, and the same quarter was open in front of him, and he looked up before Lin Hao reached the grille.

"You again."

"I have a correction."

"You had one on the sixth and you went up six floors and came back down with nothing." The clerk put a hand flat on the leaf. "Counter, or up there?"

"Here."

The form is one sheet and it is the shortest instrument he has ever signed. Four boxes. The premises number is printed. The date goes in the second. The third asks for a figure. The fourth asks, in eleven words, which person's holding is to be corrected.

He entered the date.

He entered eight in the third box.

He read the fourth box four times and left it empty.

"Box four."

"Box four."

"You have got a figure and no subject." The clerk tipped his head very slightly, which is what he did over the June leaf in June. "I cannot take that. A correction that does not say whose holding is corrected is not a correction. The book takes one figure and a name."

"I know what it is."

"Then give me the name."

Lin Hao put the pen down on the counter and did not pick it up for about eleven seconds.

"There is a woman at that address who asked me on the ninth of July, in writing, in her own hand, never to be written down," he said. "She is not at those premises and she is not on that leaf and she is not one of the eight."

The clerk's hand stayed off the pen.

"She asked you not to be written down."

"She asked to be left out of an instrument, and I promised her, and she paid for that promise herself in August by putting her own address into four divisions with her own hand, and I did not stop her because I could not have." He kept his voice where a man keeps it in a queue. "So I am not saying her name in a room with a clerk in it." 

"If you say it, it goes on a chained page in a keeper's room in the ordinary way, and there is no way to lift it off again, and the account stays open a good deal longer than you or I will." He looked at the fourth box. "If I do not say it, I cannot finish what I came to do, and you have just told me at that grille that a correction without a subject is not a correction."

The young man wrote for about nine seconds.

The whole of the district premises office could hear a nib on a counter on a street with trams on it.

"I am going to write that you were asked for the name and did not give it."

"Write that."

"That goes onto the leaf, with the date, and you know it as well as I do."

"I know it as well as you do. Write it."

The nib went. He read it back to himself and did not read it out loud, which was the single most decent thing anybody did that morning.

"That is four figures for one door," he said, "in two books and a chained book and now a refusal. Somebody in about four years is going to have to explain that to somebody."

"It will not be you and it will not be me."

"No. It will be an office." The clerk squared the form. "And the office will do it in one line, and the one line will say that the head of the premises was not prepared to identify the subject of his own correction."

He flattened the sheet on the counter with his palm and kept his palm on it.

"Where does it go?"

"In the tray, because it is a form and I cannot keep it." He pushed the tray forward an inch. "It is the ordinary way, and I have eight of those to get through by twelve."

## The Second Sub-Level

He went down the two flights at about half past eleven and the runner knew his face and did not ask for a slip.

Alma Quayle was in the corridor with the stool out and her coat over the rail, and there was a cloth on a table with a hand at the far end of it, and she had been there long enough to have a cup.

"Sit down on the bench, or stand up."

"I have brought you something."

"I can see that you have." She set the cup down. "You have stood in this corridor twice since the spring and both times you went away with less than you came with, and today you are carrying something, so I am going to guess it first and you can correct me."

"Say it."

"You have referred it."

"I have referred it."

The corridor went on being a corridor.

"Say the three parts," said Alma Quayle. "If you have referred it you have had to put three things on it, and I want to hear you say all three in one breath before I take a pen out."

"The address. Thirty-four six one one eighteen, Ferrier Street, and the district number under it, which I got at a counter on the sixth because a man on a third floor told me to go and ask for it."

He had it written on a card and did not need the card.

"The figure is eight. Eight is on a district leaf that opened on the first of this month. I will swear to that composition in front of you in any room in this building you care to name, because it is true."

"And the reason."

"The reason is a song," he said. "It went into nine hundred mouths on the fifteenth and it is on about four hundred of them now and it cannot be pulled back, and nobody who is singing it has been paid, and none of them was asked to learn it." He stopped. "I am not putting the singer's name on a piece of paper in this city either."

Alma Quayle wrote nothing.

"You have referred an account with an address and a figure and a reason, and two of the three are paper and the third is a tune, and no officer in this building has ever received that. It is valid and I am taking it. I am not going to ask you to write the words down, because you cannot sing them."

"You cannot sing either."

"I cannot sing," she said. "I have sat at the back of a room with four men in it who could, twice, and understood about a fifth of what was going on." She opened the book on the stool. "Now say the other thing, because you have said three sentences and you came down those stairs and you are still standing there."

## The Reason He Gave Out Loud

"There is a correction open to me and you know there is, because I told you about it in this corridor nine days ago, and so do I."

"Correct."

"It is the only instrument in this city that can move a number off a district leaf, it has to be in my own hand, and it would take me about nine seconds, which is the figure it has taken me twice before."

"You swore the second one on the first of October. Nine seconds both times, and a keeper told you at the mark that she keeps a list of habits in the margin of her own day book."

She had not looked at him.

"You have given me that number twice since the spring. I would rather have it once in a form that is yours."

"I said nine seconds twice, at a chained book at the Exchange, on the first of August and again on the first of October, and both of them were seven. A woman who had never met me told me a quarter of an hour later that she keeps a list of what people do twice in a margin of her own."

He put a hand flat on his knee.

"She was right. It is a habit. The third one is eight, it will be the third time in three months, and everybody who ever opens that chained book will count them."

"So you are not going to make the third one."

"I am going to make the third one," said Lin Hao, "and it is going to be eight, and it is going to carry a woman's name, and I am not going to do it."

Alma Quayle put the pen down.

"Then say the reason out loud, once, in the plainest words you have got, because in about four years somebody is going to look at a refusal and there will have to be something at the foot of it that a stranger can read."

"There is a woman in this city who asked me in writing on the ninth of July never to be written down," he said. "She keeps no address here. Her name is not on the district's leaf and she is not one of the eight. She has been the reason for about half of everything I have done for four years, and not one building in this city has ever known there was a reason."

"If I swear a corrected composition of eight, her name goes onto a chained page in a keeper's room and goes out on the ordinary stream the next working morning, and it will be in four divisions inside a week, and it will be sitting there for as long as the account is open."

"And that is the reason."

"That is the reason and it is not all of it." He looked at the floor. "The rest is that I will not say her name in a room with a clerk in it, and I will not ask her to come here and say it for herself. And I cannot complete a correction without saying it."

The runner went past at the far end of the corridor and neither of them looked at him.

"That is all of it," Lin Hao said. "It has been said once and it is not being said twice."

## What It Cost

Alma Quayle wrote for the length of a page and let him watch and did not try to keep him from it, which was a decision.

"I am not going to argue with you, and I want you to hear that it is not politeness." She did not look up.

"There is nothing in thirty years in this office that lets me argue with a man who has just told me a true thing about a woman and a sheet of paper."

"You have turned down the one instrument that would have saved you and the turning down has cost you the cleanest answer you have ever given anybody. You knew the price of it in this corridor nine days ago. You have gone on saying no for nine days."

"Then put it down."

"I am putting it down. What goes down is the referral, not the refusal."

She turned the page round.

"It says: an unrecorded account standing against no ledger, carried at premises thirty-four six one one eighteen, figure eight, referred by the head of those premises on the nineteenth of October. The reason is a statement made in public on the fifteenth which cannot be reproduced in writing and for which no author has been paid."

"That is a footnote."

"It is the reason clause of this office's only form, and the form has been printed since before either of us," said Alma Quayle, "and the form says *give the reason*, and you have given me one, and it is true, and in about four years it will be the only sentence in that file that nobody can argue with."

She signed it and dated it and initialled it once, which is a keeper's habit she has brought into a wing and has never been asked to drop.

"You have never written that premises at seven again and you never will," she said. "That is shut for you. On the first of next month there will be no production off your door, and no production in the chained book, and the last number anybody swears out of that address is eight, and every office that reads a premises book in this city will have eight standing against that door for as long as the leaf is open."

"I know."

"Now go and tell the woman."

"I am not telling her on a stair."

"She does not have to be told on a stair," said Alma Quayle. "She has to be told. And she is the only person in this business whose whole protection is that nothing can find her, and you have just refused the only thing in this city that would have put her on a page." She gathered the sheets square. "That is the best thing anybody has done in my division in four years, and I am required to say it once and then go on to the next sheet."

## The Sentence She Did Not Send

She wrote another sheet out after he had gone to the stair, on the stool, with the corridor going quiet behind her.

It was four lines. It began *the subject is a man.* It had his address in the second line and the figure of eight in the third and the reason clause lifted word for word off the song, which she could not do, because she had heard it once from a stool in a corridor and could not sing and had not asked anybody to write it down.

She read it once.

Then she put it face down on the stool.

"I am not sending that," she said, to a clerk of about thirty who had come for the day's trays and had not expected to be spoken to.

"Which one, head?"

"The one I have just written. It is not going." Alma Quayle put her hand flat on it. "He came here and heard it from me. That is the only way I want a sentence like that to reach a man. If I post it, he will read it in a room with two other people in it and he will not get the chance to do the thing he has done for four years, which is to put his hands flat on a table and refuse."

"Shall I enter that, head? That you are not sending it?"

"Do not enter that."

The clerk entered it.

He entered it the way he had been taught, with the time and the date and the words, because the sentence had come out of Alma Quayle in the tone of a woman who writes things down.

*11.40. Head states referral accepted. Head states a sentence drafted and not sent, by the head's own word, preferring the subject to hear it from her.*

He went down the stair with it. It was in a division inside the hour.

It had her whole name at the head of it and his address four lines below, and it was the first document in this business to put the two of them on one page, and neither of them had asked for it, and Alma Quayle found out at four in the afternoon and could not do one single thing about it for the rest of the year.

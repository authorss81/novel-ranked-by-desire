# The Woman At Two In The Morning

The fourth floor of thirty-four six one one eighteen had no address on any list. It held a water tank on a frame, three drying lines nobody had used since the spring, and the bin store, which was a brick recess at the back with a barrow in it and a barrow that was not on the bill.

He went up there at about ten past two in the morning on the twentieth of November because he had been awake since one, and because the count had started at ten to twelve the previous day and he had not yet worked out what a man does with his hands on the second day of eleven.

She had the store door open and a lamp hooked to a nail, and she had the first barrow half out when he came up the last nine steps.

"Ay," said Wanda Rej. "You are the top floor."

"You know me."

"I know a man comes in at one and goes out at seven and never says his name at the gate and never once has asked anybody for anything, and I know which of the four floors is his because you use the stairs and the other three use the lift." She set the barrow down and wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, and that took the lamp's light off her face for a second. "You have never been up here."

"No."

"Eleven years I have done this floor," she said. "Eleven years and about four hundred nights that you were not here, and the ones you were here you slept through. It is a good floor. Nothing on it wants carrying down but what the building made."

She went back into the store and brought the barrow out loaded, a bin lid propped against the side of it and the weight sitting high and forward the way a weight sits when a woman has carried it down four flights a night for a decade without ever once being asked how.

## The Hand On The Rail

He stood aside to let her get the wheel past him and she took the corner with her right hand on the rail, and the lamplight came down on the back of it, and he saw the marks.

Four of them, in a carpenter's pencil, each one a short bar with a date under it in small figures that were not very well written.

Not one of them was recent.

The oldest was the eleventh of March. The second was the second of July. The third was in August with a day that had been rubbed out and written again. The fourth was the fourteenth of October at ten past two in the morning, and it was the only one still black.

She had kept the count on the back of her own hand, because she could not keep a book, and she had carried it down four flights a night for nine months and she had told nobody.

He put his own hands behind his back and made them stay there.

"Marble's cold in November," she said. "That floor is the coldest in the lane. I tell them every year and they have the baize down in October for a fortnight and then it takes it away again, because the baize is for the laundry and not for us."

"You should say it on a form."

"Which form." She laughed at it, one syllable, without unkindness. "I am on the round, not on a district. They want a woman who empties. When I am ill they send a girl for a fortnight and the girl is frightened of the stair in the dark and takes two hours on what I do in forty minutes, and then the barrow sits in the hall until nine in the morning and everybody in this building walks round it in the dark."

She set the weight again, both hands now. "I have asked for a lamp on the fourth stair for eleven years."

"Then ask for it again tomorrow."

"They have heard me ask for it for eleven years," said Wanda Rej.

## What He Wanted To Do

There was a chair on the landing, a broken one with three legs, and she came and sat down on the seat of it while the barrow stood.

"You are up at two," she said. "You are not up for the water."

"No."

"Then you are up for the same reason you have been up for four months, which is a thing I do not ask about, and I am not going to." She put her hands on her knees. "You are not going to tell me and I am not going to make it easier for you, so we can both get on with the night."

He had not expected to be let off and it took him about four seconds to find the sentence.

"You know I have been in a song."

"I know you have been in nine hundred mouths since the summer and I do not know whose it is," she said. "Everyone's singing it and half of them have it wrong and that is the part that makes me laugh. My cousin has the second verse about a woman who carries things and it is not you and it is not me, and she sings it in a kitchen at eleven o'clock at night with her eyes shut, which tells you what it is for."

She stood up, and picked up the barrow's handle, and stopped.

"You have been standing there for a minute and a half doing sums," said Wanda Rej. "Go down. It is two o'clock and I have got three other buildings before six."

He wanted, concretely, with a sharpness that made his teeth meet, to take the hand with the marks on it and hold it for four seconds, because he had held a hundred hands in this city for four seconds and had never once been thanked, and because four seconds would finish her.

It would take the ache out of her chest and the shaking out of the hand that held the rail, and she would stand there in the cold on the top landing of a building and feel better than she has felt since the spring, and it would last her three days.

And she would put a fifth bar on the back of her left hand with a date under it, in carpenter's pencil, and she would tell a doctor about it in a queue, because that is what a woman who has been keeping track does with a fifth one.

He kept his hands behind his back and let her go down with the barrow, and listened to the wheel on the top four steps, and then the third, and then nothing, because a woman who has done it for eleven years does not need to hear where the stair is.

One day since the nineteenth, when eleven dates had gone down a clinic corridor in a doctor's hand, and this was the second of them.

He went down his own sixty-one steps and lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and at about four he got up again and stood at the window with his arms folded, looking at the shape of the building he lived in, which was the only object in Ostara he had ever been able to keep unsaid.

## The Man In The Hall In October

She came back up at four for the second barrow and found him still at the window on the fourth floor, which he had not expected her to notice.

"Go to bed," she said.

"Before you go down. There was a man in this hall at two in the morning on the twentieth of October."

"I know the one you mean." She set the barrow down. "He had a book and a lamp and he wrote in it with a bit of wood. He did not go up. He stood by the letter-box for about a quarter of an hour and then he went out and shut the street door behind him so that it did not bang, and I thought he was a thief and I stood in the dark and let him."

"He is not a thief."

"No, I worked that out on the second night." She wiped her hands down her apron. "Thieves do not shut the door. He comes two or three times a month and he never knocks and he never asks anybody anything, and he stands where he can see the stair and the letter-box and the two doors off the hall."

"That is what he is for."

"Then tell me one thing, and then I will not ask you again about it in the night." She looked up the stair towards the stairs of the house and not at him. "Does anybody stop him."

"No."

"That is what I thought." She got her grip on the barrow. "If he comes while I am on the floor, I will let him past. But if he comes on the second or third and the girl is on the round, tell her to let him past as well. She has been told not to open to strangers and she is twenty-two and she will stand in a doorway and argue with a man who writes in a book."

"I will tell her."

"You do that," said Wanda Rej. "And that is a thing you can do and it costs you nothing, so I am telling you about it at two o'clock in the morning because you cannot refuse a thing that costs nothing at two o'clock in the morning."

Then she went down with the second barrow, and he stood at the head of the stair and listened to a wheel go down four flights in the dark the way it had gone down four flights every night for eleven years, and understood that in this building he was the only person awake who was not carrying anything.

## Six O'Clock In The Yard

He went down to the Vestry at six and stood in the yard behind the clinic with the cold coming off the wall, because the ward was on the other side of that wall and there was one person in this city he had promised not to walk into for eleven days and she was eleven feet away from him behind brick.

The yard was empty except for a barrow with a broken wheel against the far wall, and the door came open at about ten to seven and Tessa Okonkwo came out to empty a bucket and saw him standing there and stopped.

"You have not slept."

"No."

"You were on that floor at two." It was not a question and she did not wait. "The fourth floor has a barrow and a lamp and a woman in it. She is in here with a chart and four pencil marks on her hand and a heart that is going, and you have stood at the top of that stair and not gone in and that is the correct decision and it is also the whole of what you are going to get, for eleven days."

"I know."

"Then go to work." She emptied the bucket and came back out with a second one. "You are not permitted to stand in my yard looking at my wall. A man who stands in a yard at six in the morning is a man who is waiting to be asked what he wants, and nobody in this city asks that question. It is a question they put in the record instead."

He went to work at seven and cleaned six floors of washrooms while the office filled, and the valve on the fourth floor was weeping, and he chased it, and he did not put a hand on anybody for nine hours.

At the end of the shift he stood at a gate in the dark and worked out that he was the safest man in the district and the most ordinary one, and that those two facts had been produced by the same eleven dates on a slip in a cloth ledger.

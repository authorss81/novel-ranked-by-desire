# A Deed With Witnesses In The Room

On the fifth of November at about half past six in the morning he went down to the wash-house yard behind the Fourth Branch with a bucket and a brush and did the ordinary thing.

The yard drain had been blocked since the thirtieth of October. The night porter had logged it twice. The day clerk had put a slip on it that morning giving the job to the contractor, who would come on the eighth with a machine and charge the branch for the visit.

Lin Hao lifted the grille, reached in to his elbow in cold water, and cleared it by hand.

There were four people in the yard. A driver with a cart. Two women from the laundry with a basket between them. A boy from the paper store with a bundle on his shoulder.

None of them had been placed there. He had not told the kitchen what yard he would be in. He had told Petra Ilyin the night before that he would be at work at six and that was all.

The water went at about twenty minutes to seven with a sound like a throat clearing. The driver said something short and approving. One of the laundry women laughed.

The day clerk came out with the slip still in her hand and stared at the clear grille.

"You did that."

"It was blocked."

"It was booked for the eighth."

"It is clear now."

She wrote it down on the back of the slip in pencil, with the time against it, because clerks write things down when water moves where they can see it. She wrote his name because he was standing there with wet sleeves. She wrote the four witnesses underneath because they were standing there too.

He did not ask her to. He did not stop her either.

That was the whole of it, and he carried the bucket back inside with his sleeves dripping and his hands aching from the cold, and he wanted nothing more than tea from the staff kettle and ten minutes with his hands round it.

## The Slip

At about nine the slip went into the branch day book in the ordinary way. At about ten a copy went to the district office with the morning post. By noon it was a line in a book that eleven divisions keep.

Petra Ilyin found him at the tram stop at half past six that evening and walked with him three stops without speaking. Then she spoke.

"It did not score."

"How do you know."

"Because I went to the wall at five," she said. "Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. Six under. The words unchanged. Beatrix was there with the notebook. She read it twice to be sure."

He had known the number before she said it. Knowing a thing had never once made it lighter.

"Why."

"Because the deed was yours," said Petra Ilyin. "You lifted the grille. You cleared it. The clerk wrote what she saw. A deed a man does himself and files himself is an office deed at best and an instrumented entry at worst. It splits to the staff or it gets a query. It does not attach to you."

They walked past the wash-house where the grille ran clear.

"Say the rule."

She said it the way he had said it to her once, on the twenty-sixth of August, in a room with a chained book in it.

"A deed a man does himself does not score to him. Only what other people attest of him scores, and only when they attest it without being asked to arrange it."

"Who taught you that."

"You did. You have known it since April and you have never written it down."

He stopped at the corner by the shop, four minutes' walk from Ferrier Street, and the anger from the third was still in him, smaller now and harder.

"Then what scores."

"What somebody else does because you relieved them," she said. "Not what you did. What they did after. Relief converted into somebody else's attested act. That is the only thing that ever moved any band you ever held."

"Say that again, slowly."

She said it again, slowly, the way a woman who has carried dockets for eleven years says a thing that has to be got right.

"You put your hand on a person's hand and something they are owed arrives early. They finish something they have been surviving. Then they do something in public, with witnesses, and somebody writes that down. The clerk does not score your hand. The clerk scores their act. And because their act would not have happened without your relief, the score comes back to you."

He took in the arithmetic and it made him cold.

He saw, plainly, what it meant for one woman standing three stops from home with a bundle under her arm.

## The Cost Of It

"Who pays for that."

"They do," said Petra Ilyin. "Whoever acts carries it. The deed is theirs. If it is examined in four years, they answer it. You do not."

"And the cold minute."

"That too. Whoever fed you feels the spend as cold in the hand or the chest. One to four seconds. Most shrug it off. Some keep track."

They went up the sixty-one steps in the dark. The kitchen was warm and full of the smell of soup. Six women looked up. Two did not.

He told it straight, without dressing it, because he had promised on the twenty-third in a corridor that he would not make it better.

"I did the yard drain this morning with four witnesses and a clerk's slip. It went into the day book at nine. It did not move the plate. A deed of my own does not score."

Beatrix Halm wrote it in the notebook under the date, with the hour against it.

"Then we know what does not work," she said. "That is something."

Sun Meiling ladled soup and set a bowl in front of him.

"Eat," she said. "You are cold to the bone and you smell of drain water and you did a thing this morning that cost a contractor a visit and saved this branch money, and not one grain of it is yours."

He ate. The soup was hot and plain and it warmed his hands through the bowl.

He wanted, concretely, one attested line that was not his own hand, and he wanted it the way a man wants proof that he is not invisible, even though invisibility is what kept him alive.

After supper Wren Kallo came and stood beside his chair for a moment, which she had not done since the second of October.

"You did not arrange the witnesses," she said. "That matters. Counter-forensics will see four names that are not ours. A deed with witnesses in the room who are not yours is the only kind that can ever score."

"And it still did not."

"It still did not, because the deed was yours," she said. "Next time do not lift the grille. Put your hand on somebody's hand and let them lift it."

She went out. The door did not make a sound.

He sat at the cleared table long after the others had gone up, with the notebook open beside his empty bowl, and looked at the line Beatrix had written.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. Six under. Unchanged.

## What The Branch Keeps

At about ten that night Runa Sato came down the sixty-one with her coat on and her hat in her hand, back from Cordwain where the muster room pad still held his one-line undertaking of the thirtieth, lodged, with her name on it.

"You did the yard without telling me," she said. "Good. If you had told me, the four witnesses would be mine."

"I did not tell anybody."

"I know. Petra told me at six. She walked three stops with you and let you say it badly." She sat down opposite him. "Hear one thing from the Field before you sleep. A standing order is a licence and not a warrant. Every officer in the division may come to that door unasked. That has been true since the twenty-seventh. Your yard slip is now in the same stream as my return. Somebody will match them."

"Will they match the list."

"They match everything against everything," she said. "That is what eleven divisions do at nine in the morning. Your slip says four witnesses at a wash-house. The list says eight persons at Ferrier Street. A clerk draws no line between them. A good analyst in four years draws one. That is the distance you bought today."

She stood to go.

"One more thing. The driver from the yard asked the day clerk for your name. She gave it. He wrote it on a cigarette paper and put it in his pocket. Men who drive carts remember hands that clear drains. That paper is not an attestation. It is a beginning."

She went up. The stair creaked once under her weight, a stone and a half heavier than she looks, all of it in the shoulders and the back.

He said it out loud to the empty kitchen, once, to hear what it sounded like when nobody was there to attest it.

"Tomorrow I do not touch anything."

## What The Notebook Holds

Beatrix Halm turned back three pages in the notebook and showed him the wall as she keeps it, in dates under his handwriting from the twelfth of September with the counts beneath each one.

"You agreed on the thirteenth of August to fetch me a figure carrying another name or fetch nothing," she said. "You brought me seven dates instead. I carry those. If three women attest for you, I will carry that too, but I will not attest myself. Not this week. My hearing paper runs and the guild watches my hand."

"Why not."

"Because a sheet carrying both our hands with a single correction of mine would turn every line into mine," she said. "I told you that on the night of the thirtieth of September in two rooms over a shuttered shop. I will not have that on a sheet showing 8,138 beside the seventh of June. Somebody has to keep the wall clean."

Petra Ilyin refilled the cups without being asked.

"Then the three will not be us," she said. "They will be women you relieved between five and seven while we slept. Laundry. Yard. Gate. The city is full of them. That is the point of your hour. You walk it every morning and you do not see it."

He counted. Five days since the thirtieth of October, when the list had issued and Runa Sato had written three lines in a day book with no reason against any of them. Sixteen days since the nineteenth, when he had referred the account with a song for a reason.

He counted both against dates printed in other rooms, and the counting steadied him the way counting always had.

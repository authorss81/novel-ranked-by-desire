# The Thing He Signed Instead

He wrote it at the kitchen table at Ferrier Street on the morning of the twenty-ninth of December from about eight o'clock, on four sheets of plain paper, in his own hand, with eight women watching him do it.

Nobody helped him. That was not an instruction; it was what happened, because the first thing Sun Meiling said was that a hand that helped him write it would be a hand that had to be on it, and there had been enough of that in this house already.

He started at the top of the first sheet and did not go back.

"I, Lin Hao, at premises 34-6-118, being the head of those premises. I set this down in my own hand. I will be examined about every line of it."

Second line: the address, and the date it became his, and the fact that he had asked for a description of it to be made against the head of it rather than against the door, on the twenty-seventh of October, in a kitchen, in front of eight people and a clerk.

Third: the account. Thirty years, the residue of an unadministered account, carried at that address since the sixteenth of September at a figure of seven. Initialled and not signed. Called in a chained real book at the valuation of the first of December and not answered, not paid and not produced, because there is no production off that door and there never will be.

Fourth: the composition. Eight, certified on the thirtieth of November at nine in the morning by an officer of counter-forensics, in her own division, at the address.

Then the eight, in the order of the stair, with the room against each line, and he wrote them slowly, and it took about four minutes, and nobody at that table said one word while he did it.

## The Last Line

Sun Meiling read over his shoulder and put her hand on the back of a chair.

"You have not written the reason," she said.

"There is no reason in this."

"There is always a reason. Every page in this city that has ever been made about a person has a reason clause under the address, and yours says nothing, and a clerk at a counter will read a reason clause in four seconds and a whole page in a year."

She did not move the chair.

"You are going to have to write one, and it is going to be the truest four words anybody has written this year. I would like to know now whether you can say them at this table."

He put the pen down and stood there for about nine seconds, and then he picked it up and wrote one line at the foot of the fourth sheet and signed it.

*I am the man at the top of this page and I will answer for it.*

Eight words. There was no hour against them and no clerk and no division and no reason to be cheaper, and it went onto four sheets of plain paper in a kitchen at half past nine in the morning, and it was true.

## What It Closed

"You understand what you have just done," said Petra Ilyin, from the far end of the table.

"Yes."

"Say it, because I want to hear whether you understood it or whether you were brave."

"He can never be the address again in a document," said Sun Meiling.

"From this morning, every paper in this city that touches that account has to carry his name at the top of it," she said. "Not because anybody made him. Because he went up that hill at four and a quarter past nine this morning with four sheets in his coat and put his name on them himself."

"Six years," said Nadia Ferrous. "Six years of paper with no name at the top of it, and he has gone the other way in two months, and he did it on purpose, and nobody made him."

"He did it because there is a ruled box somewhere in this city with a tick in the corner of it and his address on the line above," said Runa Sato. "And he is the only person alive who can fill it. And he filled it at his own kitchen table rather than in a hearing, and that is going to be in the answer for him and not against him, and it is the only good decision any of us have made in a month."

Beatrix Halm wrote the time against it and did not write anything else, and then, after about four seconds, she wrote four more words, and nobody asked her what they were, because Beatrix Halm has never once been asked.

Isolde Marchetti had been at the end of the table with her hands in her lap and she had not spoken since the pen went down.

"That is a reason," she said. "It is not mine."

He did not look round.

"It is not mine and it is true, and I am not going to take the room's ear for four seconds to say so."

She put it to the table rather than to him.

"I made a reason out of a tune on the fifteenth of October, and nine hundred people carried it out of a room for nothing, and there has never been a name on it. That page has a reason on it and your name at the top of it, and mine is not on it. I am not asking for it."

"I am not using yours."

"I know you are not using it. You never could. It does not go on a page."

She took her hands out of her lap and put them on the table.

"I am telling you that I noticed. I have noticed every morning since the fifteenth of October, and this morning is the first morning anybody in this room has given anything back to the person who made the reason out of a tune."

She stopped.

"That is all I have got and I am not going to make it larger than it is."

## The Bench

He was let into the room at five minutes to eleven on the morning of the twenty-ninth of December with four sheets of plain paper in his hand, and the clerk at the door said that a paper could be laid into the keeping of the room by anybody, at any hour, in the ordinary way, and that it would be kept.

He laid it on the table under the window and put his name at the foot of the last sheet in front of the room, and Anouk Ferreira read all four sheets standing up, at the rate of a woman reading an address, and did not sit down.

At the end of it she put the four sheets square and said the only sentence anybody in that room needed.

"This is a person," she said. "From this morning, this is a person, and a person can be examined, and I have wanted this room to have one of those since the second of October and I have been afraid to say so in case somebody took it as an invitation."

Then she called the people who had been in the room on the twenty-third and the fourteen of them who were not in the room that day, and eight of them came and looked at the four sheets and signed a witness line underneath, in their own hands, saying that on the twenty-ninth of December a man had laid a page into the keeping of the room with his own name at the top of it and his own name at the foot of it.

There were nine seats. Eight hands went on the line. The gap in it, in the middle of the paper, where the fourth seat was, was the size of two fingers and there was nothing whatever written in it, and four people in the room looked at it and one of them said that it would be filled or it would be explained in a year.

## What It Costs Whoever Signs

"You understand the price," said the chair, to the room and not to him. "Whoever writes under that carries his name in a book of their own for four years. That is the price of an attested act in this city and it does not vary and I am not going to soften it for a man who has never once in seven years let somebody else's act go unattested."

Eight people had signed, and eight people were about to find that out in the ordinary way, and one of the eight was Alma Quayle, who had recused herself from a requisition and had signed a witness line about a piece of paper inside four days, and who said nothing at all about it and went home.

He walked back down the hill at about eleven with empty hands for the first time since the twenty-seventh of October, because everything he had brought to that room was now in the room's keeping and would be there in four years and could be got at by anybody with a folder and the patience to use it.

Eight people had signed a piece of paper about him that morning, and the price of it was four years of their own books, and he had paid it with nothing at all, which is the whole of what legibility costs the man who goes and gets it.

He had expected to come down that hill lighter and he did not. He was a man who had put a lamp on in a house he had spent seven years keeping dark, and the house was full of people, and they could all see him now, and that was the trade and there was no second half of it anywhere.

## Four O'Clock

A copy of a paper laid into the keeping of a room is served on every person named in it. That is the ordinary way and there is no discretion in it and no division in this city has ever been asked to exercise one.

The copies left the building on the ordinary stream at four o'clock that afternoon, bound for eight addresses, and every one of the eight was inside one building on Ferrier Street, and one of them was the second floor of the building he was standing in.

He stood at the bottom of the sixty-one at about ten past four and worked out that in about two hours a sheet of paper with his name at the top of it and her name in the fourth line would be delivered to a table in a room twenty feet above his head, in a building where nine people had learned his address from a list that she had certified and signed herself.

He was barred from putting one question to her about that address, in that building or out of it, for the rest of his life, and her answer would be that she did not know what he was talking about. He had promised it in October, in a room, with three conditions in it, and had said it twice at her own table in December.

So he went up the hill and did not tell her and would not be told and could not ask, and the copy came up the sixty-one at about twenty past five and was signed for by eight women in a kitchen, one of whom was the fourth line, and one of whom did not come down.

# A Chair And A Majority

The notice came up the sixty-one at eleven in the morning on the sixth of December on the ordinary stream, in a Bureau envelope with a green tag on it, and Wren Kallo came up behind it because a requisition on a described subject is served by the division that holds the description.

Eight women were at the table. He was at the top of the stairs when the envelope came and she was two steps below him with her hat in her hand.

"It is mine to serve," she said, "and I am going to serve it at that table with the eight at it, because you have asked for a described subject to be told things with somebody standing in front of him for four years and I am not going to be the officer who finds a better hour."

## The Fourth Box

She put the envelope on the table and took out one sheet and read it out loud in the voice she uses on a counter, which is a voice about eleven words slower than a speaking voice.

*Requisition of merit. First of the quarter. Laid at the monthly sitting. To be voted at a sitting called for the purpose, before the last working day of the quarter.*

*Against: the account carried at premises 34-6-118. Figure seven, dated the sixteenth of September, initialled and not signed. Called at a valuation on the first of December at ten to nine.*

*The room is asked to take into its own keeping the account of thirty years at the figure on the line, and to administer it thereafter in the ordinary way.*

*Held by.*

She stopped there and put her finger under the last two words, and the box under them was the width of a hand and it was empty.

"Read the last line again," said Petra Ilyin.

"I have read it exactly as it is printed."

"Read it again."

Wren Kallo read it again. It said *Held by*, and the box was empty, and the empty box had a ruled border round it and a clerk's tick in the corner that said it had been checked and found to contain nothing.

"That is not a mistake," said Petra Ilyin. "A mistake does not get a tick. Somebody went round that room with a pen and put a tick in the corner of an empty box, and that means the box was empty when it was checked."

"It means the box was checked."

"She is being kind to you, Petra, and she is being kind to you correctly."

## The One He Was Not Allowed

He looked at the sheet for about four seconds and then he asked her, across a kitchen table, in front of eight women, one question about the address.

"Is that box yours."

She did not blink and she did not take it as an insult, because she had asked for it in a room with three conditions in it in October and she had been waiting since for him to be somewhere he could not argue.

"I do not know what you are talking about," said Wren Kallo.

The kitchen did not move. She put the sheet down square with the envelope, and picked up her hat, and said that the hearing was at the chair's discretion and that she would be in the room for it because her division holds the description, and that she would not be able to answer anything else today and would not be asked to.

He said, "I will not ask you again."

"That is the second time you have said that in front of me," she said, "and I have believed you both times, and there is a difference between the first one and this one and I am not going to say what it is in this room."

Then she was gone, and the sixty-one gave back no sound of her at all, which it never has.

## Nine Seats

Runa Sato put her cup down and turned the sheet round so that it faced her.

"I have stood in that corridor," she said. "Twice, on papers, not on a requisition. So I am going to tell you the room, because nobody in this kitchen has ever been in it and somebody should say it once out loud."

"Nine seats," she said. "Nine people, counted, named, with a seal each, and a seat that is empty is not a seat. Five of nine carry a requisition. Not five of the ones present. Five of nine, and one of the nine is in a chair, and the chair holds a seal like anybody and chairs nothing at all."

"Sixth."

"The fourth seat has been empty since the first of November and the man who sat in it was re-read at the valuation on that morning and came out under his own floor, and a man with no standing cannot hold a seal, so the seat is a piece of furniture until somebody in there decides it is not." She tapped the table once. "And Alma Quayle is recused."

"On what."

"On her own signature. She signed the description of those premises on the twenty-seventh of October against the head, in her own hand, and a woman cannot put a seal on anything her hand has already been on. She wrote to the room about it on the second and it took them until the fourth to accept it."

"A signed thing that she will not touch."

"A signed thing she will not touch and will not explain, and both of those are Alma, and if you want a person in this city who is never wrong it is the woman who has just been recused by her own pen." Runa Sato sat back. "So. Nine. One empty. One recused. Seven can carry it and five must, and as of yesterday four have sealed it."

"Who."

"Three are in that room every month and the fourth is a man who was in the corridor on the first with nothing in his hands at all." She looked at him. "I am not giving you names, because a Field officer who gives names out of a room where nine people sit stops being a Field officer by the following week, and because you would do something with them and I have watched you do things with things."

"Then who is the fifth."

"That is the question the room has been asking since the seventeenth of November and nobody has answered it."

"Why not simply name him," said Petra Ilyin. "He is not a stranger. He is on a list with his rooms against his name. He has stood under a plate in daylight and read it out to nine people. What is hard about writing a name in a box."

Beatrix Halm picked the pencil up off the table and turned it end over end in her fingers, which is what she did instead of looking at anybody.

"Because a list of persons at an address is not a list of what anybody holds," she said. "I have been trying to source a woman in this house for nine years off a piece of paper, and the paper will not tell me."

She turned the pencil over once.

"It says eight names and it says the rooms and it says when each of them took the address. It is the cleanest thing any of us has ever seen, and if you wrote his name under it you would have a document that looks like a holding, and it would not be one."

"You do not know that he holds nothing."

"I know that no office in this city has ever written down that he holds nothing either," said Beatrix Halm, "and a box with a tick in the corner of it is not a box with a name in it, and the tick means somebody looked and found nothing to write."

## The Part That Is About The Quarter

Sun Meiling had not touched the sheet.

"Tell him the rest of it," she said to Runa Sato, "and tell him now, because he will do it anyway and he would rather have it in the room."

"There is a hearing," said Runa Sato. "A subject may be heard before the seals go on. Once, and only once, and never in the same room on the same day as the vote, because a room that has heard a man argue and then votes on him is a room that has already decided and is doing the paperwork."

"So there is a hearing and then a sitting."

"There is a hearing and then a sitting and the sitting has to be before the last working day of the quarter, which is the thirtieth, which is tomorrow month."

She put her cup down.

"That is not a threat and nobody in that room has said it out loud. It is an ordinary rule and it has been an ordinary rule for twenty years. It is the reason a requisition cannot be left lying about over a winter, and it is the only reason anybody in this kitchen has heard any of this before today."

"And on the twenty-third."

"On the twenty-third, if the chair calls it. And the chair will call it if four seals are on the sheet, and four seals were on the sheet yesterday."

She looked at him steadily.

"So you have until the fourteenth to be heard, and the fourteenth is not the day I would worry about. The day you should worry about is the seventeenth. I do not know why and neither do you, and I have been in this city long enough to know that a woman who chairs a room offers a man something before she votes on him."

## What Agnete Said

Agnete Lindqvist had not looked at the sheet at all.

"I have a room in this city where no officer of any division can get a word out of anybody standing inside it," she said. "Eleven years it has stood, and eleven more it will stand, and I have not been inside it once, and there is one thing about that sheet at this table nobody has said."

"Go on."

"That box is the most useful piece of paper in this kitchen and it has been empty for nineteen days, and every one of you is looking at it as though it is a mistake somebody made."

She turned her chair round to face him.

"It is not a mistake. It is the only thing in this business that has ever been on his side, and it is empty because for thirty years the account has been carried at a door, and he put it there himself, in ninety seconds, on the sixteenth of September, and asked for it."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"I do not know that," said Runa Sato.

"You do not know it because no office in this city writes down the reason a thing was done," said Agnete Lindqvist. "If the reason were written down it would be the most dangerous line in the book."

She put her hands flat on the table.

"I have been indoors eleven years and I have had four years to learn how a person becomes a name on an instrument. It is never done to them. It is always done for them, by them, with their own hand on it, while somebody very reasonable tells them it is cheaper."

He looked at the empty box for a long time.

"Where do I find out who put it there."

"You do not," said Agnete Lindqvist. "You already know. You have known since the fifteenth of September and you have told four people at most and you told one of them on the twenty-eighth of November in a room where you were not permitted to ask her anything."

That was the end of the morning, and Beatrix Halm wrote the time against it in a notebook she had carried for nine years, and did not write the name of the person who had not been in the room.

He wanted two things and could have had neither of them that day. He wanted a name for the fifth seal, and he wanted to ask Wren Kallo one question and be allowed an answer.

Instead he had a ruled box with a clerk's tick in the corner of it, and eight women, and the knowledge that a room where nine people sit was going to meet again on the fourteenth of December to hear him say whatever he had come to say.

And the price of the whole of that, which is the price of the eleventh of July and every term he has kept since, was one sentence at a kitchen table that cost her the whole of what she knows and him the whole of what he came for.

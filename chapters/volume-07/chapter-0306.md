# The Office That Describes Things

She was on the bottom step of the sixty-one at eight in the morning with the case beside her, and she told him which stair and which floor and which door, and she did not ask him to come with her, and she had been on that step since a quarter past six because the household wakes at five and she did not want to knock at six.

"A hand is on the cloth in that corridor," she said, "and it is on the far side. I have watched two men try to pick it up from this side and neither of them could do it, and neither of them would admit it once they were back in the corridor, so I am telling you now in case you need not be the third."

## The Second Sub-Level

There is a runner at the top of the sub-level stair and he took a slip of paper off him and read it and read it again and put his head on one side.

"It says you are to be shown the contents."

"That is what it says."

"Then you will go down and turn left and the fourth door on that side has a cloth on a table in front of it, and the cloth is not going anywhere." The runner put the slip in his coat. "And I will say the thing everybody says at that stair, which is that nobody in this corridor knows your name."

"Not one person down here has ever heard my name."

"The rule of this floor is that a corridor knows nobody's name," said the runner. "That is not kindness. It is the only rule down here that anybody has ever broken deliberately."

The stair is warm. He noticed that and then wished he had not, because he had spent four years in a room two floors under a sales floor that has never once been warm, and there is a thing a warm stair tells you about a budget, and a budget is a document.

The corridor had a runner in it, four doors, a bench with a cracked wooden arm on it, and a table with a cloth on it.

There was a hand at the far end of the cloth and he could see it and he could not reach it.

"You will want to move that," said Alma Quayle.

She was standing at the fourth door with her coat off over one arm and a card in her hand, and she had been in that corridor long enough to have taken the runner's stool out from against the wall and use it.

"I will not," he said. "Somebody told me about it."

"Somebody always tells people about it, and it is always a joke, and it is never a joke." She looked at him without any expression at all. "Mr Neve. Sit down on the bench, or stand up, and do not touch the cloth, and I am going to ask you three questions and I am going to tell you before I ask them that I have written all three down, so that you can decide for yourself whether you are being examined."

"You are not examining me."

"I am not." She put the card face down on her knee.

"Not one person in this corridor is examining anybody. That is the second rule of the floor and I have never once seen it broken. A man on the third floor sent you down a slip of paper, and he sent it because a person who wants to know what goes into a description is entitled to be shown one. That is all this is, and it has been all this is for four years."

"Then why do I want it."

"Because you have something that will not reconcile and you have been told three times in a week that if it does not reconcile somebody else will write it," said Alma Quayle. "So sit down, or stand up, and answer."

## Three Questions

"The first question," she said. "What is the address."

"Thirty-four six one one eighteen, Ferrier Street, and there is a district number under it and I do not know it."

"Correct, and you would have been told it at a counter if you had asked for it, and you did not ask for it, which tells me something." She turned the card over and looked at it. "The second. What is the figure."

"There are two."

"That is the second question answered, and it is the only man in this building who has ever answered it that way." Alma Quayle looked up.

"Two figures, both true, in two books in two buildings. One dated the sixteenth of September, initialled, not signed, in a merchant house's real books. One on a district leaf opened on the first of this month, written by a clerk of about twenty-five because a woman of reading age walked up a counter and asked him to."

He had not said any of that in the corridor.

"You did not say that."

"You did not tell me to say anything," said Alma Quayle. "You told me two figures, and a room that has been reading since the second of August knows what two figures are, and I have had the same seven documents in front of me that everybody else in this city has had, and I did the same arithmetic you did and it took me one page."

"One line between them."

"One line, drawn in the ordinary way, by clerks, in the order the paper arrived, without anybody deciding to draw it." She put the card in her coat pocket.

"That line is an address. You did that. Six documents in four buildings since the second of August and not one of them with your name at the head of it, and a line between them does not want your name at the head of it either."

"It is an address. It is in my division. My division has been reading it since August as ordinary work."

"You have been reading me."

"I have been reading a page of figures and finding a pattern, which is the whole of what my office does between one and four in the afternoon." Alma Quayle sat down on the runner's stool.

"You came down that stair at a quarter past nine this morning believing somebody had sent for you. Not a soul did. A clerk sent down a slip because you asked a fair question in the ordinary way."

"There has been no conspiracy in this corridor and there has not been one in your month either. You have been in here about eleven minutes."

The warm stair was somewhere behind him and the corridor was not cold and it had not once been hostile.

"Third question," said Alma Quayle. "What is the reason."

His hands went down flat onto his knees and stayed there.

"Correct. And I want to give you the reason for that, because in thirty years it is the only answer that has ever surprised me." She put her elbows on her knees. "You have had two figures and an address and you cannot write the reason, and the reason is the only part of that page that has ever needed a person in it, and you are the person, and that is the entire difficulty and there is no way round it that does not go through you."

## What She Is

"I have read about you for four months."

"That is not a thing I can help you with."

"No, and I am not going to discuss it, and I am going to tell you one thing about myself instead, once." She looked at the cloth on the table with the hand at the far end of it. "I have spent thirty years arranging to be the person who can finally say what a thing is. Not what it is worth. What it is."

"And you have almost got it."

"I have got it in front of me," said Alma Quayle. "Address, figure, and a man who cannot write the third thing. That is a classification waiting for a reason and I have had men with worse reasons than yours brought in front of me by people who wanted them taken, and every one of those men had a reason written for them by somebody who cared about them, and it was wrong, and I signed it, and it took eleven years off them."

She stood up off the stool.

"You want to know whether a classification needs a man in it. It does not. It has never needed one. It needs an address, a figure and a reason, and all three of them can be assembled out of paper by a building full of clerks who are extremely good and go home at four."

"And mine has two of the three because I made them."

"Because you made them," she said. "You described the account to a woman in a cold room in your own words in September so that she would price it, and she did, and that is the single most generous thing anybody has done for this office in about four years, and I have been waiting for it since the spring."

## The Kitchen Table

"I am not asking you for anything."

"I know you are not, and I would like it recorded that I did not ask, because you will go upstairs and decide that this visit was a summons and it was not." She took her coat off the back of the stool. "You came down here to find out what a description of your account would have to contain and I have told you, and you have three of them, and the third one is in your coat pocket folded up from a tea stall."

He did not move.

"You have not read it."

"I have not had to read it. You told me on the ninth that you wrote it at a tea stall and could not turn it into a sentence that was not about you, and you told that to a woman you did not want to tell it to." Alma Quayle picked up her coat. "I am not going to read a man's paragraph about his own life off his coat."

"What do you want."

"I do not want you to come to my office," she said. "I want you to go up to Ferrier Street tonight and I want you to have the kitchen table cleared."

"Why."

"Because I have one question left and it is not a fair question, and it is the only one I have got, and I have been trying to write it for two days." She was at the door. "And I refuse to ask it in a corridor with a cloth on a table in it. I want to ask it in a room with eight women in it, at a table, with the kettle on, where everybody present can hear the whole of it being asked."

"That is a strange request."

"It is the only request I am going to make to you in four years," said Alma Quayle, "and I would like it made tonight, and I would like nobody in that room to be told beforehand what the question is, including the woman who has been sitting on my corridor floor since yesterday evening and who is, by the way, entirely correct about the cloth."

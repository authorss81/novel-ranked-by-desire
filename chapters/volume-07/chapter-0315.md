# The Version With Eight On It

Alma Quayle was at the top of the sixty-one at seven that evening with a clerk, and the woman behind her had a leather case.

That was the first thing anybody at the kitchen table understood. Thirty years in a wing and she had come up sixty-one steps with a woman of the counting staff behind her holding a leather case, and the clerk came in out of the cold and stood by the dresser with the case at her feet and did not take her coat off, and Alma Quayle said why.

"That is Mrs Imrie, and she is here because what is said in this kitchen tonight goes into a division in her hand and not out of mine, and I have found out this month that I cannot be trusted with a sentence of my own before four in the afternoon."

"A clerk is a witness."

"A clerk is a witness and a column and a number," said Alma Quayle. "I have spent thirty years writing things down in rooms where nobody could contradict me and I am not doing it a fourth time."

## The Reason Clause

She wrote it out first, at the table, with the room looking at her hand.

Four lines, and it was the sentence from the half landing on the twenty-sixth with four words in it, and the third line was the clerk at a premises counter and the fourth line was the recommendation, and when she had done it she put the date under it and signed it and initialled it once.

"That is the reason clause," she said, "and it goes out with a description, and it is true in three lines and not true in four words, and I have told the man who wrote the referral that it is not true in four words and he has written that down, and I would like the table to have it."

"Who wrote that down."

"He did, at the bottom of a stair on the twenty-sixth, in about nine seconds." Alma Quayle squared the sheet.

"I am going to come back to it. The description first, and it has to be made by nine o'clock tonight, because it cannot issue after the thirtieth and the thirtieth is the last working day of this month."

"It will sit on a desk in my office for three days with my name on the front of it, and I am going to look at it every morning, and if it is wrong I have three days in which to unmake it."

## Two Versions

Mrs Imrie took two sheets out of the case and laid them side by side on the cleared table.

"Read the left-hand one," Alma Quayle said to the room. "Read it out loud, somebody, I do not mind which of you."

Wren Kallo read it.

*Premises 34-6-118, Ferrier Street. Composition on the morning of the thirtieth: eight persons of reading age keeping an address. Account referred for classification on the nineteenth of October.*

"That is the address."

"That is the address and it is the only description of a house in this city that has ever been lawful," said Alma Quayle. "There are eight names attached to it in a district leaf. They go out as an attachment either way. What this version does not have is a person in it, and I have had it since the twelfth of October and I have been waiting eleven days for somebody to make me use it."

"And the other of the two."

Runa Sato had not been reached yet and she leaned over the table and read the right-hand sheet herself.

"Against the head," she said.

"Head of premises as subject of description. Address, figure, reason, and a line with the head's name on it and the hand and the date under it. And the list attached, because I put that in myself in the fourth year of doing this and I was told it was improper."

"Say what that costs."

"Because an office can examine a man," said Alma Quayle, "and it can argue with him, and it can open a file on him and close it, and it can be appealed to by him. And it cannot do any of those three things to a house. A house sits there, and the description of it is simply right or simply wrong, and there is nobody to put a hand flat on the table."

"Is it cheaper."

"It is cheaper and it is easier to appeal, and those are the two reasons a wing will grant it, and I am not going to pretend to you that the first reason is mercy." She looked round the table. "I have told both of those things to the man sitting at that end of this table and he has told me he is going to say yes, and I am not asking him."

The kitchen did the thing kitchens do.

## Three Words

"Say it yourself."

He had his hands flat on the cloth and he had been in that posture since the twenty-fourth and had not noticed it.

"Against me."

Mrs Imrie's pen was not moving.

"Say why," Alma Quayle said. "Not for me. For the eight of them and for the file and because a reason clause that does not say why is the only kind anybody in this building ever appeals."

He looked at the two sheets side by side on a table that had been cleared for him twice in five days.

"Because it is cheaper," he said.

Sun Meiling put her hands flat on the table about nine inches away from his and did not say a word.

Mrs Imrie wrote it down in the ordinary way, with the time against it, because she had been taught that at a desk and because she had been standing in this kitchen for about two minutes and could not tell that it was the worst sentence anybody had ever entered.

"I have entered that before," she said, to the sheet. "Not those words. The shape of it. A man gives a short reason and a clerk writes it down and neither of them is in the room with the other."

"Write it," said Alma Quayle. "Write it exactly as he said it."

"I am writing it exactly as he said it," said Mrs Imrie. "That is what makes it a record."

"That is in a division in about an hour," said Alma Quayle, "and it has your address four lines above it and the word *cheaper* at the bottom, and no officer in this city will ever take it out."

He did not take his hands off the cloth.

"I know that much."

## Eight Names

The description was made at nine o'clock.

Alma Quayle read the instrument out in full in a kitchen on Ferrier Street, which is not where instruments of this office are made, and Mrs Imrie entered it as it was read, and then she tore the list off the description and put it on the table face up and pushed it along to the woman nearest her.

Eight lines. Top of the stair down.

Nine names, if you counted the man at the head of the page.

Wren Kallo read it and did not say anything. Runa Sato read it and put two fingers on the second line and left them there. Beatrix Halm read it and shut the notebook. Nadia Ferrous read it and said "well" once, out loud, to nobody, in a voice that made two people look at her.

And at the bottom of the list the date against the last line was a year in brackets with two words after it, *from the building*, and not one person at that table had ever seen a form in this city put a word like that in.

Alma Quayle took the list back and folded it once and put it in the case.

"That goes out on the ordinary stream on the thirtieth at four o'clock," she said. "That is three days off and every one of you is going to spend them on a different thing, and I am the woman who is going to have to answer for what it does. So hear the shape of it now."

Nothing in the room moved.

"A person may give an address up. That is the ordinary way, and any one of you may walk to a counter at any hour of any day and do it, and it will be dated, and it will lower the composition of that premises tomorrow or the day after, and not one office in this city will call that a suspicious act."

She closed the case.

"And this list will still say eight, in the order of the stair, with the date of the thirtieth under it, because it is made and it is dated and it goes out at four o'clock."

"And it cannot be corrected."

"It cannot be corrected and it cannot be withdrawn and it cannot be unissued. A composition changes. A list does not." Alma Quayle picked her coat up off the rail. "You can each of you change what is true about that address from tomorrow. You cannot change what was true about it at four o'clock on the thirtieth. I did not make that rule and I would not have made it and I cannot get round it."

Eight women looked at a man at the end of a table who had put his own name at the top of a page, on purpose, in a kitchen, in front of them, and who had given the reason for it in four words that were about money.

"Was there anything else," he said.

"There was one practical thing and I have left it to the end because I did not want it in the middle of that." Alma Quayle stopped at the door with her hand on the rail.

"The stream at four goes out to every division that keeps a public list, and it will not go out before then whatever anybody asks of me."

"The first counter in this city that stands a man or a woman in front of is the Field counter at Cordwain. That counter opens at nine on the morning of the first, and the ordinary stream does not wait for anybody, and the instrument will be lying on that desk when the door opens."

Runa Sato had not moved from the table.

"Who is on that desk in November," she said.

"The rota is printed and it is not mine," said Alma Quayle. "I am told it is you."

She went down the stairs with Mrs Imrie behind her, and the case went down with them, and the kitchen was left with a cleared table and eight women and a man and a thing on the table that had been a piece of paper at seven o'clock.

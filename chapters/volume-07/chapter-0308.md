# The Room That Agrees With Her

Bellhouse Row runs behind the tram's last stop and the room they keep her in at the back is over a haberdasher's that shut in the spring, and the whole building empties into it about twenty minutes after the last house, and it is not empty, because of course it is not empty.

She was at the stage door with a card in a frame and a lamp burning on it through the middle of the night.

"You have been in this house twice in your life," Isolde Marchetti said, "and both times you bought a seat at the back with twenty pence while a verse about you went round two hundred times."

"Three times."

"Three times." She had her coat open over the work dress and she was about forty and she looked like a woman who has been making nine hundred people agree with her since two o'clock. "And I have been waiting eleven days for you to come on a night when I was not on the card, and here you are, and I have not written a new verse yet, and I would like you to sit down and tell me that you have not come to stop me."

"I have come to stop you."

"Then say it properly, because I want it said properly, and then I am going to do it."

## The Back Room

The back room has a bench along one wall, a mirror with a bulb at each end, a table with a bottle of water on it that has not been full since the spring, and four people in it: Lode, who opens the front, the woman who takes the money, and two men who had been up the ladder.

"It is still happening," she said, before he had asked. "I do not know how to make a room not agree with me. I have never once been able to and I have never once been able to turn it off, and I am telling you that at the top of this conversation so that you do not think anything I say in the next half hour is a thing I decided to say."

"You are saying something else."

"I am saying something I have said four hundred times in eleven years, and tonight it has got an address in it, and I refuse to be argued off it by a man who has been careful for seven years."

She sent the four of them out. Lode went without arguing. The two men went and one of them looked at her on the way through the door in a way that she ignored with practised ease.

"There," she said. "Now there is a room with one man in it and one woman and a thing I cannot switch off."

"Tell me the shape of what you are going to do."

"A reason." She sat down on the bench and put her hands on her knees. "Your officer has two of the three and has told you so herself, in a corridor, in words. Address, figure, reason. The address is on a leaf. The figure is in two books. And the reason is a sentence about what the thing is, and there is exactly one sentence in this city that nine hundred people have already got, and it is a tune."

"It is a verse somebody bought from a bar for twenty pence on the eighteenth of July."

"It is a verse that has been sung about nine hundred times a month since July and that people have been taking home as their own property," said Isolde Marchetti, "and it has a house in it that fits six streets, and every one of the six streets thinks it is about them. That is not a rumour. That is a thing nine hundred people agree on. Your officer cannot write a paragraph that nine hundred people will believe. I can. In about four minutes."

"And you want to do it."

"I want it very much," she said, "and the true reason is not a noble one."

She looked down at her hands.

"A man in a good coat came to a bar on the eighteenth of July and read a verse out to me off the floor of it, and would not give me his name and would not give me his street." She turned one hand over. "I sang it for him. It went out into a city and did what verses do, and every piece of it has been pointed at you ever since."

"I was the instrument. Somebody bought me and used me and I did not know it, and I found out nine days later from a man who came to ask me where the street was."

"And that is why you want to do it."

"That is why I will not stop when you ask me to." She looked up at him. "In nineteen years I have written or been handed about sixty verses and every one of them was about somebody else's arithmetic."

"I have been right about two things in my life and neither of them ever got past this room. Tonight I am going to sing the one that is true and it is going past this room, and I am going to be the woman who was right about a man in public. You cannot have that and also keep me off the card."

## The Back Room, Again

"You are asking me to say it first."

"I am asking you to say it in this room before I say it in nine hundred." She had not moved from the bench. "Not because of the gift and not because of anything else. Because I have sung a hundred and forty lines written by men who had never heard themselves out loud, and a line changes in a mouth, and I want to know what it is going to sound like before I put it in front of people who cannot argue with it."

"That is not an argument."

"That is the only argument I have got and it is a good one." She got up and turned the bulb down. "And I want to say this while I am standing up being sensible, because I am not going to be sensible again after about four minutes."

"I am not going to use it on a person. I have never used it on a person and I am not starting tonight. If you want to be told what that means, it means I can make a room believe a thing, and a room is not a woman, and I have known that since I was nineteen and have gone on doing it anyway."

"Say what you are going to say to me, then."

She thought about it for about four seconds.

"There is a house at the top of a stair," said Isolde Marchetti, "and the man in it made himself into a number so that nobody would have to answer for anything."

"A quarter of his own street goes past that door in the dark and has been feeding it for four years, and there is not one witness. And on the second of this month a woman he lives with walked up a counter and made the count wrong on purpose."

"That is the reason nine hundred people have a reason to say a thing about a house in this city, and every one of them is right."

He said it.

She made him say it twice more, standing in the dark by a mirror with three bulbs in it, in the voice he used at a washroom counter at five in the morning, and by the third time it had stopped being a thing he was reciting and become a thing he had said.

She did not write it down and she told him she would not.

"You are not going to write it."

"You are not going to write it either," she said, "and I want that said out loud between us, because if you get an ink pen tomorrow you will try to put it on a page and it will not work on paper. It has never worked on paper. It works in a room and I am the room."

He said that too.

## The Room Agreed

She got the front of her dress open with both hands and did it in about four seconds and she was not slow about any of it and she was not shy about any of it.

"Now say it while I am looking at you."

"Not that way."

"That way, and with the lamp off, and I do not want to be asked and I do not want to be helped." She got him a hand's width away from the glass. "I have spent nineteen years being a beautiful noise in a room full of people who wanted to hear something else instead. Tonight I am going to sing a true thing and I am going to be wanted for it, and the wanting is mine, and I have not had one since I was nineteen."

Then she got him a hand's width off the mirror and made him take his coat off and hang it on the bench, and did it herself, and stood looking at him in it for about three seconds the way a woman looks at a coat on a bench.

"Right," she said. "Now come here."

She was not fast.

That was the thing he had not expected and he worked it out about nine minutes later, with her laughing at him about it, that he had been waiting all his life for a woman to be quick, and that quick is what you are for when you want something to be over.

The front of her dress came off over her head because she had decided that buttons were a thing to be got through with, and underneath it she was in what a woman who has done this two thousand times has on, and she stood in front of the glass with the bulbs out and said, "Look at that, and then come here. Nine hundred people have looked at me for nineteen years and there is not one of them in this room."

He looked, because she had told him to, and she watched him do it, and she did not hurry him and she did not explain anything.

Then her hands were flat on his chest and she pushed him back until his shoulders were against the wall with the bulb at the end of it, and she leaned in with her mouth against his ear and made him say the line again, and he said it badly, and she told him it was a beautiful delivery.

"Not like that. Say it to the room. Nine hundred of them. Say it to the wall."

He said it to the wall.

"Good," she said. "Now take your shirt off and do not fold it, because I have watched men fold things in this room and I am not having it."

He took his shirt off and put it on the bench.

She stood and looked at that too, and then she put her thumb flat under his collarbone and dragged it down the middle of him until he was against the mirror and the cold of the glass went through the back of his shoulders.

"Say it."

He said it.

"Nine times tonight, and every time it has got shorter, and that means it is becoming true instead of being recited, and I am the only person in this city who could tell you that about a sentence."

She got her mouth against his throat and then took it away again, which she did three times.

"Now hear what has happened to you in the last twenty minutes. Five days ago you went into that corridor with a paragraph in your coat and could not get it into a shape that was not a description of you."

"You have said it out loud, in your own voice, in a room, to a wall, with your shirt off. You cannot un-say it. There is no instrument in this city or any other that takes a thing back once it has been said in a room with a person in it."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I know it is out of my hands now."

"It was out of your hands on the eighteenth of September in a room with no window in it," said Isolde Marchetti, "and you have spent nineteen days finding ways to make it be in your hands again. Tonight you stopped, and you did it with your trousers on, and then with them off, and I am not going to be tender about how that happened."

She stopped him twice with a flat palm.

"You are being careful with me and I will not have it, and you are going to have to work out the difference between careful and accurate, and it is not the one everybody thinks."

She took his hand off her, put it where she wanted it, and left it there.

"Careful is a man handling a thing. Accurate is a man being in a room with a woman. You have been handling me since you came through that door, and I have had nineteen years of men handling me and I could not tell you one of their names."

"Tell me what you want."

"I want you to say a true thing five more times and I want you to hear yourself say it," she said, "because in about twenty minutes nine hundred people are going to say it and I want to know whether you can stand it when you are not the one singing."

He said it five more times and by the fourth one he had stopped hearing the words and by the fifth one he heard the room change round them.

It changed the way a room changes when the wind gets up a street outside and everybody in it turns slightly at once, and he was the only man in it and so he felt it happen to her instead, which was that her shoulders came down about half an inch and her breath went out of her and she got closer to him than she had been at any point in the hour.

She noticed it before he did, the way she always did, and she went very still for about a second and then laughed at nothing at all.

"There," she said. "You have heard it. That is what it does and it does it whether I am singing or not, and I have just told you the truest thing about myself in eleven years in a room with a bulb in it and you did not do it on purpose."

"I did not."

"No one does it on purpose. That is the whole of the trouble with me." She got up on her knees over him and put her hands on the rail of the mirror and sang four lines of the verse out loud, very quietly, not to him, and the bulb in the corner went a shade brighter and the man outside on the ladder, who was taking down a board about a lamp, stopped moving and stood there with his hand on the board until she had finished.

"That," said Isolde Marchetti, "is my whole life and I would like somebody to be angry about it."

"I am not angry."

"Then say what you are."

"I am frightened," he said, "because you are going to be right and it is going to work and I will not be able to hear it wrong."

She put her forehead against his for a moment.

"Then stay where you are and listen," she said, "and do not try to be careful about it, and when you have had enough come and stand by the tape and I will sing it twice."

They went on in the dark for about forty minutes and neither of them counted it and she did not hurry him at any point and she did not let him hurry her, and when she decided she was going to be finished she told him so nine seconds before she was, because she has always been able to tell what a room is doing and this one she could hear.

She came out of it about nine minutes after the lamp went out completely and lay against him with her ear on his chest and laughed once at nothing, and he put his hand flat on the back of her neck and left it there while her breathing came down.

"Nine minutes," she said.

"Say it again so I can be angry about it."

"Nine minutes." She lifted her head about an inch. "That is not a figure. I do not do figures. I have spent nineteen years in a building where everybody says a figure at the end of a sentence and I am the only person in it who does not, and I have made you lie there for nine minutes past it so that you would have to think about a woman instead of a number for once in your life."

He slept for about four minutes and she let him, sitting up against the mirror with her coat over her shoulders and one of his boots in her hand.

## Twice

There were nine people in the room when she sang it and she did not count them and they did not count themselves either.

She sang the old tune first, verse one, the one about the six and the seven, and nine people who had heard it four hundred times heard it again and moved with it in the way that a room moves, and it was the most frightening thing he had ever stood next to.

Then she came off it and stood in the middle of the floor with her arms down.

"*There is a house at the top of a stair,*
*and the man in it made himself into a figure,*
*and a quarter of his own street goes past in the dark*
*and not one of them can tell you who it is given to or why.*

*There is a woman on a counter who put her own name on,*
*and there is a keeper with a book that does not agree,*
*and there is a man who says the reason out loud in a kitchen*
*and will not say it twice in a room he has to sign.*"

He had said it twice in a room he had to sign.

Lode had his hand over his mouth.

"Nobody put that in," said the woman who took the money. "She made that up standing up."

"Yes," said Isolde Marchetti. "Go home. All of you. Get out through the front and do not talk about it on the Row."

They went.

She scrubbed at her face with the heel of her hand, and what came off was sweat, and behind it was the look of a woman who has sung for two hours straight.

"There is no street in it," she said. "No number, no name, no district. And you will go home and sleep and you will be wrong about why, because what I have just put into nine hundred mouths is not a door. It is a reason. And reasons do not need addresses until somebody in an office asks for one, and when they ask for one they will have to come up here and ask me, and I will tell them where to stand."

"You are telling me you kept the number out."

"I have never promised you anything." She hung the card in its frame. "And I have told you where the reason is and I am not going to tell you what is in the second verse, because you have not asked me about the second verse yet, and when you do, you are going to wish you had not stood in that room with me."

## The Sentence

He was at the stage door.

"You have been in nine hundred mouths since July," he said. "Not this song. A house at the top of a stair that fits six streets, and a figure that fits six streets, and no name anywhere in it, and every one of those six streets has decided it is about them, and I have lived behind it for four months and I have not once stopped to ask whether the shape of it was an accident."

"That was not me."

"I know it was not you." He had his hand on the frame. "And I have been sitting here for twenty minutes listening to you say a true thing to nine people, and I have worked out that I have been treating the July one as a rumour that happened to fit, and it was not a rumour, and it was not an accident either, and I would like you to tell me one thing about it and then I will go home."

Isolde Marchetti stood in the doorway with the card behind her and the lamp still burning through the middle of the night.

"No," she said. "I have never known who brought it in and I have never asked, and I told you that nine days ago and I am telling you again, and if that building ever began writing down who sent what, there would be nothing left on the card inside a fortnight. That is not a scandal. It is a trade."

She pulled the door an inch further open.

"And I have said no to a man in a good coat, and to a man in a grey one, and to a woman from a bureau who asked nicely, and I will say it to a man who has just stood in this room and listened to me be right in front of nine people."

"Was there anybody else who asked."

"There is always somebody else who asks." She looked at him. "Go home. You have until the thirtieth and I have until the next house, and one of us has it easier than the other and it is not you."

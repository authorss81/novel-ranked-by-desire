# A Transfer That Needs A Seal

On the eleventh of November at about ten in the morning he went to the third floor of the Classification Wing and asked Mr Sowerby for the instrument that moves an unrecorded account away from an address.

Fourteen days since the twenty-seventh, when the description had been made. Seventeen days since the list of persons had been fixed in eight hands on the twenty-fourth. Twenty-six days since the song had been sung at about twenty past eleven on the fifteenth of October and put the reason into nine hundred mouths.

Mr Sowerby did not look surprised. He put down the sheet he was reading and folded his hands.

"There is none."

"Say it in full."

"There is no instrument in this city by which a man moves an unrecorded account away from an address," said Mr Sowerby. "An account carried at an address and answerable by the composition of that premises is carried there until it is administered. It cannot be carried forward at a figure its own district book contradicts. It cannot be produced off that door. It can be referred. It can be described. It cannot be moved."

"Then what moves it."

"Nothing moves it," said Mr Sowerby. "A movement of unrecorded merit out of an address and into an account requires a seal. Without one it is theft."

The room had no window. The floor was cold through his shoes.

"Say what a seal is."

## What A Seal Is

Mr Sowerby went to the case on the wall and took down a printed sheet with a list on it. He did not hand it over. He read from it standing up, the way a clerk reads a thing he will not describe from memory.

"A seal is the mark of a person entitled to carry merit for another person. It binds the carrier in writing and in person. One use of it costs the carrier a year of standing. Not a fine. A year. The year is entered against the carrier's own plate and does not come back."

"How many."

"Nine in Ostara that will carry an unrecorded movement," said Mr Sowerby. "Each held by a person of the highest standing. A man with no standing cannot obtain one. He cannot apply for one. He cannot be given one. He can only be carried by one, if the holder chooses, and the holder answers for the movement with a year of his own."

He put the sheet back.

"That is what a seal is and what one use of it costs. That is why the reservoir cannot be moved at all without one. That is why a man who cleans washrooms will never hold one in his hand."

He stood very still.

"And the account I am defending."

"Was not given to you," said Mr Sowerby. "An unrecorded account standing against no ledger does not accrue to a man by accident. It is handed to him by a seal. Somebody carried it to you once, and paid a year for the carrying, and you have been filling it ever since with quiet deeds where no witness could see them."

The floor tilted under him, slightly, the way it had tilted on the tenth of October when Alma Quayle had told him he had all three parts of a description and two were his own work.

"Who."

"I do not know," said Mr Sowerby. "The Wing does not keep the names of carriers. The seal that carries is matched to the account and not to the man. The reason a seal matches is a property of the account. No clerk in this building can tell you whose it is by looking at you."

He wanted, concretely, a name, and he wanted it with a violence that frightened him, because names were what he had spent seven years keeping off pages.

## The Theft

"Say the theft."

Mr Sowerby sat down again.

"If you put your hand on a person who never fed you, nothing moves. If you move merit without a seal, it is theft, and theft of merit is answered at a valuation by paying it or by a production, and you have no production off that door. There will be no production off that door on the first of next month. The last number anybody swears out of that address is eight."

He said nothing, which was assent. He had sworn seven twice, in nine seconds each time, off the same chained book, and a keeper had marked the repetition as a habit. He would never swear that premises at seven again.

"So I cannot move it."

"You cannot move it," said Mr Sowerby. "You cannot route it. You cannot spend it unseen. You can only ease by touch with the soul it came from, and only as easing, with each draw felt briefly by those who fed it."

He rode eleven stops home and got off at his own stop the first time, which he had not done since the twenty-second of October.

In the kitchen he told it straight, the way he had promised.

"A movement needs a seal," he said. "Without one it is theft. There are nine in this city that will carry one, and none of the nine is mine, and none of the nine will answer me."

Beatrix Halm wrote it down with the date under it.

"And the account."

"The account was handed to me," he said. "By a seal. Somebody paid a year to carry it to me. I have been defending a thing I did not earn."

Sun Meiling looked up from the mending she had been doing since the third.

"Who."

"The Wing does not keep the names," he said. "A seal matches the account and not the man."

She held his eyes for a long moment and then looked down again.

Grief came then, plain and adult, not for himself but for the unknown carrier who had paid a year to make him a recipient, and the grief had nowhere to go, so it sat in the room with them.

Nadia Ferrous broke the quiet.

"Then we attest," she said. "If you cannot move it, you can be scored round it. Three attestations move the plate. That has not changed."

"No," he said. "It has not changed."

He went to the window and looked out at the dark street where eight women were now individually reachable, all of them on a list that could not be corrected, and he saw, briefly and completely, what it meant for one man to be carried by somebody he had never met.

The wall would have to hear him before any seal ever would. That was the order. That was the whole order.

He turned back to the table.

"Tomorrow I stand beside people who are finishing things. I do not lift anything."

## What The Corridor Knows

He went down the warm stair past the runner with a slip, past the cloth on a board with a hand beyond it that two men had already tried to raise from that side, and out into the cold yard behind the Wing with the nine seals still ringing in his head.

A clerk in the corridor asked him for the time. He gave it. The clerk wrote it down, because clerks write down what passes.

On the tram he counted the days since the sixteenth of September, when the priced line had been dated, initialled and not signed, out of the counting room in the ordinary way on the seventeenth. Fifty-five days to the eleventh of November, if the months are counted at thirty days with no thirty-first. Fifty-six days in which an unrecorded account standing against no ledger had sat carried at his address, answerable by a composition that had gone from seven to eight at about eleven on the second of October.

He subtracted out loud under his breath, against dates printed on pages he did not carry, and a woman opposite him moved away one seat, the way people do when a man counts in public.

In the kitchen Beatrix Halm wrote seal under the date with a line round it, because a new word in the house goes into the notebook or it does not exist.

"Say it again," said Petra Ilyin. "Without the Wing in it. Plain."

He said it plain, the way Mr Sowerby had said it standing up.

"A movement out of an address needs a seal. Without one it is theft. Nine in Ostara will carry it. One use costs a year of standing. A man with no standing cannot obtain one. The account was handed to me by one. I do not know whose."

Sun Meiling set down the mending she had held since the third.

"Then we find whose," she said. "Not in a file. Files open at dates we do not touch. In shapes. I see shapes. That is my cost and my use. Leave it with me until the twelfth."

He gave her the twelfth without asking what it would cost her, which was a thing he noticed himself doing and did not stop. Fourteen days since the twenty-seventh. Twenty-two days since the nineteenth, when Alma Quayle had signed the referral with the address and the figure of eight on it and a song for a reason.

He left both counts on the table without explaining them, because the table had learned to carry counting the way it had learned to carry lists.

He slept badly that night with the nine seals still in his head, waking on the hour the way men do when a number will not settle.

At five he got up and cleaned the stairwell down to the street door, bucket and brush, cold water to the elbow, work that did not score and had to be done anyway. The building breathed round him. Doors shut softly. Somebody coughed behind a wall. Eight women slept behind a list that said eight because it was made and dated, and none of them stirred.

He wanted, that morning, to be worthy of the carrying without knowing who had carried him, and the want held him upright on the wet steps better than sleep would have.

He checked the stairwell door twice before bed, out of old habit, though the household has been frozen since midnight on the thirtieth of September only in memory now.

The lock caught cleanly. The hall smelled of soap and cold air. Above him eight women slept behind doors with rooms against their names, each reachable, each named, each still his to answer for.

He set the bucket to dry by the sink and hung the brush with its bristles down, precisely, the way four years of washrooms have taught him.

The night porter called the hour from the yard and the pipes knocked once, loud, then settled.

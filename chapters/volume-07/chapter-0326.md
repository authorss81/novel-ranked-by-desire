# The Only Seal That Would Answer

On the twelfth of November at about eight in the evening Sun Meiling asked him to walk with her to the tram stop and back, because she had something to say that could not be said in a kitchen with eight women in it.

Fifteen days since the twenty-seventh of October. Eighteen days since the description had been fixed in eight hands on the twenty-fourth. Twenty-seven days since the song at about twenty past eleven on the fifteenth.

They walked in the cold without speaking for the first four minutes. Then she spoke.

"You were told there are nine seals that will carry an unrecorded movement."

"Yes."

"You were told a seal matches the account and not the man."

"Yes."

"I know whose seal would answer yours," said Sun Meiling. "I have known since the eleventh, when you told it in the kitchen, and I did not say it there because the kitchen is not the place for it."

He stopped walking.

"Say it here."

She kept walking, so he walked with her.

## What She Knows

"I was his research partner for three years," she said. "Before he went out of public record. We traced quiet deeds settling into accounts with no instruments behind them. Through three months of plates. He found that a class of quiet deeds was settling where nobody was looking. He said so in a public hall in front of a hundred and forty people with his score on the wall behind him."

The tram went past without stopping. The air smelled of oil and cold.

"He did not smuggle anything," she said. "He used the only power in this city that requires no rank and no witness. He gave his entire accumulated merit in one movement into an account that exists on no ledger. All of it. In one go. He paid for the carrying with more than a year. He paid with everything he had."

His hands went cold.

"Say the name."

"You know the name," said Sun Meiling. "You have carried his photograph on a tram step for three years without looking at it often. You want rank nine to open his file. His file does not open until you are nine, and you are six under a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-four, and the floor does not move in this batch."

They reached the tram stop and stood under the shelter out of the wind.

"A seal fits the account, not the holder," she said. "Never the man carrying it. Your account stands against no ledger. It is carried at the address of premises 34-6-118. It is answerable by the composition of that premises. There is only one seal in this city that was ever cut to carry an account with that shape. His."

He studied her face.

"I do not understand it."

"I do," she said. "I see the same shape in different data. I lost the ability to see a single thing clearly. I see everything as a set. That is my cost and it is why nobody can tell me when I am wrong. But I am not wrong about this. The account you are defending was handed to you. By him. On purpose. Because you are the only person in Ostara low enough to be an acceptable recipient for it."

Anger rose in him again, this time at the brother and not at himself, hot and disloyal and true.

"He made me a recipient without asking me."

"Yes."

"He paid a year. More than a year. Everything. And left me to fill it with quiet deeds between five and seven in the morning for four years."

"Yes."

"And never told me."

"He told a hundred and forty people the number was wrong," said Sun Meiling. "That was the telling. By morning he was gone."

They walked back in silence. At the bottom of the sixty-one she stopped.

"I am under a permanent bar and I am not going to be sworn," she said. "I told you that in September and it has not changed. I am neither grateful nor ungrateful and I am not going to answer that either. But hear this one thing from me because I am the only person who can say it."

"Go on."

"The seal that would answer is his. You do not get an answer by asking for it. You get the shape of an answer and a date. The date is three years ago. The shape is a man who donated everything he had to an account with no number because the Bureau could not classify what he had done."

She went up the steps ahead of him.

He stood at the bottom in the dark with his hands in his pockets and looked up at the lit windows where seven women waited with a list on a shelf and a receipt on a sink.

He wanted, concretely, to refuse the gift and to keep it at the same time, and the two wants tore at him equally, and he could not choose between them.

He had spent seven years making himself the object so that nothing else had to be. Now the object had a maker, and the maker was the one person he had wanted to find.

He spoke it aloud to the empty street, once, because it needed air outside paper.

"You carried it to me."

The street did not answer. A tram passed. A dog barked once far off.

He went up. The kitchen was warm. Nobody asked where he had been.

Sun Meiling mended the cloth as if she had never left the table, and when he sat down she set a cup in front of him without looking up, and the cup was hot through his cold hands.

In four years a stranger would go looking for the subject of a figure with no subject. He saw that now as a road with a date on it, and the date was three years behind him and four years ahead, and both ends were the same man.

## What She Carries In Ink

They stood at the tram stop until two trams had passed without stopping, because neither of them wanted the kitchen yet.

"You are under a bar," he said at last. "Permanent. Since the first of September, when I signed twice, as head and as auditor, with six lines in my own hand saying the seventh returned line stands blank. You read those lines out loud at a public counter with a queue behind you. You said the bar was permanent and nobody would ever find out from that document who the line is for."

"I did."

"And the pencil row," he said. "Twenty-two stitches in a coat lining. The twenty-fourth of July under it. You wanted it counted and I said yes in front of the room. It went into ink on the first of September. That was the change of auditor made household office. Nadia witnessed it. That is all true."

"It is all true and none of it is the point tonight," said Sun Meiling. "The point is the seal. Hear one more thing about him before we go up. He traced the reservoir through three months of plates. Quiet deeds settling where none looked. He told a hall of a hundred and forty the number was wrong. By morning he was gone into a room with no window at a level nobody outside has visited. That room is not in this batch and we do not open it."

"I know."

"Then know this too," she said. "I waited three years for somebody to ask me the right question. You asked it on the eleventh by trying to move the account. That is why I answer on the twelfth. Not sooner. The question had to be asked first."

They went up the sixty-one in the dark without speaking. At the top Runa Sato waited with the door ajar, back from the counter where she sits until the end of the month and has asked for November as well.

"You walked," she said. "Good. The street watches walking less than standing."

Inside, the kitchen took them back without a glance, the way a room takes people who have been out telling truth in the cold.

He told nothing of the seal at the table that night, at her request, because some things have to be shown once in one hand before they are told in many.

Twenty-three days since the nineteenth of October. Eleven days since the first of November, when he had asked for the list at two minutes past nine and she had written the time without a reason.

He kept both counts to himself, for once, and slept past five for the first time since July, an hour, dreamless.

In the morning he cleaned six floors while the office filled, ate from the staff kettle, carried a bucket up three flights for the same clerk without being asked, and set it down without a word.

At noon he stood at the branch gate letting the scan beep him through, thinking of the photograph of two men on a tram step that he does not look at often. One plate. One knife. One chair. That was his ordinary life on the page. It had held for four years. It would not hold past seven, because seven is a figure on a public wall every week and a man on a wall cannot keep one chair for long.

He thought of the brother's whole want, stated as a number because in this city a number is a want, and of his own want beside it, rank nine for the file, and how the two wants were one want with four years between them.

At four he walked the tram line home past the wall without reading it, saving the reading for Beatrix, who reads walls the way Tessa holds bodies, past the point they should stop.

He oiled the lock on the street door before bed, working the key slowly so as not to wake the ground floor, thinking of seals and years and accounts with no number.

Agnete Lindqvist, ground floor by the street door, called softly through her door that the oil would do, and he smiled in the dark hall, and went up quieter than he had come down.

He hung his coat on its nail and set his shoes square under it, precisely, the way men do when the day has moved too fast to hold.

The yard watchman called the hour through the dark and the pipes knocked once, loud, then eased.

He slept at last with the seal still unanswered and the shape of an answer folded in a woman's pocket one stop past his door.

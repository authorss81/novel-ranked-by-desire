# The Answer That Stopped Being True

The Classification Wing keeps a counter on its third floor for people who have been sent down to it and have not been admitted to the sub-level, and it is the only counter in that building where a man can be told what a piece of paper has to contain without anybody deciding anything.

He was in the corridor at about ten on the ninth of October with two days' reason in him and a pencil.

"Referral," he said. "What goes on it."

Mr Sowerby had been on that counter long enough to have stopped pretending the question was unusual.

"Address, figure, reason." He turned a printed card round without reading it. "Three parts. The address is the address. The figure is a figure, and if it is a figure of persons it is the figure of persons on the morning it goes, and if it is a figure of money it carries the currency with it. And the reason is a statement of the thing as it is."

"How long is the reason."

"As long as it needs to be and not one line longer."

"And it has to be mine."

"The reason is not signed by anybody," said Mr Sowerby. "The referral is. A referral goes out over the name of the man who asks for it, and the reason is a paragraph underneath his name, and it goes into the building with his name on the top of it, and every officer in that wing reads it, and not one of them is allowed to ask you where you got it."

He put the card back the right way up for himself.

"You have been standing there for about a minute and a half deciding whether to write one, and I would like to help you, because I have watched men do this about eleven times and I have never once watched a man get the reason right."

## Three Attempts

The first one he wrote in the corridor on the back of a docket.

*The account is unrecorded and stands against no ledger, and is carried at an address because an address is the only description of it that can be carried.*

He read it four times.

It said *carried at an address*. It did not say why an account of merit should ever have an address, and a man on the sub-level would read *because an address is the only description that can be carried* and understand, correctly, that the thing had been described by a man who had been told what to say.

He tore it in half.

The second one he wrote at the bottom of a counter at a tea stall on the same corridor.

*The account is fed from the street on which the premises stand, at the hours when the staff of the Fourth Branch go to work, and is answerable by the composition of the premises because no witness attends it.*

That one was almost right and it was worse than the first.

Read it back and it said that a man who cleaned washrooms on six floors between five and seven in the morning had an account, and it said it in the only register in this city that cannot be checked, and it said it in a hand that went on page one.

He put it in his pocket and did not tear it, because he wanted to feel it there all day.

The third one he did not write at all.

"That is three," said Mr Sowerby, who had not been watching him and had been stamping something else the whole time. "You have been on that corridor for about forty minutes and you have had two pieces of paper on it and you have taken one of them away, which is the only information I have ever needed."

"You said you had watched men do this about eleven times."

"Eleven times in four years and four of them ended with a referral that was refused and two of those four were refused by me." Mr Sowerby put the stamp down. "I have never yet seen a man write the reason."

"Has anybody in this building ever got it right."

"No," said Mr Sowerby. "But two of them got it honest, and an honest reason that is wrong about the reason is worth more in this building than a correct one that is worded like a form, because we can check a true thing and we cannot check a well-written one."

## The Vant's Rooms

Marguerite Vale had the best rooms in this city that anybody allowed him to stand in and she put a cup beside the plate of ink rather than in front of him, which was an old trick and she knew he knew it.

"You have come to ask me to move a line I cannot move."

"I came to ask you what it would take."

"Nothing that is available." Her own book lay open in front of her and it was not the real one. "Dated the sixteenth, initialled, out of that room in the ordinary way on the seventeenth, in four divisions by the end of the week."

"Once a sheet of that kind has left the room it was written in, there is not one person in Ostara who can take four words out of it. And the woman who wrote it handed me the keys to that room on the twentieth of September and has not looked at it since."

"You could go down there."

"I have not held those keys since the twentieth of September." Marguerite Vale turned her cup about a quarter of a turn. "And even if I held them, it is not arithmetic. It is a dated fact about a street and it is not mine to unprice."

"Then what do you have."

"Four lines in a drawer, and an undertaking with my name on it countersigned and dated, and a table I cannot eat at." She shut the working book. "And about twenty-nine years of the other thing, which is knowing what this city's instruments do before they do it, which is worth exactly nothing at this table and is why you came."

"I did not come for that."

"You came because you have got it into your head that a woman who kept the real books for thirty years might be able to say out loud what the account is worth." She put her hand flat on the closed book. "It is worth seven. It has been worth seven since the sixteenth and it will be worth seven on the thirtieth of this month."

"And if it is described it stops being worth anything at all, because a described account is administered rather than priced, and there is not one clerk in this city who has ever administered one and got away with it."

"So being described is worse than being carried at the wrong figure."

"Being carried at the wrong figure is a mess that a broker can hold," said Marguerite Vale. "Being described is a mess that a wing has to answer. I have spent three weeks working out the difference and it is the difference between an argument and a verdict."

He put his hand into his coat and touched the second piece of paper.

"There is another one of these," she said, not looking at him.

"There is."

"Say what it is."

"It is what I would have to write under my own name to send a referral up to that corridor. One paragraph. It says where the account is fed from, and it is true, and it is a description of what I do between five and seven in the morning."

He took the paper out and did not unfold it.

"I wrote it at a tea stall on that corridor this morning and I have had it in my pocket for seven hours and I have not been able to turn it into a sentence that is not about me."

"That is because it is not about anything else," said Marguerite Vale, "and you have known that since the sixteenth and you went down those stairs anyway."

## Two Doors

He stood up.

"Give me the arithmetic once more, on your own, and do not do it kindly."

She did it kindly the first time and then, when he stayed standing, she did it the second time in about eleven seconds flat.

"Carried, referred, described."

"Carried means you swear a figure of persons on the first of next month. The figure is eight. The eighth name goes onto a chained page in a cold room, it is in four divisions inside a week, and every division that reads a premises book in this city will know which door it belongs to."

"Referred means a paragraph in your hand, with your name at the head of the page above it, before the thirtieth. Read by officers. Cannot be taken back."

"And described."

"Described is not a door. You have had it in front of you since the sixth and you keep counting it as one because it would make three." Marguerite Vale looked at him. "Described is what happens in an office, to a man who has run out of October. Every month in this city stops on the thirtieth, and then it stops."

"And it is done by people," she said. "Clerks with stamps, sheets with numbers on them, rules they have had since before you were at the Fourth Branch. They are not waiting for you and they are not angry and they are going to be extremely good at it."

"So there are two."

"There are two and one of them has a woman's name at the end of it," she said. "That is the whole of your month, and you have known it since the second, and you have spent a week at a table persuading eight women that a problem with a figure in it is a thing that can be shared out."

He got to the bottom of the sixty-one steps at about six and did not go up them, because there was a woman sitting on the hall floor of Ferrier Street with her back against the banister rail.

She had a coat on and a case beside her that was not a case of papers, and she had been there long enough that the light through the fan window had moved off her hands.

"Nobody told me," she said, before he had said anything. "I gave my name at the door and the woman who came down asked me whether I had an appointment, and I said that I did not, and she said that you would be back at six."

"How long have you been sitting there."

"Since four." She stood up and picked the case up. "I have not come up sixty-one steps into a stranger's hall to find out what a household is, and I have not come up to ask you for anything, and I am going to tell you that in the corridor so that it is said once and then I am going to go back down and sit on the floor again."

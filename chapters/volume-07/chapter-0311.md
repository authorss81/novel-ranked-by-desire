# The Classification Of A House

There is a counter on the third floor of that Wing for anybody who has come down the stair and not gone any further, and on the morning of the twenty-second of October there were four of them at it and Mr Sowerby was stamping the fourth of four forms of the ordinary kind.

"Your account is in this building."

"I know that it is."

"You have not been sent for, and there is not one officer in this building going to send for you, and you have been on this floor three times now without an appointment, which is more than anybody else has managed since the spring." He put the stamp down. "What do you want."

"I want to be told what a description of premises is obliged to contain."

Mr Sowerby put both hands flat on the counter and looked at the middle distance for a second and a half, which Lin Hao had learned in October is what a clerk does when he is deciding whether the answer is his to give.

"Three parts is a referral," he said. "Address, figure, reason. You have that and you have had it since the nineteenth and there is a woman downstairs who put her name under it."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know it is not, and I am going to answer it anyway because it takes nine seconds and you have been on my counter three times." He turned a printed card face about without reading it. "Four parts is a description. Address, figure, reason, and a list."

He got up and went along the shelves behind the counter, which is not done on that floor while a member of the public is standing at it, and came back with a folded sheet that had been folded and unfolded about forty times.

"Take this off me and do not put it on my counter. Premises nineteen one six, a block of twenty-two dwellings, north of here, described in March." He put it down and flattened it with two fingers. "Seven lines of names. Seven dates. A figure against the top. And at the foot of it, printed on every one of them since before I came here." He put his thumb flat on it. "*A public record.*" He moved the thumb along. "*Any division may read it. No reason is required.*"

He read the first line out loud before he handed it over.

"*Persons of reading age at those premises on the morning of the fourteenth, and in the order of the stair.*"

## What Is On It

Lin Hao read the whole thing standing up.

Nothing on that sheet said what a person was. It did not say a trade and it did not say a band and it did not say whether anybody on it had done anything at all, ever, in their life. It said seven names and it said how long each of them had been there and it said that any officer in any division in Ostara was entitled to walk in, ask for it, and be handed it without giving a reason for asking.

That last line was printed. It was not a judgement and it was not a clerk's opinion and it had never been argued about, because it was the one part of the form the whole of this building agrees with.

"It is not a judgement," said Mr Sowerby. "It is worse than a judgement and it is much cheaper. A judgement has to be argued with. That has a date against it and an appeal and a person who signed it. That is a list, and anybody in this city may carry it out of the room in their hand at four o'clock and take it home."

"Why does it exist at all."

"Because a building can be described with nobody in it at all," said Mr Sowerby. "You can describe a cellar and a stair and a water supply and a roof and have a perfectly good description of premises with not one person on it anywhere, and it is useful and it is ordinary. And the moment the description touches a household it stops being that, and the list is what makes it stop, and the list has been printed since before either of us came here."

He put his thumb on the foot of the sheet.

"And it is not a form of words in it. If it were a form of words somebody would have said so to the person who wrote it."

## What A Division Does With One

"What does a division do with it."

"That is the question everybody ought to be asking and never is," said Mr Sowerby.

"Nothing. That is the answer and it is the whole of the reason the foot is printed the way it is. A division takes a list and matches it against its own book. A plate office matches it against plates. The Vestry matches it against a list of the dead, because a person of reading age who is not alive stops keeping an address."

"That is four matches and none of them is a decision."

"None of them is a decision and every one of them is a person at a desk doing their job at nine in the morning," said Mr Sowerby.

"There are eleven divisions in this city that keep a public list. If yours goes out on one day, by the middle of next month it has been matched eleven times, and not one of the eleven had a thought about it."

"And that is not an accident of temperament. It is what happens when you build a machine out of eleven clerks and tell none of them that there is anything in it."

"Who reads it first."

"That is in the third of the eleven at the head of a sheet in this building and it is not my business and it is not yours either." He took the folded sheet off the counter and put it back where he had got it from. "You wanted the contents. Those are the contents."

## The Dates

"The dates are the part I would think about if I were you."

"Eight lines."

"That sheet has seven names on it, and it is a block of twenty-two dwellings with a room in each of them."

"A door and eight people on a stair will carry eight lines."

"That is what I am asking about. Will every one of them have a date against it."

"A composition has to be able to answer what it was on the morning of a call. The only way a book answers a question about a day that has already gone is by being able to say how long each person has been standing in the room." Mr Sowerby squared the sheet about.

"So every one of your eight will have a date, and it will be the day she took the address at that door in the ordinary way. It is a clerk's date and not anybody's opinion, and it is the part of that list that will still be there in forty years."

"And the order."

"Top of the stair down," said Mr Sowerby. "Not by seniority and not by length of keep. By the floor. Because that is the order a reader of a house already has in his head and a form that fought him would be thrown out at the counter in about a month."

He pushed the sheet across.

"You will be at the top of it," he said. "That is not a guess. A head of premises sleeps where he sleeps, and you have been at the top of that stair for four years."

## The Form He Was Not Going To Be Given

"Can it be written against a man instead of against the address."

Mr Sowerby stopped.

The counter went on being a counter for about four seconds, and there was a queue behind him, and a young man with a folded application in his hand who had been standing there since before half past nine.

"You want to be the subject of your own premises," Mr Sowerby said.

"Yes."

"There is a form for it." He said it flatly and without any pleasure at all. "It is not in this room and it is not mine."

"And I will not tell you what is on it. I have not got it in front of me, and I have said in this office for four years that I do not describe a sheet I cannot produce."

"It is in the sub-level. The woman who holds it is Alma Quayle and she has been the head of that wing for longer than I have been on this counter."

"You have told me where it is."

"I have told you where it is, and I have told you it exists, and neither of those is advice." Mr Sowerby lowered his voice about half a tone, which on that floor was the whole of what a clerk could do. "The fourth thing is the only opinion I have ever offered anybody at this counter, and after it I am going to call the next man."

"Go on."

"It is cheaper than the other one." He held up a finger before he could be answered.

"That is not a criticism of it. It is how these things work. An office can administer a man and argue with him about it for eleven years and put a file open and close it."

"An office cannot argue with a house. If you describe the address, the description stands and everybody in it is a line, and there is nobody to appeal, nobody to examine, and nobody who can be said to have consented to anything."

"And if you describe me."

"Then you are in it," said Mr Sowerby, "and every officer who reads that sheet knows which man is standing behind the eight lines, and they will come and find you, and they will be able to, and you will be able to say so."

He took the folded sheet back off him and put it on the shelf.

"There is also the other thing, which is that about four people in this city know that form exists and two of them have used it, and both of the men who used it are alive, and one of them still writes letters."

"Would you use it."

Mr Sowerby looked at the queue.

"I am a counter clerk on the third floor," he said, "and I have been here four years, and I have a stamp and I have told you where it is, and that is the entire extent of what I am going to say about any of it."

He put the counter bell under his hand.

"Next."

## Eight Days

Lin Hao got down to the street at about twenty past ten with eight days in front of him and the sub-level stair in his head and the week out on Hanneke Ruis's note.

He had not sent her a third question. It had been the fourth week.

The thing he had learned at the counter was small and it sat badly.

The door he had spent seven years keeping shut was not shut. It had been a form in a drawer in a wing he had been down twice, and it had been there since before he came, and every officer in the building who had ever been handed a thing that would not reconcile could have had it, and about four had.

He had come up to the counter to be told what goes into a description and he had been told, and the description was eight lines long. And the form that put a man in the middle of his own address had to ask one question more than the other one does. He had not seen it and was not going to see it until the sub-level, and he already knew what it said.

He stood in the street outside the Wing for about a minute and a half and then went and got the tram and rode it eleven stops and did not get off at his own stop the first time.

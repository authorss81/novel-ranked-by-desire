# Her Own Rooms, And Her Own Word

He knocked at nine in the morning on the twenty-eighth of November and she opened the door before the sound had finished, which told him she had been standing on the other side of it for a quarter of an hour.

Ines Duarte took the wallet off him in the doorway and did not open it there. She carried it to the table under the window, which is the table she has read every record on for eleven years, and set it down, and put her hand flat on it for a moment before she took her hand away.

"Sit down," she said. "You are going to be in the room. That was the first term."

"First term," he said. "Not today. Nine in the morning. Both of us in the room."

"Second and third will come when you have earned them." She untied the string. "Second. I will answer you about everything I read. Third. After today I am not the person you come to when you are frightened. Say the third one."

"After today you are not the person I come to when I am frightened."

"Good." She drew the first sheet out. "Now be quiet for about forty minutes and let me work."

## Four Sheets

She read them the way she read everything, aloud, moving her finger down the left margin about a finger's width ahead of the place her eyes were, and the two rooms filled up with an ordinary woman's voice saying out loud the shape of his life to nobody but him.

The first was the description. Address, figure, and the three lines of what the account was recommended to be treated as. Dated the twenty-seventh of October, signed at the foot by the head of classification, initialled once. Made against the head of the premises and not against the address, at his own asking, in a kitchen.

"Against the head," said Ines Duarte. "That is unusual and it is cheaper to administer and easier to appeal, and the woman who signed it thought about both of those before she granted it and was right about both of those. You did not argue her into it. You asked for it. Do you know how rare that is in eleven years."

"I know."

"You would, if you had sat in this chair," she said.

The second was the fair copy, initialled, which went out for check on the twenty-eighth and came back on the twenty-ninth. She read the check column twice and then a third time.

"Four reads," she said. "One is hers, one is the check, and two are not stamped at all, which is not unusual and is not clean. In eleven years I have learned that a file with two unstamped reads in it is a file that somebody in this city has wanted to see and has not written a reason for, the same way a person asks for a copy of a list at a counter without a reason because none is required."

"Two people in the four divisions that hold this have now read your figure and have not signed for having read it."

"Who."

"I do not know and I am not going to guess at a table," said Ines Duarte. "A guess written in the air of a room with two people in it is a guess that gets repeated. What I will tell you is that neither of them marked it, and a man who wants a figure does not usually let it go unmarked."

## The Eight Names

The third sheet was the list of persons of reading age keeping that address, arranged the way a staircase arranges itself, with the day against each holding and the room against each name, entered in eight hands in a kitchen on the twenty-fourth and checked by counter-forensics on the twenty-ninth.

She read all eight out loud, with the rooms against them, in the order a staircase decides.

Sun Meiling, top floor, kitchen. Runa Sato, top floor, rear. Petra Ilyin, first floor, front. Wren Kallo, second floor, fourth door, counter-forensics, Bureau of Merit. Beatrix Halm, second floor, second door. Nadia Ferrous, third floor, first door. Isolde Marchetti, third floor, last door. Agnete Lindqvist, ground floor, by the street door.

It took about ninety seconds and nobody in either room breathed.

"That is not a list of anybody," said Ines Duarte. "That is a list of eight women in stair order, and it is cheaper than a judgement and it says nothing whatever about what any of them is, and it went out on the ordinary stream at four in the afternoon on the thirtieth of October and it has been in eleven divisions ever since and fourteen by December. I could not tell you one thing about any of those eight that is not printed on that sheet."

"No," he said. "You could not."

"You wanted a sentence at the end of it," said Ines Duarte, "and here it is: a list does not carry work. A list is cheaper than a description of a person, and the reason your account has eight on one sheet and seven on another is that the eight is the figure that happened and the seven is the figure somebody swore in a chained book in nine seconds, and both of them are true and neither of them corrects the other."

"You are not a liar. You are a man standing between two true numbers with his hands open."

Then she said the thing he had not come for.

## The Woman Who Checks The Eight

"Counter-forensics, Bureau of Merit, second floor, fourth door," said Ines Duarte. "The composition of those premises is to be certified on the thirtieth. On the thirtieth of this month. By the Bureau of Merit, at the address, by the officer whose posting is printed on the fourth line of a list that is not a list of anybody."

"She asked for it in writing," he said. "She put herself into that leaf on the second of October at a counter in a queue and she has said so out loud four times and she is the price of her own division for it and I have not asked her a single question about that address and I am never going to."

"She asked to be the officer who checks the house she sleeps in," said Ines Duarte. "I have been an auditor for eleven years and I want you to hear the shape of that, because I do not think you have sat with it."

"The one instrument in this city built to look at eight women in a building is a woman inside the building. Not a woman paid by them, not a woman who agrees with them. A woman who took an address there at a counter in a queue, in her own name, on purpose, and who cannot now be examined about it by anybody because the person who would examine her is the head of the premises and he is forbidden to ask her."

She put the sheet down.

"Four days from now that woman will certify the composition of her own address, and the certification will be the only clean instrument in this file, because it will have been made by somebody with a conflict so enormous that it could not have been invented."

"You have not said anything I have not already worked out."

"I know you have not," said Ines Duarte. "I said I would not do it kindly."

## What It Cost Her

She squared the four sheets together and then stopped with her hand flat on the top one, and went still in a way he had seen four times in nine years and had never once got used to.

"A record held in the head etches," she said, "and the etching is paid for out of me. You know the rule. You have known it since the first months of us and you have never once been the man who spent it on me."

"What have you lost."

"I can give you the count," she said. "There were four sheets. There are four. Two of them I can hold word for word and I will hold them until I die. One I can hold in substance and not in wording. And one is gone."

She put her fingers on the top sheet.

"This one. Four sheets, and this one has no address and no words in my head. I know what is written on it because I am looking at it and I read it a moment ago, and I can see the shape of it as a shape the way you can see the shape of a room you have slept in for eleven years."

"And there is nothing behind it. There is no morning, no desk, no street outside, no reason I went there. It is the first day I have lost that I know the name of."

He did not say anything, because there was nothing.

"I have held records for eleven years," said Ines Duarte. "I have lost four years of my own twenties and I have done the arithmetic on it more times than you have done yours, and I have always known the price in proportion."

"This one is not in proportion. I lost a day I cannot date, and I know what I was holding, because what I was holding is the only record I have ever read that had something cut out of it, and I have thought about that hole for eleven years and I can no longer remember the day I first thought about it."

He looked at the four sheets and did not ask which one. Asking would have cost her another page.

## What She Put Back And What She Kept

"The term is finished," he said.

"The term was finished at about a quarter past three yesterday afternoon on a landing," said Ines Duarte. "You are the one who finished it. I want that said in this room by the man who did it and not inferred by me."

"I am the one who finished it."

"Then it is finished, and neither of us is going to be kind about it, and we are not going to sit here for an hour deciding how we feel about it." She put the four sheets back into the wallet and tied the string, and then untied it again. "And now I am going to do the thing that is going to be in this flat in four years when somebody asks me why I held that file."

"What thing."

"An auditor who has read a record writes down that she read it, with the hour, and keeps it." She drew a sheet of paper towards her and wrote four lines on it in a small level hand. "That is not a favour to you and it is not a document about you."

"It is a note in my rooms of four sheets I read on the morning of the twenty-eighth at nine, and I keep it because that is what I am, and it goes in the flat and it does not go to a division, because there is no division that has any right to ask an auditor what she read in her own rooms and I have spent eleven years making sure that stays true."

"Then what is it for."

"It is for the day," said Ines Duarte, "when you come up this stair in a worse year than this one and ask me what I am holding. It will be on this table with a date on it and neither of us will have to remember anything."

She put the four sheets into a drawer under the table and shut it, and put the note on top of them, and then sat down opposite him with her hands in her lap.

"That is what the ninth of July cost," she said. "Not a woman who could not be written down. A woman with a drawer."

Eleven days counted from the nineteenth of November, and the eleventh of them ending at midnight tonight.

He stood up to go, and stopped, and said the only thing he had come to say and had been putting off since the landing.

"I am not going to pretend that was about the file."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "You were not."

# The Count That Went Down In Public

The plate that went up at half past four on the twenty-second of November was a day old by the time he got to the square on the twenty-third, and a day is enough. By six o'clock in the evening of the day it went up, a thing he had once been invisible inside of had been read by about forty people who had never read it before.

He stood at the north end of it at ten past four with Beatrix Halm and the light going.

She read it from the bottom, the way she read everything, with her finger an inch off the paint and her body between him and the rest of the length.

Then she stopped at the top and went back three lines.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-nine," she said.

Five under the floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-four. Thirty-one under the painted line for seven. Out of band, by four more than the tolerance.

Above the plate, where a fortnight ago two words had stood in a green no painter in that square had ever mixed for him, there were now two words in flat white lead.

*Out of band.*

They had come back the way a word comes back when somebody paints over a colour that was not supposed to be there. The green was under the lead and would come out again in a wet winter in about four years, and until then there was nothing on that wall about him except a number and two words that had last been there on the thirtieth of July.

Beatrix wrote the figure down with the time against it, and wrote the two words under it, and did not write the colour.

## Forty People

"There is a boy taking money on the corner and a man who sells hot water," she said. "I counted nine distinct people read that plate between half past four yesterday and half past five this morning and two of them said the words out loud."

"They say things."

"They said *out of band*," said Beatrix Halm, "out loud, to each other, the way you would say the name of a street. That is the first time that word has come off that wall since the thirtieth of July. For four months it was a fact about a man and this week it is a thing about a street."

The painter came past with a bucket. He was not the one who mixed the green; he was a boy doing the branch stalls and he had painted over the green that morning because the weekly takes what it is given and this week's order was bands and a plate.

"Who told you to cover it," he said, when Beatrix asked.

"The office," he said. "It is not the quarterly shade. You cannot leave a colour like that up for a week. If a man went out of band you paint him flat like everybody else and if he comes back you paint him again and if he keeps coming back you ask the office for a shade."

"And if he goes out twice."

"Then you cover it twice and it comes out in the wet."

He went off with the bucket. Beatrix copied the whole exchange onto the page, exactly as he had given it, because that boy has stood in front of this wall for nine years and does not read it the way a clerk reads one.

## The Woman Who Attested

The laundry woman came up the hill at about twenty past four with her basket on her hip and stopped a yard off, and read the plate from where she stood, and read it twice.

Her name is Herta Vogel and she had finished her round early three mornings running in the second week of November, and the basket clerk ruled early against her name with the hour against it, three times over, and on the last of those mornings added in her own writing that the early starts had followed a morning in the yard when a man's hands had been on hers.

One slip. Three mornings. One hand that was not his.

She read the plate a third time.

"That is not a person being careful," she said. "That is a person being counted."

"Yes," he said.

"I put three mornings of my life into a book at a counter because a clerk wrote down that I finished early." She set the basket down on the kerb and rubbed the side of her face with the heel of her hand, the way a woman does when she has been on her feet since half past four. "And the wall says you are under the floor. So the three mornings are still in the book and the thing they were supposed to lift has gone down anyway."

"They cost you and they did not lift anything."

"I would do them again." She said it immediately, without heat, the way she said everything. "I want that said in front of this wall. I would do them again tomorrow. But I am telling you what it is to be a person who carries somebody else's legibility in a book with a date on it, because you have not had to carry anything in five years and I have had four years of it."

"You can have your name taken off the slip."

"Can I." She looked at him properly for the first time. "How."

"That is not a thing anybody can do."

"No," said Herta Vogel. "Then that is what it is to have done a good thing for a man nobody had heard of. I still have it. That is the whole of my reward and I would like it observed."

She picked the basket up and went on up the hill, and did not look at the wall again.

He watched her go and understood, standing there with his hands at his sides, that a wall is not a punishment. A punishment has a person in it who decided something.

A wall is a machine that runs once a week whether or not anything has happened to anyone, and the only human thing about it is the two or three people in the street who read it and say something out loud, and one of those had been carrying his name in a book since the first week of November and had just told him she would do it again.

## The Man Who Asked Him

Two men were arguing about a shade at the railings and one of them turned round and looked along the length of the wall and found a man standing under a plate with his own address on it and no hat on.

"You're the one," he said. "Seven, aren't you. It was green eight days ago."

"It was green eight days ago."

"Where'd it go."

He could have said a great deal, and it would all have been true, and the man would have believed none of it and repeated all of it by morning.

"It went down," he said.

"Why."

"Because I have not done anything anybody can write down for a week."

The man looked at him with the flat interest of a person who has found an explanation he would have arrived at himself.

"That'll do it," he said, and went back to the shade.

Two hours after that a woman he had never seen put her head round the edge of the plate and asked him whether it was true that the people at that address were the ones in the song, because she had heard the address out loud with a plate against it on the twentieth of October and had never put the two together until she saw the number go down.

He said he did not know what song she meant.

She looked at him the way people look at a man who is not being clever.

"You'll know what song I mean," she said. "Everybody knows what song I mean. You've been under that plate for four months and nobody could say who you were and now you're a story with a number on it and that's how it goes, isn't it. The number goes down and people start asking."

He wanted, concretely, to be a man that strangers could not ask. That was the whole of the sixth volume and the seventh one behind it, a man no division could read, and he had paid for it with three women and a green shade, and on the evening of the twenty-third of November he got his first question from a woman with a shopping bag.

## The Clerk With The Book

There was a young man in a plain coat at the south end copying the plate into a book, and he was doing it slowly, with a ruler, the way it is done when a figure is going to be compared against something.

Beatrix watched him for a while and then came over.

"He does that every week," she said. "November, the seventeenth, the tenth, the third, and every week back to March. He copies the plate and the day and the hour into a column and he does not write one word about what it means. In four months he has carried eighty-four figures down into the books of eleven divisions and eleven divisions have matched every one of them against something they hold."

"He is the reason the green mattered."

"He is the reason anything on that wall matters," said Beatrix Halm. "That boy does not care that you were seven eight days ago and are not tonight. He cares that a figure went into a column in a form that has the day on it."

"When a figure moves the other way it goes down the same column in the same form, and on the first of December there will be a man who can put the two sets of numbers side by side and draw a line from the top of one to the foot of the other, and it will take him about a second and a half, and he will not know what he is looking at."

She shut the notebook.

"Twenty-four days since the thirtieth of October," she said. "Fifty-two since the second, when the leaf took eight. Twenty-seven since the twenty-seventh of October, when the description was made against your head with your own reason clause on it. I do all of them out loud now at the bottom of these stairs because you taught me to do it in a kitchen and because there is nothing else on a wall to count."

"You have been counting the wrong thing."

"I have been counting the only thing that is mine," she said. "Ask me in December."

Four days since the nineteenth. Six of the eleven still to run, and on the twenty-ninth they stop.

He looked at the plate one more time and walked to the bottom of the sixty-one, where Beatrix Halm was waiting with the notebook shut and the pencil in her hand and the two words on the wall behind him going blue in the dark.

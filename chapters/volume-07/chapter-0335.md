# A Figure Under A Floor

He put the question to the counting room in writing on the morning of the twenty-fifth of November and the answer came back up the sixty-one steps on the ordinary stream the same afternoon, in a hand he did not know, on the back of his own question, with no covering letter.

*Q. What follows an account carried below the floor of its band.*

*A. It is callable. The floor is set by another office in another hand on the last working day of a quarter and does not move within the quarter. An account carried below the floor of its band for one week is callable at the next valuation. The valuation is the first of next month at ten to nine. A called account is answered by paying it or by a production. The mark is entered by the broker holding the book and not by the keeper of the counting room.*

Under that, in the same hand, and smaller:

*The priced line of the sixteenth of September for premises 34-6-118 was initialled and not signed. It is answerable by the composition of those premises. The books that hold it were carried to a guild table at seven on the morning of the sixteenth and are now in hands you did not appoint. I have written the answer because you asked it in writing and it was answerable in writing. I will not send a third question in this week. — Keeper, counting room.*

[Ledger. One hundred and nineteen lines. Nine turned.]

He read it twice on the top step and then he did the whole of it out loud, standing on a stair, to the stairwell, because he had promised himself in July that if a thing like this ever came to him he would hear himself say it rather than find it true in a book a week later.

The plate had printed on the morning of the twenty-second at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-nine. The floor was eight thousand one hundred and forty-four. He had been under it for three days.

On the first of December, at ten to nine, in the chained real book at the Exchange, a man with a pen on a chain round his wrist could enter a mark against an account that has been called, and there was no production off that door to answer it with, because he would never swear that premises at seven again, and eight was the last number anybody was going to swear out of thirty-four six one one eighteen.

And the books holding the line had gone out at seven that morning to nine people, and the guild read them in the ordinary way at nine, and there are now copies of the Vant's priced line in four divisions, and by December in fourteen, and every one of those divisions could enter a call on the first of December through a pen that belonged to a person he had never spoken to.

He had known this on the nineteenth of October. He had known it in a kitchen on the third of November when he asked to be seen.

Six days since the nineteenth, and five of them left, and the last one ends at midnight when the twenty-ninth is over.

The sentence that came after all of it was not about a figure.

The instrument that could take his account off him on the first of December belonged to a woman who had put her own book on a guild table nine days earlier, on purpose, in front of nine people, so that a kitchen would know what exposure cost.

He had done that in about nine hours after she had asked him for the one thing she asked anybody for, which was a position that did not depend on the Vant, and he had said no, correctly, because he has no standing and no seal and cannot carry anybody.

## The Yard Behind The Vestry

He went down to the clinic at six on the afternoon of the twenty-fifth and stood in the yard behind it with his back to the wall, which is where he had been every afternoon since the nineteenth, and which is a place where a man can stand for an hour and be nothing more than a shape against brick.

Tessa Okonkwo came out at half past seven with a basin and did not stop.

"Seven days," she said. "She has been up twice. She went up the stair to the second landing this morning and sat on it for a while and came down again, and she has asked me twice what the weather is going to do and I have told her both times."

"And the eleven."

"The eleven is the one I have not got." She set the basin down on the wall. "Seven I can do like breathing."

"Eleven I can do the way a man can carry a weight that is heavier than he is, which is to say I can do it once and from that day it is in my chest for a year and I do not know what I will be doing on the twelfth day at eight in the morning and neither does she. That is the shape of what you have bought with eleven dates."

The yard went dark from the top down the way it did.

"You knew what the under-floor would do," she said, "and you did not ask me, and I want you to know that I worked it out on the afternoon of the nineteenth, about an hour after you left, and that I told you the price of the eleven and not the price of the rest of it, and that this was not a kindness, because you would have stayed and I needed the eleven."

"You should have told me."

"You would have stayed," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and she would have died on the ninth."

She went in with the basin.

## Two O'Clock In The Morning

He was asleep at ten past one on the night of the twenty-fifth and awake at ten past two, in the way he had been awake every night since the nineteenth, in the room at the top of the stairs with the light off.

Runa Sato came down the outside stair past his door in her stocking feet and he heard her go down and out into the lane and then come back in about four minutes and go up again very fast.

He was out of bed and into the hall in one sock before she had the second stair.

"What."

"A body came down," she said. "From your fourth floor. A bin with it. She is on the tiles by the letter-box and she is awake and she is going to be extremely difficult to move and there is a lamp on her chest and it is lit." Runa Sato was breathing through her mouth. "What is on the fourth floor at two in the morning."

"A bin store," he said. "A water tank and a barrow and a woman called Wanda Rej who has done that floor for eleven years and is in the Vestry ward with a heart."

"Then get my coat and do not put your hand anywhere." She was already going up. "I am the Field and there is nobody in this city to send for at two o'clock in the morning and I have carried a man eight hundred yards off a tram platform in July and I can carry her three hundred."

## The Woman At The Foot Of His Own Stair

Wanda Rej was on the tiles on her back with her right leg out straight and her left one under her, and the bin had gone over on its side half a metre away with its lid against the wall, and the lamp on her chest was still burning and throwing a yellow circle about a yard wide onto the floor of a hall that eight women had walked through at ten o'clock that night.

She was conscious, and she got a hand up off the tiles before he was down the last step.

"Don't lift me," she said. "I know what this is. I have had it before. You get the leg and you get the other thing, and then I am a woman on a floor with everybody standing about."

The rest of it went unsaid.

He knelt on the tiles on the other side of her from Runa and his hands were about four inches off her sleeve and he put them on the floor instead, flat, palms down, the way a man puts his hands down when he has decided something and does not want to see himself decide it.

The eighth of eleven, and the ninth beginning somewhere behind her in the dark. Eight marks could have gone on her hand tonight and gone off again by morning, and the pain would have gone with them, and he would have had that, and there is no instrument in this city that could ever have found out, and he would have bought the whole of it with a thing no office in this city would accept as payment.

"Get the leg," Runa Sato said. "I have the shoulders."

They got her out of the hall and across the lane on a barrow the porter from the branch brought at Runa's word, and the woman went to the Vestry in the cart at about twenty to four because the Vestry is the only building in this city with a door that is open at three in the morning and a doctor behind it.

Wanda Rej got the barrow up off the lane herself, with one hand on the frame and one leg in the air, and while they were tipping it she turned her head and found him standing in the road in one stocking and a shirt and she knew him.

"You came up," she said.

"Yes."

"Two o'clock. I told you to go to bed." Her face was grey and sweating and she was making the joke on purpose to keep something else out of her mouth. "And I am going to tell you what I said I would and then you can go and sleep."

"Nobody knows about the marks but you and me and a doctor, and I am not telling him either because he will write it on a chart. So it is not going any further from tonight. Not to the laundry, not to your women, not to anybody. You understand me."

"I understand you."

"Say it back."

"It does not go any further from tonight."

She shut her eyes for a moment and then opened them and looked up at the sky, which had gone the colour it goes at twenty to four in November.

"Then that's the night's work done," said Wanda Rej. "Eleven years of nights and that's the one where somebody came up. Go inside, for God's sake. You've no shoes on."

Eight days gone, and the eleventh ends at midnight at the end of the twenty-ninth.

He stood in the road in one stocking until the cart had gone, and then he went up the sixty-one steps and sat down on the top of them in the dark with his shoes in his hand, and did not go to bed, and did not open his hands.

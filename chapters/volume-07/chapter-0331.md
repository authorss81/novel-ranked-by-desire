# Eleven Days Of Not Spending

The Vestry clinic kept a corridor outside the ward that nobody had cleaned since the spring, and on the nineteenth of November at about half past eleven in the morning he stood in it and waited while a doctor finished being right about a man's hands.

[Ledger. One hundred and eighteen lines. Nine turned. Nothing will be spent for eleven days.]

Three days since the morning of the sixteenth, when a woman had cleared her own table and put her books on a guild table at seven. Twenty days since the thirtieth of October, when eight names went out on the ordinary stream in a leather tube. Twenty-three days since the twenty-seventh, when a description of premises was made at nine with his name at the head of it and a reason clause in four words about money.

He had been doing that subtraction on platforms and in trams for three days and it kept arriving at the same figure: the man no division could read was now a figure on a wall, and a figure on a wall can be put back down.

"Three minutes," said Tessa Okonkwo from inside. "Do not come in with your hat on."

She came out with her sleeves pushed back and the cloth ledger under her arm, shut the ward door behind her, and stood against the far wall so that he had the length of the corridor between them.

"Do not sit down," she said. "You will only get up again and pretend you did not."

"What has happened."

"Wanda Rej came in on the eighteenth at nine at night with a basket and a slip from a district infirmary that says she should rest," said Tessa Okonkwo. "She has emptied the bins on your fourth floor and three other buildings on that lane for eleven years, from two in the morning, by the load, for a contractor who has never once written down the name of the woman doing it."

"She came because she could not get up the second stair at the infirmary and she would rather be a woman who came than a woman who cannot get up a stair."

"What is wrong with her."

"Her heart has been going since the spring and she has spent eleven years going up and down four flights with a bin at two in the morning, and a heart that has been going since the spring does not care that a woman is used to it." She shifted the ledger against her ribs. "That is the ordinary answer. Here is the one you came for."

"She is turned."

"She is turned and she is not the ninth," said Tessa Okonkwo.

The corridor had a window at the end with bars on the outside and the light coming through them in stripes, and he looked at the stripes because looking at her was going to cost him the ability to say the next thing.

"Four," she said. "Four cold minutes, over nine months, and she has written all four on the back of her left hand in a carpenter's pencil with the date beside each one. She could not keep a book. She keeps it on her hand. She knows about as much as anybody keeps and she has told nobody, and she told me on the seventeenth because she thought a doctor should know."

"Where."

"Six hundred yards," said Tessa Okonkwo. "A yard on the fourth floor of your own building. Two in the morning, the fourteenth of October, and you had not been in the building since the evening before because you were at a woman's rooms by arrangement. You had no hands on anybody that night. Nothing was given and nothing was taken. And she took it anyway."

The stripes moved a little as a cart went past in the yard.

"That is the part that frightens me and it is not the part that frightens you," she said. "Nine is a number with a shape. A good analyst takes nine and draws the shape, and the shape has a district in it. Ten is a number with an address in it."

"If you put a hand on that woman once, she feels the minute, she puts it on her hand beside the other three with the date, and from that day there is a list of five dates in her own writing and a doctor in this city who knows about all five, and I am the doctor, and I would have to write the word source on the first sheet of her chart."

"How many charts have you opened this year that had turned lines on them."

"Two," said Tessa Okonkwo. "One of them was a gate porter in March and I never found out and it has been in my frame since March like a chair I cannot get out from under."

"Then I do not spend."

"You do not spend." She let him have that without softening it. "Not once, not on her, not on the porter in the yard, not on anybody. Not on the ground floor of that building with the valve that weeps. Not even on a woman who has been standing at your gate for four days waiting for you to be ill. Nothing. Eleven days."

"Why eleven."

"Because that is what I have." She opened the cloth ledger, turned it round, and held it out flat so that he could read the line she had ruled across the foot of a page, with the dates written under it in her small hand.

"I can hold a body past the point it should stop. I have done it nine times and the longest was twelve days and that was a boy with a chest." She let him have the page. "I do not know that I can hold this one eleven. I know that I can hold it eleven and that is a different sentence and you will not improve it by asking me again."

He read the line twice. Eleven dates, the nineteenth to the twenty-ninth, and under the last one a stroke.

"On the twelfth day she is a line in my book," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and a line in my book is a record with this clinic's name on the top of it and a woman's chart in a division that answers to the Vestry. She is a turned woman who came in with four cold minutes written on her hand, and on the twelfth day there is a document about her with my name against it, and I have no power to stop an office reading a document that exists."

"On the eleventh day she is a woman in a bed with four pencil marks on her hand and nothing whatever in anybody's file. That is the whole of what eleven means and it is mine and not yours."

"You are spending yourself to hold her."

"I am spending myself to hold her," she said, "and I am telling you what it costs because you are the kind of man who pretends he does not know. Every day of those eleven days takes room in my frame that I do not get back this year."

"You have not spent a grain in five volumes. I am about to spend about eleven days, and nobody will attest any of it, and I have four turned lines in my own book that are going to get worse while I do it. Do not thank me. Thanking me is the cheapest thing you have ever done and you do it when you cannot pay."

He put his hands in his pockets because they had begun, without permission, to shake, and she saw it and said nothing, which was the correct thing and the hardest.

Then she took a slip from her coat, wrote on it standing against the wall with her own pen, and gave it to him face up.

"Nineteenth of November," she said. "Nothing given to that patient from any hand outside this ward for eleven days. The nineteenth, the twentieth, the twenty-first, the twenty-second, the twenty-third, the twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh, the twenty-eighth, the twenty-ninth. Okonkwo. Bring it back to me on the first of December or I will assume you are dead and put it in this ward's book as dead, which is a thing I have done once and would rather not do twice."

He read it out loud in the corridor, because reading it silently would have been a private promise, and he had spent five years refusing to let a private promise be the instrument anything in this city was done on.

This was the first one he had ever sworn that was worth nothing and cost everything, and it had eleven dates on it.

"Nineteenth," he said. "Twentieth. Twenty-first. Twenty-second. Twenty-third. Twenty-fourth. Twenty-fifth. Twenty-sixth. Twenty-seventh. Twenty-eighth. Twenty-ninth. Eleven. The first full day of it is tomorrow morning and the last of it ends at midnight at the end of the twenty-ninth."

He said it in the ordinary voice, and it went down the corridor and hit the barred window and came back at him, and he counted out loud because that is the only instrument this city has that cannot be forged and cannot be taken back and does not put anybody else's name on anything.

She took the slip back off him and put it in the ledger, and closed it.

"Now the part you have not said," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"I am not going to say it."

"Correct."

"It is a song and it is in nine hundred mouths and there is no author and it cannot be reproduced in writing, and a woman who has been in my kitchen since the third of October would have to be told the reason to understand the arithmetic, and if she is told the reason she is a second person alive who knows it, and a second person alive who knows it is a document."

He stopped. "I am not saying it because I do not have to and because there are six divisions in this city that would open the file on the strength of me saying it in a corridor."

"Six," she said. "Go home."

## What It Costs In Public

At the top of the sixty-one he stopped, because the arithmetic was waiting for him and had been waiting since the morning of the fifteenth, and it was cheaper to do it in daylight than to carry it.

The plate went up at half past four on the fifteenth with three women on it — the laundry, the gate, the yard — and three attestations moved it from eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight to eight thousand one hundred and eighty-four.

Four days had passed. He had done nothing that a clerk could write down in any of them, because the only things he had done since the fifteenth were sit in a woman's rooms and clear up after himself, and a man who cleans up after himself is not a deed.

A plate is not a memory of what a man did. A plate is a figure for one week.

So it would go down. He knew the direction and not the size, and the direction was enough, because a plate that goes down does not stop at the line it crossed on the way up.

The line for seven was eight thousand one hundred and seventy and it was painted on a board at the north end of the square and it had been painted there before he came. Under it was the floor, eight thousand one hundred and forty-four, set by a different office in a different hand on the thirtieth of September, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.

And below the floor an account carried at a figure is not an account any more.

It is a thing that can be called.

He had known that on the nineteenth of October, in a sub-level, with his own reason clause going out on the ordinary stream. He had known it at the third of November in a kitchen when he asked to be seen. He had known it for seven volumes, and he had spent seven volumes arranging for nothing to be able to ask him for anything.

He was going to keep one woman alive for eleven days with a slip in a ledger in a clinic, and the price of it was that everything he had built would come down in front of strangers, in daylight, in the only colour a painter could get.

He wanted, concretely, to be a man who could do both, and there was no instrument in this city that would let him, and he had spent five years refusing to find one and he was now paying for the refusing in a currency nobody had told him about.

Down the hall Sun Meiling had the table laid with nine places and eight of them were full by the time he got in, and the ninth was the empty one that had been the empty one since the beginning of October.

She looked at the place at the head of the table and then at him.

"Eat," she said. "You look like a man carrying a date."

He sat down and picked up his cup, and the tea was hot, and the day began with the whole of the arithmetic sitting across from him at eight chairs.

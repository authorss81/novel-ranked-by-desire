# The Report That Was True And Useless

The flat was on the second floor of a chandler's on Cordwain and it had been requisitioned for eleven days as a post for the holding of a return, and there was a slip in a wire frame on the door with a number on it, and there was a mattress on the floor of the front room and a table with a lamp on it that had been on since about six.

Runa Sato had been awake since four and she had written for two hours and she had three-quarters of a sheet done.

"You have come to see what I am putting in it."

"I have come to see what you are putting in it and I came at eight because I thought you would have started at six."

"I started at six because it has to go up at four and it is my hand and not a clerk's, and I do not write a return in about nine minutes." She did not look up. "Take the chair. Not that one, the other one. That one has the working on it."

He took the other one.

## The Return

She had the whole shape of it on the sheet and it was seven lines and a table, and he read it standing behind her shoulder because she was not going to stop.

*Composition return, premises 34-6-118, Ferrier Street, under standing order of the twenty-seventh, October.*

*Persons of reading age keeping an address there on the morning of the thirtieth: eight.*
*The composition stood at seven on the first of October and at eight from the second, the change being made at a counter, in the ordinary way, by a person then resident there.*

*The account carried at that address was referred for classification on the nineteenth and a description was made on the twenty-seventh.*
*On the nineteenth the head of those premises would not complete his correction, and the refusal went onto the district leaf with a date against it.*

She turned the lamp down about an inch because the sun had come round onto the yards.

"That is five lines and a table and every word of it is true," she said. "There is not one sentence in it that a person could take and go to a division with."

"How can that be."

"Because a return is a figure and a date and it answers the question it was asked," said Runa Sato.

"They asked me how many. It is eight. That is the answer."

"They did not ask me what the eight of them are. They did not ask me who fed the account. They did not ask me whether the change of composition is connected to the classification. They did not ask me anything about the man."

"A standing order on a composition does not mention a man, because a composition is an address and not a house and not a person."

"Then it will satisfy them."

"It will satisfy them completely. There will be a figure on a sheet at four o'clock this afternoon, and the sheet will go up the chain, and somebody will read it and find it correct." She put the pen down.

"And then in six months somebody will ask the same question again, because that is what a standing order is."

"It is not a question. It is a standing invitation to the same desk, and the answer goes in a file, and the file is a reason to ask the next one."

"How many times."

"Four times in nine years I have had one on an address." She squared the sheet under her palm and did not let go of it.

"The first one I did not answer and I was wrong. The second one I answered and it was used against a woman at that address and I was wrong again."

"The third one I answered and it was used against the same woman and I did not know for two years."

"The fourth one I answered the way I am answering this one, and nothing came of it for a year and a half."

She picked the pen up again.

"This one I have counted on my fingers, and it is nine. It is the first of the four in nine years that I have been in the same building as the people it is about."

## What He Said About It

He said the thing on the way in and he said it badly, standing behind her with his hands at his sides in a flat above a chandler's on Cordwain at about twenty past eight in the morning.

"File the truth."

"I am going to file the truth."

"Then that is all I came to say and I will go and let you finish it."

"You will sit down," said Runa Sato. "You came up sixty-one steps to this address four times in the last year and I have watched you walk out of this building twice without saying a sentence that had anything in it, and this morning you have said one sentence and it was four words and it was an instruction."

"It was a request."

"It was an instruction and I am going to take it, and you are going to sit down, because in about eleven minutes I am going to finish this sheet and then there is nothing to be done in this flat until four o'clock this afternoon, and neither of us has anywhere to be."

She signed it, dated it, squared it, and slid it into an envelope with a number on the outside, and she did all of that in about two minutes, and then she put the envelope on the table under the lamp with her hand flat on top of it.

"Now sit down."

## The Lamp

The lamp was the only warm thing in the flat and the sun on the yards came up off the boards at about half past eight, and the mattress was against the wall where she had not put it that morning, and the whole room smelled of cold wool and chandler's oil and the paper of the envelope.

She took the copy off the top of the envelope and gave it to him.

"Take that. It is the only document in this business that says something true and cannot be used for anything at all, and I want a man who has spent seven years making instruments to hold one in his hand for about a minute."

He held it.

Then she came round the table and stopped in front of the chair.

"Right," she said. "Four things first, and none of them are a condition, and every one of them is true, and I am saying them in the order I have got them in."

"Go on."

"One. I have wanted this since the twelfth of October, I have not told you, and I am not going to pretend it arrived this morning."

"Two. I am not going to be told it was a kindness. If you say the word kindness I will stop. I have not had the chance in about four years and I am not giving it up on a morning when an envelope is going up the chain at four."

"Three. I am filing that sheet whether you were on this floor or not, and I decided that at about seven this morning, and it had nothing to do with you." She put her hands on his shoulders.

"Four. You have an afternoon in it and I have nothing in it until six, and the building is cold, and I am not going to be careful with you and you are not going to be careful of me, and if either of us starts being careful the other one stops."

"I am telling you that now so that you cannot claim in a month that you did not have the terms."

"You have never in your life not had the terms."

"I have never in my life been this frightened," said Runa Sato, "and you are going to find that out in about four minutes and you are not going to be kind about it."

She got the coat off him first because he was in the way and because she wanted to do it and did it the way she did everything, in about ten seconds, and dropped it on the back of the door, and then she got her own belt off over her head with her teeth and dropped it on the chair and did not ask him once for a hand with it.

"Right," she said. "Shirt."

He took it off and did not fold it and she did not ask him not to fold it, because she had told him the terms about being careful and she was not going to start a second conversation about how he held a shirt.

She is a stone and a half heavier than she looks and all of it is in the shoulders and the back, and the only part of herself she has ever handed to anybody is the part that carries, and she let him have that for about half a second before she took it back.

"You have been treating me like a woman doing an operation," she said. "For eleven days. You have been in and out of this city asking me to tell you what your office will call things, and I have answered every question you have put to me, and you have never once in eleven days put a hand on me, and this morning you have come up sixty-one steps to give me an instruction in a flat above a chandler's."

"I did not know you wanted—"

"I have wanted it since you came down those stairs on the nineteenth and I did not tell you, and that was mine."

She got a fist in the front of his shirt and pulled him up off the chair and turned him and put him face to the table. Not roughly, and not fast either, with her palm spread between his shoulder blades for a second and a half, as though she were checking that something was level.

"There. Now you are a man doing an operation about something, and you can tell me what your office calls it and I will tell you what I call it, and it will not be the same word."

She was right about the fear and he had not expected it to be a physical thing.

His hands were on the wood and she did not ask him to move them. She picked the belt up off the chair, put it round both of his wrists and the rail behind him, and did the knot with her teeth the way a woman does a knot she has tied some hundreds of times, and he felt the whole apparatus of his own politeness come apart in about four seconds and understood most of it by the second.

"Turn your head. No. The other way. I want the window behind me and not the door."

The window was behind her. It had been behind her the whole morning.

She got him clear of his trousers and her hand closed on him and he made a noise in the front of his throat that he had not planned to make. She stopped for about half a second to look at what she had done to him, and then started again at the pace she wanted and not the pace he wanted.

"Wrong," she said.

He was not slower.

"That is one of the two," said Runa Sato. "You get two, and I do not tell you which ones they were until the hour is up. That is the rule and the rule is mine, and you may put the whole of it in a report on me."

He put his forehead on the table and she put her hand in his hair at the back of it, once, hard enough to be felt and not hard enough to be a correction, and went on working him with the other hand in the same rhythm, and told him about the window.

"The sun is on the yards and it is going to be on this room by about ten. In about an hour you are going to want to put your coat on because you will think you are cold, and you will not be cold, and I am going to let you think it."

He was cold. That was the part he could not get round for the rest of the morning.

She got her mouth against his ear about a minute in and told him what the sheet going up the chain at four o'clock was going to cost her, in order, in the flat voice she used for a briefing, and she did not stop while she did it.

The names were two. The first was the woman who would open his file when those eight lines were matched against every book in the city that keeps one, and the second was the division that would send it to her, and between them they took about nine minutes off somebody's life and she described both of them the way she describes a route off Cordwain to the sub-level.

She was gone through the middle of the fourth item and made a noise in her throat nine seconds later, and she did not stop talking.

"That is the list and that is the order it will happen in, and it is not a complaint, and if you turn round now and try to discuss it I will stop where I am."

He did not turn round.

She took him off the table and made him sit down on the edge of it with his back off it and her hands underneath him, and she was not careful about that either. Then she got his shirt open down the front and put her mouth on him under the jaw where a man keeps his pulse, and held it there long past the point where he could have counted it, which was the first thing in eleven years he had not been able to reduce to a figure.

He put his hands in her hair and she let him, and that was the whole of the permission and she gave it early and without being asked.

She got down onto her knees on the boards and took him into her mouth, slowly, and then at a speed that was not a consequence of anything he had done, and she looked up at him the entire time with the briefing face still on, handing him dates in order.

"You are going to want to close your eyes," she said around him. "Do not. I have been in four rooms today and in three of them somebody in this city closed his eyes and did not see what he was agreeing to."

So he watched her, and she watched him watching her, and at some point the two of them stopped being able to hold the arrangement up.

"Say it," she said, when she got him turned round and up against the wall with the cold of the plaster through the back of his shoulders. "Not to me. Say it in this room."

He did not know what she meant and she told him, and what she wanted was for him to say the reason clause out loud in a room with a woman in it, which he had done twice in five weeks and never once to anybody who was going to answer.

"It goes up the chain at four," she said, "and there is not one officer in that building who is going to know what is underneath it, and you are going to say it into a wall in Cordwain so that I know you have said it into something that is not paper."

He said it to the plaster.

She made him say it twice more, badly the first time and better the third, and at some point in the second one she stopped listening to the words and started listening to the room, which is what she does, and she laughed once at nothing at all.

"That is better than the first time and you will not believe why," she said. "The first time you were reciting. You are not reciting now and you have not noticed it, and I have, and that is the whole of what I have wanted out of any man for four years and I have never once been able to ask for it because it takes about forty minutes and no officer in this city has forty minutes."

She had him back to the table for that, and then off it, and then on the mattress against the wall with the sun coming up off the boards and finding her shoulder, and neither of them turned the lamp out and neither of them turned it up.

The boards had a cold in them that came up through the wool blanket and she worked around it without once mentioning it, and when she had him where she wanted him she sat back on her heels and looked down the length of him and took her own time.

She was not tender at any point, and she said so out loud twice. The first was when he got a hand round the back of her neck and stopped, and she lifted it off him and laid it on the mattress and told him that if he was going to do that then he was going to do it properly, and he did.

The second was a minute or so later and she said it into his shoulder without stopping what she was doing, and by the time she had finished he understood that she had not been telling him anything at all.

The second wrong one came without any warning at all, and it was not the pace. It was that she stopped telling him what anything was going to cost and started telling him what she wanted instead, in the same voice, and he understood about four minutes after that the two were the same voice and had been the whole time.

Somewhere in the middle of it she gave the briefing voice up entirely and started talking in the other one, the one with nothing measured in it, and he found out in about two minutes that he had never in his life heard her use it and that he had been waiting since the nineteenth for it without knowing there was a thing to wait for.

When she decided she was going to be finished she said so out loud nine seconds before she was, which she can do, and she came out of it lying along his back with her hand over his sternum and both of them breathing.

"Nine minutes," she said.

"You keep giving me figures."

"I give figures because that is what I have instead of the other thing," said Runa Sato. "And I have had four years of a lamp in a room with nothing else to do and I am not going to say the other thing at eight in the morning."

She stood and got her shirt off the chair and put it on and got her belt, and put her hair up off her face, in that order, and sat down on the mattress with her boots still on.

## What She Filed

She picked the envelope up off the table and held it.

"This goes up at four. It is true and it is useless and it is the first return I have filed in nine years that I would be willing to be cross-examined on by anybody, including the man it is about." She looked at him. "And it is the last one I file that does not tell them anything."

"You said you would answer the same question again."

"I said that a standing order is a standing invitation to the same desk." She stood up and put the envelope under her coat. "And I have been sitting with it since six this morning, and I have worked out that I would rather that the next invitation went to a desk with me on it than to a desk with somebody else on it."

"That is a standing order on those premises."

"That is a standing order to observe the address of premises 34-6-118 and the persons keeping it," said Runa Sato, "which I applied for on the twenty-seventh with my own name at the foot of it, and which was granted in about four minutes by a division officer who has never heard of the eight of you and did not ask, and I applied for it before I knew I was going to answer any order at all."

"You told me last night that you could have refused."

"I can refuse it. It is in my hand at the top of a column and I can strike my name off it in a moment, and this division will put somebody else's name on, and that person will never stand in a kitchen." She kept her palm over the envelope through the wool of her coat.

"I have not refused it, and you cannot protect me from it, and I want you to hear the shape of what I have just done."

"Because I have done it on purpose, and about four people in this city will call it a stupid thing, and one of them will call it career."

"You are telling me you want it."

"I am telling you that a man who keeps himself unfindable is a man who has arranged for a woman to be findable instead," said Runa Sato. "And that I would rather be the one holding the order. And that this is not about him and it is not about the eight of you and it is going to cost me something I have not found yet."

She got the case off the table.

"Now go and be somewhere else for six hours, because I have to be on that desk at half past three and I cannot take this envelope up in a street with a man I have been in bed with, and this division has four women in it who will talk."

# A Line That Went Cold

Beatrix Halm was at the bottom of the sixty-one at ten past six on the morning of the twenty-first of November with her notebook shut and her pencil behind her ear, which is the arrangement of a woman who has come to say something and has not decided what it is yet.

She did not start with the wall.

"Nine years of walls," she said. "Eighteen hundred mornings, give or take a fortnight. I have got every plate you have ever stood under in this book in this hand, including the one in the green, including the morning the green went on and the morning the man at the wall said it out loud like a joke."

She took the pencil out from behind her ear and did not use it. "I have never once come down these steps to tell you about anything except a wall. This is not a wall."

"Then what is it."

"Something went out."

She said it flatly and looked up the hill past him, at the row of windows where the kitchen light was still on, the way she looked at a signal cabin.

"Give me the shape of it."

"I cannot." Beatrix Halm said it with what looked, from ten feet, like a kind of anger. "That is the part. If it were a person I could have it in a minute. I have had three people go out of the current in four years and every one of them I could stand on a platform and put my hand on and say this is the one."

"Last night at about eleven something that had been giving for years stopped giving, and it had a shape, and it was the shape of a stove in a room with the door open, and it went out in about the length of a breath."

"How big."

"Small," she said. "That is what has me standing here in the cold. A tram load. Not a house. A tram load of heat coming out of a room, and it had been coming out of it every evening for years, and at about ten past eleven on the night of the nineteenth it stopped, and it was not an easing. Nothing in this city eases like that. Things are on or they are off."

He had said in a corridor the day before that he did not have a single reason to give her and would not invent one, and he stood on the step and discovered that the sentence he had prepared was useless because it was the truest one available and true things do not stop a woman who reads the current for a living.

"What did you do about it."

"I got on the line at half past five and I went the whole way down and back twice." She shifted the notebook under her arm. "I put my hand on the rail at every stop where I have ever felt anything from that kind of room. Nothing. Not a trace of a cold-out. I have never in nine years found nothing."

## What She Is Going To Do

He could have told her. The whole of it would have taken ninety seconds: a woman on the fourth floor, four marks in carpenter's pencil, a doctor in the Vestry, and a line in his own head with nine faces on it.

He did not, because a woman who is told where a source is becomes a second instrument pointed at it, and there were eleven divisions in this city that would sooner or later ask a woman with a notebook why the current of a whole district had a hole in it shaped like a stove.

"You were not doing anything yesterday," he said.

"No," said Beatrix Halm.

The word came back without any edge on it at all, which was how she put a thing down when she had decided to keep it.

"You have not slept," she said.

"No."

"That is two things you have told me and neither of them is an answer." She wrote something in the notebook with one line, no flourish, and shut it.

"I am not going to ask you again about it. I have known you for five years and I have watched you refuse to say a thing for the sake of six other people so consistently that I could set a clock by it, and last night something went out in this district about the size of a tram load, and you had been awake, and I am writing down that you told me you were doing nothing."

"Write it."

"I have written it." She put the pencil behind her ear again. "And here is what I am going to do with the rest of it, and I want you to hear it now instead of at the end of the week. On the twenty-fourth I start at this table."

"I have eight names on a list and every one of them keeps an address here and every one of them is a person I can feel, and I am going to put my hand on the eight of them one at a time in an ordinary morning and find out which of us gives anything to anybody at all."

"You will not find it."

"Maybe not." She took a step up. "And if I do find it, Lin Hao, I am not going to be able to do anything about it. I want that in my own notebook before I start, because I know what I am. I will find it and it will be a person in this building and there will be nothing in this city I can do with it, and I will have to go on taking your cups at that table."

"Then don't do it."

"I have to do it," said Beatrix Halm. "You have spent a month making me into a woman with a use. A use means somebody depends on the answer. You cannot hand a woman a job and then take the work away and expect her to go back to being a tram operator with a headache."

She went up the stairs without waiting, and he stood at the bottom of them in the cold.

## The Man In The Hall

She had not been up thirty seconds when Runa Sato came down past her with her coat already on, going the other way at a fast walk, and she did not stop but she spoke over her shoulder going down.

"Eleven o'clock yesterday. Twenty minutes. He wrote the time and the count of persons and one line I could not see from the landing, and he shut the door behind him so that it did not bang, and I let him past because he has a paper and I do not have one that says anything." She was at the bottom now. "Nobody in this building stopped him, Lin Hao. Not one woman on this list, and one of them could have, and you know which one."

He did know which one.

Agnete Lindqvist's chapel was four streets away and a person inside it cannot be compelled to testify. It had been true for eleven years and it was the only room in Ostara where a Field officer with a book could stand in the doorway and get nothing at all out of anybody inside.

"That is the shape of the thing you built," said Runa Sato, "and it works, and I have just walked past it twice in one morning. Write that down as well."

She went down the hill at the pace she kept for everything.

## The Safest Man In The District

He went up the sixty-one and cleaned his room and did not eat, and worked out what had happened in the six hours since he had stood in a clinic corridor, which was that he had done one thing and it had worked, and that the shape of what it cost was not yet visible from the inside.

The relief he carried was not stored. He had believed for about four years that it gathered, and it did not; it moved, and it only moved when it was put into somebody, and a man who does not put it in does not have less of it. He has the same eleven years behind him on the twentieth of November as on the nineteenth. That had always frightened him and it had never once been the kind of fear that made him careful.

This morning it made him careful.

He was the safest person in the district. There was no relief going into anybody in a radius of six hundred yards with his hand on it.

In three volumes there had been a man in a good coat at a bar, a date gone missing from a hearing, a clerk who found the wrong record, a shape on a page, a shape on a page, a shape on a page, and he had lived on the fact that every one of those was thirty years old and referred to a man called Lin Hao at premises 34-6-118, which is an address on eight lines.

Right now that man did not exist as anything at all. Right now there was a hole in the current of a whole district, and it had the shape of a stove, and there was no instrument in this city that could connect a hole to a man who was not spending.

Two days since the nineteenth. Nine of the eleven still to come, and the eleventh of them puts out the lamps at midnight at the end of the twenty-ninth.

He wanted, with a plainness that surprised him, to be able to put his hand on somebody. Not on the eight. On anybody, in a street, for a second, the way he had done five times a day since he was a junior clerk, and to watch a stranger let out a breath they had been carrying since the middle of October.

Instead he walked to the wash-house yard at half past four in the afternoon, and the grille on the branch side was running, and water was going over the lip and pooling where it pooled, and the clerk who wrote the slips was on the far side of the yard and did not need him.

He stood there for a minute with his hands in his pockets and watched a thing he could have cleared with a bar in nine seconds going down a drain by itself, and it was the first time in this book that the ordinary good had been available to him and not to his hands.

Then he went home and told eight women at a kitchen table that he was not going to be able to give anybody anything for eleven days, and eight women looked at him, and not one of them asked him a question.

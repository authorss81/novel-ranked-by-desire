# The Sentence She Wants To Write

He reached the bottom step of the sixty-one at about ten past six on the evening of the twenty-sixth, and Alma Quayle was sitting on it with her case between her boots, in the ordinary way, as though she had been let out.

"You have not written the reason."

"I have not written the reason."

"You have had four days and you have not written a line of it, and I have had four days and I have not either."

"I am going up these stairs with you, and I am going to read you something on the half landing, and you are going to stand on the other side of me while I do it."

They went up together at the pace of two people carrying nothing.

They were still on the second landing when a woman came down past them with a bucket and said good evening to neither of them, and the door at the top shut, and neither of them had said anything before that could not be said later.

"The building knows I am here," said Alma Quayle. "The woman on the second floor has told about four people since the nineteenth and about one of them is a clerk at a district office, and by the end of the week there will be a version of me in this street that does not have a wing in it."

"Do you want her to stop."

"I want about nine people in this street to know that the woman who classifies houses is the woman who asked eight of them to fill in their own lines in a kitchen, and I cannot have that and I have had to choose, and I have chosen the eight."

## The Half Landing

The fan window on the half landing has been painted over twice and never opened since the war, and the light in it at six in the evening is the colour of weak tea, and there is a place on the wall about shoulder height where nine coats have been on it.

She took the sheet out of the case and gave it to him and did not say anything while he read it.

It was four lines and it took him about eleven seconds and he read it three times.

*Referred for classification on the nineteenth of October. An unrecorded account carried at premises 34-6-118, Ferrier Street, standing against no ledger and answerable by the composition of those premises.*
*It has moved for four years, with no witness to any movement and no instrument behind it.*
*A correction was left half filled at those premises on the nineteenth, and a clerk wrote down what had been asked and what had not been given.*
*It is recommended that the account be treated as unadministrable, being a figure with no subject.*

He raised his head.

"That is shorter than I thought it would be."

"It is four lines and it has been four lines since the morning of the twentieth," said Alma Quayle, "and I have taken about four minutes a day off it for six days, which is more revision than I have done on any sentence in this building since I came into it."

"Where is the reservoir."

"There is no such word." She said it without any weight at all. "There is no such word in this city and there is no such office and if I put it in the first line then the description is a description of your contents before it is a description of a street, and a stranger opens the file at four o'clock and reads one word and stops reading."

## The False Part

"The last line."

"The last line."

"That is not true."

"No," said Alma Quayle. "It is four words and it is not true, and it is the only part of that sheet I have not been able to make myself leave out."

She took the sheet back off him and held it by the bottom corner with the window light behind it.

"A figure with no subject. And there is a subject," she said. "There is a man who gets up at five and cleans six floors of washrooms for the Fourth Branch and walks a tram line home past a wall with his own figure on it, and I have never met him, and there is a subject, and he is the reason that sentence is false and he is the reason the other three lines are true."

"You wrote it to keep me out of it."

"I wrote it because in thirty years I have signed a great many sentences that were true in three parts and false in the fourth, and the fourth is always the part that decides what happens to a person."

She took the sheet back down.

"That is not a confession of a practice. It is an explanation of an instrument."

"A description that says an account cannot be administered because it has no subject closes the file. A closed file is administered by nobody, ever. The account stays where it is, priced, at a figure the man's own household gave it."

"And a description that says nothing about the subject."

"A description that says nothing about the subject is a description of a number, and a number cannot be examined and cannot be argued with and cannot be appealed, and it will simply go on being wrong in two buildings until somebody in an office with a stamp runs out of patience." She turned the sheet face down. "They will find him inside a week. Not because I wrote it down. Because I did not, and the space where it is not is a space an officer fills."

The stairwell went on being a stairwell.

## Why She Did Not Send It

"You wrote this on the nineteenth and you did not send it."

"I wrote it on the nineteenth and I read it once and I did not send it, and a clerk wrote down that I had not sent it, and I found out at four in the afternoon that he had."

"You knew he would."

"I knew it exactly, and I said it in the tone of a woman who writes things down, because I am one." Alma Quayle did not sound remotely sorry. "Thirty years of arranging to be the woman who finally says what a thing is. I did not intend to have it entered in a division because of how I said a sentence out loud in a corridor to a man taking trays out of it."

"Why did you not send it to me."

"Because I wanted you to hear it from me and not from a pigeonhole."

"And because you would have answered it, and you would have answered it in a form, and your answer would have gone into this file above my four lines."

"A man who has never met you would have read both and believed yours. You write like a man who is being careful and I write like a woman who is being fair. There is one of you per office and I have thirty years of that and I know which of the two gets believed."

"That is a long way round."

"It is the shortest way round and I have walked it for thirty years." She looked up the stairs towards the door at the top.

"And I will tell you the rest of it, because you will otherwise think I have been protecting you."

"I am not protecting you. I wrote the sentence on the nineteenth and did not send it because I wanted to be in the room when you heard it. Then I went back and did not write anything else, and it has been seven days."

## What She Is Going To Ask Him

"The sentence has to go out with the description."

"The sentence has to go out with the description, and it has to be written, and it has to be written by me, because I am the officer who signs the reason clause and there is not one other person in this building who may write it."

"Can you not write it."

"I have refused to write a reason clause about a house for four years and there is a form in my office with four years of refusals on it." She folded the sheet once. "I am going to write it on the twenty-seventh, in your kitchen, in front of the eight of them, and then I am going to date it and sign it and put it under the description, and the description will be made and issued at four o'clock."

"And the reason you wrote this one on the nineteenth and have not sent it."

"Because you are not going to answer me tonight, and I would rather say the sentence out loud than watch you read it."

He turned his head towards her.

"You have not asked me anything yet."

"I am going to ask you one thing and I have not asked it yet, and I am going to ask it on the twenty-seventh at seven o'clock in front of eight women." She turned the sheet over again and put it back inside the case.

"A description of that house, or a description of you. That is the question and it is the only one."

"I am going to put it in a room with eight people in it, because a man who is about to be described in front of his own household deserves to have the household in the room."

She shut the case.

"Read it at that table tonight," she said. "Read it out loud if you can get through it. And work out which four words are not true, and come up those stairs on the twenty-seventh and tell me, and if you tell me what I already know then you have understood it, and if you tell me something else then I will think about it for the rest of the evening and probably give you what I have written."

She went down four steps and stopped.

"It is a figure with no subject," said Alma Quayle. "Four words. And the reason a person who writes them for thirty years gets them wrong every single time is that a figure with no subject is the only figure in this city that every officer wants to be the man who found the subject of."

He went up the rest of the sixty-one steps and stood at the top of them in the dark with his hand on the rail and understood the whole thing at once, standing up, which is the way it always happens to him.

The sentence she wanted to write was a refusal to describe him.

And a refusal to describe him was a description of him.

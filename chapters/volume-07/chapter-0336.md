# The File He Asked Her To Open

At seven in the morning on the twenty-seventh of November he was in the second bed from the window at the Vestry with a plaster cast from the knee to the ribs on her left leg, and the doctor standing at the foot of it with her arms folded.

"Three places," said Tessa Okonkwo. "The upper one went through clean and the middle one turned. She put four hundredweight down a stair on a bad hip that has been bad since the spring and the hip went first and the leg went where the hip went."

"Will she walk."

"No."

She said it without softening it, the way she had said everything about this woman since the eighteenth, and she let the ward get on with its work while she said it.

"Eleven years carrying bins down four flights. That leg has been the shape of a leg that is being asked to do one job and has been doing it alone. I have been a doctor for long enough to know that when a leg has been held together by work it does not come apart in the place where a book would say it comes apart, it comes apart in the work, and the work is four flights at two in the morning."

Wanda Rej said nothing. She was looking at the window, and there was nothing out of it, and she had been looking at it since about four.

"She asked me for a lamp on the fourth stair," she said, after a while. "Eleven years, one lamp, and now she is in here."

"You are going to be able to go out of this ward and go up stairs by February."

"I know what February is." Her hands were flat on the blanket and they were not shaking any more, which was the first good thing in two days. "Listen. I am not going to be difficult about it. I want that said in the ward so that nobody writes down that I am. I asked for a lamp for eleven years and I never once wrote to anybody, and a lamp is not a leg, and this is not anybody's doing except mine and a stair and a barrow."

She turned her head. "You come in at two o'clock at night for nothing, and you want me to be angry at you for it, and I will not."

He stood in the ward for another four minutes and did not say a word, because there was no word in this city that would be true and would also not be a man asking to be forgiven for being alive on the fourth floor at two in the morning.

## The Wallet Under The Coat

Lantern Row is two floors above a laundry, and the stairwell there is full of soap and steam that comes up through the well from the mangle room, and the door at the top of it stood shut until about half past three on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh, when a man came up it with a leather wallet under his coat and four sheets inside the wallet and a hundred and thirty-eight days of not carrying paper up a stair.

In all that time he had gone up it once, on the ninth of August, with his hands empty, to tell a woman the true reading of a term he had sworn in the dark. She had not asked him for anything. He had come down again.

The wallet had come from the second sub-level on the twentieth of October. A description of premises 34-6-118, made at nine in the morning, dated, signed at the foot by the head of classification and initialled once, against the head of the premises and not against the address, on his own asking in a kitchen in front of eight women and a clerk.

With it: a fair copy, initialled, sent out for check two days later and returned the day after that. A sheet of persons of reading age keeping the address, arranged as the stair is arranged, each line carrying when it began and where the person sleeps.

And the reason clause, four lines, true in three and false in four words, entered in a clerk's hand with the hour against it because a woman of the counting staff came up sixty-one steps to make sure that nothing went into a division out of the head of classification's hand alone.

The address was on all of it. The figure was on all of it. There were no eight names anywhere else in the city except that list and the fair copy in a division that answers to the counter-forensics office.

She opened the door before he knocked and looked at his face and then at the shape under his coat and did not step back.

"You have paper."

"Yes."

"In your coat."

"Yes."

"Then say what you came for before you take it out," said Ines Duarte, "because if you take it out of that coat in this doorway I am going to have to make a decision in a corridor, and I have made all of my decisions in rooms with a table in them for eleven years."

## What He Said On The Landing

"I want you to read it," he said. "Here. In these two rooms. I want you to be the only person in Ostara who has held that file and knows what is in it, and I want it to have happened in your own rooms and not in a sub-level and not at a counter."

"And the reason you want it to have happened in your own rooms."

"Because in a sub-level a woman who reads it is a woman who can be asked what she read. In my own rooms you cannot be asked anything, because there is no document that says you were ever in the room."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "That is true and it is the whole of the reason and I am not going to thank you for it, because you did not do it for me. You did it because a file that has only ever been read in offices has one kind of person in it, and a file that has been read in my rooms has two, and one of them is answerable to nobody at all."

She put her hand on the door frame.

"Say the rest of it. You have had it ready since the stair."

"Thirty days since the description was made against my head. Twenty-seven days since the list went out at four on the thirtieth of October. Eleven days since the books went onto a guild table." He said the numbers the way he said all of them.

"I am under the floor of my band, and an account under its floor is callable at ten to nine on the first. I have spent eleven days not touching anybody so that a woman with four pencil marks on her hand does not become a fifth line in a chart. I am asking you to read a paper because I am out of instruments and you are the last one I have, and I am ashamed of the order."

The landing was the width of two people.

"I am not reading it today," she said.

"Why not."

"Because today I would read it angry," said Ines Duarte, "and an auditor who reads a record angry reads it twice, and the second time she is not reading the record."

"She is reading for the thing she wants to find, and everything she finds on the second pass is contaminated and she will not know which half of it came out of the paper and which half came out of her own head. I have been an auditor for eleven years and I have watched four good women ruin a finding that way, and two of them ruined mine."

"Then tomorrow."

"Tomorrow at nine in the morning, and you will bring it inside, and we will do it at the table with the door shut and both of us in the room." She looked at him. "Say those three things back to me in the order I said them."

"You said not today. You said nine in the morning. You said both of us in the room."

"Three," she said. "I have said more than three."

He stood on the landing and got it right the third time, and she listened to all of it without moving her hand off the frame.

## The Terms

"Now the terms," said Ines Duarte, "and I am putting them in the corridor because a term given in a corridor cannot later be described as having been made in a mood."

"Go on."

"First. You will be in the room while I read it. I will not read that file in the other room with the door shut while you sit on my stairs. An auditor who reads a document alone cannot later be a witness to having read it, and I have spent eleven years refusing to be the only person who knows what is in a record, which is not a professional principle. It is vanity, and it is the only vanity I have left."

"Agreed."

"Second. I will answer you about everything I read. All of it. Not the parts that are about you, which are the parts you will want, and not the parts I think you should not have. If a page is in that file I will tell you what is on it, and I will tell you when it has been touched, and I will tell you what I have lost by holding it, and I will not do it kindly."

"Agreed."

"Third." She came out into the stairwell and shut her own door behind her, which she had never once done in nine years, and stood on the step above him so that the laundry below them was going about its afternoon with the doors open and the light coming up the well.

"After tomorrow I am not the person you come to," said Ines Duarte. "Not when you are frightened, not when you have made a mess, not at three in the morning, not because you have run out of anybody else."

"You have eight women on a list, a kitchen with a table, and a standing order on your door that one of them applied for on purpose."

"I am two floors above a laundry and I pay my own rent and I am not in that building and I am not on that list. The reason you are able to come up this stair with a paper in your coat at all is a promise you made me in the dark on the ninth of July and broke in about ninety seconds on a landing."

"I know."

"Say that you know what you have broken and not what you are about to break, because those are different and I want the one that is true."

"I know what I am about to break," he said. "Nothing has been broken yet."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "Nothing has. That is the only thing about the last hundred days that has been any use to me."

She went back inside and shut the door, and the stairwell was full of soap and steam from below, and he stood on the landing with the wallet still under his coat and the whole of the nineteenth of July — the dark, the sill, the promise in a room with no window — standing behind him in his own head.

Eight days since the nineteenth, and three of them left, and nothing after the twenty-ninth has a number attached to it at all.

He walked down the two flights and out onto the pavement of Lantern Row and stood there for about four seconds without moving, in the way he had stood on the bottom step on the third of October, and then he went down the hill and up the sixty-one steps to Ferrier Street and told eight women at a kitchen table about eleven days in four sentences, and not one of them asked him a question, which was the entire answer he got and which was worth the whole week.

# A Security Carried At The Wrong Figure

He put three sheets on the table at about seven on the eighth of October and he had written them out at the table in the Vestry the day before with nothing on them but headings, because a man who brings a kitchen four pages is doing a thing to a room.

"Three doors," he said. "That is all the district offers and I have been in all three rooms this week and this is what is in each of them."

He did not read the headings out loud. The room could read.

Runa Sato went first, because she always went first and had said on the nineteenth of September that he had been hiding behind a word for four days and had not repeated it since.

"Carried," she said. "Say what it costs me."

"The next valuation is on the first of next month. What answers a called standing there is a count of the people of reading age at those premises on that morning, and only the head of those premises can swear it." He did not look up. "It will have to be eight. I will have to write eight. And a woman I did not ask will be the eighth name in a book in a keeper's room."

"That is your cost."

"That is my cost. Yours is that you will not be in the room when it happens, because of a promise you made me give you on a step in August."

"My promise stands and you keep it." She turned the sheet about so it was the right way up for her and then turned it back. "What I want said in that room is that I will not be in it. Say it in front of the people who will be."

"I said it."

"Say it again, because it is my one piece of paper in this business and I would like the women who carry it to hear it from a mouth."

He said it again, with the table looking at him.

Nadia Ferrous came next, because Nadia Ferrous had never in her life waited to be asked twice about anything.

"Referred," she said, reading it upside down without turning it. "That one is you walking into a room and handing somebody the address of a house where eight women live."

"That is the one."

"Then don't," said Nadia Ferrous, "and I am not being clever, I will do it, I will go to the counter on the first working morning of next week and say I never kept an address there and that it was a mistake and that somebody put me in a book to make trouble. I have been in this city since I was small and I have never once been frightened of a counter."

"If you say that you have committed something, and it will be true within a week, and you will not be able to take it back, and it will be a fraud with your name on it in a division that does not do this for fun."

"Then I will do it properly, or I will not do it at all." She pushed the sheet back. "I have been on twenty floors in this city and I have never once been asked to lie in a book about where I sleep. If the answer is that somebody has to, then the answer is that this house should stop keeping the address and somebody else should be the head, and I would vote for that tonight."

"Say that again in about four days," said Runa Sato. "In a room where somebody has to write it down."

## What It Costs Each Of Them

Beatrix Halm read hers twice and then shut the notebook and did not write in it, which for Beatrix Halm was a thing that happened perhaps once a season.

"I have nine years of a wall at the north end of the Square and one question I have never been able to ask," she said. "A description of a house. What is it obliged to include."

"That is the wrong question, and you know it is."

"I am asking the only question I have got," she said. "If somebody writes down what a house is, then either I am in it or I am not, and if I am not in it then I have spent nine years standing at the north end of that square being correct about a wall and being nothing whatever, and I have a good ear and a bad life and I would like to know which of those is happening before the thirtieth."

"Somebody will tell you."

"Somebody will tell me after the thirtieth," said Beatrix Halm, "and by then it will be a fact."

Petra Ilyin came in with the bundle and stood at the near end and listened to about half of it before she spoke, which was longer than she usually listened.

"The second door is the one you will take," she said.

"Yes."

"Then I will tell you what the round is." She shifted the bundle onto the chair. "There is not going to be one at our door on the first of next month. There has not been one since the first of October, and there will not be another one until a broker needs a figure off us."

"And if there were one, the figure under the address would be eight, and I would carry it, because eight is what the door says." She did not raise her voice. "I have never carried a figure that was wrong and I have never once in nineteen years put down a piece of paper carrying something I did not believe."

"You would carry eight."

"I would carry eight because eight is the truth and I do not have the standing to carry anything else." She looked at him. "And if you tell me on the morning of the first not to knock, I will not knock, and that will be in a room somewhere in a file in about nine years with a date on it, and you will have written it."

He had no answer to that and it was the fourth time in eight days.

Agnete Lindqvist said the only thing anybody said for about ninety seconds.

"I keep a room where not one person can be compelled to testify," she said, "and eleven years ago I understood that the only way a person is safe in a document is if the document cannot find them." She turned her cardigan sleeve back over her wrist. "That is all of it. And I have stood in the same four walls since before most of you were in this building at all."

Tessa Okonkwo was not there and had sent nothing and he did not say anything about her in the room, because there was a difference between a woman who was late and a woman who was gone.

Sun Meiling's certificate was on the shelf over the sink with the bar on it and the ink under the bar and her own handwriting over the top of that.

"Third door," she said.

"Third door is referred. Address, figure, reason. The reason has to be written by me."

"And a description that is refused becomes a description written without you."

"That is the district clerk's sentence and I have had it for two days and I have not been able to put it down since."

"Then we come to it." Sun Meiling came to the end of the table, which she does about four times a year. "You have been in this room every evening for four months telling us a version of your position. I have listened to it and I have believed most of it and I have not once asked you for the rest."

"Sun—"

"I am going to ask you for it now, and I am not going to be tender about it, because you have been careful with this room for six years and I have been the one sitting under a bar through all of it." She put one hand on the table. "Why is the account in the shape it is in."

He put his hands flat and looked at them.

"Because of the eight of you," he said.

The room went quieter than it had been all evening.

"Say the rest of it."

"It is carried at that address because that is where I live," he said.

"It is answerable by the composition of that premises because I said so, in my own words, in a cold room with no window in it, because a woman told me in July that she would not price a thing she could not describe."

"And it is carried at an address at all because a quarter of this district goes past a door in this street in the dark and does not know what it is doing." He put his thumb down hard on the table. "It has done that for four years and the only reason it has not found anything is that there has never been a single witness to any of it."

He put his thumb on the table.

"And you are the reason it is describable. All eight. Because a thing with no household in it cannot be priced, and it could not be priced, and an unpriced thing cannot be called and cannot be answered and cannot be requisitioned, and I would have got away with it for the rest of my life." He looked up. "And then seven of you decided to keep an address at a door together, and that made it a house, and a house can be counted."

## The Sentence

Sun Meiling did not move for about four seconds.

"Then here is what you have been trying to write away from yourself," she said.

She put her hand flat on the certificate on the shelf and then took it off it and put it on the table in front of him.

"A description of a house is a description of a household. A household in this city is a household in law. And a household in law is bound to its head."

Not one of them spoke. The kettle had gone off the boil behind them and the room was as cold as the bottom of a stair in an empty building.

"So the description you are trying to write without you is not a description of an address and it is not a description of a figure." She put her hand flat on the wood. "It is a description of you. It is a sentence about the man who is at the top of the eight."

"And every door you go through to get away from it arrives at the same address, and every address arrives at this table, and one of the women at this table is in ink under a bar because of a certificate you signed in your own hand in September."

She stopped.

"You have spent seven years making sure this city could never get your name to the head of a sheet." Sun Meiling did not raise her voice. "There is going to be a sheet with your name at the head of it, and there is not one thing you can do about that."

"And the thing that is going to put it there is not an enemy." She picked her hand up off the table. "It is me, on an ordinary morning, doing an ordinary thing, because a household is bound to its head and I have been bound to one since I was twenty-two."

## What Wren Kallo Did

Wren Kallo had been sitting at the far end with her forearms on the cloth and she had not moved through any of it.

"There is an arithmetic nobody in this room has done," she said, "and I have been doing it since the night of the second, and it comes out the other way, and I would like to say it before anybody makes a speech."

"Say it."

"Before the second of this month this house had two things in it that no instrument in this city could ever hold on to." She put one finger on the table. "A line in a closed book with nothing on it, and a woman who was on nothing at any time. That is what it means to be unwritten. It is not one thing. It is two, and they are the same idea twice, and it took two people to build it."

"Wren."

"One of them was built by a woman who asked for it and was refused the asking twice, and the other one was built by me," said Wren Kallo, "because I am the only person in this building who has never had an address anywhere and it suited me, and I told six people in a records room with the door open and I filed it and made it ordinary on the twenty-second of August."

She put her hand flat next to his.

"The first one spent itself on the thirtieth of September and it spent itself for a reason and I am not going to say the reason out loud in this room." She looked at him. "The second one I spent on the second of this month at about eleven in the morning, at a counter, in daylight, in front of a clerk of about twenty-five who did nothing wrong at all."

"Nobody made you—"

"I made myself," said Wren Kallo. "And I would like it noted in whatever room it gets noted in that I did it myself, and that no man in a corridor asked me to and no woman at this table asked me to, and that I have known since the fifteenth of September what it would cost, and I chose the morning and the hour myself."

The window was two inches open.

"Twenty-two days," said Runa Sato.

"Twenty-two days," said Wren Kallo, "and eight of us, and the description is going to have a head on it, and we found that out about ten minutes ago, at this table, from the woman who has been under a bar since the first of September."

She pushed her chair back.

"And I am not ungrateful about it," she said. "I told Sun on the fifth of August that I would not be, and I am saying it again now so that she never has to ask me."

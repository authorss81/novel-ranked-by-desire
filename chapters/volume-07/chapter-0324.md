# Four Years Of Being Looked Through

On the ninth of November at about half past five in the morning he woke before the bell and lay in the dark listening to the building breathe round him.

Twelve days since the description had been made on the twenty-seventh against his head and not against the address. Ten days in which eight women had slept behind a list that said eight on the thirtieth because it was made and dated.

He got up, washed in cold water, and went down to the yard with a bucket before the household woke, because there was work that did not score and had to be done anyway.

At about seven Tessa Okonkwo came up the sixty-one steps with a bag over her shoulder and knocked once, which she had not done since September.

"I have come to assess him," she told Sun Meiling at the door. "In front of you. Because what I say about him concerns you."

They cleared one end of the table. Six women sat. Two stood.

## The Assessment

She did not touch him at first. She looked at him the way she looked at a body in the ward, from the door, taking the whole shape before the parts.

"Four years of being looked through," she said. "Scanned at a gate every morning. Nodded at by clerks. Stepped round. That is what made the account yours. The account accrues to a person who cannot be audited for the deed. No filed record. No witness who will still speak. No instrument. In practice it accrues to people who are not looked at."

Beatrix Halm wrote nothing. This was medicine and not evidence.

"He is the most looked-through person in his own building. That is not accident. It is a job description. Washroom attendant, six floors, five to seven in the morning, while the office fills. Nobody looks at a man cleaning a washroom. Everybody's merit settles where nobody is looking."

"And now."

"And now he has asked to be looked at," said Tessa Okonkwo. "On the third he asked this table to let him be seen. On the fifth he lifted a grille with four witnesses. On the seventh he came to my clinic and heard what rank is for. What he would have to become is a man who stands where people look, on purpose, every day, and lets the look attach."

Nadia Ferrous, who had been standing, sat down hard.

"Say what that costs. Plainly."

Tessa turned to her.

"It costs whoever looks," she said. "Whoever attests carries the deed in a book with a date. Whoever relieves through him feels cold for one to four seconds in the hand or the chest. Most shrug it off. A person who has felt it three or four times starts keeping track. A person keeping track is a turned line. Nine turned lines in one season is a legible pattern and a good analyst can triangulate it to a source."

She paused.

"There are a hundred and eighteen lines in his ledger. Nine turned. The ninth still himself."

The room was very quiet.

Shame moved in him then, hot and specific, because the numbers were his and the women counting them were his, and he had never put the two together in one sentence before.

## What He Would Have To Become

"You would have to stop hoarding," she said to him directly. "Every advantage feels like theft from somebody smaller, so you take it quietly and keep it small. That kept you alive for five years. It will not keep you legible. Legibility is large or it is nothing. Three names on slips shift what one cannot. You heard that on the third from a woman who has carried dockets for eleven years."

"What do I stop doing."

"You stop doing the deed yourself," she said. "You stop lifting grilles. You stop carrying buckets up three flights for clerks with bad backs. Those are office deeds at best. They split to the staff or they get a query. What scores is what somebody else does after you relieved them. Relief only moves on contact with the person it came from, and only as relief. Not strength. The pain that was owed arrives early. Then they act."

Sun Meiling spoke for the first time that morning.

"Give him the posture."

Tessa nodded.

"Stand beside people who are finishing things. Put your hand on a hand that fed you. Let them lift. Walk away before the clerk comes. Do not arrange the witnesses. Do not write the attestation. Do not take it out of their hands if they write it. That is the whole posture and it is the opposite of everything you know."

He looked down at his own hands on the cloth.

"I know how to be underestimated," he said. "I do not know how to be attested."

"You will learn it the way you learned the other," she said. "By doing it badly in front of witnesses."

She packed the bag and stood.

"One sentence more, and it is the sentence you asked for on the seventh."

"Go on."

"The cost of becoming legible is not paid by you," said Tessa Okonkwo. "It is paid by whoever attests for him. Whoever writes his name down carries it. That is the price and there is no other version of it. If three women attest, three women pay."

She went to the door.

He followed her to the half landing, out of hearing of the table, and said the thing he had carried since four o'clock the day before.

"Did you attest for me. Years ago."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"I held a body past the point it should stop," she said. "I took on the damage. I carry, in my own frame, a working inventory of every injury I have held. One of those lines is yours. I have never written your name against it and I never will."

She went down four steps.

"That is medicine. Do not make it evidence."

He stood at the top of the stair in the cold morning air and watched her go down out of sight.

He wanted, concretely, to thank her without making her a witness, and he did not know how, and the not knowing hurt more than the assessment.

## What Remains Unwritten

In the kitchen behind him somebody laughed, short and startled, and somebody else hushed her, and the building settled for the day.

Beatrix Halm wrote the date under nothing, because there was nothing to enter, and that empty line annoyed her more than any figure.

"You let her examine you in front of us," said Nadia Ferrous when he came back in. "On the twenty-fourth Alma Quayle made us write eight lines in eight hands. On the twenty-seventh you said against me with a clerk writing the time. Today a doctor told you what you would have to become. Each time you let the room watch. That is new."

"It is the only way it scores," he said. "A deed seen by strangers in the room who owe me nothing. I am learning to be watched the way I learned to be overlooked. Badly."

Wren Kallo spoke from the corner, where she had stood since six and where she would stand until the certificate she asked for in writing either came or did not.

"Counter-forensics will see whatever is attested," she said. "Three attestations are a pattern. One is an entry. Remember the fifth. The yard slip went into the day book at nine and the plate did not move. Your own deeds do not score. Only what they do after you relieve them."

"I remember."

"Then remember this too," she said. "I may not be asked anything about that address again, ever, in any room. You promised that on the twenty-eighth in a forensics room with three conditions. So whatever attests for you will not be me. I am already on the list fourth from the top with my posting on it. I carry that. I carry nothing else."

He nodded. He had promised, and the promise held every day, and it would hold when he broke his own term at a later date in another woman's rooms.

Twenty days since the nineteenth of October, when he had left a correction half filled at a counter and a clerk had entered the refusal with a date. Ten days since the twenty-ninth, when Runa Sato had told the kitchen about the Field order.

He counted both without meaning to, the way he counted everything, and went inside to wash the cups.

## Who Watches

Nadia Ferrous refilled her own cup and did not sit. She had stood through most of the twenty-ninth and she stood now out of habit.

"On the eighth I asked what a description of a house is obliged to include," said Beatrix Halm from her corner with the notebook shut. "I was told after the thirtieth. Now it is after. It includes us in the order of the stair with dates. It says nothing whatever about what any of us is. It is cheaper than a judgement. Mr Sowerby told him that on the twenty-second on the third floor and he rode eleven stops home without getting off at his own stop the first time."

"Why repeat it."

"Because somebody has to say it in this room without dressing it," she said. "The list went out at four o'clock on the thirtieth in a leather tube."

"At two minutes past nine on the first he asked for a copy at Cordwain without giving a reason," she said. "A merchant house came at eleven minutes past. A guild house at twenty past. A district clerk at half past who walked twenty minutes to be told to ask. Three strokes in a day ledger with no cause entered beside any. That is what attestation looks like when it is not us."

Agnete Lindqvist, who had gone to the door, turned back.

"And in about four years somebody reads those lines and asks where they came from," she said. "That is the examination Sun Meiling asked about on the twenty-ninth. Not Alma Quayle. Not a keeper. Some man with a stamp you have never been inside. He will have the file. He will want to know where it is fed from."

He met her eyes.

"I get to answer it," he said, too quickly, the way he had on the twenty-ninth.

"You said that too quickly then as well," said Sun Meiling without looking up. "Hear it this time. What stands in front of that counter is not you. It is the eight of us, because you set yourself atop a sheet with us below in stair order."

# The Table She Cleared Herself

He went to her rooms at about eight on the morning of the sixteenth of November and found the door open and the table cleared.

One day since the fifteenth, when three slips had become a pattern and the plate had gone to eight thousand one hundred and eighty-four in a green the square had never had. One night since he had left by the back lane with her wetness still on his thighs.

The bound book was gone. The thinner book was gone. The attestation slips were gone.

The table had nothing on it but a cloth, smoothed flat, with the creases pressed out by hand.

Marguerite Vale stood at the window with her gloves on.

"You cleared it yourself."

"I cleared it myself before you came, and I have left nothing on it, and the absence is the statement," she said. "I wanted you to see it empty before anybody tells you what was on it."

He shut the door behind him.

"Say what was on it."

## What Went To The Table

"It went at seven this morning," she said. "To a guild table with nine people round it. My working book. My attestation book. Eleven strokes across four years cut with a holder not of my making. Your seven from mid-September, initialled only, tied to the household count of those premises. All of it."

She turned from the window.

"The Vant is no longer protecting me and nobody else has started. That is the shape of this morning. The guild read my books in the ordinary way, at nine in the morning, without a thought about any woman in them. By noon there will be copies in four divisions. By December, fourteen."

He sat down at the empty table because his legs would not hold him.

"What did they do."

"They minuted that I offered, not that I was asked," she said. "I asked for that to go in the minute too, the way I asked for the pen to go in four years ago. They minuted it. Then they took the books."

"And you."

"I am out of the counting house until further notice," she said. "Which means out. The keys changed on the seventeenth of September to a woman I have never met. Now the books have changed to hands I did not appoint. I am a broker with no book. That has not happened to me since I was twenty-two."

She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way she had said everything since the spring.

Grief came up in him, for her and not for himself, sharp and adult, because she had put her own book on a table for him and he had not been in the room.

"I told you I could not give you a position."

"You told me last night and you were right," she said. "I did it anyway. No woman in this book has ever been with a man who could pay for what she was doing. Runa Sato told you that on Cordwain on the thirtieth of October. Isolde Marchetti told you on the fifteenth. I am telling you now."

She came and sat opposite him at the cleared table.

"Here is what changed and is still changed," she said. "I stopped being a woman with a position inside a guild and became a woman who has put her own book on a table. That does not come back. A book once shown cannot be unshown. A list once issued cannot be unissued. You know both shapes now."

He nodded. Eight names in stair order with rooms beside them had issued at four on the thirtieth past recall. Her books had gone at seven this morning and could not be taken back.

"Why show me the empty table."

"Because last night you saw the books," she said. "This morning you see what they cost. I wanted the two sights in you in order. Full and then empty. That is the whole statement and I cleared the table myself so that nobody else could make it for me."

## The Absence

They sat at the empty table for a long time without speaking. The fire was out. The rooms were cold despite being the best he would ever stand in.

At about half past eight somebody knocked once downstairs and went away. Neither of them moved.

"Will they requisition," he said at last.

"Not this month," she said. "A described account is administered rather than priced, and there is nothing yet to administer it against. No finding has been entered. Nothing has been administered. In about four years a stranger goes looking for the subject of a figure with no subject. That is still true."

"And seven."

"Seven is yours," she said. "Three women attested things you did and the figure went up. You can never be unscored again. The man no division could read is now a figure on a public wall every week. That is the price you paid for being heard."

She stood and went to the dresser and brought back two cups of cold tea, and set one in front of him.

"Drink. You look as if you did not sleep."

"I did not."

"I did," she said. "For the first time since September. After you left by the back lane I slept four hours with the door locked. That is what exposure does for a woman who has carried too much paper. It empties her."

He drank. The tea was cold and bitter.

He wanted, concretely, to carry her the way somebody had once carried him, and he knew he had no seal and no standing and no instrument to do it with, and the knowing hurt more than the wall or the books.

She saw it and shook her head.

"Do not promise what you cannot seal," she said. "A movement without a seal is theft. You learned that on the eleventh from a man on the third floor. Learn it here too. What you can do is be scored, weekly, in public, by deeds other people attest. That is the thing you refused since you were a junior clerk. You did it. Do it again."

He stood to go. At the door he stopped.

"Will you eat at Ferrier Street."

"Not this week," she said. "The Vant watches that door now the way the Field watches it. Runa Sato holds a standing order to observe those premises, applied for on the twenty-seventh with the reason box left blank. Any officer may be sent to that door without asking anybody. If I eat at your table this week, somebody writes it down."

She smiled, briefly, without pleasure.

"Ask me again when the stream slows. I will say yes quickly. Saying yes is enjoyable for me and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

He kissed her once at the door, briefly, the way he had the night before, and went down the back lane in the morning light with the empty table still in front of his eyes.

At the bottom of the hill Beatrix Halm waited with the notebook open, writing down the morning wall before the day blurred it.

He told her the books had gone at seven. She wrote that too, with the hour against it, because that is what she does with things that move.

Thirteen days since the third, when he had asked to be seen. Two days since the fourteenth, when he had learned to stand beside people finishing things.

He counted both, out loud, against dates printed in rooms he was not in, and went up the sixty-one steps to tell a kitchen that the most exposed woman in it was no longer protected by anybody.

The table there was not cleared. It was laid for breakfast, with eight cups round it and one empty place where somebody who was not on the list had always sat.

He sat down in his own place and picked up his cup, and the day began.

## What Cordwain Writes

At about ten Runa Sato came up the sixty-one with her hat in her hand, back from the grille where she keeps the desk through the month, where her day ledger carries three bare strokes and the persons sheet rests squared under a weight since twenty to eight on the first.

She looked at the empty place at the table and did not sit in it.

"She put the books on a guild table at seven," she said. "I heard it at nine at the grille from a guild clerk who gave no reason. Vale and Son will have a copy by noon. Four divisions by night. That is the ordinary stream. It does not wait for anybody."

"Will they come here."

"The division may send any officer to that door on any working day unasked," she said. "That has been true since the twenty-seventh, when I applied with my own name at the foot and the reason box left blank. My undertaking from you is lodged. You will not ask for my release for the life of the account. It has no figure against it. It is not a debt. Nobody will ever invoice it. Do not convert it."

She put her hat back on.

"Eat," she told the table generally. "You are eight lines on a public list and one man seven on a wall in green. That is the most legible this house has ever been. Legibility needs breakfast more than secrecy does."

She went down. The stair took her weight without a sound.

Sun Meiling ladled more soup into his bowl without being asked, because that was the work she did when a man had been out being seen.

"Three attestations moved the plate," she said quietly, for him alone. "Three women paid. Marguerite paid more. That is the arithmetic of being wanted on purpose, which is a very different thing from being wanted. Remember the difference. The kitchen can tell it and the wall cannot."

He nodded. Sixteen days since the thirtieth of October, when the list had gone out. Twenty-seven days since the nineteenth, when he had referred the account. Forty-four days since the second of October, when Wren Kallo had taken an address at about eleven and made the composition eight.

He counted all three out loud, against dates printed in rooms he was not in, and the table heard him and let the counts stand.
He washed the cups himself, slowly, and set them to dry in order, and the order held for the rest of the day.

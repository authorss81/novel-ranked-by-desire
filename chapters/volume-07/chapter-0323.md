# The Plate That Had Not Moved

On the seventh of November at about six in the morning he walked the tram line to the Fourth Branch past the ranks wall and did not stop to read it, because Beatrix Halm had read it the evening before and there was nothing new on it.

Ten days since the twenty-seventh of October, when the description had been made at nine o'clock with his name at the head of it. Eight days in which the plate had not moved a grain.

He cleaned six floors of washrooms while the office filled. He ate from the staff kettle. He carried a bucket up three flights for a clerk with a bad back and set it down without being thanked, which was ordinary.

At about noon Petra Ilyin came to the branch gate with a bundle and asked for him by name, which she had never done in eleven years of delivering on that street.

"You are to come to the Vestry at four," she said. "Tessa wants to look at your hands."

"My hands are well."

"She wants to look at them anyway. She said to tell you it is medicine and not kindness, and that you are to bring nothing."

He went at four.

## What Rank Is For

The Vestry clinic smelled of soap and cold air. Tessa Okonkwo kept him waiting twenty minutes, which was her way of showing that the room was hers, and then took his hands in hers and turned them over once.

"When did you last sleep past five."

"In July."

"When did you last draw."

"Never."

She let his hands go and sat back on the stool.

"Good. Then hear this a single time, because I will not repeat it and I will not write it down."

He sat down on the edge of the examination couch.

"Go on."

"Rank is not power," she said. "Every junior clerk thinks it is. Rank is time. A rank-six person is not rich. A rank-six person does not wait. Rank is the right to be asked before you are searched, to have your petition heard this week instead of in March, to hold a machine for an hour. That is what you are trying to buy."

"I know."

"You do not know it the way I know it," she said. "I keep the gifted alive. I hold bodies past the point they should stop. Every body I hold takes room in my own frame and I am running out of room in it. Rank is the only thing in this city that gives a body more room. That is what it is for."

She stood and went to the cold store door and came back with a ledger of her own, bound in cloth, with a hundred turned pages in it.

"You have never held a rank you earned," she said. "Every band you ever held came from relief converted into somebody else's attested act. Somebody you finished did something in public and the score came back to you. That is the engine. It has been the engine for five years."

He looked at her.

"Say why you are telling me this now."

"Because you asked the kitchen on the third to attest for you without asking them, and you tried the yard on the fifth with your own hands, and both failed in the way I knew they would." She closed the ledger.

"The reservoir cannot raise your rank. Not one grain of it is scoreable. This city only counts what can be argued about later. Quiet merit where no witness and no instrument could see it does not exist as far as the wall is concerned. You carry the part that cannot be counted. To be counted you have to stop carrying and start being carried."

The rebuke landed where she had aimed it, in the place where he kept his careful accounts of who owed what to whom.

"And the cost."

She told him plainly, as medicine, without softening it.

"The cost of becoming legible is not paid by you. It is paid by whoever attests for you. Whoever puts a name to a deed carries it. Three attestations will move that plate. Three women will carry your legibility in books with dates on them for four years."

He was quiet.

He saw what it meant for one person standing in a clinic with cold hands and a bound ledger full of other people's damage.

## The Hands

She took his hands again and held them longer this time, palm to palm, the way she held a body she was assessing.

"You have damage in you from eleven days of drain water and nine seconds of swearing productions," she said. "Seven both times. In nine seconds each time. Off the same chained book. A keeper marked the repetition as a habit and she was right."

"How do you know that."

"Because Runa Sato told me on the twenty-ninth, in this room, at two in the morning, while a stranger slept in the ward." She released him. "You carry things in your hands the way I carry them in my frame. The difference is that I write mine down."

"What do I do."

"You do not lift another grille," she said. "You go where people are finishing things and you finish them. You put your hand on a hand that fed you and you let them do the lifting. Then you walk away before the clerk comes."

"And if nobody writes it down."

"Somebody always writes it down," said Tessa Okonkwo. "A clerk wrote the yard slip with four witnesses on the fifth because water moved where she could see it. People write down what moves. That is the whole of this city."

She walked him to the door of the clinic.

"One more thing, and it is not medicine."

"Go on."

"You have been looked through for four years," she said. "Every morning scanned at a gate, nodded at by clerks, stepped round by anybody holding a reading card. You read people well because your whole job is being looked through. To be scored you have to stand where people look. That is a different posture entirely and it will hurt in a place you have not used."

He went out into the dark yard behind the Vestry with his hands warm for the first time in two days.

He wanted, concretely, to be looked at without being used, and he wanted it with a sudden sharpness that surprised him, like thirst.

## What She Carries

At the gate of the yard he stopped, because she had come to the doorway again with the cloth ledger still under her arm.

"You asked if I attested," she said. "Hear the true answer instead of the one I gave you on the step. In July I held a porter who had carried an injured man to a tram stop because carrying him was quicker than waiting. The porter had fed you once with a door held open. I held the porter's body past midnight. I wrote porter in my book and not you. That is how attestation works when it is clean. The hand is never on the page."

"Why tell me."

"Because you think attestation is a favour," she said. "It is not. It is a record. Whoever writes carries. I carried that porter for a week in my frame. I still have him in the inventory. If you ask three women to carry you, know that each of them carries you the way I carried him. In the body. For years."

She lifted one hand, briefly, and went inside.

The door shut. The light went out.

He walked home past the wall without reading it, and at the bottom of the sixty-one he stopped and said her name out loud once, to nobody, to fix the question for later.

"Tessa."

Above him the kitchen windows were lit. Eight women behind a list that had gone out at four o'clock on the thirtieth and could not be corrected, withdrawn or unissued.

Three of them would have to write his name down for him to live. He did not know which three.

## The Inventory

She opened the cloth ledger to a page near the back, where the lines ran close together in her small hand.

"Forty-one minutes," she said, pointing to one line. "Seven people. A woman who went in with her hands empty and came out with her hat in them. Petra stood across the street for two hours and forty minutes on the twenty-ninth of August and reported only what a person on a pavement can know. That is in here too, because everything that waits ends up in here."

He read three lines without touching the page.

"Why show me."

"Because you think your ledger is the only one," she said. "A hundred and eighteen lines, nine turned, the ninth still yourself. Mine is bound in cloth and kept at two in the morning in a ward with a cold store beside it. Yours is kept in your head with faces and streets and hours. Both cost the keeper room. Mine costs frame. Yours costs sleep."

She closed the book.

"Go home past the wall and do not read it. Beatrix reads it for you. That is what she is for. You are for the hour between five and seven. Keep to it. Do the ordinary thing where people can see it and do not lift what they can lift themselves."

He nodded. Ten days since the twenty-seventh of October. Twenty-two days since the fifteenth, when the card came down at two in the morning at the singer's own word.

He subtracted both out loud on the tram platform, against dates printed on cards and slips he did not carry, and the arithmetic held.

He walked home past the board on the railings without stopping, the way she had told him, with his hands in his pockets and his collar up.

At the Fourth Branch gate the morning scan beeped him through without a glance. Four years of being scanned on his way to work and worth less than the equipment he cleans. That had not changed. What had changed was that he now heard the beep as a question instead of a dismissal.

He wanted, that morning, rank nine for the file and not for the standing, and the want sat clean in him beside the newer want to be looked at without being used.

Both could be true. Both were.

He slept that night with his hands open on the blanket, palms up, the way she had left them at the clinic door.

At five he woke before the bell and lay listening to the pipes knock, thinking of rank as time and not as power. Time to have a petition heard this week instead of in March. Time to hold a machine for an hour. Time to be asked before being searched. He had never had time. He had only had the hour between five and seven, and he had spent it cleaning while the office filled.

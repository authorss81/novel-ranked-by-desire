# The Room Where Nine People Sit

He had stood in that yard every afternoon at six since the nineteenth of November, and on the morning of the first of December he was in it at ten to eight, because a slip had a date on it and the date was that day.

Tessa Okonkwo came out with a basin and the cloth ledger under her arm and did not stop.

"Give me the slip," she said.

He gave it to her. She read the eleven dates off it, which she did not need to do, and wrote *returned* against the twelfth with the time against that, and shut the book.

"You are not dead. That was the other branch of it and I would have taken it at four this afternoon."

"And her."

"The plaster comes off on the twelfth and she is going to walk about four in this city for the rest of her life, on that leg."

She set the basin down on the wall.

"The four marks are on her hand and they have not gone anywhere and no one has asked her about them, because you said they would not and I have not asked her once. I am the only person in this city who knows they are there and you are the second, and if either of us ever says a word about it the other one is a liar."

## Ten To Nine

A note had come up to Ferrier Street on the previous afternoon in the ordinary way: *the line at premises 34-6-118 will be marked at ten to nine.*

The counting room was two floors under the sales floor. It had no window and the stone had never been warm, and the chained real book lay open on a board, and Solveig Rask had a pen on a chain round her right wrist with the chain long enough to reach the page and short enough that it could not be carried away from it.

Hanneke Ruis was at the table at the far end with her own book open and did not look up, which was a rule of her floor and not an opinion about him.

"Sit down," said Solveig Rask. "You are here because the line is about to be marked and a line that is marked ought to be marked in front of the person it is about. That is not sentiment. It is that I would like the word in the margin to be the one you would have used."

She read the entry to him first.

*The line of the sixteenth of September. Premises 34-6-118. Figure: seven. Initialled, not signed.*

"That is what it has said since the sixteenth of September and it has said it in four divisions and the ordinary stream of the fifth. It is answerable by the composition of those premises," she said.

She turned the chained book half an inch so that the light came off the page.

"The composition is eight. You certified that, or the woman who keeps that address certified it, on the thirtieth of November, at nine in the morning, in her own division. So the line has been under the floor of its band since the plate of the twenty-second and this is the first valuation since."

She took the pen off the chain and held it over the line.

"It is not a figure going in," she said. "Understand that before I put it down. A figure going in is a valuation and I would have to swear to it, and I would not swear to seven for a door with eight in it. What goes in is a word. The word is *called*, and under it the rule in the ordinary words, and then the date."

"And if it is neither."

"Then it waits at the next one, and the next, and it goes on waiting until somebody answers it, and there is no clerk in this city whose job it is to make an office answer it." She wrote it. "There. Read it. It is dated the first of December at ten to nine and it is in the chained book and it cannot be scraped off a chained page without a line in the destruction book, and I would have to write that line myself."

He read it twice. The mark was in a small level hand and it said exactly what she had said it would say and not one word more.

"Can it be paid."

"It can be paid. That is the ordinary answer and I will give you the ordinary answer before the other one."

She closed the pen back into its chain and turned the clasp.

"Paying a called line means bringing the figure into the room that holds the book and settling it there, at the figure on the line, which is seven. Not at eight. The eight is a composition of persons and it is a beautiful document and it has nothing whatever to do with what a line is paid at."

"And the room that holds the book is not yours."

"Those books were carried out of this room at seven on the morning of the sixteenth, and they were read at nine by nine people. I did not appoint them. I was not one of them, and I have not laid eyes on a page since that morning."

She put it down flat on the stone, without any inflection at all, which was how she delivered the things that had cost her something.

"So the call stands in a chained book, and the only hand in this city that could pay it would have to come up to a counter on the sales floor and ask, in front of a clerk, which of those nine it trusts with thirty years of a man it has never met."

## The Ordinary Stream

At about ten past nine he was on the sales floor with the plates and the racks, and the building was doing what it does at ten past nine on the first of a month, and a clerk at a counter gave him a fact in the ordinary way because he had asked a fair question and was owed one.

"There was a request entered yesterday at nine in the morning on the ordinary stream," the clerk said, "out of this building, for the whole of a file and a check column, to be served before the valuation, and it was served at nine fifty on the door and the whole of it went with it. Nine fifty, this morning."

"And where it went."

"Nobody at this counter writes down where a request goes." The clerk pushed the rack along. "That is the whole point of it, sir. If we wrote it down it would be a record and it would not be a door."

He stood on the sales floor for about four minutes and understood that a woman two flights above a laundry on Lantern Row had, at nine fifty that morning, taken delivery of every sheet of a file that had come out of a sub-level on the thirtieth of October and had been kept out of every division in this city ever since.

She had asked for it herself, in her own name, on the ordinary stream, on the last working day of the month, and she had not told him and was not going to.

There are two people alive who have read both sides of it. One is an auditor of record who cannot be examined about it and will not be asked, and the other one is not a party to any of it and has her own rent.

## Eleven O'Clock

He went up the hill at a quarter past ten with nothing in his coat, and eight women were at the kitchen table, and he did not tell them one word of any of it.

Sun Meiling looked at him for about as long as it takes to count a room.

"You have been somewhere with a floor in it," she said, "and you have come back with your hands empty and you are not going to tell us until you have decided what it is, and you are going to decide it alone in a stairwell and then tell us at dinner."

"Yes."

"Then say one thing now, and I will hold to it and ask nothing else." She put her cup down. "Is it about the account."

"It is about the account."

She nodded and picked her cup up again, and that was the entire cost of eleven days in a kitchen, repaid.

The room where the nine sit is on the first floor, at the end of a corridor that smells of wax and hot dust, and there is no plate on the door, because nine people who are counted do not need a plate.

He had no business on that floor. He had been sent to nothing and summoned by nothing and owed nothing, and he went up at eleven in the morning because a man whose account has just been called in a chained book in a cold room two floors under a sales floor has nothing left to do until somebody answers it.

Nine of them went in.

He counted them from the corridor, which was the whole of what the corridor was for. Nine went in between ten past eleven and a quarter past, one at a time, at the pace of people who have been coming to the same room for a long time, and the ninth went in last and did not hurry.

One of them was a woman with grey hair who was carrying nothing at all, not a case and not a bag and not a coat over an arm, and one of them was a man who stopped on the threshold to say something to the clerk at the desk, and eight of the nine came in by the stair and the ninth came up the other stair.

The door shut. The clerk squared a stack of slate, and the room went on behind it for four hours.

He was not a fool about it. He did not put an ear to a door. He sat on the bench at the end of the corridor with his hands on his knees, the way a man sits who has decided to be somewhere and is not going to make a performance of it.

At about half past twelve a runner went along the corridor with a box of plates under his arm, the way runners go along corridors at half past twelve every day in the city, and did not stop at that door, because the nine do not take the plate.

At about twenty past three the door opened and eight people came out, and he counted that too, because by then counting was the only work he had.

## Eleven Words At A Desk

The woman at the desk under the window had been at that desk a long time and had a slate and a blotter and no particular interest in him.

He asked her, standing up, in the ordinary voice, the only question he had brought to that floor.

"What is a requisition of an account."

She did not look at her slate. She answered the way people answer a question at a counter, which is to say in the fewest words that will stop the asking.

"A requisition of merit is laid against a person," she said. "Five of nine to carry it. It takes what the person holds. An account with nobody's name on the head of it cannot be requisitioned, because there is nobody to serve it on."

Then she put her hand on the next slate and pulled it towards her, which is the end of every conversation at that desk in the history of the city.

He went down the stairs with eleven words in his head that he would not be able to put down, and out into the light of the first of December, where a thing he had been arranging for seven years had been called in nine words by a woman with a blotter, and it did not have anybody's name on the head of it, and that was the only reason he was still walking about the city.

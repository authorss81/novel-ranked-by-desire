# The Address She Took In The Ordinary Way

He got to the district house at ten to eleven on the second of October with a sentence ready, and he had been carrying it since six in the morning, and it was eleven words long.

There was a cheaper way out of a priced account than any of the three he had spent four days pricing, and he had worked it out at about half past five on a tram platform, and it was not available to him.

The cheap way was to stop being the sort of thing that can be priced.

An account that belongs to no one is a rumour. An account that is fed at one door by a quarter of a district going past it in the dark is a household with a figure under it, and the figure is the composition of the house, and the house is him.

There was no arrangement of his life in which he was not the part of it that could be counted, because the counting had gone into a real book on the sixteenth of September in a hand that was not his and under a date that would not come off.

He had gone at six. He had put his hands flat on the table at about half six and Sun Meiling had watched him do it. He had said the thing out loud to a kitchen and had found out that eleven words was the entire content of it.

Now he stood in a corridor with the eleven words in him and four people went past him, and the premises counter was a counter with a grille and a clerk behind it and a queue of six.

Wren Kallo came up the corridor at about five to eleven.

She had a folded paper in her hand and her coat on over the working one and she was walking the way she walks, which is the way of a woman going to a counter and not to an examination, and she was not looking at the floor.

He had about four seconds.

The eleven words were: *you do not have to do this today and you know what it does.*

He let her pass.

She went in at the grille and stood at the left-hand place, and the clerk said good morning, and Wren Kallo said good morning back in the voice she uses at a counter and not in the voice she uses at his table.

He stood about nine feet from the grille for four minutes and eleven seconds.

## The Leaf

Inside, the clerk was a young man of about twenty-five with the last quarter's box still out on the counter behind him because the last quarter's box was still sitting out.

"Taking one?"

"Yes."

"Name."

"Wren Kallo."

The clerk wrote it. He had the leaf flat on the board in front of him, and it was not the third-quarter leaf and it was not the June leaf. It was a leaf he had opened two mornings ago, because a book for a quarter opens on the first and there is no other day in the year that one opens.

"Were you keeping an address here before?"

"No."

"Right." He ruled a line under what he had written without appearing to think about it. "Anywhere else?"

"No."

"None?"

"None."

"Right."

The rule went in under the name. The clerk came back down it, put the figure in the box at the top of the leaf, and wrote the date.

Eight.

She said thank you, and she asked whether the same counter took a change, and he said it did, and she asked whether it took one after four, and he said it did, up to four. She said she would remember that.

Then she came out past him and went about eleven steps down the corridor and stopped.

He waited for her to say she was sorry.

"Does that counter open at nine," she said, "or at ten."

"Nine."

"Thank you." She put the folded paper into her coat. "I will be back at about three, and I will tell you which one it was, and you can tell me if I have it wrong."

He did not have anything.

She went down the stairs and out of the building, and he stayed where he was with his ear to the grille until the clerk had called somebody else.

## What The Leaf Says

The book for the quarter that opened on the first of October is a fresh leaf, ruled against the leaves either side of it, with no line in it standing blank, because there is nothing in it to leave blank.

Her name went onto the face of it at about eleven o'clock on the second of October in an ordinary hand, with the date, and the figure at the top of the leaf went to eight, and the district had done nothing except write down what a woman told a counter in the ordinary way.

A person of reading age who keeps an address at a place may be put into that place's book at any hour of any day of the quarter, and the only hour it may not is the one while a book is shut. The third-quarter book went into a crate on a handcart at about half past eight on the thirtieth of September and went onto a store shelf in Ferrier Street, and it weighs four pounds and a bit, and it is not coming out.

There is a seventh returned line in it. It sits a rule's width under the one above it, toward the bottom of that leaf, with nothing entered against it, and beside it, in the pencil of a man who signs shelves, are six words and a clerk's number.

That pencil and that number are on a page that is shut for ever.

Her name is on a page that is open until the thirtieth.

## The Table

She told them at about seven in the evening. She was on her feet at the near end of the table with her hands held behind her, which is where Petra Ilyin stands when she has something to say and which Wren Kallo has never once done in five months.

"Eight," she said. "On a fresh leaf, with the date, and the clerk's number went on it too, and I did not ask for it and did not want it."

Runa Sato put her hands flat on the table.

"Go on."

"You should all know what I did and what I did not do, and I am going to say both, and then I am going to sit down and I do not want it made into a thing."

She looked along the table rather than at anybody.

"I did not ask him to stop me. I saw him in that corridor at about five to eleven, and I knew exactly what he was standing there for, and I went past him, and he had something ready and he did not say it."

"That is on you," said Runa Sato.

"That is on me, and I would like it written down that I did it myself." She still had her hands behind her back. "Nobody in this room talked me into it, nobody asked me to, and nobody is going to be able to stand up in a room in four years and say a man in a corridor stopped a woman at a counter."

Sun Meiling went to the shelf over the sink and laid her palm on the certificate that had been sitting there since the first of September, and left it lying there.

"Ink," she said.

"Yes."

"You are in ink under a bar in my handwriting. Petra is on no roll at all. Nadia has a page with nothing on it on purpose. Runa has a hearing paper that has been stale since the end of July. Agnes has eleven years indoors in a card in a file that is hers." Sun Meiling did not look round. "And now you are on a fresh leaf at the top of a figure of eight, in a district house, with a clerk's number, in the ordinary way."

"I do," said Wren Kallo.

"Do you know what that bar was for."

"I know."

"It was for me. It was the only thing in this building with my name written nowhere on it, and I asked for it on the fifth of August, and you cost me six lines in an office and three weeks of a man's sleep to get it." She took her hand off the certificate. "And I told you on the thirtieth that I would not be grateful and would not be ungrateful, and that is still the whole of what I have."

"I know."

"And now there is a fresh leaf," she said, "and a fresh leaf takes anybody who walks up to a counter."

He had put his hands flat on the table and he left them there.

"What it breaks is the arithmetic, not the bar. The bar is on a certificate that says seven and goes on saying seven, and the leaf says eight, and both of those are true, and an account carried at a figure the district's own book contradicts cannot go forward."

"Can it go back," said Runa Sato.

"Not by me."

"Not by you." She did not raise her voice. "Say the rest of it, because I have been in three rooms this week with somebody holding that shape in their mouth who did not have the end of it."

"It goes to one of three places, and I have not chosen, and there are twenty-eight days."

He put the number where all of them could hear it.

The room did the arithmetic on its own. Beatrix Halm had her notebook out before he had finished the sentence, which is what she had been doing for nine years in rooms where numbers are spoken.

"Twenty-eight," she said. "And it stops on the thirtieth, and the thirty-first is not a day this city has, and every one of those three things has to be finished before the thirtieth, because there is no thirty-first to finish it on."

"Noted."

"Noted by me and not by the room," said Beatrix Halm, and she shut the notebook.

Wren Kallo sat down, and the chair went back about two inches on the flags, and she put her forearms on the cloth and did not say anything else about the corridor.

Petra Ilyin came in late with the bundle under her arm and stood in the doorway with the door open behind her.

"Eight," she said.

"Eight."

"And I will be on that step on the first with a docket, and the figure under the address is going to be eight, because a clerk wrote it on a leaf two mornings ago." She shifted the bundle. "And in nineteen years I have never once carried a figure that was wrong."

She came in and shut the door.

## Two Figures

He did not sleep much, and at about two in the morning he got up and sat at the table with the docket from the first of October in front of him, because it was still in his coat.

A docket with a figure of seven under an address, put under his own door by a woman who has not read a docket in eleven years.

And a leaf in a bound book on the ground floor of a district house, with a figure of eight on the face of it and a clerk's number in the corner.

Two books. Two buildings. One door. And both figures are true because a woman walked up a counter at about eleven in the morning and asked a clerk a question about opening hours on her way out.

A classified account needs three things, and the second of them is a figure, and a figure has to be one figure.

There was a young man behind that grille who had done everything he was required to do in about four minutes, and who would go on doing it on the following morning and the one after, and who would one day sit down opposite a woman with a spine of quarter-books on her desk and read the third of June and the second of October in the same sitting, the way anybody reads two pages.

He would find the seventh returned line empty with a clerk's pencil beside it. He would find a fresh leaf with a new figure at the top of it and a name at the foot of the eighth line. And he would want somebody to explain the difference between the two.

There is not one person in this city who could.

He would not put it in a file. He would go and stand at a window for a minute and come back and say to whoever was nearest that the city was untidy.

In about four years one of the divisions that read quarter-books will ask why a household of eight was carried at seven from the sixteenth of September, and the answer to that will have to be a page.

He sat at the table in the dark and found out that the cheapest way out of a priced account is to stop being the sort of thing that can be priced, and that it is the one thing he is not allowed to do, and that a woman he had sworn a figure in front of nine seconds earlier had done it for him in four minutes without asking him for anything.

# The Thing He Had Never Done

He put it to them on the third of November at about seven in the evening, with the lamp lit against the dark outside and the receipt from the runner still on the shelf over the sink where Nadia Ferrous had left it on the thirtieth of October.

Three days since the list had gone out at four o'clock. Two days since he had stood in a queue at Cordwain at two minutes past nine and asked for a copy of it without giving a reason.

He had not slept past five any morning since. That was not new.

"I have to be scored," he said. "In public. By deeds other people attest."

Sun Meiling did not look up from the cloth she was mending.

"Say the rest of it."

"The plate stands at 8,138 against a floor of 8,144. Six under. The words on the wall have not changed since the thirtieth of July. The ledger is a hundred and eighteen lines and nothing has been spent."

"That is not the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I have refused to compete openly since I was a junior clerk," he said. "Because every advantage felt like theft from somebody smaller. So I hoarded it. Which is its own waste."

Beatrix Halm shut the notebook she had carried for nine years and laid her palm flat on the cover of it.

"Who told you that last sentence."

"You did. On the twelfth of September. At the bottom of the sixty-one."

"I did not use the word hoarded."

"You used the word cheap. It was the same sentence."

## What Had Never Been Asked

Petra Ilyin poured tea and set the cups round without being asked, because that was the work she did in a kitchen when a man was about to say something badly.

"Say what you want from us."

"I want attestation," he said. "A band is made of deeds other people attest in public. The reservoir cannot raise a rank. Not one grain of it is scoreable. That has been true for five years and I have never said it in this room."

Runa Sato leaned back against the dresser.

"Say how you know it."

"Because I have tried," he said. "In August. Twice. Quiet deeds where no witness and no instrument could see them. They settled and they did not score. The plate did not move a grain in two hundred and forty readings."

Wren Kallo looked up from the corner where she had been standing since six.

"And office deeds."

"Office deeds score to the whole staff and split by a formula that has never been published. Branch attendants score at all because the office is obliged to be able to say it scores them. That is the only reason I score at all."

He put his hands on the table.

"So to be heard by anybody entitled to hear me I have to do things where somebody is standing there, and that somebody has to write it down, and stand in front of witnesses, and say it was me."

Nadia Ferrous laughed once, without any pleasure in it.

"And in five years you have never asked one of us to do that."

"Never."

"Say why."

"Because the cost is not paid by me," he said. "It is paid by whoever attests for me. Whoever puts a name to it carries it in a book with a date on it. If it is wrong, it is on them. If it is right, they are the woman who stood up for a man who cleans washrooms."

Agnete Lindqvist had been quiet since the twenty-ninth. She spoke now without raising her voice.

"That holds, and there is more behind it. What sits behind it is that nobody in this room has ever asked you for anything in public either."

He held her gaze.

"Say that again."

"In five years not one of us has stood at a counter and said his name out loud for a thing that scores," she said. "We have asked you for relief. We have asked you for figures. We have asked you for rooms and rows and bars. We have never asked you to be legible where anybody could hear it. Because you taught us not to."

The lamp made a small sound.

Anger came up in him then, sudden and plain, at himself and not at them, and he let it show because the room had earned it.

"I wanted to keep you out of it."

"You kept us out of the only part that could keep you," said Sun Meiling. "That was the choice. It was made on the second of August and again on the first of October and again on the nineteenth. It is still made."

## The Shape Of It

Beatrix Halm opened the notebook again and turned to a clean page at the back.

"If you do this, you do it badly," she said. "You do it in public, on the record, with witnesses in the room. You do not arrange it beforehand. You do not pick the witnesses. You do not write the attestation yourself and ask somebody to sign it."

"Why."

"Because a deed you arranged and wrote yourself is your own deed," she said. "And your own deeds do not score. That is the rule you have lived on for five years without telling us, and I have worked it out from nine years of watching a wall."

He nodded.

"Is that true."

"It is true," said Wren Kallo from the corner. "Counter-forensics sees it every week. A man who files his own relief gets an instrumented entry and a query. A man whose neighbour files it gets a line."

Runa Sato pushed off the dresser.

"So the thing you have never done is not helping people," she said. "You do that between five and seven every morning. The thing you have never done is let anybody say so where a clerk can hear it."

"Yes."

"And you are asking us to be the ones who say it."

"I am asking you not to stop anybody who does," he said. "That is the difference. I will not ask for attestation. I will do the ordinary thing where people can see it. If somebody writes it down, I will not take it out of their hands."

Sun Meiling set the mending down.

"That is still asking."

"It is asking to be seen," he said. "I know."

He wanted, concretely, to be heard before the finding was entered, and he wanted it the way a man wants a door to stay open, with his whole weight against it.

Petra Ilyin finished the round of tea and sat down.

"Then we hear the shape of it now," she said. "You go out tomorrow and you do not hide. Three attestations will move a plate that has not moved since July. One will not. You know that."

"How do you know that."

"Because I have carried dockets for eleven years and I know what a clerk counts," she said. "One is an entry. Three is a pattern."

He looked round the table at eight women who were now on a public list with their rooms on it, in the order of the stair, with a date against every line, and who could each of them walk to a counter at any hour and lower the composition and still not touch the list that said eight on the thirtieth.

Four of them met his eyes. Four did not.

"What happens to whoever attests," he said.

"Whoever attests is examined," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is the price. A name on a deed is a name in a book. In about four years somebody reads it and asks where it came from."

She folded her hands.

"And you have spent seven years making sure nothing could put your name at the head of a sheet. Now you are asking four people at this table to put theirs underneath yours."

The room went quiet.

Isolde Marchetti's name was line seven on the list and nobody at that table spoke of what she had done on the fifteenth of October. The quiet held that omission without touching it.

"What happens to whoever is examined after," said Petra Ilyin. "Not in four years. Next week. When a clerk matches the deed against the list that went out at four o'clock on the thirtieth."

"Then the clerk does ordinary work," said Runa Sato. "Eleven divisions this morning, fourteen by December. A match is a clerk doing a job properly. Not one of them will be thinking about anybody at all. That is what makes it dangerous."

Nadia Ferrous had been quiet since the outburst. She spoke now with her hands round her cup.

"On the eighth of October I offered to go to a counter and swear I never kept that address. I withdrew it on the twenty-ninth because he would be at the counter within a week. I am telling you now that I will not offer it again. If attestation comes, it comes true."

"It comes true or it does not come," said Wren Kallo. "Counter-forensics checks the fair copy that went for check on the twenty-eighth and came back at a quarter to eight. A false line would be seen in a day. A true line stands for four years."

Sun Meiling picked up the mending again and set two neat stitches before she spoke.

"You asked what is different about you at the end of this," she said to him. "Not what you know. What is different. Answer that before you leave this table."

He looked at his hands on the cloth. They were clean, nicked at the knuckles from yard work, with cold still in the joints.

"What is different is that I have said it out loud where eight people heard it," he said. "I have spent seven years keeping my name off the top of a page. Tonight I put my want on a table. That cannot be taken back."

Agnete Lindqvist nodded once, slowly.

"Then hear the price in full before you go to bed," she said. "A term is money put down early for what has not come yet. You bought one on the twenty-seventh with eight lines. You buy another now with three attestations that do not exist yet. The second term is longer than the first."

He said the sentence that closed the evening, and he said it badly, because there was no good way to say it.

"I am going to do it anyway."

He looked at the receipt on the shelf a moment longer, with its number and its time and its signature that was not Alma Quayle's, and he left it where it was.

That receipt had come up the sixty-one at ten past five on the thirtieth with nothing else, and nobody on the stairs had heard of the tube again. It sat there now like a stone in the wall.

# The Order She Took On Purpose

The muster room at Cordwain is on the ground floor and the standing orders are printed at the head of it in a case the size of a door, and Runa Sato made him stand under it and read.

"That is the third of the eleven," she said. "And it is the only one in that case about a woman going to a place on purpose without a warrant, and it has been printed since before I came and I could recite all eleven with the door shut."

The room had a table in it with four chairs and a telephone on the wall with the bell off, and it was a quarter past two in the afternoon on the thirtieth of October and the envelope with her return in it was on the table under her hat.

"Who applied for it."

"I did." She put two fingers on the case. "the twenty-seventh, in the afternoon, on a form, at the second desk on the left, and it went up the same day because it is a small thing and there is not one question on it that has to be asked. My name at the foot. My division at the head. The address at the top and *the persons keeping it* underneath."

"No reason asked."

"There is a box for a reason and I left it blank on purpose and it went through blank, and I have worked out since that a blank is a louder answer than a written one." She took her hand off the case. "Every officer in this building has read that form in nine years and I would put money on one in three of them having left that box empty at some point, and not one of them can tell you which of the other answers they have had."

## What It Actually Is

"Say it in one line."

"It is a licence." She perched on the edge of the table. "It is not a warrant and it is not an instruction and it does not give me the right to go anywhere. It gives this division the standing to ask the question again, and it means the question comes to my desk instead of to a clerk's, and it means I answer it, in my hand, with a date, and it means I am the woman who keeps going."

"And what else does it do."

"It means any officer of this division may be sent to that address on any working day of any week without asking anybody, at any hour the division chooses."

"There is no instrument in this city that stops that being done, and not one person in this building is going to stop it." She looked up.

"Including people who are not enemies. That is the part you keep getting wrong, in both directions, and I have been saying it to you for four years."

"There are four divisions who would send somebody to that door this afternoon and only one of them would enjoy it."

"Then what are you protecting."

"Nothing," said Runa Sato. "I am not protecting anything. I am the one holding it. That is the whole of the difference and it is not a large one and it is the only one there is."

## The Thing He Cannot Do

He had come up those steps with an argument and she let him have about a minute of it and then stopped him with a hand.

"You are going to say that you can get it struck."

"I am going to say that I can ask for it to be reviewed."

"You are going to say that you can ask for it to be reviewed, and I have already worked out what happens if you do." She put her hand down flat on the table.

"A review puts a line against my name in this building. It says a man with a file in it asked a division to drop a standing order on an address."

"It does not strike the order. It puts my name next to the only thing in this city that has ever been able to reach me. And the next man who wants to walk through that door sends it to me, and he sends it because he can."

She took her hat off the envelope.

"I have been in nineteen years of this building and I have watched three good officers do that with a thing they cared about. All three of them were protecting somebody. All three spent the next four years being the woman in the building who had somebody in it." She looked at him flatly.

"That is the argument, and it took me about eleven minutes to get it out of you this afternoon, and you did not once have a better one."

"I want you to go on being the man who does not have a better one, because I would like to see what you do when somebody is holding the pen who is not me."

"So it stands."

"It stands, and I applied for it, and I am going up at half past three with an envelope that says eight." She squared the hat. "And here is the second thing, and it is the one I actually sent for you about."

## The Only Rule She Wants

"I want a rule."

"Go on."

"The one thing in this city that ends a standing order is a request from the head of the premises that the officer be released from it, in writing, with a date on it." Runa Sato put her hand flat on the table.

"I have read the third of the eleven about four hundred times. One request, in writing, from the man whose address it is, and the order lapses at the next monthly sitting."

"There is no appeal and no reason and no question asked of anybody. That is how they are lifted. All of them."

"You are telling me there is one line of writing that ends this."

"I am telling you there is one line of writing that ends this, and that it is in your hand, and that nobody else can write it, and that it has to be asked for." She did not raise her voice.

"You will not write it. I know you will not write it, and I have known since about the tenth of September."

"I am not asking you about it and I am not going to ask you about it again, because the moment either of us treats it as a question, the whole arrangement is a conversation about whether you will. I cannot work inside that."

"Then what is it you want."

"I want it to be a rule between us instead of a thing you have to keep being asked about," said Runa Sato.

"Write to me on the ordinary stream. One line, no reason, on any day you like, saying that you are not going to ask for my release from any standing order on that address for the life of this account."

"Lodge it. Let it go into a division. Let it be on a page with my name on it, so that in four years when somebody takes it out and looks at it, there is your handwriting on it and it is dated, and it is not a thing anybody can say I made up."

"You want me to write down that I will not protect you."

"I want you to write down that you will not protect me," said Runa Sato, "because protection in this city is a thing you do to somebody by asking them for something, and every time it has been done in this business for four years it has cost the person who was protected more than the thing being protected her from."

She picked the envelope up and tucked it under her arm.

"There is a pad in the room at the end of the corridor and you can write it now, or you can write it tomorrow, and I will not mention it again either way."

He wrote it on the pad in the muster room at about ten to three in the afternoon, one line, signed, and dated the thirtieth of October, and she read it standing over his shoulder and did not take it off him, she read it and put her thumb on the bottom of it and left it there for about a second and a half.

"That is the first piece of paper you have written since the sixteenth of September," she said, "and it is about not doing something, and I am going to keep it, and I am going to be the only person in this city who knows you wrote it."

## Nine O'Clock

She got her coat on at half past two and he walked with her as far as the top of the stairs because she said she would, and at the top she stopped.

"Now the last thing, and then I am going up at half past three and you are going to Ferrier Street."

"Go on, then."

"Tomorrow morning at nine this counter opens," said Runa Sato, "and there will be an instrument lying on the desk that came down the ordinary stream at four this afternoon, and it will have eight names on it, and no reason is required to read it, and it will be the first thing anybody in this city can lay hands on that says where those eight people sleep."

"And you are on that desk."

"I am on that desk until the end of the month and I have asked for November as well." She put her hand on the rail.

"And I have worked out that I am going to be the one who hands it over. Not because I am on the desk."

"Because I have decided that if that thing comes off that desk at nine in the morning it is going to come off it into a hand that has been in the kitchen on Ferrier Street, and not into a stranger's."

"That is a very large thing to decide at ten to three in the afternoon."

"It is about the size of the envelope under my arm." She went down one step and looked up at him. "And if you want a copy of it, you come to that counter at nine in the morning and you ask for it, in the ordinary way, at the grille, like a man asking for anything, and you do not give a reason, because there is not one required and because the moment somebody gives a reason in front of me I will write it down."

"That is what you want me to do."

"That is what I am going to make you do," said Runa Sato, "and it is the first thing in this business I have ever arranged, and I arranged it in about nine seconds standing on a stair." She went down. "Ask me for it at the grille. Not in here. I want it to be a queue."

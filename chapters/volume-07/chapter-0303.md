# The Book That Would Not Reconcile

The premises office was on the sixth floor of the district house and it smelled of dust and of the particular cold of a room where four hundred bound books stand on shelves a man has to reach.

He went up at about eleven on the sixth of October wearing two hats, and he had chosen neither of them on the way.

"There is a correction," he said.

Mr Sedley took his stamp off the blotter and did not pick it up again. He was a man of about fifty who had spent his whole working life in that room and had a way of letting a queue of paper get on with itself.

"Who from."

"From me. I am the head of the premises at thirty-four six one one eighteen and I am also the auditor of record of the schedule, from the first of August. I would like to know which of those two I am talking to you as, because it changes what you can do, and I would rather we found that out before either of us said anything else."

Mr Sedley set the stamp down on the blotter and put both hands flat on the desk.

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said at that counter in about a month," he said. "I am going to answer it and then you are going to be disappointed, and I would like the disappointment to be about the rules and not about me."

## Two Hats

"A district return is not amended by a head," he said. "You may give an address up and you may put one on. Both of those go onto the leaf in the ordinary way with the date and the clerk's number, and the leaf is what the district has."

"And the auditor."

"The auditor of record stands over the schedule. The schedule is not the premises book. The auditor certifies the schedule, and the schedule says how many persons the district returned in total, against the figures the divisions gave in." Mr Sedley opened a drawer and did not take anything out of it. "An auditor who uses that to alter one line of one premises' book is not certifying anything. He is writing."

"So you cannot take the name off."

"No."

"Then it stands."

"It stands until somebody of reading age gives that address up, or until the quarter's book closes, and then it is a fact." He pushed the drawer shut. "I am not able to strike a line out of a book that is open, and I have wanted to about nine times in thirty years, and I have not done it once."

"I am the auditor."

"You are the auditor, and you are six under a band floor on a public wall." He said it the way a man reads out a box. "That has nothing to do with your standing here. The schedule requires me to notice it once a quarter, and I have noticed it, and it does not stop you auditing and it does not let you write in my book."

He put his hands flat on the desk, which is what a man does at the point where he has said the whole of a thing.

"There was another thing," he said, "and I am going to say it before you ask, because I would rather you did not spend an hour deciding how to."

"Go on."

"You could have gone down to the counter on the second and said that the woman who put herself on that leaf is not a person of reading age. The clerk would have believed you, because you are the head of those premises and you are the auditor of the schedule, and a head is the best witness a district has got."

He let it stand.

"That is not what I came for."

"You came for it at about ten past eleven, before you came up these stairs, and you have been carrying it since the second," said Mr Sedley. "Then you decided you would rather be refused on a point of law. Do not do that to me. If you mean to lie to a district about a woman's age, do it to the boy at the bottom of the stair and not to a man with a stamp. The boy will at least remember being lied to."

The room was the cold of a shelf.

## Three Doors

"What do I do, then."

Mr Sedley went and got a sheet of the district's own printed matter out of a drawer behind him and did not read it.

"You know three of these better than I do. Carried, referred, described." He turned the sheet round and put it on the desk with the printing the wrong way up for him. "Say them back so that I can tell you which one you have been believing."

"Carried, at the next valuation, on a production."

"That is right. A production is the figure of persons answerable at those premises on the day, put down by the head of those premises, in that hand, with the day written under it. Eight, then."

He tapped the desk once with a fingernail.

"Eight. Your writing. In a chained book. Under a woman's name."

"I know."

"And you have sworn a figure of persons out of that house twice already, seven both times, off the same chained book, in nine seconds each time, and a keeper told you at the mark that she marks habits."

He turned the stamp over on the blotter.

"You have not mentioned the habit to me. I have noticed it on my own."

"And the second."

"Referred. A referral is a thing a man asks for. It goes to the office that describes things, and it carries an address and a figure and a reason, and the reason has to be written down by somebody." Mr Sedley let that sit where it was. "In about nine cases out of ten it is written by whoever asked for the referral."

"And the other one."

"That one is not a door anybody opens." He squared the sheet against the desk. "An account that has not been carried, produced or referred by the last working day of the month in which it was opened gets described. That is what happens to it. Not by a decision. By nobody having chosen."

"By which office."

"By whichever one is holding it when the month turns. In your case that will be the Classification Wing, because that is the office that describes things."

He looked up.

"And I will tell you what I have heard and what I have not. A boy on the ground floor says somebody went down and asked about this address on the third. The woman who holds the counting-room keys has had your address in a book for a fortnight. Neither of those came out of my office."

"And I am not going to repeat another division's corridor to you," said Mr Sedley. "That is not mine to give away and you are not here as a person who matters to another division."

## Twenty-Four

"There is no thirty-first."

"There is no thirty-first. October has thirty days, the thirtieth is a working day, and the first of next month is the turn."

He put the printed sheet back in the drawer and shut it.

"The valuation is on the first, at ten to nine, the same as it was on the first of this month. A called standing is answered there by paying it or by a production."

"So everything you have to do happens between now and the thirtieth, and not one minute of it happens on the first."

"Say that again with the days on it."

"You are not getting a fourth hour of my morning for arithmetic you can do standing on a wall," said Mr Sedley. "But since you asked properly. Today is the sixth. The book closes on the thirtieth. The turn is on the first of next month. The leaf has been open since the first of this month. Twenty-four days, and a book that shuts at the end of them."

He picked up the stamp, looked at the face of it, and did not use it.

"Before you go I am going to say the thing I am required to say, and then I would like to get back to my shelves."

"Go on."

"If you walk out of this room without doing one of the three, then on the morning of the first of next month there will be a description of your account with an address on it and a figure on it." He did not look up. "The figure will be the one a woman put into a book on the second of the month at about eleven in the morning without asking anybody. And the office that writes the description is not going to be asking you anything either."

"So they will write it without me."

"They will write it exactly, and completely, and in the ordinary way, and they will be right." He set the stamp down on the blotter and kept his hand flat across the top of it, the way a man holds a door that he is not going to open. "That is the part I would like one man to hear from a clerk before he hears it from anybody else. Nothing written about you without you is wrong. It is only not yours."

He straightened the blotter.

"That is the whole of what this office holds about you," he said, "and I have been in the premises office for thirty years and the only thing I will add is this. You have sworn a figure of persons out of one door three times in nine days, and two of them were seven, and you are going to have to swear a third."

He looked at him over the stamp.

"I do not think you have decided yet which number it is going to be, and I do not think you are going to be allowed to leave here without deciding it, and I have watched two heads of premises sit exactly where you are sitting and take an hour over a decision they could have made in a second, and in both cases they made the wrong one on purpose, because it felt like something they had earned."

He picked the stamp up again.

"Seven is a number this house can carry," Mr Sedley said. "Eight is a number this house can carry. There is no figure of persons that this house can carry that keeps you out of that room. That is what nobody tells a head of premises, and I have been a clerk for thirty years and I am telling it to you because in about four years nobody will be able to."

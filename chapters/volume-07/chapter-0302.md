# Two Figures For One Door

He went down the two flights on the fourth of October at about four in the afternoon, and there is no window on either of them and the bottom step has been worn into a shallow bowl by about four years of women carrying books down it in the dark.

The door at the top of the stair was shut and there was a runner in the corridor with a list, which was new to him and had not been there in July.

"Name and division."

"Lin Hao. The Fourth Branch, washrooms."

"Have you been in that room."

"No."

"Then you have the second door on the left and you knock once and you wait, and if it is shut when you knock you go away and you come back at four, and that is not a threat, it is because she is in the middle of something."

It was not shut.

The floor at the bottom is still the coldest floor he has ever stood on in this city and the room has a table in it and four books open on the table and a fifth shut with a strap over it, and the lamp is a lamp that somebody in this building chose rather than inherited.

"Sit where you sat in July," said the woman at the table. "I have been told there was a chair at that end and I have not moved it, and if I have moved anything you will tell me."

"You have not moved anything."

She had the leaf flat in front of her with a paper weight on it, and it was not a page out of a bound book. It was a single leaf, copied, with the district's stamp in one corner and the date of the copy in the other.

"Then read it," Hanneke Ruis said, "and tell me what it is before I tell you what it is, because you sent a question in on the second of the month and I am owed an answer in the same shape."

## The Leaf She Did Not Ask For

"It is a district premises return for the quarter that opened on the first of October, for premises thirty-four six one one eighteen, and it is a copy and not the original, and there are eight names on it."

He read the rest of the leaf down the ruled lines to himself and did not say any of it out loud.

"Eight," said Hanneke Ruis, not believing it. "Eight persons of reading age holding an address at those premises as of this morning." He spread his hands on the stone either side of the leaf, a thing he does at a kitchen table and has never once done in that room. "The one at the foot of it was put in by the woman herself, on the second, at about eleven in the morning, at a counter, doing nothing unusual."

"I know who put her in. It came up on the ordinary stream yesterday with the rest of the quarter's movements, because a line of mine is answerable by the composition of a house and when a composition moves the copy moves with it." She turned the leaf about a quarter of a turn so that it faced him. "I did not ask for it and neither did anyone else. It arrived because it is what arrives."

"There is a line under your column that says seven."

"There is a line under my column that says seven," said Hanneke Ruis. "Dated the sixteenth of September, initialled, not signed. I have had it seventeen days."

"I have read it about forty times and I cannot make it agree with a leaf that came yesterday." She turned her chair about an inch. "Explain the difference to me in words that are not clever. I am new in this room and I have a bad feeling about it."

"It is two figures for one door."

"It is two figures for one door and both of them are in books I cannot reach into," she said, "and the district keeps theirs on a bound leaf and the Vant keeps ours in a spine and neither office has ever asked the other one anything."

He had rehearsed three answers on the stair and all three of them were for a different question.

"The sworn answer," he said. "I swore a figure of persons out of that premises on the first of October at ten to nine in a chained book at the Exchange, and it was seven, and I counted them, and it took nine seconds both times."

"Nine seconds twice," said Hanneke Ruis. "You said that at the mark and I wrote it in the margin of my own day book because it is not a normal way to produce a figure of persons."

"Was it wrong."

"It was not wrong." She said it without any weight at all. "It was accurate on the morning you swore it and it is one day old. Those are the two things I have and they are both true and you cannot have one without the other, and that is the entirety of what I have to say about your answer."

"And the other thing."

"The other thing is that an answer which has been overtaken does not correct itself." She put two fingers on the leaf. "It sits in a chained book with a date under it and a figure above it, and it stays there."

"There is no instrument in this city that takes a sworn answer back. What there is instead is a second sworn answer, dated, in the same book." She let her hand stay where it was. "The two of them lie next to each other for as long as the account is open, and whichever one of us is standing here in nine years reads them as a pair."

The room was very cold and it had been very cold every day for four years.

"I have sworn a figure of persons from that house twice," he said, "on the first of August and on the first of October, and both times it was seven, and you told me at the mark that a book with the same number twice from the same man is a habit."

"Yes."

"What is it now."

"Now it is three instruments with your handwriting in them carrying one figure of persons from one door in a space of two months." Hanneke Ruis squared the leaf against the edge of the table. "And the third one is going to have to be eight, because it is the only figure that door can give, and that is not a thing you can decide. It is a thing a house does while nobody is looking at it."

## The Woman Who Asked For Nothing

He set them down the way he sets them down in a kitchen, well clear of the paper.

"Who is the eighth."

"I am not going to tell you that. It is on the leaf and you can read it, and a keeper does not repeat a name off a composition return to a man whose own address is in the book."

For the first time since he came in, she brought the chair round to face him square and folded her hands over her knee.

"But I will tell you whether she was keeping an address there before. It is the one question a keeper has to ask and the only one any of us may."

"Ask it."

"She was not." Hanneke Ruis folded her hands. "She has no prior holding of any kind at that address. She was on nothing whatever, at any time, until the second of the month, and then she walked into a fresh leaf of a book that had opened on the first, and a clerk ruled a line and wrote eight at the top of it. That is the whole of what she did and she did it at a public counter in daylight."

"She did it on purpose."

"She did it at about eleven in the morning and she came out of the counter and asked me nothing at all," said Hanneke Ruis, "and a woman who comes out of a counter asking me nothing has usually just done something that she knows I cannot do anything about, and I am not going to be the one who tells her."

He had been carrying that sentence since the night of the second and it came out of him in a room with no window, which is the only sort of room he has ever said it in.

"The woman who asked not to be written down is not in this room and not at those premises."

Hanneke Ruis did not move.

"Say the rest of that."

"The request was made on the ninth of July, in writing, in her own hand, and it was made to me, and I put it into a file in front of three officers on the sixteenth of August because I thought a file was a safe place for it."

His thumb went along the edge of the stone and his eyes stayed on his own hands.

"I said her name out loud in a kitchen on the thirtieth of September and she made me say it a second time and she would not let me carry the reason for her."

He turned his hand over on the stone.

"She is not on that leaf and she is not on any leaf in this building. If you write a description of this account down, her name does not go into it. That is not a preference. It is the whole of what I have."

"She is aware of what you are saying to me."

"No."

"Then she will be, when it is written." Hanneke Ruis picked up her pen and put it down again without using it. "I am not going to promise you a thing on that, and I want you to hear the reason, because I have not been in this room long enough to be able to do you a kindness in it and I would rather be plain than kind."

"Go on."

"There are two ways to put a hand at the foot of a page in this room," she said. "You initial it, and then it is arithmetic, and it is mine, and a keeper's arithmetic can be redone by the next keeper when she reads it wrong. Or you sign it, and then it is evidence, and evidence is a woman standing behind a piece of paper saying that she saw it."

"And you would stop being the keeper of this room."

"I would stop being the keeper of this room the afternoon I put my whole name under a figure about a household."

"There is a strap on the fifth book and I have been in here seventeen days, and I am not putting my name under a street because a man came down two flights and asked me to."

She squared the leaf again.

"Sign it yourself. In your own hand. Then the page is yours and it is evidence about you and not about the women at that address, and I will initial it as arithmetic and go on being the keeper."

"I am not going to sign a correction to eight."

"Then we are agreed, and there is nothing in this room that is going to argue with you about it." Hanneke Ruis turned the chair back to the books. "And I would like it understood that you came down here and told me the name is not yours to give before I asked you a single question about it, and that is the second time in a week that a person has done something for me that they did not have to do."

## Where It Is Fed From

He was half up off the chair and then he was back down in it, and neither of the two movements had been decided on.

"The broker cannot move it."

"The broker cannot move it. She has not held these keys since the seventeenth, and on the fifteenth of September she refused a thing you asked her for and made you write her a dated paper saying you would not ask her again."

"I have read that paper. It is in the strapped folder and it is in your handwriting." Hanneke Ruis did not look up. "It is the most careful piece of writing by a man in this room that I have seen, and I have read forty years of keepers' hands."

"She was right to take it."

"Everybody in this city takes it," she said. "That is not a complaint about the woman. It is what you do when a document comes at you."

He had about four seconds of stair left in him and used all of them.

"One more thing," said Hanneke Ruis, and she had her hand flat on the shut book with the strap over it. "You told a woman in September that this is fed at one address and that it has never gone anywhere else, and she wrote it under my column, and I have read it, and I have believed it, and I am going to keep believing it for as long as it is the only thing on the page."

"That is right."

"So here is the question, and it is the only one I have wanted to ask you since the seventeenth, and I have decided that I am going to ask it and that you may refuse it." She took her hand off the strap. "Where is it fed from."

He had an answer ready for anybody who asked him what a reservoir was.

He had no answer at all for a woman who held the keys to the only book in this city that had put his address on it.

"It comes there because of me," he said, "and I am not going to be able to tell you any more than that without telling you what I do between five and seven in the morning, and if I tell you that, then there is a third thing on this page with a man in it, and this office has spent four years not having one."

Hanneke Ruis wrote nothing down.

"Sit down again, then," she said. "And go up the stairs before somebody sends a runner after you, and know that I have asked you one question and you have given me nine words and an address, and that in four years it is going to be somebody else's turn to ask it."

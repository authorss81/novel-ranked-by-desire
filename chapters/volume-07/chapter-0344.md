# The Woman Who Held A City Still

The hearing was at two in the afternoon on the fourteenth of December in the room at the end of the corridor, and he was made to wait outside it for eleven minutes, and not one person in that corridor apologised for them or explained them.

There were nine chairs. Eight of them were the same chair. The ninth was at the head of a table that had been made for nine and had been repaired at some point near one end, and the repair was not hidden and had not been painted over.

The woman in the chair at the head had a slate in front of her and her hands flat on either side of it, and she was reading the requisition sheet from the top, in her own voice, at the rate of a woman reading an address.

"Second of October," she said. "The twenty-second of November. The third of December. Four seals."

That was the whole reading. She had not read the sheet aloud before and she read it now, all four parts, and she did not read the empty box twice, or once in a special voice, and that was the first thing he took in about her.

"Lin Hao," she said. "You have eleven minutes. Sit down if you want to sit down. The rule about sitting is not mine."

He sat down.

## What The Room Is For

"I am going to tell you what this room is, because you have been in this business for seven years and I do not think anybody has ever told you."

She put one finger on the slate.

"There were eleven of us once, in the year before I came to this chair, and we could take a person, and the eleven could do it in a morning, and in the fourth year of that arrangement a man was taken off a street in the east for a figure he had not earned, and in the years after that no clerk in this city could find the paper that said who had wanted him taken, because eleven men can all be right and nine men can be checked."

She turned the slate round.

"That is the whole of the arithmetic and there is nothing else in twenty years. If a person is taken, it is taken in front of nine people who are named, counted, and findable. Not kind. Not gentle. Findable. Any person in Ostara may stand at a counter and be told, on the ordinary stream, which nine, and where they sit, and what they last sealed."

"That is not mercy."

"It is not mercy. It is insurance, and it is also the only reason a person in this city can be said to have been wronged, and I have never in twenty years pretended to anybody that it is a kindness."

She squared the slate.

"You want to know why nine countable people could hold a city still for twenty years. That is why. Not because they are good. Because they can be counted, and a thing that can be counted can be argued with at nine in the morning in a room with a door."

## The Other Half

"And I am going to tell you the other half, because you will not get it from anybody else in this building and you have earned it, and because you have spent seven volumes arranging to be unarguable with and I would like you to know exactly what you spent it on."

She did not soften it and she did not enjoy it.

"The same arithmetic takes people out. A man whom nine people cannot find is a man this city does not look for. That is not theory. I have signed two in twenty years, and I watched one of them walk into the next district and be gone inside a month, and there was not one clerk in this city who could have described his face, and the clerk wrote *not traced* in a book, and the book was correct."

"You signed it."

"I signed one and I did not sign the other, and I have never once been certain which of those two decisions was the right one, and that is the honest state of a woman who chairs a room where nine men can take a city apart over a morning."

She let it stand and then she went on, which was the thing about her.

"You put eight women on a public list in October to get an account out of a priced book. I have read the list. Eight names, the order of the stair, the rooms against them, and a date against every line, and it went out on the ordinary stream at four o'clock and eleven divisions took a copy before nine in the morning."

"Yes."

"Every one of those eight can now be reached by a man with a piece of paper and no reason, and there is no instrument in this city that can undo a list, and I have wanted to say that to you since the thirty-first of October and I have had no standing to say anything, because the reason that list exists is the reason you are sitting in this chair, and I am the chair."
## What Alma Quayle Said

Alma Quayle was sitting in the seventh chair and had said nothing for the whole of the eleven minutes, and being recused does not mean being absent, and she had come.

She said it when the chair looked at her, and she said it to the chair and not to him.

"I will be asked about this in about four years and I would like it minuted now."

She put her hands together.

"I put my name to a description of that house on the twenty-seventh of October, against the head of it, on his asking and in his own request, in a kitchen, with a clerk in the room. I recused myself from the seals on that ground and I will not unseal myself."

"I want the minute to say that the chair heard me say it, and that I said it before anybody voted."

"Will you say anything else," said the chair.

"No. If I say anything else it will be an argument from a recused seat, and there is a person in this room who has spent seven volumes refusing arguments and I am not going to be the first one to offer him one."

The chair wrote four lines on the slate and looked up again.

## The Question She Asked

"You have nine minutes gone and you have not once asked me what I am going to do," said Anouk Ferreira. "Everybody asks me inside a minute. It is the first thing they say."

"What are you going to do."

"That is much worse," she said, "and it is the reason I have been in this chair for eleven years instead of the six the last chair managed."

She took her hands off the slate.

"So here is what the rest of this table will not tell you, because every one of us has wanted the sentence the other way. If the room does not carry that requisition, nothing happens to the account. It stays called. It is called at the first of every month and answered at none of them, and a called line that nobody answers is not a safe line. It is a line that a hundred offices can point at and none of them has to do anything about it."

"And then."

"And then it goes on being yours, and being called, and being unreachable, for as long as you live, and the only thing standing between you and a clerk with a pen is a ruled box with a tick in the corner of it that is empty." She looked at him. "Not wanting to be taken is not being safe. I have wanted a man to be safe for twenty years and I have never once been able to arrange it for anybody, including myself."

## What He Said

They gave him eleven minutes and he used about forty seconds of them.

"I want it to be a page with a name at the top of it," he said.

"I have never once wanted that in my life. I wanted the opposite for seven years and I was very good at it. And on the twenty-seventh of October, seventeen days ago, I put my own name at the head of a description in a kitchen, in front of eight women and a clerk, and I gave them the reason in four words about money, and the clerk wrote it in a book with the hour against it."

He stopped.

"That is the whole of what I came to say and it is not an argument. It is a thing I did four months ago for a reason I gave at a table in four words, and I do not know what it costs, and I am telling a room of nine people about it because there is nowhere else left to tell."

The chair looked at him for about nine seconds.

"That is the first honest thing anybody has said in this room in eleven years," said Anouk Ferreira, "and it is also the answer I was hoping you would not give me."

## The Hinge

"Do not tell me what you are going to offer me."

"I have not offered you anything. I have told you what this room is."

"You have told me what this room is twice," he said, "and both times you stopped one sentence short of the only reason you wanted me in a chair for eleven minutes, and I have been in enough rooms this year to know what a sentence that is being held back sounds like."

She looked at the slate for a while and then she said it, which he had not expected.

"Her hearing was on the seventeenth."

"Yes."

"Then you will be here on the seventeenth and I will put a thing in front of you that I have put in front of two people in twenty years, and neither of them took it, and one of them is alive and in a house on the west side and one of them is not alive." She squared the slate. "I would rather you heard the shape of it here, in a hearing, than in my rooms."

"Then I would rather you had not said that," he said.

"Neither would I," said Anouk Ferreira. "I have said it anyway. That is what a chair is."

## What The Chair Would Not Say

He had four minutes and he spent one of them on the only question worth a minute.

"If the room votes and the vote carries, what happens at nine o'clock the next morning."

"Nothing. That is what happens. There is no door and no cart and no hour." She closed the slate. "There is a piece of paper in a room in this building with a figure on it and your holding under it, and after that the room does with it whatever the room does, at whatever pace, for as long as the room exists, and the room is nine people and I am one of them and I have promised them nothing this year."

"Have you promised them before."

"Twice," said Anouk Ferreira, "and both times I kept it, and I would like you to understand that the offer I am going to make on the seventeenth is not an offer of safety. It is an offer of a machine, and you are the only person in seven years who has been in this business long enough to know what that is worth."

She stood up and put the slate under her arm.

"Nine seats. One of them has been empty since the first of November and I have not been able to fill it, and I have not told the room why."

"The reason is that I cannot find anybody I would trust to put a seal on a piece of paper. I have been looking since the second of December. I have not found anybody. That is the state of a room about to vote on the only thing it has voted on in four years."

Then the clerk came along the row of chairs and read the minute aloud, as minutes are read, and he wrote his name at the foot of it, and the room adjourned for the purposes of a hearing, and it was a quarter past three in the afternoon on the fourteenth of December and the chair had eleven minutes left and had given him none of them.

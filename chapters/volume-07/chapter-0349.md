# The Card Comes Down At Six

The last working day of the quarter is a day like the other twenty-nine in a city that does not keep months, except that the offices which set floors set them on that day and in a different hand, and by six in the morning there was a boy on a ladder at the north end of the square with a brush and a pot of paste and a card under his arm that he did not know how high to put.

He had been standing in that square since half past four. Beatrix Halm was with him and had her notebook open and her pencil out and had been looking at the north wall, which had carried two words on it since the twenty-second of November.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one," she said, to nobody, for the fourth time since the thirtieth of November, which was a thing she had done in the dark at a quarter to six for nine years and had never once done in daylight to a stranger.

## The Card

The card was not the plate. The plate was green and it went up at half past four once a week and the branch had been doing it for years. The card was a different thing, on different paper, in a different hand, and the boy had a list of numbers on a slip and did not know which line of it went where until a man from the plate office came out and told him.

At six exactly the card went up over the north wall.

"Floor of the quarter," said the man from the plate office, to anybody within earshot, because that is what they do. "Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five. It stood at eight thousand one hundred and forty-four since the thirtieth of September and it is eight thousand one hundred and forty-five today, and it does not move again until the thirtieth of March."

Then he read the standing figures the way he read them, at the rate of a man reading a list he has read many times.

"Premises nineteen one six, eight thousand one hundred and fifty-one. Premises six two four, eight thousand one hundred and forty-nine. Premises thirty-four six one one eighteen," he said, and then he stopped for about a second and a half, which he had not planned to do.

"Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five," he said.

The square did not make a sound. About eleven people were standing in it at six in the morning and not one of them said anything, and in about four seconds he took in that the reason was not that it was a good number.

It was the floor.

## The Arithmetic

The plate had read eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one since the twenty-ninth of November and had not moved once in all of December, because a plate is not a memory of a month.

A plate is a figure for a week. A figure moves when somebody else puts a deed in writing, and in December not one deed of his was written down by anybody except himself, and he had not stood in front of a wardrobe, a door or a gate doing anything a clerk would put in a book.

It had gone to eight thousand one hundred and thirty-nine on the twenty-second of November and eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one on the twenty-ninth, and it had stayed there for a month on the wall, thirteen short of where the floor stood since the thirtieth of September, and the two words had gone back up on the twenty-second and had not come down since.

Now the floor was eight thousand one hundred and forty-five, because the floor belongs to a different office and moves on the last working day of a quarter.

And he stood at it.

Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five, on the nose, on the first morning of the last day of the quarter, in a hand that had never once had anything to do with him.

[Ledger. One hundred and twenty-four lines. Nine turned. Nothing spent.]

Eight people had signed a witness line under a page on the twenty-ninth. That was the whole of the arithmetic. Eight hands had carried him from the bottom of his quarter to the line of it and not one grain past the line, and a man on the floor is not under it and a man on the floor is not over it.

He has been under a floor since the plate of the twenty-second of November, and eight other people put him on one in about eleven minutes.

## The Wall

Beatrix Halm turned round and looked at the wall.

"There is nothing on it," she said.

"What?"

"Those two words." She put her notebook down on the sill of the window and left it open. "They have been there since the twenty-second of November. *Out of band.* Forty-five points down in one week and eight in the next and the boy painted the green over with lead, and then the wall said it out loud, and then it stopped saying it because people get bored of a fact about a street."

She picked the notebook up again.

"It went up on the thirtieth of July and it came down in the middle of November when you reached seven, and it went back up on the twenty-second and it has been back ever since. Fourteen weeks and six days off that wall and five weeks and three days back on it, and this city has spent the whole of its memory on the five weeks."

She wrote the time against it.

"It is gone," she said. "It is not because you are liked. It is not because anybody has forgiven you, and it is not because anything has been resolved."

"It is because the number on the card is the number on the floor. A man standing on the floor is a man in band, and a wall in this city does not put two words on a man who is in band. It goes back up the day you go under, and it goes back up inside a week, because that is what it is for."

"That is a worse thing to be told at six in the morning than the other thing."

"It is the first true thing anybody has said to you about that wall since November," said Beatrix Halm, "and I have wanted to say it since the twenty-second and I could not say it with two words standing behind it."

She did not write anything else, and that was the whole of what she had come for.

## The Woman Two Flights Above A Laundry

There was one person in this city he wanted to tell, and she was about two hundred yards away from the bottom of this hill, and he had wanted to tell her every day since the second of December.

She had told him, on the night of the thirtieth of November, in her own rooms, that she was not the person he came to when he was afraid, and she had said it in the imperative and had made him hear it as one, and she had been right to.

He stood on the corner at twenty past six and thought about the two flights and the smell of soap coming up through the boards and about a drawer under a table with four sheets in it and an auditor's note on top of them dated the twenty-eighth, and about a request on the ordinary stream that had been served on a door at nine fifty on the first of December.

And then he did not go, and the not going was not an accident and was not a promise.

A woman who has told a man what she will not be, in her own rooms, is owed one evening of not being visited by him with good news.

She had said it was the only door she had, and she had built it herself out of a request on a form. He was not going to stand in it at twenty past six in the morning because a card said eight thousand one hundred and forty-five.

## What It Cost

Sun Meiling came down at about ten past six with her coat on over her nightshift and stood in front of the card with her hands in her sleeves and read it the way she reads everything, which is slowly and once.

"Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five," she said. "On the nose."

"Yes."

"Not over. Not by a grain. You have been asking for that line since the third of November and you have landed on it and not a step past, and the line is the floor and the floor belongs to somebody else." She took her hands out of her sleeves. "Do you know how it happened."

"Eight people signed a line under a piece of paper on the twenty-ninth."

"That is how it happened and you will not be able to do it again. That is the price and I want it said out loud at six in the morning on the last working day of a quarter, because you have spent the whole of the autumn being afraid of exactly this, and you have done it in the cheapest available way, which is by making other people write your name down."

"You have told me the price of an attested act eleven times since the seventh of November."

"I have told you the price to whoever was carrying it. It was the laundry and the gate and the yard, and a woman who would do it again and got nothing."

"Yesterday it was eight people in a room. One of them is a woman who signed a description of that house against the head of it two months ago and has recused herself from a requisition over it ever since. She will carry your name in a book of her own until the thirtieth of December four years from now. She will not say a word about it to anybody. And she asked for it to be minuted before anybody voted."

"There is one of the eight I cannot ask about that address."

"There is," said Sun Meiling, "and it is not the one who signed, and I am not going to tell you which, and I have never once in three years worked out how she holds a thing like that either."

He looked up at the card for a while. The boy had gone down the ladder and the paste was drying and about thirty people had come into the square since six, and not one of them stopped at the north wall, and that was the other half of it that nobody was going to put in a notebook.

He had spent seven volumes being a sentence on a wall.

At twenty past six on the thirtieth of December the sentence stopped.

The reason was eight people he had stood in front of on the seventeenth, and a page he had written himself at his own kitchen table on the twenty-ninth, and he went up the hill to a building where eight women lived and one of them had a copy of that page on a table and had not come down to the square and would not, and he was not permitted to ask her why.

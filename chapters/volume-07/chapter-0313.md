# A Woman With Thirty Years Of It

Alma Quayle was on the bottom step at seven on the evening of the twenty-fourth with nothing in her hands, in a coat over a working dress, and the kitchen table had been cleared because he had told them on the twentieth that it would have to be.

Eight women and a man and a chair pulled up to the door with a coat still on.

"I am not here for the classification," she said, before she had sat down. "I am going to say that first and I would like the whole table to hear it, because I have been in a kitchen once this month and it went badly and it was not anybody's fault but mine."

A single sheet went down on the cleared wood.

"Eight lines. A name, the date you took the address at those premises, and the room." She put her finger on the third column. "And I wrote the third column in myself, because the printed form has two, and a form with two obliges somebody to work out which of the two they would rather leave off."

"You are asking us to fill it in."

"I am asking each of you to write your own line in your own hand," said Alma Quayle. "And I want to be exact about what that is, because in about a week eight of you are going to have a reason to hate me and I would like you to be angry about the correct thing."

Her eyes went round the table.

"It is a courtesy. That is the whole of it."

"When this office makes a description of premises, the list is the composition, and the composition is a composition whatever anybody writes on a piece of paper in a kitchen."

"Your eight names are on a district leaf with a clerk's number in the corner and they have been since about eleven o'clock on the second of this month. My asking you to write them again does not make them exist. It makes them yours instead of a young man's handwriting."

"Then why do it."

"Because a list written in one hand by a clerk can be disputed and a list written in eight hands in one room cannot," said Alma Quayle. "Not by anybody in this building. If it is wrong, the eight of you know it is wrong before I have finished reading it, and you can tell me while I am still standing in your kitchen. That is what I have bought and it has taken me thirty years to work out that I am allowed to buy it."

## Eight Lines

Sun Meiling wrote hers first, because she had been under a bar since the first of September and there was a version of this she had wanted to get over with.

She wrote the name, and the date, and the room, and then she sat looking at it.

"That address has not been on anything I have written in nine years," she said. "And I would like it recorded that I did it in about eleven seconds and did not make a speech about it."

"You do not have to make a speech about it here."

"I know I do not. I have been making one about it for nine years and you have been the first person who has let me put a pen on paper instead." She pushed the sheet along. "Do not put the bar on it."

"The bar is a certificate matter and it goes on a certificate."

"Then leave it off this."

Petra Ilyin read her own line over the shoulder of the person on her left.

"Why the room."

"Because a description of premises is read by people who are imagining the premises," said Alma Quayle, "and the room is the only part of that line that stops them imagining wrongly." She turned the sheet about. "And because if I leave the third column blank I am going to be asked at the counter who filled this in and what each of them was doing at this address, and a clerk cannot answer that, and a woman who has lived at a door for nine years can."

Wren Kallo took the pencil when it came to her and did not read the line before she wrote it.

"Two October," she said, out loud, before she filled it in. "I am telling the table the date before I write it so that nobody has to be surprised by it in a week."

"You have been telling the table since the eighth of October," said Alma Quayle.

"I have been telling the table since the second, and you have been in my division since the twelfth, so you had it from a leaf on the twelfth and I have given it to you out loud in a kitchen on the second, and those are two different documents and I would like both of them to exist."

She wrote the date in.

Beatrix Halm wrote hers and did not close the notebook, which is not a thing Beatrix Halm does.

"What happens to it when it is finished."

"It goes into a description with a list attached to it, and the description is made on the twenty-seventh and it issues on the thirtieth at four in the afternoon, because the thirtieth is the last working day and this office holds an instrument to the last working day so that there is room at the end of it for somebody to be wrong."

"And after four o'clock."

"After four o'clock it is a public instrument and any division may read it and no reason is required, and I signed the rule in the fourth year of doing this after being told it was improper."

"That is what I asked and I did not know I was asking it." Beatrix Halm wrote the room down. "Nine years, and that is the answer, and I would rather have it on the twenty-fourth than on the twenty-ninth."

## The Question That Was Not Asked

Runa Sato had hers written and read the sheet from the other end of the table without touching it.

"Refusing," she said. "What does refusing buy."

"Nothing at all," said Alma Quayle.

"Then it is not a courtesy, it is a pantomime."

"It is not a pantomime and I am going to give you the exact reason, because you are the only person at this table who is going to ask it and I would rather answer you than be asked it in a corridor in November." She put her hands flat on the wood.

"The list exists. It is a composition return and it has been since the second of October and it has a clerk's number in the corner of it."

"If the eight of you sit here with your arms folded until midnight on the thirtieth, then on the morning of the first of next month the composition of those premises is eight and the list of those premises is eight, and the two of them agree, because they have always agreed. One was made out of the other."

"So nothing we write changes it."

"What you write changes what the description costs, and what it costs is decided by how many of you have to be examined about it later in life." Alma Quayle looked down the table.

"Eight women who wrote a line in their own hand are eight women who have been asked and answered. Eight women who wrote nothing are eight women a description has been done to."

"In about four years somebody opens that file and the eight names are on it either way. The only difference is whether there is a line in eight hands above them saying that they were in the room."

The kitchen went on doing what a kitchen does.

Nadia Ferrous had been holding the pencil since Petra Ilyin gave it back and had not used it.

"Say the last part of that again."

"What part."

"The part where she tells us the filling in is for us and not for her," said Nadia Ferrous. "I have been handed about four hundred forms in this city and not one of them was ever handed to me so that I would sign something that was already true."

"That is not four hundred forms."

"It is one hundred and forty, and I have been handed every one of them since I was a child in this city," said Nadia Ferrous. "And I would like the woman who classifies houses to know that I can tell a door from a machine at about six feet."

Wren Kallo had hers written before the fourth line went down and had read every other line twice since, and she had not said a word about any of them.

"Does the office go on it."

"No. The list carries where a person sleeps and a person does not sleep at a division."

"That is worth saying twice."

"It is not a rule," said Alma Quayle. "It is a thing nobody has ever asked to put on, and I have been the woman who decides what goes on for thirty years, and I decided it this afternoon at about four o'clock, and if you had asked me in August you would have got the other answer, because I have never once been asked."

Sun Meiling said, from four seats along: "That is the first rule anybody has ever given me that is on the side of the people in it."

"And I am going to have to be careful with it," said Wren Kallo, "because I have been asking questions my whole life and have never once been told that the answer was mine to choose."

## Agnete Lindqvist

She had not written hers and she was not going to, and everybody worked it out about a minute after she stopped picking up the pencil.

"There is no date," she said.

"There is a date."

"There is a date and I do not have one." Agnete Lindqvist had her cardigan sleeve turned back over her wrist and she was not looking at anybody. "I have been inside the Vestry chapel for eleven years and before that I was a rank-four clerk in an office in this district, and the date a form wants is the day I took the address at those premises, and I have never in my life taken an address anywhere."

Alma Quayle looked at the sheet for about four seconds.

"Then I will take it from the building," she said. "The keeper of that house has a book and it will have the year, and I will enter the year and put *from the building* against it in brackets, which is a thing I have done twice in thirty years and which no officer has ever queried."

"You have done it twice."

"I did it for a woman in the eleventh year who had come out of a confinement and I did it for a man in the twenty-second who had no address at all for eleven years, and I did it because the alternative was an invented date and an invented date is a lie with a form on it." She put the pencil down. "You have been indoors eleven years. You are not going to have a clerk invent a day for you because a column wanted filling."

Agnete Lindqvist wrote the year.

## The Line That Came Back

Beatrix Halm counted the lines on the way a clerk counts them, top of the stair down, and did it twice.

"How many lines are there."

"Eight. Seven are on this table, and one came back on the twentieth in the hand of a woman who sings at the Bellhouse, sent by a woman who sells flowers on the corner, because I asked her for her line in the ordinary way and she was working."

Alma Quayle put the eighth line down beside the other seven and did not make a point of it.

"She is not in this building tonight and she is not at a counter and she did not have to be either."

"I have been in this business for thirty years and that is the first composition figure I have ever assembled without asking one person to stand in a room."

Eight. Sun Meiling looked along the table at him and he did not ask the ninth question, and there was a stretch of about four seconds in that kitchen in which everybody at it knew precisely the shape of the thing that was not being asked and what it had cost him on the nineteenth to leave a form half filled in at a counter with a young man watching.

He did not put the question at all.

Alma Quayle gathered the eight lines into the order of the stair and squared them, and did not hurry.

"Thirty years," she said. "I have spent thirty years asking rooms what a thing is, and this afternoon eight women wrote it for me in eight hands in about eleven minutes and I did not have to describe one of them, and I did not have to guess one of them, and not one of them had to stand in a room being looked at while somebody worked out what to call them."

She put the sheet into her case and closed it.

"That is the whole of what the courtesy is. And it is over, and the next time I come up these steps it is for the real thing, and I am going to ask you all to be in this kitchen on the twenty-seventh, because a description is made in front of the people it describes."

"Seven o'clock."

"Seven o'clock, and I will bring no form," said Alma Quayle, "because there will not be one left to bring."

# The Council Minute In Four Hands

The minute went out on the ordinary stream at four in the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of December and it was attested in four hands before it left the building.

The chair's hand was first because the chair signs everything first. The second was Alma Quayle's, and she had asked for it on the fourteenth in front of a room, and it was written into the minute that she signed as a witness to what was said and not as a seal, and that the chair had heard her say so before anybody voted.

The third was the clerk's, because a clerk's hand on a minute is what makes it a copy of a thing and not a copy of a rumour.

The fourth belonged to the woman in the sixth seat who had put a seal on a sheet at thirteen minutes on the twenty-third and had withdrawn it in the same morning.

Four hands. That is the shape of every fact that has mattered to him since the twenty-ninth of June and he had stopped being surprised by it somewhere in the fourth month of that year and started being frightened of it, because a fact in four hands is a fact that four people have to be asked about, and one of the four is a woman who may not be asked and will say she does not know what he is talking about.

## The Copy

A runner brought it up the sixty-one at about ten past five on the evening of the twenty-sixth in a Bureau envelope with a number on it, and Beatrix Halm took it at the top of the stairs and gave it to him without opening it, because she had been on a platform all day and the corridor was full of people coming down.

He read it at the top of the steps with the light off.

*The minute of a sitting called by two members, held on the twenty-third of December. The subject was heard. A requisition of merit was tabled. The requisition named the account carried at premises 34-6-118 and the holding of thirty years and the figure of seven and the ground, and did not name the holder. Five seals were entered. The fifth was withdrawn by the member who had entered it. The majority was spent and the sheet was not in order. No requisition was carried. Nothing was taken.*

*Lin Hao was served with this minute on the twenty-sixth of December.*

Four lines of it were his name. He had spent thirty days getting his name to the top of a page and seven volumes getting it out of documents, and here it was in the middle of a record made by nine people in a room he had never been inside for longer than an hour.

## The Table

He read it out at the table because a document that comes into a kitchen with eight women in it belongs to all of them whether they asked for it or not.

Petra Ilyin read it twice with her hand over her mouth and then said what everybody was thinking.

"Four hands."

"Four hands."

"That is worth more than a song," said Petra Ilyin. "I have been in this city eleven years and I know what four hands is and I know what a song is worth, and the song is on every street in six of them, and this is in a room where nine people sit, and only one of those four is a woman who can be frightened."

"Alma Quayle," said Sun Meiling.

"Alma Quayle, who cannot be unseated and will not explain herself. Yes."

Sun Meiling had her glasses off and was turning them in her hands.

"You have read the fifth line of the document and I have read the first, and we have read the same document," she said, "and neither of us wrote it, and that is what it is for. That is the entire point of a thing in four hands. It does not make it true. It makes it so expensive to say otherwise that the room has usually got on with something else."

"And if the room does not get on with something else."

"Then four people have to be asked, in their own divisions, about a minute of a sitting, and one of them is a woman at this address, and one of them is a recused woman who signed a witness line, and one of them is a clerk." She put her glasses back on. "In four years. And they will be found, because a minute in four hands can always be found. That is what it is *for*."

## What It Does Not Do

Beatrix Halm had the notebook open and had not written anything.

"It does not say you lost," she said. "Everybody at this table is going to say you lost today and everybody at this table is wrong. It says no requisition was carried and nothing was taken, and that is the first time in this year that a piece of paper about you has said what did not happen."

"Then it is a piece of paper about him that does not say he won."

"Nothing in this city is going to say he won. There is no box for that."

He put the minute down on the table where all eight of them could see it, and put his hand flat on the wood beside it, and did not put the hand on the paper.

"It does not say he won," he agreed. "It says a thing cannot be carried without somebody to carry it off, and there is nobody to carry it off. That is all."

"That is all," said Beatrix Halm, "until somebody writes their name down."

## What A Quarter Is

Runa Sato had been standing at the back of the kitchen with her coat on for twenty minutes, which is how she stands when she is in a building and not in it.

"You want to know how long you have," she said. "So here is how long you have, and it is less than anybody thinks."

She took her hands out of her coat pockets.

"A requisition lies in the quarter it is laid. A quarter closes on its last working day and there is no carrying anything over the line. A sheet that is void in one quarter is not a sheet in the next one. To come at that account in January they must start again, and start again means a new sheet, laid fresh, with the ground done again from the beginning."

"How many would seal a new sheet."

"Two to call the sitting and then five to carry it." She counted on her fingers without looking at them. "Nikolai Vester has walked out of that room and has not been in it since the twenty-third. The fourth seat has been empty since the first of November. Alma Quayle is recused until somebody in that room decides she is not, and she has written to them twice."

"So three."

"So three, and one of those three is a chair who made you an offer on the seventeenth and has not repeated it and does not intend to."

He put his hand flat on the table.

"Then a filled box may not be enough."

"That is the arithmetic of the room and it is not my arithmetic." Runa Sato put her coat collar up. "You fill that box and the box may still not carry anything, because five people have to want it and there are not five people left who want it. And that is the good news, and it is also the reason you should do it anyway, because a filled box is the only thing in this business that does not care whether five people want anything."

## The Part He Did Not Say Out Loud

The kitchen was very quiet for about nine seconds, and in that kitchen nine seconds was a long time, and he let all nine of them go past without filling them, which was a thing he had learned in the middle of October and had not forgotten.

Then he said it.

"The first day of the next month is a valuation," he said, "and the line will be called again, and a requisition can be laid again in a new quarter, and the fifth seal has not gone anywhere. It has simply gone to a different quarter."

Nadia Ferrous put her cup down.

"So they will come back."

"They will come back." He looked at the minute. "And they will come back with the same sheet, because there is no other sheet, and on that sheet the *held by* line is empty and somebody has already ticked the corner of it to say so. And the only person alive who can put anything into it is the man it is about."

"You have not put anything in it."

"No."

"Then say the true thing at this table," said Nadia Ferrous, "because we have all been sitting in this kitchen since November and we have watched you refuse four false slips and a woman's leg and a term you swore in the dark, and we would like to know what you intend to do on the twenty-ninth of December."

He said it.

"I intend to write it down."

Sun Meiling took her glasses off again.

"Not the reason," she said. "Say what you are going to write and where it is going and who will hold it, in that order, and then I will put my glasses back on."

He did not do it. He stood at the head of the table with his hands at his sides and could not get the three parts out in the right order, and eight women watched a man fail at an ordinary sentence, and none of them helped him, because helping him would have been the same as writing it for him.

That was the end of the evening. Beatrix Halm wrote the time against it, and then wrote one more line under the time, which was the first line she had written about him in nine years that was not a figure or a shape or a place.

## The Top Of The Sixty-One

He sat on the top step at about eleven o'clock with the minute on his knees and the hall light off, and it was cold, and the stair took other people's weight without a sound and it would take his in about four minutes.

There were two days left before the card came down and it was two days before a quarter closed, and he had told a kitchen full of women that he was going to write something, and he had said it out loud, which was the instrument he had and the only one.

A thing in four hands could be got at with four hands.

A page with a name at the top of it in his own hand was not a thing anybody could hold. It was a thing anybody could read.

He had known the second thing since the seventeenth of October, in a kitchen, in a clerk's hand, with the hour against it.

He had spent two months and nine days being unable to make his hands do it, and on the twenty-ninth of December he was going to have to sit down at a table with a pen and do it in about eleven minutes.

There was not one person in this building he could ask to help him, and there was one person he was not permitted to ask anything at all.

The stair did not make a sound when he came down off it either.

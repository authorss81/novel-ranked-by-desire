# The Reason They Could Not Use

Marguerite Vale came up the sixty-one at about four in the afternoon on the tenth of December with her coat over her arm and nothing in her hands, which was the way she had come the first time in October and had not come since.

She was not on the list. She was not at the premises. Her books had gone to nine people at seven on the morning of the sixteenth of November and had been read at nine and had not come back, and since that morning she had no position, no roster, and no office in this city that would answer a letter for her.

She sat down and did not take tea.

"I have been shown the sheet," she said. "One of the nine was at my guild table on the seventeenth of November and he read it out to four of us over the books, and he read it twice, and on the second time he stopped in the middle and asked whether anybody knew what a requisition was laid against, and not one of us did."

## Four Parts

She put her handbag on the floor instead of on the table, which Sun Meiling noticed and nobody else did.

"A requisition has four parts," said Marguerite Vale. "The subject. The holding. The figure. The ground. Three of them are written in and they have been written in since the second of October, which is the day a fresh leaf of a quarter-book took eight against a line that says seven."

"The subject is the account at premises 34-6-118," he said.

"The subject is the account. The holding is thirty years of a man, and it is stated, and I will come back to the holding. The figure is seven. The ground is that the line was called on the first of December at ten to nine and cannot be paid and cannot be produced, and there is no production off that door because you refused to swear that address at seven on the nineteenth of October and a clerk wrote the refusal on a public leaf in front of you."

"That is the strongest ground anybody has had this year," he said.

"It is the best ground in this city and I have priced better than it for twenty years."

She turned her cup round twice without lifting it.

"They have had it since the second of October. They had it in a clerk's hand on a fresh leaf by the fourth. They had it in four divisions by the sixteenth. They had it in fourteen by December. And not one of the nine wrote the word requisition until the seventeenth of November, which was six weeks and three days after they could have."

## What It Is Laid Against

"The subject of a requisition is never the thing," Marguerite Vale said. "That is what nobody in that room has understood, including the man who read it out at my table. The subject is the account, never the holder. What is laid against is the holder, and out of the holder comes the taking, and the price goes into the room's keeping, and from that hour the room administers it."

"So it is not a seizure."

"It is a purchase. It is a compulsory purchase at a stated figure, made by nine people who can be counted, and it is the most orderly instrument in this city. People are not frightened of it because it is violent."

She let him sit with it for a second before she went on.

"They are frightened of it because from that hour the thing is administered. Administration is forever, and there is nobody in this world you can appeal to about a thing that is simply being administered."

Sun Meiling said, "Say the other half of it."

"The other half is that he has no holding to take. Not a small holding. None. A requisition cannot be laid against a door, and his account is carried at a door, and there is no document in this city that says he holds it, because a document saying a man holds an account only gets written when somebody swears to it, and he has never sworn to it."

## Ninety Seconds

"You did it," said Sun Meiling.

"I did it," said Marguerite Vale.

She said it to the table and not to him.

"On the sixteenth of September he came to me with a description of a house instead of a figure, and I had ninety seconds to price it, and I priced it at seven in ninety seconds, and he knew exactly what he was doing and did it anyway."

She put that to the table rather than to him.

"I did what he asked me to do in the time I had, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have never once been sorry. The price of it is that there is no office in this city that will answer a letter for me, and my books are in a room I am not in."

"I did not ask you to—"

"You asked me for a figure and I gave you the figure you asked for, and I am not here to be forgiven for arithmetic." She turned to him for the first time. "You are not going to say thank you, so do not start. If you thank me I will get up and go, because I have been thanked by a great many men for twenty years and it has never once cost anybody anything."

The kitchen let that stand.

## The Fourth Of December

"There is one more thing and then I will go, because I have nowhere to be and that is the whole of my life at present."

She took her handbag up onto her knees.

"On the sixteenth of November, at seven in the morning, nine people read my books at a guild table. That is where this started. Not at the Exchange, not in a division. At my own table, with my own figures, in front of nine men and women whose entire interest in the world is being able to count."

"That is not his fault," said Runa Sato.

"It is not anybody's fault, which is worse," said Marguerite Vale. "I put them on that table because a woman who keeps a position and a woman who has a position are not the same thing, and because I was told in the spring that I would be replaced in the year by a woman who had never met one of us, and because I would rather be exposed and standing than protected and gone."

She looked around the table at eight women and one man.

"Nobody at this table has blamed him and nobody here is going to, and I am saying it out loud anyway, because a thing that is only half-known gets told twice and comes out worse the second time. The exposure that saved me handed a room the best reason anybody has had this year, and it happened because a man asked me for a figure, and both of those are true, and neither of them can be got rid of by anybody in this kitchen."

## What She Would Not Do

Petra Ilyin had been leaning on the table with her arms folded for about ten minutes.

"You could go in there," she said. "You could stand in that room tomorrow and put your books on the table again and ask four people to look at a line. You have more right to be asked than anybody in this kitchen."

"I have no position."

"That is exactly why you would be asked," said Petra Ilyin. "There is not one of the nine who would be able to make you stop, because none of them has anything you could refuse them with."

Marguerite Vale shook her head.

"If I go into that room they will hear a broker," she said, "and they will hear a broker whose books were on a table of nine people on the sixteenth of November, and every word I say will be priced before it is finished. I have nothing to sell that room. Everything I know how to price, it can price better than me, and it does it without needing me in the room."

She turned her cup round in the saucer and did not look up.

"And I am not going to be the woman who went in and tried. I have been the woman who tried. It was very expensive and it was a very long time ago."

## What A Fifth Seal Is

"The fourth seal went on on the third," she said. "I know that because one of the nine asked a guild clerk a question on the fourth and the clerk was not slow about it. Five of nine carry it. Four have carried it and the fifth has not, and the fifth has not because there is no paper to put in the box."

"There is a box on the sheet."

"There is a box on the sheet with a clerk's tick in the corner of it and nothing in it, and I have seen the sheet. The tick is against the *held by* line, and it is the only tick on the whole of the four parts."

She put her handbag down on the floor again.

"Everything else is full. That box is checked and empty, and it has been checked and empty for as long as that sheet has had a copy taken of it, and the room cannot carry a requisition without it, and it has been sitting in front of four seals for a week."

"Then somebody writes his name in it," said Petra Ilyin.

"Somebody has to write it in it," said Marguerite Vale, "and it cannot be one of the nine, because a member of the room does not establish a holding for the room to take. It cannot be an auditor, because an auditor records. It cannot be a woman on a list of persons, because that is where eight of us started and it is a list of who sleeps where."

She stood up and got her coat off the back of her chair.

"It has to come from the man, and it has to come from him in a document of his own, and there is no such document in this city, because he has never in his life written one."

He said nothing at all, which was the correct thing and cost him the whole of what he came for.

He wanted to say it at that table. He had the sentence and he had had it since the fifteenth of September and he had given it out loud twice, in a kitchen and in a room, and he had never once put it on a page in his own hand.

Marguerite Vale went down the sixty-one steps and the door at the bottom shut, and eight women looked at a man who had said nothing for nine minutes, and Beatrix Halm wrote the time against it.

Nothing had moved all day except a word in a box on a sheet in a room where nine people sit, and it had moved in the direction it had been moving since the second of October, and there was nobody in this kitchen who could stop it and one man who could have written it down in about nine seconds and did not, and everybody at that table understood that he would be asked again on the seventeenth, in a room with better chairs in it.

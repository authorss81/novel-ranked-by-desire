# Seven On A Wall In A New Shade

On the fifteenth of November at about half past four in the afternoon the weekly plates went up on the ranks wall in the square, and Beatrix Halm was at the north end of it with the notebook she had carried for nine years.

Eighteen days since the twenty-seventh of October, when the instrument had been settled. Twelve days since the third of November, when he had asked the kitchen to let him be seen. Ten days since the fifth, when he had lifted a grille with four witnesses and it had not scored.

She read the lower half the way she always read it, bottom to top, with her finger in the air an inch off the paint.

Then she stopped.

She read it again.

She took the pencil out of her coat and wrote the figure down with the time against it, in the small level hand she used for walls, and she did not say a word until she had written it twice.

## The Card

Beside the ranks wall, bolted to the railings at shoulder height, there is a painted board with the bands on it in white on black. It has been there since before he came. Nobody reads it except newcomers and women with notebooks.

On the fifteenth it said, in fresh paint at the foot of it where the painter had touched it up that morning:

*Band seven: weekly attested eight thousand one hundred and seventy and above. Requisition of merit from a person. The sealed archive.*

He had never read that board all the way down. Beatrix had. She had it by heart the way she had every card since March.

Under it the new plate for premises 34-6-118 said:

Eight thousand one hundred and eighty-four.

Forty above a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-four. Fourteen above the painted line for seven.

The words that had stood since the thirtieth of July were gone. In their place, in a green the square had never had, were two words that no painter in that square had ever mixed for him.

She stood in the dark at a quarter to six with her hands cold round the pencil and read those two words three times before she believed them.

Then she wrote them under the figure, exactly as painted, with the shade named beside them in brackets, because that is how she writes a wall when the wall changes its manner.

## Three Names

He came down at about six with his collar up and found her still there, with two other women beside her who had no business being at a wall in the dark.

The first was the woman from the laundry whose hand he had held for four seconds on the fourteenth. She had finished her round early three mornings running and the basket clerk had written early against her name with the time, three times, and on the third time had added, in her own hand, that the early finishing had begun after a morning's relief in the yard.

The second was the clerk with the bad back who carried returns up three flights every morning at about eight. She had got to the top sooner three mornings running because a man held the door, and the desk clerk had written it down, and on the third morning she had written herself that the man was the attendant and that she had seen him do it without being asked.

The third was the day clerk from the wash-house yard who had written the slip on the fifth with four witnesses on the back of it. She had watched the grille run clear for ten days and had written, on the fourteenth, that the clearing had saved the branch a contractor's visit and that the attendant had cleared it by hand on the fifth with witnesses in the room.

Three slips. Three hands that were not his. Three names underneath his work.

None of them had been arranged. He had not picked the witnesses. He had not written a word of any attestation. He had stood beside people finishing things and let them lift, and walked away before the clerk came, and the clerks had come anyway.

That was the posture Tessa had given him on the ninth. That was what had moved a plate that had not moved a grain in two hundred and forty readings.

He stood beside Beatrix in the dark and looked at the green words and registered nothing for about four seconds. Then it hit him.

It was not triumph. It was fear, plain and cold, in the stomach, because for the first time in this book he was a figure on a public wall every week, and the man no division could read was now legible where anybody could see him.

## What It Costs

"Who pays."

Beatrix shut the notebook.

"They do," she said. "Whoever attests carries it. Three women have put their names to things you did. In about four years somebody reads those slips and asks where they came from. That is the price. There is no other version."

"And the quarterly card."

"The quarterly card does not move," she said. "The fifteenth is not the last working day of a quarter. The floor stays at eight thousand one hundred and forty-four until six on the thirtieth of December. This is the weekly plate. It moves every week on attested deeds. That is the difference and you know it."

He dipped his head.

He grasped what it cost one woman waiting in a square after dusk with nine years of his plate kept in her own hand.

He had been band six on a piece of paper and band five on his plate for five years. He was now seven on a wall in a shade the square had never had. The option was closed. He could never be unscored again.

Beatrix put the pencil away and did the thing she had done on the thirtieth of August, on the thirtieth of September, and on every wall since March. She read the figure out loud, once, so that there was a witness to her reading.

"Eight thousand one hundred and eighty-four."

"Say the words."

She said them.

He listened the way he had listened on the fifth when water went with a sound like a throat clearing, and he did not write anything down, because the writing was hers.

They walked home together past the wash-house where the grille ran clear. At the bottom of the sixty-one she stopped.

"One more thing," she said. "Marguerite Vale was at the wall at five. Before me. She read it before I did. She stood there for about a minute with her gloves on and did not write anything. Then she went up the hill."

"Did she speak."

"She said one sentence to nobody in particular," said Beatrix Halm. "She said the Vant's whole exposure is on that wall now. Then she went."

He went up the steps with the green words still in front of his eyes.

In the kitchen eight women waited round a table that had not been cleared. He told it straight, with the figure and the shade and the three names, and when he had done nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Sun Meiling set a cup in front of him.

"Drink," she said. "You are seven. You did it badly, in public, and it cost three women something. That is what you came to do on the third."

He drank. The tea was hot and plain.

He wanted, concretely, to go back to being unscored, and he knew he never would, and the knowing sat in him like a stone he had swallowed on purpose.

## What The Painter Mixed

The painter who mixed the green came down at about six with his bucket still wet and stood beside Beatrix looking at his own work.

"That shade was ordered for the quarterly card," he said. "It came down wrong. Too much copper in it. The office would not take it for December. I used it for the weekly because the weekly takes what it is given."

"Will it stay."

"It stays until the rain takes it," he said. "It is not the quarterly shade."

"The quarterly goes up on the last working day of a quarter by a different office in a different hand," he said. "8,142 on the thirtieth of July. 8,143 on the thirtieth of August. 8,144 on the thirtieth of September. 8,145 on the thirtieth of December, which is the card this run ends on."

Beatrix wrote that down too, word for word, because painters know walls the way clerks know books.

"And the board."

"The board was touched up this morning," he said, nodding at the painted bands in white on black. "Seven at eight thousand one hundred and seventy and above. That has been there since before you came. Nobody reads it except newcomers and women with notebooks."

"We read it," said Beatrix Halm.

He laughed once and went off with his bucket, leaving them in the dark with the green still wet enough to shine under the lamp.

He stood a while longer with his collar up, thinking of tolerance three, six under turned to forty over, out of band turned to seven. In the morning the ordinary stream would carry the weekly figure to eleven divisions. By December, fourteen. It could not be stopped and it could not be unpicked and it was not a weapon. It was a piece of paper that said where a man stood.

He thought of the three women who had put their names to things he did, laundry and gate and yard, each carrying his legibility in a book with a date for four years, and grief and gratitude rose in him together, equally, and neither cancelled the other.

At the bottom of the sixty-one Beatrix stopped again, the way she had the night before.

"Three attestations move the plate," she said. "That is still true. What moves next is not the plate. It is the Vant. Marguerite read it first for a reason. Go home and wait for the knock. Do not go to her. Let her send."

He waited. The knock did not come that night. It came the next afternoon, by a boy with no message but her name.
He touched the wet green paint once with one fingertip, lightly, and Beatrix slapped his hand away the way she slaps tram doors shut.
The lamps held steady against the wind coming off the yards.
The crowd thinned as the cold bit, leaving only those with business at the wall.
Beatrix breathed on her cold fingers and wrote on, steady.

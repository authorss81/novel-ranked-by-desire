# Thirty Years Arranged Into One Question

He took the cloth off the table at about half past six on the twelfth of October and put the cups on the dresser and the docket book in the drawer under the stairs where it lives, and he did it in front of them, deliberately, so that the room would know the table had been cleared for a reason.

Eight women and a man, and one chair pulled up to the door with a coat still on.

"She has been on the Bellhouse since two," Wren Kallo said. "She came in about twenty past six and stood there and would not take her coat off, and when I asked her if she wanted tea she said she would rather hear it."

Isolde Marchetti put her hand on the back of the chair.

"I would rather hear it," she said.

Alma Quayle came up the sixty-one at seven without hurrying and without knocking and she was not carrying the case.

She stood in the doorway for about four seconds and looked at eight people and a table and a shelf over the sink with a certificate on it, and then she took her coat off, put it over the rail of the fourth chair, and said nothing about any of it.

"Thank you for clearing the table," she said. "I know what that costs a kitchen and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

"You have been in this building before," said Runa Sato.

"I have been in the building behind this one, twice, in a corridor, and I have met one woman in this room once, in that corridor, and both times I asked her a question I had already written down." Alma Quayle sat down. "I have never been in this kitchen and I am not here as a policeman, because I do not have one and I could not use one, and I am not here to take anything, because there is nothing in this room I am entitled to take."

"Then what are you here for."

"To ask one question."

## The Question

"There are forty-one forms in the Wing," said Alma Quayle, "and I have written or amended every one of them at some point, and I have a clerk who can tell you the number of boxes on each. There is no box on any of the forty-one for what I am going to ask you, and that is not an oversight, and I want that on the table before I ask it."

The kitchen did what kitchens do, which is nothing.

"Where is it fed from."

Runa Sato put her hands flat on the cloth.

"That is not a question you may ask in this room."

"It is the only question I have and it is the one the description needs, and it is being asked in a room with eight women in it because a description of this household is going to be read by about forty officers who will never stand in a kitchen." Alma Quayle did not raise her voice. "And it is being asked in front of the household because a woman who is described with an empty room is described with nobody standing in front of her."

Beatrix Halm had her notebook out and had not written anything in it.

"How many pages is a description," she said.

"That depends entirely on how much of it you have prepared for me."

"That is an answer from somebody who reads a lot."

"It is the only answer I have and I am not going to dress it up at eight o'clock in the evening in a kitchen."

Agnete Lindqvist said the thing that everybody else was circling.

"You have asked it in a room where nobody can be compelled to answer," she said. "That is a choice and it is the only one in this city that is genuinely yours, and you made it by coming to a kitchen rather than a counter, and I would like you to know that I noticed it and that the rest of the room did not."

Alma Quayle looked at her for about two seconds.

"Noted," she said.

## What She Is Not Here For

"I am not here for your freedom," she said. "I am not here for it and I have not come about it, and if anybody in this room has been expecting an officer of the Classification Wing to walk in on a weeknight and take something, they have been expecting the wrong woman and the wrong evening."

"Then what has happened to you," said Sun Meiling, "that makes a person put thirty years into a shape like this one?"

Alma Quayle did not answer for long enough that the room understood she was going to answer it.

"My son scored two." She said it flatly and with no preparation at all, the way a clerk reads a box. "He was two hours from the front of a queue at the Exchange on the morning of a valuation when his plate went. A man behind him told the queue to move along because the reading was finished."

"He walked out and told two people on the stairs what his score was, and that is the last thing anybody in this city has on record about him."

Nobody said anything.

"He had a condition and he was not going to talk to a physician about it," said Alma Quayle. "That is the second half and I have said it once. He is not the reason I do this and I am not here about him and I have not spent thirty years about a boy of sixteen who could not be bothered to be ill in the right building."

"Then what is the reason," said Petra Ilyin from the doorway with the bundle still in her hand.

"The reason is that for thirty years I have been the person who is asked what a thing is, and for thirty years every one of the things has been a thing," said Alma Quayle. "Somebody has lost a fortnight of a hearing, and the answer is a mislaid slip. Somebody has a plate that does not add up, and the answer is a reading."

"I have never once been handed something that is not a thing, and I have built an entire working life on the certainty that there is nothing else."

She put her hands flat on the cleared table.

"And then in September a man carried a paragraph down two flights of a cold stair and handed it to a keeper and asked her to put a figure on it, and that is the first time in thirty years that anything has come into this building from a person who could not describe what he was carrying, and he described it perfectly, and I have been trying to get to the bottom of it ever since."

## Thirty Seconds

"May I say something," said Runa Sato, "that is not about the question."

"May you say anything."

"My division will call the sheet a security that has been overtaken. The Field has no category for that, and when we cannot find a category we write the nearest thing that is true, and the nearest thing that is true about this is that a man in Ferrier Street lied at a valuation on the first of October."

She put her hands flat on the cloth.

"He did not lie. He counted seven women out loud in nine seconds and all eight of them were in the building. That is not a lie. That is a room changing between two dates."

"That is correct and it is the reason I am here," said Alma Quayle. "A security that has been overtaken is the paper. What I need is what is under the paper, and there is one person in this city who knows what is under the paper and does not live at the address it is carried at, and that is the reason I am asking the question in a room instead of at a counter."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"You do not have to answer it," she said. "Nothing in this room can make you, and I have not come with anything that could. You can sit there and say nothing for as long as you like and I will have my answer and it will be a good one."

He had about thirty seconds.

He turned both palms down onto the bare cloth and started in.

"It comes out of a street," he said. "Not out of a person. Out of a street, in the dark, between five and seven in the morning, four times a night, and there is not one witness in it."

"By the time it has moved three times the first thing that fed it has gone to bed and nobody alive knows it has been given to anybody at all."

"And if I sit here and tell this room what that street is, then every person at this table is inside a description, and not one of you chose it."

He put his hands down flat.

"I have been the only thing standing between this house and a sentence for seven years, and I am the sentence."

He stopped.

The room did not move.

"That is the end of it," he said. "You asked where it is fed from. It is fed from me."

Isolde Marchetti took her hand off the back of the chair.

"Nine hundred," she said.

Every head in the kitchen turned.

"Not in this room," said Isolde Marchetti. "On a street behind an haberdasher's that shut in the spring, at about a quarter to eight in the evening, four times a night for four years. Nine hundred seats and a bar at the side of it and a chorus that goes round twice."

"Sing about what."

"I sing about a house at the top of a stair." She let go of the chair. "I have sung it about eleven times and I have never once been asked where the house is, and I am telling you now that I know it is six streets from here and that every one of the people who have sung it with me has decided it is their own block."

## The Thing She Said In The Corridor

The kitchen went back to the question and stayed on it for about forty minutes, and she did not get an answer she could use, and she said so herself before anybody else could.

Beatrix Halm wrote the figure of eighteen days at the top of a clean page and shut the notebook.

Runa Sato asked what her division would be told and Alma Quayle told her, exactly and in order, and Runa Sato wrote it down in her own shorthand and said that was fair.

Wren Kallo asked whether a description of a household had to list the household and Alma Quayle said that it did, and Wren Kallo said *yes* and did not say anything else, and Sun Meiling said that she had known that since about half past six that evening.

Sun Meiling was the one who said the name.

"There is a bench upstairs," she said, "in a walled place above the tram line, where nine of them sit once a month, and they can take merit off any citizen in this city by a majority of nine. Every person at this table knows it and it has never once been said out loud at this table."

The room let her have it.

"There," she said. "Said out loud, in a kitchen, at my own table. It is a room upstairs and not a knife, and I would like to go back to the work now."

Alma Quayle put her coat on at the door and took her case from the hall and came back up to the top of the sixty-one.

They walked down together as far as the fourteenth step.

"What you said in that kitchen," said Alma Quayle, "about it being the sentence and about your having stood between the house and a sentence for seven years, is the truest thing anybody has said to me in four years, and I am not going to pretend that I did not hear the other thing in it."

"Which other thing."

"That the sentence would not have been one person." She stopped on the step. "And it would not have been two. It would have been eight, and it would have had a figure at the top of it, and there is an instrument in this city that can put a thing in front of nine hundred people in one evening and make them believe it, and it is not an office and it is not a wing."

He turned round on the step.

"Say the name."

"Say it yourself at some point this week and do not write it down." Alma Quayle went down the rest of the steps without hurrying. "And then find out whether she will do it for you, because you have eleven days before the thirtieth and I have nine minutes before my office stops taking my referrals, and I would rather she did it before either of us."

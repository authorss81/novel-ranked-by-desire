# The Head Of Classification

The sub-level stair was warm again on the morning of the twenty-third, and the runner at the top of it read the slip, looked at Lin Hao, and waved him down without saying anything at all, which is a thing the floor had apparently decided to do.

Alma Quayle was in the corridor outside the fourth door with the stool out and her coat over the rail, and the table with the cloth on it had a hand at the far end of it that had been there since the tenth of October and had been there every day he had been down.

"You have come to ask me about a form."

"I have come to ask you what a form is for."

"That is the same question with the manners on it and I have been asked the polite version twice this month." She took a sheet off the rail and held it out of reach. "Sit down on the bench or stand up, and either way I am going to read you something that is printed, because you have had four people tell you things about this building in their own words and you have come upstairs to the person who is paid."

## Three Offers

"You have refused three things in thirty years."

"Four."

"Three of them were mine to refuse and the fourth was a joke."

"Three were mine and the fourth was a joke two men in this building made together, and I enjoyed it." The sheet went face down on the stool.

"The first was in the year the district offices changed over and it was a seat. Records. Nine clerks, a ledger, and a room with a window in it."

"The woman who ran it then went out the way women in that building went out in those years, and I was the obvious person, and I said no in four words, and I have never regretted it."

"And what was that one for."

"Because records decides what a thing was and I do not want to decide what a thing was," said Alma Quayle. "I want to be the one who has to say what it is while somebody who has an interest in the answer is standing in front of me, with the date on it."

"The second was nine years after that and it was the whole of the Correction Office under one roof. It came with a title that had the word *integrity* in it."

"I read the warrant before I answered it. The warrant gave me the power to reopen anything I had signed." She did not raise her voice. "You do not want a warrant to reopen what you have signed. You want to have been right the first time, in a room, out loud, with a man who can go to a board and say that you said it."

"And then the third."

"The third is the one people ask about." She turned the sheet over and did not look at it. "I was head of a wing. Not this one. Assessment, which is the wing that decides what a person is worth. It is the best room in this building and the warmest and there is a window. I said no, and it took me nine days, and I did not sleep on the eighth night, and I still think it was the correct decision and I have never been sure."

"What decided it."

"A boy of sixteen scored two and stood two hours from the front of a queue and walked out of it," said Alma Quayle, "and everything that has happened in my working life since has been the other answer to that, and I have wanted to be the woman who is standing in the room when somebody finally has to say what the thing is."

## The Form

She picked the sheet up and gave it to him.

It was one side of a sheet of the same paper as the referral, and it was not filled in, and it had a box in the middle of it that the referral did not have.

"Head of premises as subject of description," he said.

"Head of premises as subject of description." Alma Quayle nodded. "One line of address. One line of figure. One line of reason. One line for the head, with the date and the hand. And then the list, which is attached and goes with it, because otherwise the thing is a man's opinion about himself and it is worth the paper it is on."

"Attached."

"Attached and issued together and not one of them can go out without the other." She sat down on the stool.

"That is not in the instructions. That is thirty years of me, and I put it in myself in the fourth year of doing this."

"A man with a bar above me told me it was not proper. I told him what I thought of his bar. It has been in ever since, because I am the head of this wing and nobody has ever overruled me on paper."

He read the whole side.

It did not take him long.

"Any division may read it."

"Any division may read it, and it goes out at four in the afternoon in the ordinary way on the day it is made." Alma Quayle put her hands on her knees.

"The list does not become public by being described. It becomes public by being issued."

"Up to four o'clock on the thirtieth, that sheet is a piece of paper on a stool in this corridor. At four it is on the ordinary stream, and by nine the next morning it is at every counter in this city that has a reason to ask, and at every counter that hasn't."

"The list has to be attached."

"The list has to be attached, and the list has to be true on the morning it goes, and the list is the composition of those premises, and I have to sign it." She looked at him. "Not him. Me. That is the only signature on the whole instrument and it is mine, and I know what I am signing, and in about four years somebody will read my name under eight lines and ask me whether I stood in a kitchen and watched eight women fill in a form."

"Have you?"

"I have read it," said Alma Quayle. "In my own division, in the ordinary way, since the twelfth, at a desk, about eleven minutes' work. That is where I found out what the eight are called."

## What She Said Next

"You have had your account in this building since the nineteenth," she said. "You have seven days. On the thirtieth this corridor either describes your address or describes you, and if it does neither, a thing happens at midnight that neither of us will ever have to look at."

His mouth had stayed shut.

"You are going to say yes."

"You cannot know that."

"I have known it since about ten past nine this morning and I have known it since the twelfth of October. I am telling you now, on purpose, so that you have the choice of knowing that I know." She put her elbows on her knees.

"You are going to say yes. You are going to say yes because you have spent seven years making yourself the object of everything and letting nothing else be."

"You are going to say yes because it is the merciful door, and you have never in your life walked past a merciful door without reaching for it. And because it is what you have always done, and because nobody in this building is going to make it difficult."

"That is not a reason to tell a man what he is going to do."

"It is not a reason and it is the only thing I have that you would believe." Alma Quayle stood up off the stool. "So here is the price, in plain words, and I am going to give it to you once and I am not going to soften it and you may take it away with you or leave it here."

She held up the sheet between two fingers.

"A description of a man stops nowhere. It goes down to the address he is answerable at, and it lists what is standing at that address, and it dates each of them."

"A man described against nothing is a sentence about a man. A man described against an address is a fact, and an office can only administer a fact." She let the sheet go.

"You will save the eight from being a list of a house. You will not save them from being a list. You will change the head of the page and not the page."

"That is what I am asking for."

"It is what you are asking for, and I am telling you what it costs, and then I am going to put the sheet down."

The sheet went down on the stool, face up, in the middle of the corridor, at an angle where a man standing in front of it could not avoid it.

"I am not going to ask you today. You have until the thirtieth, and there are eight women at an address that has just found out what it is worth."

"They have had a stranger in their kitchen once already this month and I am not going to be that again."

She went to the door and then stopped with her hand on it.

"The reason on your referral is still a tune," she said. "That is the one thing in this instrument I cannot write for you and I am not going to try. If you put this sheet out on the thirtieth, it will go out with a reason that is a song which nobody in this building can reproduce and nobody has been paid, and about forty officers will read that line and not one of them will be able to do anything with it."

"Is it enough."

"It is enough for me, and I am the woman who has to sign it, and in about four years somebody else will decide." She opened the door. "Go up the sixty-one steps and tell them, or do not tell them, and come back on the twenty-seventh if you are going to. Bring nothing. There is nothing to bring."

He was in the corridor for another twenty minutes after the door shut.

The form was face up on the stool at the angle she had put it at, and the bench was hard, and he read it four times without learning anything new, and when the runner came past at the top of the stair and looked at him and then at the sheet and then at him again, he got up off the bench and went upstairs.

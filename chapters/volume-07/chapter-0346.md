# Nine Minutes And No Majority

The sitting was called for the twenty-third of December at ten in the morning by two members of the room, and the notice of it went out on the ordinary stream on the eighteenth, and it named the subject, and the subject came.

He was let into the room at four minutes to ten and there was a chair against the wall for him that was not one of the nine, and the room read the rule about it aloud to him: a subject is heard once, before the seals, and waits outside while the seals go on.

Wren Kallo was in the room in a Bureau coat with a satchel, at a table by the wall with a clerk, because her division holds the description. Not one person in that room spoke to her, and not one person looked at her, and she kept her hands flat on the satchel for an hour and did not open it.

## Four Minutes

The chair called the sitting at ten and said the words she had said on the fourteenth to a man and not to a room, and then the clerk read the sheet.

*The subject: the account carried at premises 34-6-118.*

*The holding: thirty years, being the residue of an unadministered account.*

*The figure: seven. Dated the sixteenth of September. Initialled, not signed. Called on the first of December at ten to nine, and not answered.*

*The ground: that the line cannot be paid and cannot be produced, and that no production is available off those premises.*

Then the clerk read the fifth line, which was not a part and was only a line, in the flat voice of a man reading a column.

*Held by.*

Nothing.

"Order," said Anouk Ferreira.

A man in the third seat stood up. He had a prepared face and had not brought a coat and had the look of a person who has been to a room like this for eleven years.

"Chair. The ground is the best ground anybody in this city has had since the war and it has been true since the second of October. I move that it be carried."

"Seconded," said a woman in the sixth.

"Then it is before the room and I will take it in order." She wrote three words on the slate. "There are nine minutes on this sheet from the moment the first seal goes on. Is that understood."

It was understood.

## The Seals

The first seal went on at about four minutes past ten, and the second at six, and the third at nine, and the room had the particular quiet of nine people doing a thing they had agreed to do.

The fourth went on at eleven minutes.

The fifth went on at thirteen minutes and the man who put it down said nothing at all and kept his hand flat on the table for a while, and Anouk Ferreira wrote the time against the fifth and did not look up for about four seconds.

Five of nine.

That was the machine. He was in a room on the first floor of a building in this city and five people who could be counted had each put a seal on a piece of paper with his name on the address of it, in a room with a door, at ten o'clock in the morning, and nothing whatsoever had been taken off anybody.

## From Whom

"Stop," said the chair.

She stood up, which the room did not expect, and walked to the table the clerk was at and read the sheet herself from the top, and she read the four parts in about eleven seconds and then she put her finger on the fifth line and left it there for a while.

"From whom," she said.

The room did not answer.

"I am going to say this once and it will be in the minute." She took her finger off the paper. "A requisition is not laid against an account. It is laid against whoever holds it, and what it takes comes out of that person, and the price comes into this room."

"There is not one line on this sheet that names a person. It names a door."

"You have a composition of eight certified on the thirtieth of November—"

"That is a list of who sleeps there," said Anouk Ferreira. "I have read it. It is signed by a Bureau officer in her own division and it is the cleanest document in this city about that house, and it does not say one word about what any of the eight holds, including the man who put his own name at the head of it in October."

The woman in the sixth seat took her hand off the table.

"I put my seal on that sheet at thirteen minutes," she said, "and I did not read the fifth line, and I have now read the fifth line, and I withdraw it."

Not one person argued with her, because she had read it correctly, and because the whole of what nine countable people are is that a person in the room may say a thing out loud and be believed by the room about their own conduct.

"Recorded," said the chair. "Four on the sheet."

## The Ninth Minute

"Chair." The man in the eighth seat had not spoken in an hour. "A requisition that names no holder names nothing, and if the room carries it, then this room has bought nothing and spent a majority doing it, and a spent majority may not be recalled at the same sitting. Is that in the rules or is it your ruling."

"It is the rules," said Anouk Ferreira. "Second from the chair and not in dispute."

"Then the ruling stands at the ninth minute." She picked up the slate. "The majority of five is spent. The sheet is not in order and cannot be made in order in this room, because the thing that is missing is not a word. It is a person, and I cannot put a person into a box with a pen."

She looked along the row.

"I am going to say this to the room and not to him, because he is in this room and I would rather he did not have to hear it."

"A majority that has been entered and then found to be void is the most expensive thing this room can do. I have watched one happen once in twenty years. The four people who put a seal on it remember it for the rest of their lives, and that is the entire cost. No house burns. Nobody is taken. Four people remember."

## The Man Who Tabled It

Nikolai Vester stood up.

"I have been in this room eleven years," he said, "and I tabled that sheet on the seventeenth of November, and I tabled it because on the morning of the sixteenth nine people read a broker's real books at a table and one of the nine looked at a line of seven carried at a door that had been eight for a fortnight."

"That was the ground."

"It was the ground. The ground was correct and it is correct now and it will be correct in a year." He was not angry. That was the thing about him. "I tabled a sheet with an empty box on it and I have known about the empty box since the eighteenth of November and I tabled it anyway, because I thought five hands would find a way and I now know that five hands cannot find a way, because there is nothing to find."

He put his hand flat on the table for a moment, which was a thing men in that room did instead of swearing.

"If that box had been filled on the eighteenth of November," he said, "this would have taken four minutes instead of nine."

He went out. He did not say goodnight to the chair, and the chair did not say anything to him, and the door shut, and the room was eight people and a chair that had been empty since the first of November.

## Being Told

He was brought back into the room at about ten past eleven and the chair told him in front of the eight, in the ordinary voice, because there was no reason to tell him anything else.

"It failed," said Anouk Ferreira. "Four of nine and no majority, and the sheet is void, and the account is untouched, and there is nothing in this building this morning that anybody can take off you."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me in that room." She put the slate down. "Now hear the rest of it, because you have spent four months arriving somewhere and this is the road."

"A called line is answered at a valuation," she said. "There is a valuation on the first of January at ten to nine and the line will be called again, and it will be called on the first of February, and it will be called until somebody pays it or somebody produces. Nothing happened this morning. Nothing happens on the first of January either. That is not a rescue. That is an account going on being called for as long as you live."

"Then what did this morning do."

"This morning put your name in front of nine people and let five of them put a seal on a document with your address on it."

She looked at him across nine feet of room.

"In about four years there will be a document in this building that says a requisition was laid against Lin Hao and five people carried it as far as a form. A stranger with a folder will read that line and he will not care that the sheet was void. He will read *requisition*, and he will want to know what the man held, and he will start looking."

She picked her cup up off the table. She had a cup on a slate in that room, which she had not mentioned.

"Four people in this room remember putting a seal on a piece of paper with your name on it, and that is not a weapon anybody will use against you this year, and it is a weapon anybody could use against you in four years, and you asked me on the seventeenth for a document with a name at the top of it, and I have just handed you the first half of one without anybody's permission."

## Outside

He stood in the corridor at five past twelve and the waxed-paper window at the end of it made the light the colour of weak tea, and he found that he was shaking, which took him about a minute to notice.

Nothing had been taken. He had a called account and no mark on him and no folio and no classification, and he was free, and he had been free for four hours and had spent the whole of it being frightened.

There was nothing else in the city to do about it.

The minute would go out on the ordinary stream at four in the afternoon and it would be attested in four hands, because that is what the room did with everything, and a minute in four hands was the shape of every fact that had ever mattered to him since the twenty-ninth of June, and it would have his name at the top of it.

He went down the stairs with his hands empty, and he had been carrying the same figure since the twenty-fourth of August, and for the first time in all of that the figure had been written down by somebody else's hand in front of him, and it had not been a figure at all.

# The Chaplain And The Column

About nine hundred people were outside the arcade when he came up at half past nine on the evening of the sixth of October, and they had gone home by nine, and then about two hundred had not, and then by a quarter past there were nine of them standing about four feet apart under the arch and not saying anything to each other at all.

They had been saying one thing each for about half an hour. Nobody was answering anybody. He stood at the door of the Vestry and listened to nine people not answer each other and then went in.

One day from the fifth of October to this evening.

## The Room With No Door On It

She had the lamp on the bracket turned down about a finger's width, which she does, and her coat was over the back of the chair, and she was forty-five and had been chaplain of the Church of the Quiet Number for twenty years.

"There is one rule in this room," she said. "It is not mine and I did not build it. One thing each, and nobody answers anybody, and the last one says hers last. It is the rule of a kitchen off Ferrier Street and I did not invent it and I have never once moved it, and you are in it while you are in here."

"I know."

"You go first."

## What He Said

He sat down on the end of the bench against the wall, the way a man sits in a room that has a bench.

"On the fifteenth of October I am going to write four lines and a name into that book," he said. "In my own hand, in the column that has been empty for five quarters. That the thing that went out of that account on the first of October was taken from about a million people and that I decided who received it."

"You have said that in a kitchen on the second of August in four lines and I have read four lines once."

"That is my one thing."

He put his hands on his knees and left them there.


He had one more and she knew it and she did not ask for it, and for about nine minutes neither of them said anything at all.

Outside, under the arch, one of the nine said a thing to nobody in particular about the ninth of April, and four of them did not answer and one of them said a different thing to nobody in particular, and after about four seconds they all stopped, and stood, and then one of them said a third thing, and the four did not answer that either.

Then she turned her head about nine degrees.

"You have a second thing," she said. "You have had it since the second of August. And you are not going to give it to me, because the rule is the rule and nobody answers anybody and if you give me a second thing then I have to answer the first one, and I have not answered a question in this room in about twenty years."

"Correct."

"And if I do it, then this room has an answer in it, and about eleven thousand people know where this room is and not one of them is permitted to come into it, and that has been the price of it for twenty years and it is the only reason I have ever been able to sit in a room with anybody."

"Yes."

"Then why are you still standing there."

"Because I have asked you one question and you have not answered it, and you have not answered it on purpose, and I want you to do it on purpose."

## The Question

"Are you going to tell them not to fill it," said Lin Hao.

She did not answer.

Nine seconds. She looked at the lamp and then at the door, and out through the door the arch was empty now and the yard was empty and the street had about nine people on it going home.

"Twenty years," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And nobody has ever asked me a question in here, and about eleven thousand people believe that nobody answers anybody in this building, and it is the single thing I have built and it has never once been tested and you have just tested it at half past ten on an evening when a woman on my steps has told nine hundred people that the column is theirs."

She did not get up and she did not leave the chair.

"I am not going to tell them anything," she said. "That is the answer and it is the only one I have got, and I have had four years to find a better one and I have not found it, and I am going to say to you why, and then I am going to say my one thing, and then this room is going to stop being a place where nobody answers anybody."

"Say it."


"I stood in that yard about nine hundred people in front of me in the eleventh month of the third year and told them what you had printed," she said. "And about four hundred of them stopped believing me and about nine hundred of them stopped believing me and I spent the next three weeks writing to every house a woman spoke to in. That is the sentence at the wall and the sentence was right and I have never said so."

"They believed you before that."

"They believed me because I had been right in public twice and being right in public is the only thing this building has," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"And then I did not go to a counter for a year. That is all. I did not go and I did not write and I did not mention it in this yard, and about four hundred people worked out on their own that the chaplain had gone quiet on the one thing she had ever been loud about. There is not one instrument in this city that can put a name back on a person for that."

"So the yard has decided it without you."

"The yard decided it at a quarter past five on the first of August and it took four seconds and it has not changed since," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"I have been the only person in this city who was ever right in public and I have spent about a year being the only person in this city who was ever wrong in public, and there is no sentence I can say tonight that puts it back, and I know that, and I have known it since about the ninth of August."


She stood up and put her coat on, which is not what a woman does before she leaves, and Lin Hao did not get up.

"I am going to say the thing I came to say and then this room stops," she said. "I have said it twice."

"I said it to a kitchen off Ferrier Street in the second year, and I said it to a clerk at a counter off a third court on the thirtieth of December of that year with a certified copy of it in her hand, and I am going to say it now in a room where nothing can be used against anybody, and that is the last time I am going to say it."

"The words on that wall are nobody's. I am not going to claim the column for the Church of the Quiet Number and I am not going to claim the two words at the foot of that wall, and I am not going to ask you to keep the column for the Church either."

"Because a column that is kept for the Church is the Church's, and I have said so and I was right about it, and I am not going to say it again in a year when it has cost me everything I had to say it with."

"That is my one thing and I went last and I am not answering anybody."

She came across the room and took hold of the front of his coat with one hand and did not take it off.

"And now I am going to tell you what I have wanted to tell you since the second of August," she said, "and I am not going to ask you for it, because asking is what people do when they are not sure, and I have been sure since a man told me in a corridor on the sixth of March of the first year that there was nothing at all under a tile and then stood under it anyway for four years."

## The Lamp On The Bracket

She took her coat off again and dropped it on the bench and there was nothing on her face at all, which is not what her face had been doing all evening.

"I have not been warm in this building for a long time," she said. "Twenty years in a cold room and a woman on her feet in a yard in every month of it, and my hands do not come up to about the second joint, and I have stopped apologising for it."

She put her hands flat on his chest and found what she was looking for with the first hand and then again with the second, which she did on purpose and did not explain.

"You are not going to last," she said. "You are rank one with a chest that has been spent for four years and there is nothing in this city anybody could hand you that would help, and I want you to hear me say that I know it and that I am not being kind about it."

"That is fair."

"I am not being merciful about that," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have not been in a room with anybody for four years who did not have to be somewhere at six in the morning, and I have got about as much tenderness left as this lamp has oil in it."

She got his coat open with one hand and did not take her own dress over her head the way she had in a chapel behind a mat in the first year, and she did not say a word about that, and he noticed it and did not put it into words either.

She was broad-shouldered and heavy, and her hair was white at the front and wet at the part because she had washed it that morning in a building where a woman washes her hair because there is nothing else to be done with a morning.

"I am going to be very ordinary about this," she said. "I want to tell you that before we start, because you have had four of these in four years and three of them were not ordinary and I am not going to pretend to be a woman being taken by surprise."

"What are you going to be."

"Exactly what I am," said Agnete Lindqvist. "A big woman of forty-five who has been on her feet in a yard since she was twenty-five and who has not been wanted by anybody in about four years and has not told one person in this building that, and who is going to take what she wants out of the next half hour and is not going to lie about what it is."

She got his shirt off him over his head and put her mouth on his chest and then on the flat of him and worked down the length of him with her mouth open and her hands behind her own back, and she was not fast at it and she was not slow at it.

He came back into her mouth and she held him there and looked up at him over the length of him and said, "You can answer me now and I have not said the question yet, and when I do, I am going to want one."

He did not say anything at all, because there is one thing in this building and he has been in it for an hour.

"That is correct," said Agnete Lindqvist, around him, and he felt her mouth move and did not understand the words at all.


She stood up and got her own dress to her hips and then did not take it any further, and looked down at him sitting on the end of the bench with his trousers still on.

"Come here," she said. "I have spent the whole of this evening being the only one in the room who says things and I have not had one thing asked of me in about four years, and you are going to come here and do what I tell you and I am going to answer every single thing you ask me, including the ones you have not asked yet."

He came to her.

She got her back against the wall beside the lamp and pulled him in against her and put one of her cold hands at the back of his neck and held on there, and she made him go into her in her own time with her hips and not with anything else, and neither of them said a word about it and neither of them needed to.

And then she said: "Is this the same as the roof."

He said yes.

"Are you on somebody's roof."

He said no, and she laughed about nine seconds later, and then she said that it had better not be somebody's roof, because a man who gets taken on somebody else's roof in the fourth year of the cold has been somewhere he should not have been, and then she made him hold still and did not let him move for about a minute and a half while she got her breathing into a place she liked.

"You are going to ask me about the yard," she said, "and you are going to ask me whether I am frightened, and I am going to answer both, and then you are going to stop asking."

He asked her whether she was frightened.

"Yes," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Not of you and not of the column. Of being the only person in this city who was ever right in public and being about nine hundred people who worked that out on their own while I was not looking. That is a thing I am frightened of and it has nothing to do with what is happening to me at the moment and I would like that said out loud in this room while you are inside me."

He asked her about the yard and she answered him, and what she said was that the column would fill and that it would fill with people who could describe themselves and that she had told him in a kitchen in the second year exactly that and had been wrong about one half of it and had never found out which half.


She went at it without hurrying and she took her time over every part of him, and there was nothing performed in it and nothing for an audience, because there was no audience, and the only other thing in this building was about nine people under an arch saying one thing each and not answering anybody, and she could hear them through the wall for the first quarter of an hour and then she could not hear them at all.

She put him face down over the bench and came down onto him from behind with both her hands flat on his shoulder blades and worked him back into her with her hips, hard enough that the bench went across the boards on its old feet and stopped, and she did it again and the second time one of the feet made a noise.

"Twice," she said. "In twenty years. Same bench. That is the whole of what it has ever been used for in this building and you are welcome to it."

He said her name.

"I know you have not said it in about four years," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I am not going to make you say it again, and I would like you to know that I heard it and that it went about a foot further into the room than the other thing you have been saying all night, which is *that is fair*, and which is the single most irritating sentence a man has ever said to me in a bed."

He came and she came with him and she was not quiet about it and she was not fast, and afterwards she got him turned over and lay down along the front of him with her chin on his chest, and her hands were still cold and she put both of them flat on his ribs and left them there until they were not.

Then she said, out loud, to the lamp, "I am going to answer every single thing you ask me for the rest of the night."

And he said, "You do not have to."

And she said, "I know. That is why I said it."

## After

She drank four glasses of water out of a jug on a shelf, the same as she had done in a chapel behind a mat four years before, and put the jug back.

"I am not going to say that was the last one," she said, "because I have not got anything to spend on it and neither have you, and you are going to be in a kitchen off Ferrier Street on the twenty-second with nine women in it and I am going to be one of them and I am not going to be helped by any of them."

"You are not going to be last."

"I am going to be last," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have been last twice and I am going to be last a third time and I am not going to say one word about whether you are right, and if you get up in that kitchen and ask me, I am going to tell you the rule of the room and I am going to leave."

She put her coat on.

"About the column," she said. "I have not stopped them and I am not going to, and on the fifteenth you are going to put your own name in it, and I want to tell you what I think is going to happen and then I am going to go home."

"Tell me."

"Four thousand of us are going to read about it in about two months," said Agnete Lindqvist, "because about four thousand of us can read a public desk. And not one of us is going to be angry, and about nine hundred of us are going to understand it, and about forty are going to come and stand in that yard where you cannot reach them and wait."

The lamp on the bracket was still turned down a finger's width and she did not turn it up, because the bracket lamp in this building does not get turned up, and she went out through the door of a room that has no door on a street and up the length of Cordwain in the dark.

*Sixth of October of the fourth year, the Vestry, half past nine to about twenty to twelve. The yard empty and nine of them under the arch for half an hour before I came in, saying one thing each and answering nobody.*

*I said my one thing and it was the second time in four years anybody has asked me for anything and the first time anybody has asked me a question and got one.*

*And I have asked nothing and I have been asked nothing and I have been last twice in this life and I am going to be last a third time on the twenty-second, and there is a rule in this building that nobody answers anybody and it has stood for twenty years and it did not stand tonight and it stood again by about a quarter to twelve, because the two of us did not argue.*

*And she has lost her yard. That is on me and on a sentence I printed on a sheet in the thirtieth of September of the third year and she said so herself, to me, in a room where it could not be used against her, which is the only room in this city where she could say it and be believed.*

*In nine days I put my own name in a column that four thousand people are going to read about and about nine hundred are going to understand and forty are going to come and wait for.*

*And I have not got a grain of anything to put in it and I did not want anything out of tonight, which is the first time in four years I have not wanted something out of one of these.*

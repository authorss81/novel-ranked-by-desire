# Lamplit Row

The man who let the rooms off Lamplit Row came to the counter at about eleven on the morning of the thirtieth of November with a lease under his arm and a question he had already asked four other people in four other rooms.

He was not a crook and he was not careful either, which is a different thing and costs more.

"You let rooms," said Lin Hao.

"Six of them and a shop under the one at the end, and I have had the six since before the return," said the man. "Corvin Ashby. I do the water and the stairs and I do not have a book, because a book is a thing you keep when you want to remember a figure and I want to forget one."

## What He Asked At The Counter

He put the lease on the table and squared it and turned it round so that it faced away from him, which is what a man does when the paper is the reason he is in the room.

He had a hand like a man who has been carrying other men's furniture.

"I have written a figure into that lease," he said, "and the woman in number four has not paid me since the ninth month, and I am not asking you to make her pay, because she has told me what is wrong with her money and it is that there is none of it, and I believe her, and I have believed her for two months and I am about out of belief."

"Then what you are asking is whether the figure is right."

"I am asking whether it can be produced," said Corvin Ashby. "That is the whole of what I am asking and four people have not heard it."

He put one finger on the lease.

"I asked at the town hall and a woman there told me that if I wanted a figure produced I had to put the question in writing and pay for the answer. I asked the Bureau desk off the hill and the man there said a figure is a figure."

"And I asked a man in a delivery coat at the bottom of the hill, because he has got a piece of slate with a number on it the size of my gate. He told me his number was his own and made up in three days and that he had never once said the Board took it off him."

He put one finger on the lease.

"And he was right. That is what has happened to me. Every person I have asked has been right. I have got a figure on a lease that a man invented and I cannot prove it and I am not going to be able to prove it and I am going to have to open a woman's door on the first of January."

## The Figure

The figure was not on the lease in his own hand. It was the figure off a slate in a lane four streets away, which is the figure a man in a delivery coat has been chalking at the top of a column every working morning since the evening of the second of October of the year before year one, in letters about the height of a name on a plate, with a grain a head taken off anybody who wants to stand in the line.

And it is about four times what that room takes anywhere else in this city.

"She has been in number four for nine years," said Corvin Ashby. "She has never once been late and she told me in the ninth month that she had lost the work and not that she was withholding, and I believed her because I have had that room empty before and an empty room is worth less than a woman who tells the truth about having nothing."

He took his hand off the lease.

"And now I find out the number I put on her came off a gatepost four streets away, and I want to go back to the number I would have put on her if I had not had it off a slate, and there is not one place in this city I can go to get it, and I have been to four of them and one of them took my money."

## What The Office Can Do

"Wenna," said Lin Hao.

The clerk had been at the counter for the whole of it and had said nothing, and she is a clerk and may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and what she was given on the thirtieth of November of the second year was a lease with a figure on it that came off a piece of chalk in a public lane.

"That is a document," she said.

"That is a document that says a man wrote a number on it."

"That is a document," said Wenna Raut, "and it is not one of the four. It is not a thing a person did. It is a thing a person is owed, or is not owed, and this office has got nothing for what a person is owed."

"The one time anybody put one of those in front of me in September of last year I entered a name and not the deed, because the deed did not have the third condition on it. And you told me at the door of this room that I was right."

He looked at the sheet on the wall behind her, which was the standing sheet of the last quarter with a line on it near the bottom and an address at the head of it.

"Enter a name," he said.

"What name."

"His. And the date. And the street. And one sentence you are permitted to add."

She looked at him for about four seconds and then she squared the page and wrote it, and she read it back once at the speed she reads everything, and she did not hurry.

*Corvin Ashby. Lamplit Row. States that a figure written into a lease came from a slate in a lane and cannot be produced.*

"That is what is entered," she said. "It does not help him."

"No."

"It does not help her either."

"No," said Lin Hao, "and it goes out at four with an address at the head of every line, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain will read a line about a man who cannot prove a number and none of them will be able to do one thing about it, and that is what this office is."

## Why He Did Not Go Down The Hill

He wanted to go down the hill about as badly as he has ever wanted anything, and he did not go, and the reason was given out loud to a clerk who had not asked for it, which is a thing he does when he is about to do something he has already decided against.

"The man in the delivery coat would tell me what that street really takes," he said. "He has been writing those figures up and down this city for two years and he made them up in three days and he says so to everybody who stands in the line."

"If I went down there and asked him what Lamplit Row really takes, he would tell me. He would tell me in about four seconds and he would not want anything for it, because he has never once wanted anything from anybody who was standing still."

Wenna Raut did not look up.

"That is why you are not going."

"That is why I cannot," said Lin Hao. "On the thirtieth of September of last year I sat in a room with two chairs in it and told a Registrar that I would not take another man's two words off a piece of slate for standing on the wrong surface."

"That sentence went out at four in a clerk's hand with an address at the head of every line. I did not write it for the sheet."

He put his hand flat on the table.

"I wrote it because a man who has said it cannot go up the hill eleven days later and ask for a third word off the same man in the same week."

He put his hand flat on the table.

"And because the day this office can be asked about a slate is the day the slates are the second instrument that can be reached, and there is exactly one of those in this city at present and about nine thousand people a day walk under a wall that has never once been able to reach anything."

## The First Of January

Corvin Ashby folded the lease and did not put it under his arm.

"I want to be plain about the date," he said. "I have given her until the first of January. I have not told her that yet, because I have been sitting on it for about eleven days waiting to find out if a number could be produced, and I could have told her in the ninth month and then I would not be sitting here at eleven in the morning on the thirtieth of November."

"Then she has the rest of December and that is thirty days, and one of them is mine," said Corvin Ashby. "I am aware of how that looks. I am a man who has been right about every fact he has had for two years and who has had one fact too many this month, and if I could pay it out of anything but other people's money I would."

He got to the door and stopped with his hand on it, the way people do in that corridor.

"What is this office," he said. "Not for the sheet. I want to know what it is for, because a man at the town hall took my money for an answer."

Lin Hao did not have an answer that would fit in the mouth of a man at a counter on the thirtieth of November, so he gave him the one he had.

"It registers things people did that were not written down," he said, "and it cannot make anybody pay anybody, and it cannot put a number back, and it cannot find out what your street really takes. You did not ask the right question and I would rather you knew that you did not than have gone away thinking you had asked a clerk who was slow."

"Then I asked the wrong one."

"You asked the wrong one," said Corvin Ashby, "and I am glad, because a man who has asked the wrong question can come back with a better one, and you have thirty days and the first of January is mine, and I do not know of a single door in this city you can bring it to."

## What Was In The Notebook

He wrote the whole of it up at about four in the morning of the first of December, standing at the top of the third flight, in the third column, which was the one that was going to be the largest.

*Came and said nothing at all.*

Corvin Ashby said a great deal at that counter. That was the point and it took him nine minutes to say it and the clerk wrote it and the nine minutes are the row.

Under it, in the other hand, because he had started doing this on the twenty-fourth of October and could not stop:

*A man with a figure that cannot be produced is not a person doing an unrecorded deed. Not one of the four conditions fits him. The second column wants a person who carried and there is no room in it for a rent. And not one instrument in Ostara can go out and get a street's worth.*

And then, after a while, with the mop standing in the bucket beside him:

*Everything this office can do about a person who has been done wrong is write his name and let two hundred people a week read that somebody did it to him.*

He read that back and did not cross it out, and then he did the second flight and the first.

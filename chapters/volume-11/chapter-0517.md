# Forty Days Of Drafting

The drafting began on the ninth of March and it was finished, or as finished as it was going to get, on the evening of the nineteenth of April, and the forty days are twenty-one days left in March after the ninth and nineteen days of April to the nineteenth, and that is forty.

He did the sum out loud on the stair on the way down to the counting room because he had done it nine times since the ninth of March and the ninth time was the one that agreed.

Forty days is not a long time to write four lines. It is long enough to find out that four lines is the wrong shape.

## What Forty Days Was

He asked nine people. One at a time, in a corridor, on a landing, at the end of a counter, and never in the room off a third court, because he had told a Registrar in a corridor in October that he was not writing a sentence nine people had not tried to break and he was going about it the way he had said he would.

Nobody argued with the first three.

The first three were about words he had used loosely. A cold minute is not a thing. The unrecorded is not a person. Gratitude is not owed. He cut all three out of the fourth line and the fourth line went from about ninety words to about forty and every word in the forty had a person standing behind it.

The fourth woman gave him a case: a man who carried a parcel up four flights for a neighbour every working afternoon for two years and died, and not one person in this city could say whether anything is owed to the man for it, and he wrote the case into the draft as an example of what it does not take, and then cut it out because a paragraph about a dead man and four flights is the kind of sentence that gets quoted at somebody's door.

The seventh one was a doctor off Cordwain and she gave him the sentence that is now in the second line, and she said she was going to say one thing and not answer anybody, and she said it, and she did not answer anybody.

## The Two Flights

The counting room was two floors under a sales floor and there was a lamp on a stand and the fifth book open on the table and a strap across it, and Hanneke Ruis was on her feet when he came in, which she is for one line of the quarter and is not for the other three.

She had the foolscap in front of her and the pen beside it and she had not written anything.

"Sit down," she said. "That lamp is not free and neither is that chair."

He did not sit down.

"I have come down two flights to ask you for six words," he said.

"You have come down two flights to ask me whether a street I have kept in my head for thirty years is the right size," said Hanneke Ruis. "Go on."

## What He Asked For

"Premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight," he said. "The composition of it. I want you to certify it."

She put her hand flat on the foolscap.

"That has not had a certificate behind it since the fourteenth of March of the year before last," she said. "You know that, because you live at it and I have written the figure in the fourth block four times a year since then and not one of them has a thing behind them."

"I know."

"And you want a certificate."

"I want six words out of you under your own name," said Lin Hao, "because the office I am part of is going to have to stand on something, and there is one address in this whole business that everybody in Ostara can name, and if that address is going to be in it then it is going to be in it on six words in your hand, and there is going to be nothing whatever behind them,"

"and I would rather that be written down by you than discovered in about four years by a stranger with a folder."

## What She Wrote

She took about eleven seconds.

She took the nib off the string and did not uncork the ink first, which is a thing you do when you have written the same three lines four times a year for thirty years and you could do the fourth one in your sleep and have twice.

Then the figure, which is eight, and which she wrote on the thirtieth of September of the year before last out of her own memory and which was right, and then six words in the column.

*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*

And her own name under them, in her own division, in her own hand, which is the one item on that page she has never once left out.

"There," said Hanneke Ruis. "That certifies it."

## What She Said The Six Words Are Worth

"Do not say thank you," she said, "because I have had those six words written one hundred and twenty-odd times in my own hand under my own name and not one person in this city has ever said thank you to me for them, and you are about four minutes into this conversation and you are about to."

He did not say it.

"Now I am going to tell you what they are worth," she said, "and it is the only thing I have got and I have been waiting four years to say it to somebody and I did not think it was going to be a man who comes down two flights with a cough."

"Say it."

"Everything." She put the pen down. "That is what those six words are worth. Not a great deal. Everything. And I want the whole of it in one piece and I want you to hear the reason, because it is the reason I have written them since before I came."

"Go on."

"An entry that can be checked is worth whatever the check is worth, and in this city a check is a division and a reading and about nine months, and every time somebody has put one of those behind a thing, that thing has been true for exactly as long as the division lasted."

She turned the book round so that he could see the page and did not offer it. "Those six words cannot be checked. Behind them: nothing. Not a certificate, not a page, not a shelf, not an index, not a division, and not one instrument in Ostara that can go and look at a woman's memory of a street and tell her she has it right."

"So if I have it right then the thing is true on my word alone, and if I have it wrong then it is false on my word alone and neither of those can be found out by anybody, ever."

She squared the book.

"That is worth more than a certificate," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is worth more than a division and a form and a building. It is worth everything, because it cannot be worn out by an office that stops existing, and it costs a woman nothing to hold, and it has been sitting in this room for thirty years at the price of one afternoon a quarter, and not one thing can be done to it by anybody but me."

## And What Is Behind It

"Nothing," he said.

"Nothing whatever," said Hanneke Ruis. "I have been signing a certificate for thirty years with an empty room behind it and I have never once been asked what happens if I am wrong, and if I am wrong then it is wrong, and there is no appeal and no second copy and no second pair of eyes anywhere in this city,"

"and that is the arrangement and I would sign it again tomorrow because the alternative is a figure nobody wrote down at all, and I have seen what that looks like from the other side of this table."

## The One Question She Asked

She put her hand flat on the foolscap again and left it there.

"I have one question and I have never asked anybody one in this room in about nine years and I have been asked for a great many things, and I am going to ask it and you are not going to answer it in a way I can use."

"Ask it."

"What are you going to do with them."

He did not answer for about four seconds.

"I am going to put them under a definition," he said, "and the definition is going to be narrow, and it is going to have to take in a street and a house off Ferrier Street and a loaf of bread four times in a month,"

"and it is going to have to leave out nine things in ten of what people will bring to a counter, and I do not know yet how a sentence does that and I have forty days of it and I am on the last of them."

"That is an honest answer and it is the first one I have had in nine years," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I am going to put the pen down now, because I have written the address and the figure and the six words and my name and there is nothing further in that column and there has not been for thirty years."

## The Slate In Cordwain

"There is a piece of slate in a lane off Cordwain with those six words on it in half-size chalk under a whole column," he said.

"I know," said Hanneke Ruis. "A man in a delivery coat told me he was going to and I told him not to and he has done it, and he wrote the name of this room under them as well, and I have not been able to work out for about eight months whether I am obliged to be pleased about it or not."

"Are you."

"I have worked out that I am," said Hanneke Ruis. "I have a hand that has been invisible for thirty years and it is now in chalk in a public lane where about a hundred and sixty people a morning walk past it, and if it is wrong then a hundred and sixty people a morning are looking at a wrong thing,"

"and that is more accountable than a drawer and not by a long way. That is all I have got to say about it and I have said it and I am going back to the block."

## What He Took Up The Stair

He took nothing up the stair. She had given him six words and they were in a book two floors under a sales floor and he could not take a copy of them, because a copy would have been a certificate, and there is nothing he can carry.

He stood on the landing between the counting room and the floor above and worked out, in about four minutes, the rest of it.

Thirty years of one handwriting, out of one woman's memory, with nothing behind it, and six words that cannot be checked and are therefore worth everything — and if that is true of a composition of premises then it is true of every unrecorded thing in this city that anybody has ever been owed.

The whole of the difficulty of what he was about to write was that almost none of them can be held by one woman for thirty years. The ones that can be are a street and a house off Ferrier Street. Everything else that anybody would bring to a counter off a third court is a thing that happened once, to one person, a long time ago, and cannot be got at again.

Which meant the sentence he read out in a kitchen in three months would have to take almost everything out.

He had known that in the abstract since the ninth of March. He had not known the size of it until a woman with a lamp told him that six words in her hand were worth everything.

That is what the fortieth day of drafting was and he went back up to a branch office off a third court and cleaned three floors of washrooms from eleven at night until half past five and did not write a word for another eleven days.

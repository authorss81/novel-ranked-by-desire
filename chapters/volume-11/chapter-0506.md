# A Quarter Of A Cordwain Morning

The counter in Cordwain opens at eight and shuts at four and the clerk at it had been there about two years and had refused about eleven thousand things and had never once had to be told why by anybody.

At about ten past eight on the fifteenth of March a woman came to it with a piece of paper in her hand and put it on the counter face up, and it was a sheet of foolscap with a figure on it and the figure was in chalk, and she had copied it off a slate.

The figure said nine hundred and forty.

## The Question

"That is my premises," she said. "It is a room over a laundry on the Cordwain side and it has been my premises for nine years and I have got a figure on a piece of paper that says what it is worth and I would like to know whether it is right."

"Where did you copy it."

"Off a slate in the lane. The man with the stools chalked it up on the second of March and anybody in that lane could read it and I read it, and it has been on that wall every morning since and every morning it is the same figure, and I have come down here four times and I am the fifth time."

"How many is the figure on the wall."

The clerk turned round and looked at a board at the back of the counter, which is a board with a column down it and a figure at the head of every line, and a figure standing against a room over a laundry on the Cordwain side of it.

"Seven hundred and six," she said.

"That is not the figure on the slate."

"No."

## What The Clerk Can Produce

"Then one of them is wrong," said the woman.

"That is the first thing anybody says and it is the one thing I am not allowed to let go of," said the clerk. "Two figures can both be right and this city has got about four hundred premises that have got two."

"The one at the column is a reading of who is in the room at a time on a certain morning and it is nine years old this month and it is right about that morning and it does not say one word about you."

"And the one on the slate."

"The one on the slate is what a man in a delivery coat has chalked up against that address for the people who live in it standing in a lane in the cold, carried forward and added to every morning, and there are a great many of you and some of you have stood an enormous number of times," said the clerk, "and that is not nothing and it is not a mistake either."

"It is a figure about a thing somebody did, put up by a private person on a piece of slate that nobody in this building has any power over. I want to be careful here. I am not saying he is wrong. I am saying I cannot ask him."

"Then ask him."

"I cannot ask him anything," said the clerk.

"I can ask a Bureau to produce a reading. I can ask an office to produce a return. I have got a line in a book that says what a person must give to a person in my building and it is a line I like very much, and every name on it is an office in this city, and the man with the slate is not an office in this city and he has not put himself under anything at all."

## What The Clerk Went And Looked For

She went and got a book.

It is about four inches thick and it is the standing book of that counter and it has been in that room since before the cordwain, and she put it on the counter and turned it round and put her finger on a line and then went along the lines with it, and it took her about eleven minutes, and Lin Hao watched her do it from about nine feet away where he had been standing since about half past eight.

She did it properly, which meant she read the head of every section first and then the exceptions, and she checked the index twice, and at one point she went out to the board at the back with her finger on a line and came back and went along it again. About two hundred people came through that counter in the time she was doing it and every single one of them got the same three or four sentences and none of them got the truth.

"That is the part I would like you to understand about this counter," she said, without looking up.

"I am about eleven minutes into a thing that has been in this building since before the street it is named for, and about two hundred people have gone past me this morning and eleven of them have asked me a question and I have answered every one of them in the wrong way, because the true answer to all eleven of them takes about four minutes and it is the only answer that is any use."

"Heard of this?" he said. "The lane."

"I know about the lane," said the clerk. "I have read the sheet of the first of October at a public desk like everybody who wanted to and there is a line on it about the Bureau and a wall and about ninety people in a room, and I did not understand one word of it and I have not understood one word of it since."

She shut the book.

"It is all there," she said. "Page for a figure that is wrong: correct it, carry the correction, say so on the sheet. Page for a figure that is missing: carry it forward unclosed and put a date on the carry. Page for a figure that a person has given in and it is wrong: refused, with the reason. Page for a figure that has come from a hearing: entered and signed."

"And a figure that has come from a slate."

"There is not one line about a slate in that book," said the clerk, "and I have looked at all four of those and I have looked at the index, and the index is right, and it is not an oversight. Nobody has ever needed that line before this year. Everything in that book is about figures that came out of this city, and a slate did not come out of this city."

## The Part She Is Not Allowed

"You may only enter," said Lin Hao.

"I may only enter," said the clerk, "and I cannot improve what I am given and I cannot be asked to hurry."

"And there is nobody in this building whose job it is to decide whether seven hundred and six or nine hundred and forty is the figure for that room. If a figure comes to me I enter it. If a figure is wrong I correct it against something. If a figure is somebody's, I cannot take it off him and I cannot weigh it against mine, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that can do either of those things either."

"So what do you say to her."

"I say the true thing," said the clerk, "and the true thing is that this city has two instruments and not one rule about which one wins, and that I am a person at a counter and not the place where that is decided."

## A Quarter Of A Cordwain Morning

The woman stood there for about two minutes and then said, "So he has it right and the wall has it wrong."

"I have not said that."

"Then which."

"I have said that I cannot find out," said the clerk.

"And I want to say the rest of it because you have been here five times and you look like a person who will stand here a sixth."

"He is not right and he is not wrong and there is not one thing in this city that can tell you which, and that is worse for you than being wrong, because if you are wrong you can go and argue and I have told you exactly what to do about it."

"You cannot argue with nine hundred and forty. You can only believe it or not believe it, and if you believe it, you will believe it on the first and on the thirtieth and on every morning for as long as he stands there."

"And if I do not believe it."

"Then you go on paying what the wall says, and the wall is nine years old, and the wall at least has a column down the side of it," said the clerk, "and I have been at this counter for about two years and I would take the wall every time of the two, and I do not think I am the only person in Cordwain who would."

She wrote nothing. There was a sheet in front of her and she turned it a half inch and squared it and she put nothing at all on it, because she had been given nothing, and because a clerk cannot go out and get it.

## What The Woman Did Next

The woman put the foolscap back in the front of her coat when she left, and she did not go up the hill.

She went back down to the room over the laundry and she sat down on the edge of the bed with the foolscap in her lap and asked herself the question that everybody asks, and could not answer it, and found that not knowing was now the official position of a public counter in this city.

Then she did the thing that made Lin Hao follow her four streets up the hill and stand in a corridor for about nine minutes.

She went up two flights to the landlord and told him the slate figure was nine hundred and forty and that the wall said seven hundred and six, and that a public counter had said it could not say which was right.

And the landlord said: "Then I shall carry on at nine hundred and forty," and she said: "You may carry on at whatever you like. I have just told you that there is an office in this city that has been asked which one is right and has written on a sheet that it cannot find out."

"That is not the same as being told I am right."

"It is not the same," said the woman. "It is the same as there being one place in Ostara where a person could go and put the question, and that place has said out loud in its own hand that it does not know. That is worth more than being told you are right, and I want you to sit down, because you are not going to believe that today and I am not going to be here tomorrow to say it again."

## What Goes Out At Four

He had gone down with her on the second of March, which was thirteen days earlier, and she had put the foolscap with the chalk figure on it under the glass and asked whether it would be copied.

"No," he said.

"Will you say that in a minute."

"I will not."

"Then it is not going in the minute and I am not going to put it on a paper and lose it again," said the clerk.

"The sheet goes out at four this afternoon with an address at the head of every line and every line on it that anybody in this city may read at a public desk on a working afternoon, and I have got about nine lines and four of them are refusals and one of them is a cistern. If you want a thing in it, it has to go in as a line and not as an account of it."

"Write that there is a second instrument," said Lin Hao.

"That is not a thing anybody asked me."

"Write that there is a second instrument and that it is not checkable and that this counter cannot weigh it," said Lin Hao. "Write that it is worse than the problem, not better, and that the reason is that there is no rule about which of two answers wins when one of them can be checked and the other one cannot, and you will not find that rule in the book and I have looked."

The clerk wrote it.

She wrote it in about forty seconds, in her own hand, and she did not improve it, and she turned the sheet round and read it once and then put it in the folder and did not read it again.

And then she said, "That is all of it, and it is the worst answer anybody has ever taken off this counter, and it is in my own hand and it is going to be read in about four years by a stranger with a folder who is going to have no way at all of knowing that four people came in here this morning with a piece of paper."

That is the shape of a quarter of a Cordwain morning. It is a counter that opens at eight and shuts at four, and a woman who put the better of two figures on it in the end, and a man who has one question he is allowed to be asked and has not spent it, and a line on a sheet that goes out at four.

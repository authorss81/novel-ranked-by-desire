# The Book She May Not Read Out

Her rooms are on the fourth floor of a building off Ferrier Street with a window that goes the whole way round two sides, and there is a fire in them and a table with nothing on it and a chair he has never once been offered again.

On the table there was a book.

It came in under her arm and it did not leave her hand, and when she took her coat off she put it down on the table and kept her left hand flat on the cover of it, and she did not do that on purpose and she did not stop doing it either, and by the time the fire had gone over twice it had become the shape of the evening the same way the room had become the shape of the evening.

She has been the Vant's senior broker for eleven years. She trades in favours and she keeps the real books and she has kept investing after the return stopped making sense.

"Marguerite."

"Sit down. If you stand over that table for four hours it becomes a meeting."

## What She Cannot Say Out Loud

She sat down opposite him and did not take her hand off the book.

"Before anything else," she said. "The rule of that room is that nothing in it is said out loud, and the rule is not a courtesy and it is not for you, and if you ask me a question tonight whose answer is in the book then the answer is that the book may not be read out, and you will get that answer whatever the question was."

"What is the rule for."

"It is for the city." She said it evenly. "Every figure in there is a figure two people agreed and neither of them may ever say they agreed. A debt you touch binds both parties in writing and if the writing is read out then one of the two has been told in public that the other owed them something, and about a third of this city's trades die the day that happens."

She turned her head about nine degrees.

"So we do not say it, and you may ask me as many things as you like tonight and about a third of the answers will be that sentence, and you will not know which third until you have said the other two thirds."

## What Has Happened To Her

"Rask's books meet on the sixteenth of November," she said. "They meet once in the year and they did not move at seven in the morning on the sixteenth, which is a thing they have never done, and the reason they did not move is a figure that is in that book and is not in the Vant's own procedure."

"Your book."

"The real book." She did not move her hand. "On the second of October, four years ago, at about ten past four in the afternoon, I closed an entry in it by hand. Not struck. Closed, with a line under it and my name against the line and the word *held* at the end of it."

"Because a thing that has been given away in the open is not a thing anybody can keep a claim on. And because if I had struck it, then in about four years a stranger with a folder would have found a gap where the largest number in this city used to be. A gap is worse than a number."

Her hand did not move.

"That is not the Vant's procedure. The Vant's procedure is that an entry that is closed by hand is an entry that has been dealt with by a broker and not by the guild, and that is the one thing in this city that a senior broker is not allowed to have done without a form."

## The Form She Does Not Have

"There is a form," said Lin Hao.

"There is not." She said it flatly. "I have had the Vant's own people look for it for nine months. The form that reopens a hand-closed entry is made out by the guild and is countersigned by the keeper of the real books, and the keeper is not permitted to countersign anything about a figure that is already in a room on a wall."

"I have known that since the second of October and I have had four years to get used to it and I have not."

"What happens on the seventeenth."

"Nothing happens on the seventeenth. What happens is that in about four years a clerk in that building reads an entry in the senior broker's hand that no broker has the right to have written, and the question he asks is not whether the number was right."

She turned the chair about nine degrees toward the fire.

"It is whether I closed it to cover a shortfall that I had made personally," said Marguerite Vale. "And that question is the correct one and I could not answer it in front of the guild, so they will take me off the books."

She looked at him.

"You cannot fix it. I want that said in this room and in the first two minutes, because you have spent four years thinking you can fix things. You have no instrument. The Register of Unrecorded Merit has no instrument. I am not asking you for an instrument."

"No."

"Then ask me for the other thing."

## The Other Thing

"What is the other thing."

"A figure," said Marguerite Vale. "Not out loud. On paper. In your hand or mine, I do not care which, and it is not going into the real book and it is never going near the real book."

"For which street."

"Lamplit Row."

He put his hand flat on the table beside hers and did not touch it.

"A man called Ashby wrote a figure off a slate into a lease and he cannot produce it and a woman in number four cannot pay it and the door goes on the first of January."

"Yes."

"He came to a counter off a third court on the thirtieth of November and I entered his name and not one word of use."

"Then I will give you a number that is not a claim and cannot be made one, and it will be on no ledger of the guild and it will not be in my hand if I can help it," said Marguerite Vale.

"How can you keep it out of your hand."

"I have just kept it out of my hand," she said.

## What She Did With Her Right Hand

She took a card out of the side of the book without lifting the cover, and she did that with two fingers of her right hand while her left stayed exactly where it was, and she wrote on it in about eleven seconds with a pen she did not have to uncork.

She turned it round.

A street and a figure. That is all it was.

"That is what Lamplit Row takes," she said. "It is out of my memory and not out of that book, and I wrote it in about eleven seconds without opening it, and you are going to ask me how I know and the answer is that I have known it for eleven years and that knowing it is not the same as being able to say where it is written down."

"It is the same thing a keeper does."

"It is exactly what a keeper does," said Marguerite Vale. "Two floors under a sales floor, four times a year, for thirty years, under her own name, with nothing whatever behind it. I have never once met her and I have thought about her roughly once a month for four years."

"The reason is that she is the only person in Ostara who has proved that a figure nobody can check is worth more than a figure somebody can. And I could not think of a single way to say that to you in a room until about eleven seconds ago."

"Do not put it in the Register."

"I know," she said.

## The Middle Of The Evening

She got up and put the card face down under the ashtray, which was the only flat thing on that table, and came round.

She did not take her hand off the book for that either. She took the book with her, under her left arm, and set it down on the floor by the chair, and left her hand flat on it there, and that was the arrangement for the rest of the evening and neither of them commented on it after about the fourth minute.

"You are not going to ask me what it costs," she said.

"No."

"You have been at this four years and you still do not ask what anything costs, and it is the single most expensive habit a man in your position can have. I have made money out of worse than that."

She was close enough that he could see the fire in her eyes and nothing else of her face.

"So I am going to tell you, once, and then it is off the table for the evening. You will not thank me for it and you will not be able to."

The fire went over twice and the room got very quiet, and she took her time the way a woman takes her time when she has put a thing down after carrying it for four years.

She had her coat over the back of the chair and her sleeves to the elbow and nothing on her hands, which is not what four hundred people a year are shown.

He was in a hurry and she felt it.

"You do that too well and too fast," she said. "And you have done it four years with nobody and it shows. I am going to leave it about nine seconds and then I am going to make you listen to something."

He made himself wait, and it was the hardest thing he did that year.

## What She Told Him While He Was Learning It

"You are listening," she said, "and you are also doing arithmetic. The arithmetic is that I am a position that does not depend on the Vant and this is the only asset in Ostara a court cannot seize, and that is what you are thinking about. You may keep thinking it. It is going to make you slow in about four minutes, and that is the arrangement."

He slowed. She felt that too, and answered it by shifting her hips a half inch and waiting, until he came to her on his own.

"That is what I wanted," she said. "Eleven years of men who come to this house to be told something. Do it properly and do not say a word about what it is for, or I will go back to my chair, and I will not explain it afterwards, and you will spend a year working out whether I was disappointed."

He got a hand under her and she went up onto her knees over him, and her left hand came down off the chair's edge and found the book on the floor and stayed on it, flat, the whole way, and she did that on purpose and he understood that she had done it on purpose.

"Like that," she said. "Slower than that and I will start thinking about the sixteenth of November."

So he went slower than that.

Her back was bare from the waist to the shoulder blades and he put both hands on it and learned the shape of her, which was a different shape from every shape he had had his hands on since he was nineteen, and she let him learn it without hurrying him and without helping.

When he had it she put one arm back and hooked her fingers in his hair and pulled his head up.

"Look at me and not at the book," she said. "The book will still be there. I have carried it for eleven years and I am not going to lose it between now and the small hours."

## The Middle Of It

He got inside her and she took all of it in one long go, and then held still above him with her weight on him and nothing moving at all, which she had done twice before and was about to do again, and which was the single most expensive thing anybody had ever done to him and cost her nothing.

Then she moved.

She set the pace and the pace was not his. It belonged to a woman who had been standing since the morning and had decided in advance that the fact would not be in the room tonight.

Her left hand stayed down on the closed book beside her knee the whole time and her right went into his hair and pulled when the pace got to a place.

About a minute in she made a noise that was not for the room and put her mouth on his shoulder to stop it, and he did not let her.

"Twice a year," she said against his skin. "Every March and every October, and in between I am at a counter in a building being pleasant to people I would not have a coffee with. I have not had anybody in this room since the second of October and I am not going to think about the date while you are inside me, and if you say a word about the Vant tonight I will put you out on the landing in your coat."

"I was not going to."

"I know. That is the whole of the reason."

She put his hand where she wanted it and held it there, and about four minutes after that she laughed at nothing at all and stopped laughing before he did.

It went on longer than that room's fire lasted. At some point the book on the floor was under her knee and neither of them had moved it. At some point later she was saying one word into his ear on a loop with nothing in it. At some point after that she told him the correct answer to a question he had not asked her, and it was correct.

## Afterwards

When she came she stayed exactly where she was, on him, and let him put his hands flat on her back between the shoulder blades and did not move for about half a minute.

Then she got off, sat on the edge of the chair, reached down, and put her left hand flat on the book on the floor, and left it there.

"You are the only man in this city I have never had to explain anything to," said Marguerite Vale. "You have cost me the most and you have never once asked me for the terms, and I do not know what to do with that, and I have had eleven years of practice at knowing what to do with everything."

## The Card Afterwards

She picked the book up off the floor and put it back on the table and sat down in her own chair and looked at it for about four seconds.

The fire had gone.

"The card stays where it is," she said. "I am not going to say what I want for it and you are not going to ask. That is the arrangement and if either of us breaks it this stops being the best thing either of us has done in four years."

He looked at the ashtray.

"No," said Marguerite Vale. "It does not go in the Register."

She said it to the table.

"I have thought about that for eleven days and I have thought about it harder than I have thought about anything else since the second of October, and here is what I got."

She turned the card round under the ashtray so that the number was facing the fire.

"If that figure goes into an office that cannot check it, then in about four years there is a page in a building with a number on it that a broker of this guild said out loud once to a man with no instrument. A man from this guild will come and read that page, and he will want to know how the broker knew, and there is one answer, and the answer is the book."

She squared the card under the ashtray with two fingers.

"So it does not go in."

"Then the man has the first of January," said Lin Hao.

"Then the man has the first of January," said Marguerite Vale, "and I have the sixteenth of November. One of those two dates costs a woman a room. The other one costs me eleven years of standing."

She put her hand flat on the closed book.

"I have set them next to each other on purpose, and I have not asked you for either, and I am not going to."

## What He Did On The Twenty-Fifth

He went down the four flights and out into Ferrier Street at about half past one in the morning and stood on the step for about nine seconds, and then he walked to the branch off a third court and up the third flight with the bin room open and the mop standing in the bucket where he had left it.

The card was in the inside of his coat and the notebook was in the inside of his coat and they went in the same pocket.

He wrote one line.

*Lamplit Row. Figure out of a woman's memory, nothing behind it, given for nothing, at about one in the morning on the twenty-fifth.*

And then he ruled a line under it and wrote the second one, in the same hand, at about twenty to two in the morning, in a column he had ruled in the book on the first of December for things that were not conditions.

*Entered at the counter on the twenty-seventh. Her instruction was that it was not to be.*

He read it back.

Then he wrote a third line under that one, because he was a careful man and because a thing left where a stranger could find it was a thing he had not finished with.

*The fourth condition says it is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

That was the whole of the argument and it took him about four seconds and it was the first time in four years that he had wanted to put something into an institution for a reason that was entirely about himself.

## On The Twenty-Seventh

He carried the card up on the twenty-seventh and put it on the counter, and a clerk read it twice at the same speed both times and entered a street and a figure and no name, because a clerk may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and she did not ask him once why there was no name on it.

He worked it out on about the first of February, standing at the top of the third flight, and what he had worked out was that he had paid, and that the payment was a line with an address at the head of it that would go out at four on the thirtieth of March to about two hundred people a week in Cordwain, and would go out again at the end of every quarter after that, for as long as there is a quarter.

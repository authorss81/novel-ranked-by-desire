# The Auditor And The Chalk

At about ten past three in the morning of the sixth of March he came down the second-floor stair with a bucket because the cistern on that landing has been dry since the sixth of January and the standpipe at the end of it works if you mean it, and the corridor at the bottom had a lamp on the counter that shuts at four and a woman sitting behind it.

She had been there since about half past eleven.

## What Was In The Corridor

A bundle under the counter with the strap done up. An audit slate. A sheet of foolscap with about two inches of writing at the top of it and the rest of the page empty, and a pencil, and a rubber, and the lamp turned so that it lit the paper and not her face.

She had a coat on over her working clothes and the lamp was on the counter and not on the wall because she had moved it herself and the counter is where the light already was.

"You are at the washrooms until half past five," said Ines Duarte.

"I am."

"Then you have two hours and twenty minutes and I am going to use about ninety of them and I am going to tell you when."

She did not turn the sheet round. She had it square to herself with the top edge level with the lamp and her two fingers flat on the margin at the top, and the fingers on that hand had ink in the first two nails and the ink has been in them for eleven years and no soap in this city lifts it.

## The Rule Of The Room

"I am going to read this out," she said, "and you are not going to like it, and you are not going to stop me, and if you touch the page I will put my hand on it and that will be the end of the conversation."

"Read it."

"I started it on the second of March and I have been back every working afternoon since, and I do it the same way every time and it takes about forty minutes, and it is not about the figures."

She put her thumb on the first line.

"I read in order. The date first. The hour with it. Nothing skipped. That is not a rule I learned for this, it is what I have audited by for eleven years and it is the only rule I have, and if I stop reading in order then in about four years a stranger with a folder opens this and he cannot tell what I checked and what I only looked at, and he is going to assume I did it properly."

"You do it properly."

"I do it properly," said Ines Duarte, "and that is the whole of my standing and it is eleven words long."

## The Sheet

"The second of March," she said. "I went down there in the afternoon because a question was put to me at a counter on the first and I could not answer it, and the answer was that I had never been to the lane, and I went and I stood at the back of it for an hour and a half and I did not pay a grain and I did not tell anybody I was there."

"And you read a slate."

"I read a slate," said Ines Duarte. "A slate propped against a yard wall with figures on it in four-inch chalk going back about a fortnight, because a man in a delivery coat does not rub them out, and I want that understood before I say the next part: he does not rub them out, which means every figure he has written since October is still standing on that wall, and about nine thousand people a day go under it."

"And the figures have names."

"The figures have names beside them and the names are smaller and lower down," said Ines Duarte, "and there were about a hundred and forty of them in fourteen days, and I copied the night of the twenty-eighth of February off that wall in pencil into this sheet because it was a night I was going to want again."

She turned the sheet round at last and pushed it four inches along the counter towards him, and kept her hand on the corner of it.

## Why An Auditor Would Copy A Wall

"I want to say the reason for it before you read it, because the reason is the part that will look wrong," said Ines Duarte.

"I have copied about nine hundred words out of rooms in eleven years and every one of them was out of a book with a spine and a shelf and an index at the back that says where it goes."

"That slate has got none of those things. So if I had come back to this building on the sixth of March and written *a slate off Cordwain, a figure of merit, a number of names, said to be the case* and put nothing else on the page, then in about four years a stranger with a folder would have had a sentence about a rumour, and he would not have been able to do one thing with it."

"And with the dates and the hours."

"With the dates and the hours he can at least see whether I was there or whether I was somewhere being told about it," said Ines Duarte.

"I am not going to get him to the bottom of it."

"I am going to get him a page where the dates run in order and the hours are against them, so that when he finds whatever there is to find, he is looking at a thing that a woman walked to a wall and copied down and not at a person saying something happened. That is the whole of what an audit is and it is worth less than people think and it is the only thing I have."

## The Twenty-Eighth Of February

She did not point at the line. She read it.

*The twenty-eighth of February. A quarter past ten in the evening, about twenty in the lane. Half past midnight, twenty-two figures entered in chalk against the four hours, one for standing and one for the hour, the table stopping at nine. Against one of them, at about half past midnight, a name written in chalk about two inches high and lower down, which is where he puts them.*

Lin Hao read it twice.

"That is my name."

"That is your name," said Ines Duarte, "in my hand, in pencil, off a wall, on the second of March."

"I have had it on that sheet for four days and I have not asked you one question about it and I am not going to until I have read this page out in order and got past it, because that is the rule, and because if I ask you at the top of a page then the answer goes at the top of the page, and in about four years somebody reads it as the first thing anybody asked."

She took her hand off the corner.

"Now. Twenty-eighth of February. Half past midnight. A figure of merit of nine and a name, and a figure of merit of nine is the most a man in a delivery coat thinks any person in this city is worth, and you have lodged a whole standing at a public counter and you have a guaranteed subsistence for the rest of your life, which means that figure has cost you nothing and it has cost twenty-two other people between one and nine grains apiece, and it cost me two."

"Including you," said Lin Hao.

"Including me," said Ines Duarte. "I gave two. I want that in the page as well and I am writing it in now."

She wrote it in, in pencil, in the margin, and put the date and the hour with it, because that is the rule.

## The Hour

"And here is the part I cannot get past," she said.

"I have been auditing this building since the first of October. It is a corridor and a counter that shuts at four and four floors, and the page this building keeps is an hours page."

"And on the hours page for the night of the twenty-eighth of February your name is in your row from eleven at night until half past five in the morning, and there is nothing wrong with it, and I have checked it, and it is correct."

"And the slate has you in a lane at half past midnight."

"And you cannot be in this building from eleven at night and in a lane off Cordwain at half past midnight, and one of those two pages is wrong, and I cannot tell you which."

"The slate has *a name* in a lane at half past midnight and I cannot tell you that name is you except that it is your handwriting in my hand, and you have not denied it in ninety seconds, and that is the only evidence there is and it is a pencil copy of a piece of chalk on a wall." She squared the sheet.

"There is no instrument that can go from one of those to the other. I want you to hear the shape of that before I ask the next thing, because I have been sitting on this page for four days and the shape of it is the whole of my trade."

"What is the next thing."

"You tell me," said Ines Duarte.

## The Four Hours

He told her.

Not the nine. Not the table.

The parts of it that had no figure in them: a lane four feet wide and then eleven feet wide where the wall had gone, about thirty people in it by eleven, a man with a bicycle, a boy who had been sent and was going to be collected, a woman with a wrapped parcel she held against her chest the whole time and did not look at anybody, and an old man who came at about ten and knew two of them.

And a woman in a shawl who came in about ten minutes behind him and stood four feet off his shoulder for an hour and ten minutes without speaking, and then said, without turning round, that standing in the line was agreeing to it, and that a man is not worth more than nine whatever he has done, and that she had been standing in this city for nineteen years and had never been told anything so short.

"She was right about you," he said. "She was not talking about me."

"She was talking about the man with the chalk," said Ines Duarte, "and you are going to tell me that in about four seconds, and I am going to let you, because I have found out this month that you will say a true thing about other people very quickly and you will put a thing about yourself at the end of a room."

He was quiet for about four seconds.

"The grain cost me nothing," he said.

"That is the part I have not been able to put down for four days."

"I have a plate with my name on it and no figure on it and a guaranteed subsistence underneath it for the rest of my life because I gave a whole standing away in a queue with a pen, and a grain of that is nothing."

"It is eleven in the mouth and nothing at the end of a week. I paid it at about eight in the evening and got into a lane at a quarter past eight and stood in it for about four hours, and the price of that to me was nothing whatever."

"That is not the price of it."

"That is not the price of it," he said, "and I have been standing on a step for four days working out what the price is and I have not got a figure for it either, and I want you to hear me say that I know I have not."

Ines Duarte put her hand flat on the foolscap and left it there.

"Then that is what I came down the stair for," she said. "Not the name. I have had the name for four days."

## Nothing Tonight Names An Address

She got up off the stool.

"There is one thing I have to say about where we are and it is the only thing I am going to say about it, because if I do not say it then in about four years it will look as though I did not think about it."

"Go on."

"Nothing tonight names an address," said Ines Duarte, "and I have not asked you about it since the day you entered it, and I am not going to start at three in the morning, and you can stay."

And that was the whole of the arrangement and she did not improve on it, and she has never in her life improved on anything anybody handed her.

## The Lamp And The Counter

She turned the lamp round so that it lit the wall instead of the paper and put the sheet face down under the bundle, which is what an auditor does with a page she is not finished with, and the pencil went into her coat and the rubber went into the other pocket and the slate stayed where it was because a slate is not evidence of anything.

Then she took hold of the front of his coat with both hands, on the second floor's side of the corridor, and pulled it open, and put her hands inside it and then took them out again and undid the top three buttons herself, which she can do and he cannot at that hour with cold hands.

"You are no use at this," she said. "You have been on your feet since eleven and you have a stair in you and I can hear it. Turn round."

He turned round and she got the coat off his shoulders and hung it on the end of the counter where the shutter comes down, and it did not fall because the shutter does not go past about two feet and the coat is short.

She is not a soft woman and she has not been one since about the second of July, and what she wanted at about ten past three in the morning on the sixth of March was not comfort and was not the four days she had been in a corridor not saying anything.

She wanted him and she said so in about nine words, standing at the counter with her back to the lamp, and he said yes, and she did not wait for a second one.

Her mouth was on his throat and then on the place under his jaw where it hurts when he has been carrying water, and her fingers had ink in the first two nails and they were cold and they were very quick and they knew in about two seconds where the cough lives, the same way a hand that has known a thing knows it.

There is a grey at her left temple with the etches in it that has been there since the summer of the second year and it does not move when she is angry and it does not move when she is not, and he put his mouth on it once, without meaning to, about a third of the way through, and she let him and neither of them said anything about it then or afterwards.

The counter edge is at the height of a counter and he is a man who has spent eleven weeks putting his weight on other people's counters and it took her about four seconds to get him up onto it, one hand behind the back of his neck and her hip between his knees, and she was not careful with the lamp and it did not go over because the shutter is there.

## After The Standing One Is Cold

She got his shirt open and put her mouth on him and he made a sound about it that she had not heard from him in about two years, and she looked up at his face to make sure of it, and then did not stop looking.

"Here," she said, and put two fingers inside him at the front where she had found out four years ago that he could take it, and worked them in a slow circle and watched him come apart around them in about a minute and a half.

And she put her mouth on him again at the moment it went and swallowed the whole of it and did not hurry and did not make a thing of it, and he had his hand in her hair and was pulling and she let him.

"Now me," she said.

He got her back against the shutter and got her skirt up over her hips and there was nothing under it because it was three in the morning and she had not come here to put anything on, and she was already wet, and when he pushed in she said his name, once, in the ordinary voice, and then bit the heel of her own hand to stop saying anything else.

He went slowly. It is not a thing he does well and she has never once pretended to him that he does, and at about ten past three she put both her hands flat on the counter behind her and told him in four words to stop being careful, and he stopped.

She took it at that pace for about a minute and a half and then she took it off him, one hand flat on his chest, and set the pace herself, and it was faster and it was not better and she knew that and did it anyway. She made a noise about four times in ten minutes and twice she put her mouth on his shoulder to stop it.

Then she turned him round with a hand on his hip and put him into the counter with his hands on either side of her on the wood and told him to hold on to it, and he held on to it, and the shutter came down about an inch on his coat where he had hung it and neither of them noticed until afterwards, and there is a crease in the shoulder of that coat that was not there at eleven o'clock at night.

Afterwards she said his name again and stood with her face turned into his collarbone for about a minute and a half, breathing, with one hand still on the front of his shirt, and neither of them looked at the sheet under the bundle.

## The Hour

"It is ten past three," said Ines Duarte.

"Yes."

"I did that on purpose and I want you to know that I did it on purpose, because that is the rule of a room and I have broken it in about nine rooms in eleven years and this is the ninth." She got her skirt down and got her coat off the counter and shook it.

"And I am going to sit back down at that counter and look at that page and go back to the beginning of it and read it in order again, and I am going to lose about forty minutes, and you are going to go up those stairs and finish your floors, and we are both going to do exactly what we were doing before I asked you for anything."

"That is a strange thing to do."

"It is the only honest thing to do," said Ines Duarte, "and I have thought about it for four days and it is the reason I did not ask you sooner, and because an hour either side of three in the morning is not evidence of anything at all and I am not going to put it in a page I have been reading in order since the second of March."

She sat down and squared the sheet and turned the lamp back towards the paper.

## The Question

He was half way up the stair with the bucket when she said his name.

"There is one more thing and then I am going to read this page."

"Go on."

"I have been in six buildings in four months for the standing sheet and I have read four hundred lines off a district round in a small level hand with a time beside every one and not one figure on any of it," said Ines Duarte.

"I have stood under that wall at half past four about nine times since October, and I read the last line of it every time and it says two words and a dash and two words and nobody in this city has ever been able to tell me what it means, including the man who emptied the pot and including me."

He stopped on the stair.

"And at the foot of the wall there is a white tile," she said, "with your name on it in chalk, and nothing at all under it, and you have stood under it about two minutes at a time about sixty-five times since the first of January, and about four hundred people went past you doing it by the twenty-eighth of January, and that is four hundred people and not four hundred a week."

"Ines."

"What is under that tile," said Ines Duarte. "Who put it there. And why have you stood under it sixty-five times without asking."

She did not say it as an auditor and she did not say it as one of the nine and she said it in the plain voice she uses at about four in the morning when she has stopped working.

And then she turned the lamp back towards the paper and put her two fingers on the margin of the twenty-eighth of February and waited, because an auditor waits, because that is the other half of the rule and she has never once broken both halves in the same room.

And he stood on the stair with a bucket in his hand and did not come back down.

That was the whole of the sixth of March. The page stayed open at the twenty-eighth of February and the corridor stayed cold, and it was a long time before either of them said another word about that tile, and the tile is still there.

# Carried By

The standing sheet goes out at four on the last working day of the quarter, and on the thirtieth of March of the third year the Register's line had two columns on it instead of one, and there was nothing whatever under the second of them.

The second column was written in a clerk's hand in the office's own hand, and nobody in that building had asked her for it, and she did it anyway, because a clerk may only enter and cannot improve what she is given, and what she had been given was a request from the only person the office may ask, which is the one thing she is permitted to refuse.

## Two Words He Wrote Out For Her

He had written it on a piece of the office's foolscap at about half past six in the morning of the ninth of March, in four words and no sentence round it, and he had read it back to her twice before she took it.

*Second column. Carried by.*

"That is the word off the wall," said Wenna Raut.

"It is the word off the wall."

"It is the heading of a column and not the heading of an office," she said. "If I put that in the office's hand on a sheet that goes out at four with an address at the head of every line, then in about four years a stranger with a folder reads a Register of Unrecorded Merit with a column called *carried by* against it."

"And he reads the second line on the ranks wall, and they are the same two words, and nobody will be able to say whether that is a door or a coincidence."

"Yes."

"I am going to do it," said Wenna Raut, "because everything I have entered off this counter for eleven years was something somebody wanted entered, and this is the first request I have had for something nobody wants entered."

She squared the page.

"And I am going to tell you what I think it will do, because thinking about what an entry does is the only thing I am good at and I do not get paid for it."

## What She Thought It Would Do

"You think it will fill."

"I think it will be the largest thing in this city inside two years," said Wenna Raut, "and the only thing anybody in this city has ever been able to hand in without being asked for it."

She put one finger on the empty part of the page.

"A woman in the sixth month of last year went out at about six in the morning and came back at about eight and did it again for nine days and never told anybody, and there is not one column in this city she goes in."

"A man mended a stranger's shutter every time the wind came off the hill, for years. It was not a job. He was not paid for it and not one person in this city knows his name, and not one column in this city he goes in."

She looked up.

"A person took in somebody who had nowhere, and the taking is not a deed, because nothing was held and nothing changed. A person sat with a person. A person kept going back."

She looked up.

"Those are the entries. Not one of them is a deed by your four lines, and the second condition wants a day, and a great many of those are not once. They are every day. They are the same afternoon for six years, and you cannot put six years in a column, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that can go and look at whether it went on."

"Then it is the same condition."

"It is the same condition with one thing taken off it," said Wenna Raut. "A deed wants what a person held, and a place, and a second person, and no page. Carrying wants a person who carried, and the person who carried is a servant or a family or a creditor or somebody's employer, and every one of those four has a word this city already has, and not one of those four words is the one."

## The Two Kinds Of Person Who Are Not In It

"There is a second thing and it took me most of the night of the twenty-fourth to get to it," she said, "and I am going to tell you and then I am going to enter a column."

"Go on."

"There are two kinds of person who will never come to this counter and I do not want them confused with each other, because they are confused with each other in every other room in this city."

She turned the page round so that it was facing him.

"One is a person who cannot name a day. About a fifth of them. They are not lying and they are not stupid, and the sentence in the second line is right about them, and they are going to be told by somebody in about four years that they have nothing, because what they have is nothing that can go in."

"And the other."

"The other is a person who can name a day and would not come," said Wenna Raut. "A person who carried something for somebody, got nothing for it, and told nobody, and every one of those three is a reason to keep it out of a book. The one that stops them at this door is not the money. It is that they have stood in a lane once already and the lane asked them something, and they are not going to be asked it twice in the same city."

She put the nib down.

"So the column is going to fill with people who cannot name a day, and those are the people your definition was built to leave out, and about nine in ten of what comes will land in the second column and be in the second column by accident."

"That is a good thing to have printed and it is a bad thing to have true."

"It is the same thing," said Wenna Raut. "That is what an entry does. It is the same thing and I am going to enter it and I am not going to improve it."

## What Went Out At Four

She read the day's page from the top because a page is read from the top, and she read the whole of it once before she touched anything on it.

Under the disposition, in a clerk's hand, sits a figure this office has carried for twenty-six months and has never once discussed in public. She wrote the address at the head of the line and the figure under it without looking at it.

Twelve lines down there was a street with six words under a figure in a small level hand and a keeper's own name under the six words, and there has not been a certificate behind any of it since the fourteenth of March of the year before last.

Four lines below that was a landlord's return off Lamplit Row carrying a figure that had come off a chalk, and under it the line entered on the twenty-seventh of January of the third year: a street off Cordwain and a figure that came out of one woman's memory at about one in the morning, with no name against it and nothing whatever behind it.

And at the bottom, under an address off a third court, the Register's line.

*Entered: nineteen.* Five of them before the definition took effect on the thirtieth of September of the second year, and fourteen in the six months since, out of about a hundred and forty people who came to that counter with a thing in their hands.

And after the figure, the two columns. The first of them full.

The second of them headed *carried by*, ruled, and empty from the ninth of March to the thirtieth of March, and empty again on the thirtieth of June, and empty again on the thirtieth of September of the third year.

And at the foot of the office's line, in the space where a clerk writes a figure when there is a figure, there was nothing at all.

At four the sheet went out.

About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read it. A man in a delivery coat read it standing up, and a chaplain stood in a yard behind a chapel off Cordwain and heard about it from two hundred people in about nine minutes, and about four thousand of them understood in the course of the afternoon that the second line off the foot of the ranks wall had a heading against it at last.

She read it back once, at the speed she reads everything, and squared the page, and put it in the drawer, and did not hurry.

## The Last Thing She Said About It

He was in the doorway at about ten past four with his coat on.

"You have printed a heading and a hole," he said. "In four years somebody is going to ask what the hole is for."

"They will not ask what it is for," said Wenna Raut. "They will ask what is in it and I will tell them what is in it, which is nothing, and that is the correct answer and it will be the correct answer in four years and in forty, and it is the first thing I have ever entered at this counter that I am certain of."

She shut the drawer.

"That is not a figure and it cannot be checked and there is nothing behind it," she said, "and a man who keeps two floors down understood that thirty years before either of us."

## What He Wrote Down

He wrote it up at about four in the morning of the thirty-first of March at the top of the third flight, with the mop standing in the bucket.

*Second column. Carried by. Written the ninth, entered the thirtieth, out at four on the thirtieth, empty.*

Then he wrote four more lines and they were not about the column.

*Hanneke Ruis. Six words, thirty years, nothing behind them, never entered into anything.*

*A broker. One figure. One night. Nothing behind it. Entered on the twenty-seventh of January against her instruction.*

*A man in a delivery coat. Nine districts, nothing behind them.*

*A man at rank one, cleaning three floors of washrooms, who has now printed a heading and a hole and cannot tell which of the two of them is the instrument.*

He read that last one back and did not strike it out, and then he went and did the second flight and the first.

That was the thirtieth of March of the third year, and the second column was going to go out at four on the thirtieth of June with nothing under it, and nothing under it on the thirtieth of September, and there were about four thousand people in this city who had just read a heading and a hole on the same piece of paper as an address at the top of it.

A threshold under a wall is a floor, and a threshold with a step under it is a stair, and he had laid both of them in his own hand, and neither of them could be checked by anybody including the man who had laid them.

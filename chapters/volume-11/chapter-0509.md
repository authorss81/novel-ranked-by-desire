# Cordwain Keeps Its Own Figures

By the thirtieth of June of the first year there were slates in nine districts in this city and no office in Ostara knew that there were nine, and the reason no office knew is that not one of them can be produced by anybody on request.

The man who writes them all writes them all himself. He has been in a lane off Cordwain every morning since the evening of the second of October and the first morning was the third, and it is two hundred and sixty-eight mornings: twenty-eight days left in October including the third, and thirty each of November, December, January, February, March, April and May, and thirty days of June.

Two hundred and sixty-eight, and not a morning missed.

## The Nine

A bakery off Cordwain has kept one since the middle of January and it is on the wall behind the counter and it is not his slate and he has never once touched it, and the woman who keeps it sends him the count at the end of every week on a card with a bread tally on the back.

Two laundries have them, one on the Cordwain side and one two streets down, and they do not agree with each other about anything, and neither of them agrees with the bakery.

A woman who runs a rooming house has one and it is the oldest thing on her premises and it is in her own hand and she has never let anybody near it.

Five others are in districts that have never had a counter in this city's life and they are held by people who have been asked about them by nobody.

## The Landlord Off Lamplit Row

He has quoted the standing of a premises at that column to a tenant every quarter for nine years.

On the ninth of October he could not find out what the figure at the column was, because for two days about two hundred people in Cordwain had decided that the wall had been rewritten at six in the morning, and on the tenth of October he wrote *as per the Board* on a quarter return for the first time in nine years and did not like the sound of it and left it.

He has said since that the sound of it was the sound of a man admitting that the thing he had been doing for nine years was a thing somebody else was doing.

Since the middle of March he has been quoting something else.

A woman who runs a rooming house two streets down the chandler's has a slate on her own wall with a figure of merit chalked against one of her rooms, and the landlord went to look at it, and it was bigger than the one at the column, and he told his tenant on the first of April that the standing of the premises had gone up.

The tenant's name is Dagny Vessel and she was not in that room for long.

"You may go and check it," he said.

"I have been. I went to the counter off Cordwain with it on a piece of paper."

"And?"

"They said they could not find out."

The landlord was sorry about that, and he said so, and he said it the way a man says he is sorry about the weather.

"That is not what I asked you for, Dagny. I asked you to check it. I have given you the number and where it is written and I have told you the hour of the morning you can go and see it, and if you cannot get anybody to say it is wrong then it is not wrong, and in nine years I have never once been wrong about a column in this city."

She paid the difference for two months.

A figure nobody can check has been used to take something off a person, in a room with a window, without an instrument, and not one office in this city has any idea that it happened.

"She is not a fool," said Lin Hao. "That is the part I cannot get past. She has a piece of paper with a number on it that she cannot argue with, and the number is being taken off her by a man who has not lied to her once, and the only thing anybody in this city could do about it was what that counter did."

"Which is write a sentence about having two instruments."

"Which is write a sentence about having two instruments," he said, "in a clerk's own hand, on a sheet that goes out at four, and she has not read one word of it and does not know that it is about her."

## The Figures Do Not Agree

Nine slates, nine districts, and they do not agree.

They do not disagree about how many people stand in a lane, which is the easiest figure to get and the nearest to true. They disagree about what a person gets for standing, and about whether the four days in October are in the count at all, and about whether a grain paid at the wall end buys a place in a morning or only a place in a night.

The bakery's is the tightest and the one in the landlord's district is the loosest by a long way, and there is not one thing in this city that can say which of the two is a number about the world.

## Two Of Them Are Right

Now the part that took four days to establish and that was not asked for by anybody.

On the night of the twenty-eighth of February, the Register of Unrecorded Merit carries one entry. One act, one address, one hour: a man in a delivery coat chalking a figure of merit against a room over a laundry, at about half past midnight.

Two of the nine slates — one in a laundry two streets down the chandler's and one in a district that has never had a counter — carry the same night in their own chalk. They carry a number against it. And they carry hours.

And the hours are right.

That night, nine districts had a lane in them, and every one of them was open, and the number of people who stood in them between about half past eight in the evening and about half past midnight was not one, and it is not nine, and the office entered one because one is what it was told, and an office may only enter what it is given and cannot go and get the rest.

So there are two instruments in this city and on one night in February one of them is wrong about how many people did a thing and the other two are right about it, and the Register is wrong, and the Register is wrong for the best reason anybody in Ostara has ever had for being wrong, which is that it has one entry and the night had more.

## How He Found Out About Two Of Them

It took four days and it took Beatrix Halm's district round and it took the fact that nobody would tell him anything.

He had been in two of the nine districts in June.

He went to the other two in the last week of the month on the strength of what the bakery sends on the back of a bread tally, which is a number and a street and nothing else, and the street in that number was a district four days' walk from Cordwain and he went and stood in it at about seven in the evening on the twenty-sixth of June with no coat over his coat.

There is a lane there. It is not his lane and it has never been his lane and there are no stools in it, and a man who keeps a slate there chalking what nine people say their district is worth has done it since about January and has never met the man off Cordwain in his life and does not know that the man off Cordwain exists.

And on the wall, in chalk, in a column, against the twenty-eighth of February: a number, and three hours.

Not a name. Not a figure of merit. A count of how many people in that district stood still in a street on that night, and the hours they were chalked, which were about a quarter past ten, about half past eleven and about half past twelve.

Three hours, and the number was four, and he stood in front of it for about a minute and a half and did not write it down because there was nothing to write it down on and he had learned that about nine weeks before in a lane off Cordwain with a bucket in his hand.

## And Nothing Weighs Them

Beatrix Halm read the two slates' hours off their own walls on the twenty-eighth of June with a district round behind her and did not enter anything, because a round writes a line when there is something on it and there was not a person on either wall.

"I could put those two hours in my return," she said.

"I am not going to, and here is the whole of the reason and it is the reason I gave you in the autumn and I have given it about eleven times."

"If I put them in, then in about four years there is a page in a building with two hours on it from a small level hand, and the two hours are right, and then a man goes to the office and says the Register is wrong about the twenty-eighth of February. And then what."

"And then what."

"Then the office has to be able to say the two hours are right," said Beatrix Halm.

"And the only way an office can say a thing is right is by going and looking, and the only way you go and look at a slate is by asking the man who owns it, and the day the office starts asking him is the day that lane is a thing the office is allowed to touch."

"And then it will be shut, or fined, or made to put a figure in a book where a clerk can weigh it, and one of those three is going to happen inside four years and I have watched this city pick one of those three every single time in thirty years."

## What He Says About It

"They are right," he said.

"They are right and so is the Register, about its own act," said Beatrix Halm.

"It is not a question of right."

"It is a question of which one anybody can check, and about nine thousand people a day go under a wall that this city can check and a lane off Cordwain takes a hundred and sixty people into it on an ordinary morning and not one of them can be checked by anybody, and the second of those numbers is going to go up and the first of them is going to come down, and that is the whole of what has happened in four years."

## What He Does Not Say

He did not say which one was better, and he was asked, and Beatrix Halm did not ask him and Runa Sato was not there and Ines Duarte was about nine streets away in a corridor with a lamp on a counter that shuts at four.

He did not say it because in a lane in Cordwain in January he had told a woman who had come down five times with a piece of paper that the true thing about a private figure and an official one is that this city has two of them and not one rule about which one wins.

He had put that sentence into a sheet that went out at four with an address at the head of every line, and he had watched a clerk enter it in about forty seconds in her own hand and not improve it, and the woman had read it at a public desk six weeks later and had not understood one word of it.

The Register has one entry in it and the entry is wrong about a night in February, and two slates in two districts are right about that night, and none of the three can be weighed, and by the thirtieth of September there will be more districts, because a thing that cannot be touched in one lane is not a thing that stays in one lane.

There were nine slates in Ostara on the thirtieth of June of the first year and the Bureau of Merit knew about none of them.

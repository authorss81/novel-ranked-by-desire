# One In Nine Hundred

The notebook came from the same place the branch got its paper, which is the second counter on the ground floor of a building off a third court, and it cost him eleven cents and it had a printed label on the front that said quarter of the year in a clerk's hand before he ever opened it.

That is why he took it.

He wrote the first line on the second of October of the second year at about four in the morning, standing up, at the top of the third flight of the branch, with the bin room open behind him.

*Door. Corridor counter, read by the Registrar, not by me. People past the door. People at the counter with a thing. Things brought. Conditions met.*

Five lines under the printed label. He looked at them for about nine seconds in the way a man looks at a form he has filled in for the first time and has not been told is wrong.

Then he went down and did the second flight and the first.

## The Book He Had Thrown Away

On the first of October, four years back, in ninety seconds at a public counter, he lodged his whole standing and the ledger went with it.

He had carried a private book for four years before that. It had a thousand faces in it and a figure beside most of them and nothing behind any of the entries, and it was the only object in this city that had been entirely his.

It had been in the inside of his coat against his chest for four years and he had handed it across a counter with a pen, and it was gone, and he was a man with a white plate in his coat and no figure cut into it anywhere.

Twenty-five days later he bought a notebook with a printed label on the front.

He knew exactly what the thing was while he was writing the five lines, and there were four people in this city who would have understood it faster than any of the others would have.

He knew what a book in a drawer under a counter is. He had spent four years being the only person in Ostara low enough to be a place where a thing like that was allowed to accumulate, and he had handed the last one over in ninety seconds, and twenty-five days later he had started another one.

The difference between the two books is that nobody will ever read this one.

That is not a defence. That is the entire reason he wanted it.

## What He Actually Wanted It For

The first line he wrote properly was on the eleventh, at about half past six in the morning, and it was the thing he had not been able to get at four o'clock on the second.

Nine hundred and eleven people past the door in twenty-one months. Three things in the drawer. One of those three came in off a street.

One in nine hundred.

He put that in and then he sat on the bottom step of the branch stair with the book open on his knee for about eleven minutes, doing the most obvious thing available to a man in his position, which was to work out whether the ratio was a fact about this city or a fact about him.

It was not a fact about the city.

Four people in forty had brought something the four lines could take, and those forty had been past that door in about seven months after the first name went into the book, and about nine more had come since.

Nine hundred and eleven is not nine hundred people who did a thing. It is nine hundred people who walked past a door. The two numbers have never been the same number in any office in the history of this city.

## The Line He Wrote On The Twelfth

*The four lines leave out nine things in ten. Nine people in ten come to that door and go away having said a thing out loud to a woman who may only enter and may not improve it and cannot be hurried. Not one of them is written down anywhere.*

*Not by me. Not by the Church, which has been promising to do it for two years and keeps the promise in a yard. Not by the keeper, who has been doing it for thirty and keeps six words under her own name. Not by the man with the slate, who does it in chalk and charges a grain a head for it.*

And underneath it, because he was a careful man and had been careful for four years:

*And I cannot go and ask them for it either. A thing nobody can check is worth what the check is worth. There is not one check.*

He read that back twice.

Then he went and got the mop.

## What He Said About It On The Fourteenth

He told one person, because he had learned in twenty years that a thing kept in a drawer is a thing that grows in the dark, and because Adeyemi had read the counter and the figure had to land somewhere.

He found him at about ten past seven on the fourteenth at the bracket with the ratchet on it and the counter's sheet in his hand and his own reading on the back of it.

"You have written something else," said Adeyemi.

"How would you know that."

"Because you have had the same expression on your face since the sixth of September, and you had a worse one on the second of this month, and a worse one than that is not a number. It is a sentence." He lifted the sheet. "Out. It shuts, and it is the only room on this corridor with a door on it, and I have been telling you since February that this is a factor."

The room was the same two chairs and the same table and the same window that does not open.

"I am keeping a count," said Lin Hao. "Of what comes to that door and what the four lines do to it. All of it. Including the nine in ten that never goes in and never will."

"In what."

"A book."

"Whose book."

"Mine."

"Then I am going to say four things and then go and do a quarter," said Adeyemi, "and you are going to stand there and take them, and you are not going to answer one of them, because in about four years I would like it on record that I said them first."

## Four Things Adeyemi Said

"One. A book of what people brought and what was thrown out is a ledger. You had one of those. You put that one across a counter in ninety seconds in front of a clerk and a queue, and the clerk has not thought about it since, and if you write another one then what you did on the first of October has been done a second time in a drawer where nobody is watching. That is not returning it. That is putting it somewhere quieter."

He did not answer.

"Two. The nine in ten are not lost. They are exactly where they were on the first of October, which is in about nine hundred people who did not come to my door and are never going to come to it, and your book does not move them one grain."

He put the pen down beside the sheet.

"You know that, because the whole of what you did four years ago was move them out of a man and into nine hundred people. What you are writing on the stair is a list of nine hundred people's names in a different hand."

He still did not answer.

"Three. If you get it right it is the only figure in Ostara that measures the unmeasured. If you get it wrong there is a notebook in a branch office off a third court full of one man's handwriting about what about forty people brought to a door in seven months, and a stranger with a folder in about four years is not going to be able to tell which of those two things it is."

Adeyemi turned the counter's sheet over and flattened it with his palm.

"Four. You did not come down here to tell me about the book. You came down here because nine hundred and eleven is the first figure this office has ever had about itself and you wanted it to go somewhere, and you picked the one man in this building who cannot improve anything and cannot be hurried and cannot be asked a question twice."

"I did not come down here to be told what I am."

"You came down here to be told what you are, by a man who will not do it kindly," said Adeyemi. "I have known you four years and I would rather you had it in a corridor at ten past seven than find it out at four o'clock out of a number on a sheet."

He picked up the sheet and the foolscap and both pens and stopped at the door.

## Nineteen Days Of It

He kept it for nineteen days and wrote nothing at all in it for eight of them.

The three lines at the bottom of the page were the ones he cared about and they were the ones a man counts without deciding to count.

*Conditions met.*

*Conditions not met.*

*Came and said nothing at all.*

By the twenty-seventh of October he had a rough figure for the third one and it was more than a third, and he wrote it down on the twenty-fourth, which broke a rule he had made for himself on the second and did not break again.

The rule had been that a count is only a count if it is written at the time it is counted, because a count written up afterwards is a thing a man has decided, and he decided that at four in the morning on the second of October in the voice of a woman who said out loud what her six words were worth and said they were worth everything.

## What The Notebook Is

It went in the inside of his coat that night, under a letter on the paper the Correction Office has that had lain there since the first of February of the first year, and over a torn page from a small level hand that had lived in the same pocket for a year before that.

He cleaned the second flight at about one in the morning with the cough in him and the book against his chest, and at about four he went up to the third and stood at the top of it and did not open it.

Three hundred and sixty-nine days back from the twenty-seventh of October is the first of February, which is the day the office was made and the day he was told it existed and the day the only person it was permitted to ask was named in a letter on the paper the Correction Office has.

Nine hundred and eleven people had walked past a door since then and one thing had come in off a street.

He had given away a whole city four years back to move a million grains out of one man's chest and into about nine hundred other people, and in twenty-five days he had found the one number that measured what he had done, and he had arrived at it by counting the people who had come to a door to tell somebody about a loaf of bread.

Both figures were right.

That was the part he had not expected, and he stood at the top of a stair off a third court at four in the morning and found that he had no answer for it at all.

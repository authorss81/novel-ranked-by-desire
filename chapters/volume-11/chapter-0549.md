# A Room With Nine In It

One table off Ferrier Street, nine chairs round it, and about four hundred meals a year have gone across it since the year before last. On the evening of the twenty-second of October all nine of the women who come to it were sitting down and he was at the head with his coat on.

Seven days from the fifteenth of October to this evening.

He eats standing up at his own table and he has done for four years.

"One thing each," said Marguerite Vale from the broker's chair. "Nobody answers anybody. And somebody goes last, and whoever it is goes last."

Nobody answered that either.

## Why The Six Of Them Were In The Room

The minute went out on the morning of the fifteenth with the Register's mark on it and it named premises, because the only address in the whole of his record that anybody in Ostara can name is the one this building is on and the composition of it has had no certificate behind it since the fourteenth of March of the second year.

He entered the clause himself on the twenty-first of June of the first year and it is thirty-eight words and it is not lifted.

*Where a minute names premises, no person keeping an address so named may be produced by that minute otherwise than in the presence of the subject, and the subject may not be absent from the making of it.*

Six of the nine keep the address. He was in the room when the minute was made. There are six people in this city who are, by his own sentence, entitled to be in this kitchen tonight, and there was no form for calling them and no clerk who could have written one, so six of them came.

## Ines

"The fourth condition is not met and it is entered anyway," said Ines Duarte. She had her coat over her arm and her sleeves down and she did not sit for the whole of it. "I audit returns. That is what I do and I have done it for eleven years and I could not have stopped it if I had wanted to and I did not want to. So I am going to tell him what happens to that line."

"Go on."

"It stays," said Ines Duarte. "It goes out at four on the thirtieth of December with an address at the head of every line, and it is on a public desk in this city on a working afternoon, and any person may read it and none of them may do one single thing about it."

"That is not a threat. There is no form. You have made this office un-attackable and you have done it by making it incapable of being right, and I have never seen it done more cheaply and I would have liked about nine more years out of the four conditions before it happened."

She put her glass down.

"That is my one thing and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be asked whether it was the right thing to do."


## The Round

"Nine divisions went out on the morning of the thirtieth of March at about five and they were finished on the thirtieth of June," said Beatrix Halm. "Nine hundred doors. Four hundred and nineteen accounts with an hour against each and not one figure on any of them."

"They are in a book now, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read that book from the top on a public desk, and they can see their own street in it and they cannot see the name of the street on any line."

"What do you want done with that."

"I do not want anything done with it and that is what makes me say it," said Beatrix Halm. "You asked nine divisions to go and find out what people did and they found four hundred and nineteen things, and now there is a man in this city who has put his own name against one of them and not hers."

"She is in a bed two flights up off Cordwain, and that is the whole of what the round is going to be able to say about itself in four years."


"The column is going to fill," said Runa Sato, who was standing at the end of the table with her coat on and a round at four.

"I know."

"You know because a woman on a yard told eleven thousand people so at a quarter past five on the first of August, and about nine hundred of them believed her," said Runa Sato.

"And there is now one line in that column and it is yours, and it is going to fill, and not one line of it is going to be about a person who carried something and got nothing. It is going to be about people who decided, and a woman told me in a counting room in the thirtieth month of the third year that it would fill faster than anything in this city's history, and that not one grain of it would have been earned by anybody in it."

"You have said that before."

"I have not said it before and a woman said it, and I am saying it now because you have put your own name in it and you will want it on the record that I told you what it was going to become." She did not sit down. "That is my one thing and I am going to a round."


"I cannot put a day to any of it," said Nadia Ferrous.

Nobody answered her.

"I have been on nights at that branch since the eleventh month of the third year and I go down a lift shaft four nights a week, and I gave about nine hundred strangers four minutes each in the cold, and I could not tell you one of the days and I would not be able to tell anybody here," said Nadia Ferrous.

"And your second condition throws me out and has always thrown me out and it is correct and I have never once said so. I am saying it tonight because you have put a claim in a book and you have the third condition and I do not, and I am not going to be the one who is glad about it."

"You are not," said Marguerite Vale.

"I know I am not," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is my one thing."


## The House

"The box under the step is cleared out on the first of January," said Isolde Marchetti, from behind the stove, and the room did not turn round, because in that room she said things from behind the stove.

"I have said that in this kitchen twice and I have been wrong twice," she said. "It went in the eighth month of the second year and they took two hundred and six sheets out of it and put them in a room with a window."

"The room has a window and I have been in it and there is a man in it who has a book, and your ninth line is in that book and about four hundred and eighteen other things of yours are in it, and any person in this city may ask for a copy of any one of them at the desk of a building off a fourth court."

"Since the eighth month of the second year."

"Since then."

He did not move.

"That is my one thing," said Isolde Marchetti. "You are not the first thing that has been made permanent in this city by a person with a pen and a good reason. You are about the fourth, and I have kept the count since the second year and I have never given it to anybody, and I am giving it to you now because you are going to want to know how many there are, and there are three more."


"You wrote that you decided it in one room," said Petra Ilyin. She had her hands flat on the table in front of her and they were not working and she had not made them work in about a year and it was not mentioned. "Six of us were in that room."

He did not answer.

"I have been in this room every working evening for four years, and on the night of the first of October I was still sitting at it at about two in the morning, and I came back off a night shift and Marguerite was up, and neither of us said one word about it then and neither of us has said one word about it since," said Petra Ilyin.

"There were nine women in this room the night you read out four lines about a deed, and one of us had a loaf of bread in her coat that she gave away in the second month of the first year and has never been able to write down why."

She looked at the middle of the table.

"And you have written into a book that you decided it alone, and it is false, and it cannot be taken out, and you knew it was false when you wrote it, and I am not going to ask you why because this kitchen does not answer anybody. That is my one thing and I am not angry, and I want it understood that I am not angry, and that I am going to be thinking about it for years."


"The woman who dug the street came up two flights on the tenth of October without a hand on the rail," said Tessa Okonkwo, from the far end, where she had put her things down. "She is the creditor now, a man at a counter told her so, and she went down those two flights the same way and she will not be back inside a month. I have nine days of my own left in this quarter and I am going to put all of them on her."

"What do you want."

"I want you to know that I am not going to say thank you to you in this room," said Tessa Okonkwo, "because you did it for her and she is two flights up and she is not going to hear about it and she has told me in nine days that she does not want to be the kind of person who is owed. That is her sentence and not mine and I am not passing it on and I am telling you it exists."

She did not say anything else.


"A debt that cannot be paid is not a debt," said Marguerite Vale. "It is a sentence, and somebody has to be able to do something about it, and in this city there is nobody."

She turned her chair about nine degrees.

"You have made a man into a debtor and there is no fund, no schedule, no hearing, no seal and no counterparty who can be made to accept anything."

"In eleven years at the Vant's chair I have never once seen a book with a name in it against a person and nothing behind the name, and I want to say to you that the thing you have just done is not a gesture and is not a confession, and the reason it is not a gesture is that it has cost you the only thing you had, which is the right to be asked for a fee."

"It is four words."

"It is not four words and I would know," said Marguerite Vale. "It is four words and about eleven days and a man carrying a page down a stair in the dark. And I am not going to tell you it was worth it and I am not going to tell you it was not worth it, because you have said in this kitchen before that you would rather somebody found a mistake in it in four years than had it explained to them now."

## The Last One

Agnete Lindqvist came in last and sat down and took her coat off, which she had not done on either of the two occasions before.

"I am not going to say anything about the column," she said. "Four thousand of us can read a public desk and about nine hundred of us understand what you did and about forty of us are going to come and stand in a yard where you cannot reach us and wait, and that is going to happen over the next two months and I am not going to be there for it."

"Somebody has to be—"

"You are not going to ask me," said Agnete Lindqvist. "You have asked me two questions in four years and both of them were in the right order and this is not one of them. That is my one thing, it is the only thing I am going to say, and I am not answering anybody, and then I am going to stand at that sink, which is what I do."

She put her hands flat on the table in front of her, and they were cold, and she had washed her hair that morning because there is nothing else to be done with a morning.

"On the nineteenth of July of the second year the nine of us were in this room and you read four lines out and not one of us said a word about whether they were any good," she said, "and you told us you would rather somebody found a mistake in them in four years."

"And in the eleventh month of the third year you printed a sentence on a public page, and it cost a woman in this kitchen her entry and it cost a chaplain her yard."

"And in the third month of the fourth year you sent nine divisions to nine hundred doors and it cost every person at those doors their fourth condition, and on the fifteenth of this month you put your own name in a column, and it has cost you the right to be a man who did a thing."

She looked at the middle of the table.

"That is my one thing and I went last and I am not telling you that you were right and I am not telling you that you were wrong and I am going to be here in this kitchen next week and the week after that, and I am not going to say this again, and I went last."

Nobody answered her, and that was how the room had worked for four years and it worked again.

Nine women had said nine things. Not one of them had said a word about whether the claim in that book was a good one. He did not tell them that it was. Nobody thanked him and it did not occur to him that he wanted to be thanked.

He could not get up and leave the room, and he has not been able to since the twenty-first of June of the first year, and it is his own sentence and it is thirty-eight words long, and six people at that table are sitting in it.

*Twenty-second of October of the fourth year. Nine chairs and nine things and not one answer, and not one of them told me I was right.*

*A woman at the back of the stove has kept a count since the second year of the things that have been made permanent in this city by a person with a pen, and there are two more and she gave them to me tonight, and one of them is me.*

*And a woman with a plate in front of her said the thing I would have had to write and would not have been able to write: that a debt that cannot be paid is not a debt.*

*And the last one told me what it has cost, in order, since the nineteenth of July of the second year, and did not tell me what to do about any of it, and I have not got one thing to do about any of it.*

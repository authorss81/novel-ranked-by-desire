# The Register Takes A Name

The thirtieth of September is the last working day of the quarter and the sheet goes out at four, and the Register of Unrecorded Merit had been in the world for twenty months and three days, and on the afternoon of the thirtieth of September of the second year it entered a person's name.

Not a deed.

## What Came Back On The Ninth Of July

The definition went up the two flights on the first of July and came back down on the ninth with a class against it in a hand that had classified about nine hundred words a week for thirty years, and the classification said what a classification says: what the thing is, when it takes effect, and who holds it.

*A definition. Effective the thirtieth of September. Held by the Register of Unrecorded Merit.*

It carried no argument and no condition and no note, and Alma Quayle had not spoken to him between the first of July and the thirtieth of September.

The four lines went out on the sheet of the thirtieth of June in a clerk's hand with an address at the head of them, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read them and could do nothing whatever with them except obey them.

## The Woman At The Counter

Her name was Wenna Raut and she had been transferred in from the registry upstairs on the first of August because a woman who may only enter cannot be left to enter things that are about a reservoir, and she had been doing it for eleven years, and she had one habit that Adeyemi had noticed in three weeks and had never commented on.

She reads a page twice before she enters it. Both times at the same speed.

"You have read this before," said Lin Hao.

"Twice," said Wenna Raut. "I read everything twice. It is not caution, it is that I have entered about nine hundred things off this counter in eleven years and I have got nine hundred exactly right and I have never known which of the nine hundred was the one that mattered, and reading it twice is the only thing I have ever had that is any use at all."

## What Came To The Door

She came at about eleven in the morning of the thirtieth and she did not stop in the corridor this time.

She put the folded paper on the table and it was the same paper and it had been folded and unfolded about nine hundred times in twenty-one months.

"I told you I would use it if you found it," said Sela Voel.

"I have not found anything," said Lin Hao. "That is the honest answer and it is the answer you came here for. Nothing in this building lets me put the other half on a page. Nothing did on the thirtieth of December of last year and nothing does now, and I have had a year and nine months to find something that does and I have not found it."

"Then I have wasted a year and nine months of carrying it."

"You have carried it for three years and it was not a waste in December and it is not a waste now," he said, "and I am not going to let you stand at that counter and be told by an office that the reason it cannot go on a sheet is a rule, when the reason is that not one instrument in Ostara can take a sentence out of a woman's mouth and set it on a page."

## Why A Name And Not A Deed

The four lines said a great many things and one of them decided the morning.

*An unrecorded deed is a thing all four of which are true.*

Her half page had the second and the fourth and could not have the third, because the third wants another person and the woman who could not put her shoes on in the ninth month of last year has not been found and was never going to be, and Sela Voel had known that since about the middle of March and had brought the paper anyway.

"So it is not a deed," said Sela Voel.

"It is not a deed under four lines I wrote in a kitchen," said Lin Hao. "It is a thing that happened to you and a thing that was owed to you, and the second half of it cannot go on this sheet because of what you are not able to write, and I am sorry about that and I am not going to pretend it is anybody's fault."

"I want my name."

"I cannot give you a deed."

"I want my name," said Sela Voel. "That is what I have come for. You can put a name in a book without the rest of it. That is what a book is."

## What The Clerk Was Given

Wenna Raut had been at the counter the whole time and had not said one word, and she was a clerk who may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and what she was given on the thirtieth of September of the second year was a half page of foolscap in a woman's own hand with the middle taken out of it and a sentence said out loud in a room off a third court.

She asked for the name.

"Sela Voel," said Sela Voel. "Needlework for four houses off Cordwain. I keep a book of who owes me what and it is in the same hand as that page and there are about thirty entries in it and it is not here because it is not a deed and it is not about anybody but me."

The clerk wrote the name.

And she wrote the date, and she wrote the street, and she wrote the line the Register had been carrying since the first of February of the first year with the figure it takes its figure from, and she wrote under it, in her own hand, the one sentence she was permitted to add and could not have improved.

*The Register of Unrecorded Merit has entered the name of a person and has not entered a deed, and the reason is that four conditions were given of which three were met and the fourth was not given and could not be asked for. The entry is made in the office's own hand and not on the report of anybody.*

She read it back, once, at the speed she reads everything.

Then she squared the page and put it in the drawer and wrote the time beside the address at the head of the line, and did not hurry, and did not look up.

## What Changed In The Room

"A name," said Sela Voel.

"A name."

"Three years of a thing and there is a name on it and it is the first thing in this office that is not about a man with a piece of chalk or about the man who would not let it be taken down."

"That is correct," said Lin Hao. "Both of the other entries are about me. That one is the first thing this office has entered on its own hand and not on a report, and it is a name and it is on a page in a drawer and there is not one instrument in this city that can do anything whatever with it, and you are not owed anything this morning and nothing has been paid to you and nothing will be."

"I did not come for that."

"Then you came for the right thing."

She got the folded paper out of her coat and put it inside it again, and it took her about nine seconds because it had been folded and unfolded about nine hundred times in twenty-one months.

"There will be about four more of you at this door this year," she said, "and every one of them will be told that the reason is a rule, and I am going to tell nine people this week what you told me, and the nine of them will come here, and you will say it to them as well."

"Yes."

"That is what I am for," said Sela Voel. "I am not a person who does anything. I am a person who tells nine people where a door is, and it has taken me three years to find out that that is a job."

## What The Register Cannot Do With It

He went and stood at the door of the room with his coat on while she read the sheet and did not say anything to her about any of it, and about eleven minutes later she said, without looking round, that a person who enters a name and then stands in a doorway is not being reassuring, he is being watched.

"I know."

"Then go and stand in the corridor and come back in four minutes and do not come back and watch me do it," said Wenna Raut.

He went and stood in the corridor for four minutes.

There was not one thing in that office that could be done for Sela Voel. It could not pay her the four loaves.

It could not find the woman and it could not strike the half page out of a book, and it could not say in any document in this city what a person is owed when the reason cannot be written down. It could enter a name. That was the whole of its capacity on the thirtieth of September of the second year and the clerk had used it and had used it correctly.

## Four O'Clock

She went at about half past one.

He stood in the room with Wenna Raut and the drawer and the two chairs and read the day's page from the top, because a page is read from the top.

The disposition stood at the head of the line for his own address and the figure sat under it in a clerk's hand. This office carries that figure and has never once discussed it in public, and exactly one page of this city's paperwork sets it out in numbers, and that page is a long way from this room.

Halfway down was a street with six words under a figure in a small level hand and a keeper's own name, and no certificate behind any of it, as always.

A third of the way down was a landlord's return off Lamplit Row with a figure in a column that came off a chalk.

And at the bottom of it, under an address off a third court, three entries instead of two, and the third one was a woman's name and a street and a time, and under it the clerk's sentence saying that the office had entered a person and not a deed.

At four the sheet went out.

## Three Days Short Of Two Years

He went down the hill at about five, which takes about twenty minutes, and at the bottom of it there were five stools standing against a yard wall and a slate propped on top of them about the height of a man's chest.

Seven hundred and eighteen mornings. Twenty-eight days left in October including the third, and thirty each month after that for twenty-three months, and thirty days of the second October, and that is seven hundred and eighteen. A year in this city is three hundred and sixty and two years is seven hundred and twenty and the second anniversary is the third of October, and it is three days short of that, and there will be seven hundred and twenty-one mornings on it.

The five stools were all taken. There was a woman with a pram and a man who had come out of a chandler's and was standing at the wall end with his back to it and a piece of chalk in his right hand and nothing at all on the left.

Two thirds of the way down the column there was a nine, and a name beside it in lower letters, and it was the twenty-eighth of February, and it was worth nothing, and it had cost him a grain.

Under the whole column in half-size chalk there were six words and the name of a room two floors under a sales floor, and there has never been anything behind those six words and there is not going to be.

And in a drawer off a third court there was a woman's name in a clerk's hand, under a heading with an address at the top of it, entered on the thirtieth of September of the second year by a woman who reads everything twice, and no deed, and nothing paid, and nothing owed by anybody to anybody yet, and it was the first thing in that book that had come in off a street.

Three days short of two years, and there was going to be somebody at that counter before the year was out who had said out loud in a kitchen off Ferrier Street that she would be the first, and the first had been a woman who did needlework and told nine people where a door was.

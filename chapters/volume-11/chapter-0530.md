# A Threshold Is A Floor

She came at about a quarter past ten on the morning of the thirtieth of September, which is the last working day of the quarter and the day the sheet goes out at four, and she was not the first person at that counter that day and she was the only one who came twice.

Rosalind Keck does needlework and clean work in four houses off the Vestry and she fed a boy from a school off Cordwain for nine days in the sixth month of the first year and paid for it out of her own hands and did not tell her family, and on the fourth of April of the third year a clerk read a half page twice at the same speed both times and entered four conditions of which four were met.

She put her own copy of the entry on the counter. It was the line off the sheet of the thirtieth of April and it had a time against it and a street against it.

"I want it struck," said Rosalind Keck.

## What Her Brother Said

"Two weeks ago," she said. "In the kitchen, at about nine at night, with my sister there, and he had the sheet off the board at the bottom of the hill, because somebody in the washhouse had brought it up and it had been on our table since the morning."

She did not sit down.

"He said, that is Rosalind, that is on the City's sheet, that is the second one down, that is you in the sixth month of the first year feeding a boy off Cordwain for nine days. And I said it was not me, it was a woman who did needlework, and he said there is one of those and it is you, and then he read out the bit about a second person and he said the boy is my son."

She put two fingers on the copy.

"And then he said that if it is written down then it is a thing I do, and a thing I do is a thing I can be asked to do again, and my son is fifteen and he is at the school off Cordwain for two more years and he has a bed in my mother's house that nobody pays for, and if it is on a page then I am the woman who feeds him, and he said it in front of my sister."

## The Thing She Wants

"He has not asked me for money," said Rosalind Keck. "I want that said first, because I know what this is and I have read what a sheet is. He has not asked me for money and he never will, and that is the worst of it, and he said it kindly."

"Then what he wants is the entry gone."

"He wants the entry gone, or he wants me to have said no in front of my sister and be the woman who does not."

She put her hand flat on the copy of the line.

"I could have said no nine days in the sixth month of the first year. That was when I did it. No one asked me and no one saw me and it cost me about four weeks of my own hands. That was the whole of the arrangement and it took nine days and it was over."

She looked up.

"And now there is a piece of paper in a building with my name and a day on it, and a boy who is fifteen has read it, and I have to do it again or I have to stand in a kitchen and be told that the City has me down as a woman who feeds children."

## What A Clerk May Do

"Can it be struck," said Lin Hao.

Wenna Raut had the copy before he had finished asking.

"No."

"What is the rule."

"A clerk may only enter," said Wenna Raut. "There is no striking in this office, or in this building, or in any building in this city that I know of."

"A thing that has gone into a book at this counter goes in and stays. The only way a thing leaves a book is if somebody in the room above this one writes a line saying the person who put it in was not entitled to, and that is a different page in a different building, and it is not done at this counter."

"Could he be found."

"He is not to be found and you know he is not to be found and you have known it since the thirtieth of September of the first year."

She put the copy down square.

"I am saying it early, not to be cruel. You are about to ask me to enter something, and I would rather we both had the shape of this room in our heads at the same time than that one of us found out at four o'clock."

## What He Could Have Done And Did Not

There were three things available to him on the morning of the thirtieth of September of the third year and he took about four seconds over each one.

He could have entered the entry again with a sentence under it that gave the reason. That sentence would have gone out at four with an address at the head of every line, to about two hundred people a week in Cordwain, and Rosalind Keck's brother reads that board.

He could have asked a question of a woman in a room with no window in a building with no name. He stood nine feet off that door himself in June of year two, and he lifted her recusal on a slip about the size of a quarter of a page, and she has been back on the record at fifty-seven ever since, and she would have had about nine days.

And he could have gone down the hill to a lane off Cordwain and asked a man in a delivery coat for a name to write under a line.

He did none of them.

## The Sentence

"Wenna."

"I have been at this counter eleven years and I can enter about four hundred words on a quarter and I have never entered one that was not in somebody's hand and I am not going to start," said Wenna Raut. "You write it and I shall enter it, and I shall read it twice, and I shall enter exactly what is on the paper and not one word more, and if you want it improved that is a thing you have to do yourself before the page gets here."

"I know," said Lin Hao. "That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the first of February of the first year."

He took the office's foolscap. He wrote for about nine seconds.

*Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing.*

He read it back to the room and Rosalind Keck did not answer him and Wenna Raut did not answer him.

"That is about a quarter of a page," said Wenna Raut, "and it is four words longer than the sentence is permitted to be, and I am going to enter all of it because I am permitted to enter what I am given and not to make it shorter."

## What It Does

"That does not take it out," said Rosalind Keck.

"No."

"Then what does it do."

"It goes out at four with an address at the head of every line. About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read it, and about four thousand people in this city who have spent six months learning four conditions will read it inside a fortnight."

He put the pen down.

"Any person who wants to use a number from this office against a person now has a sentence in front of the number saying that a hole in this Register is not a finding that a person did nothing."

"It does not protect you. Nothing on that sheet protects anybody."

"What it does is that the next person in your kitchen at nine o'clock at night is holding a piece of paper that says the City knows the difference between a thing nobody brought and a thing nobody did, and that the City printed that in its own hand on its own page on purpose."

Rosalind Keck picked her copy up off the counter.

"My brother is not going to read that sheet."

"Then it is for the next nine hundred people in Cordwain and not for him."

## What It Cost, And What It Bought

The sheet went out at four with an address at the head of every line.

*Entered: thirty-four.* Three more than the thirtieth of June, and eleven of them in the last week of September from about forty people who came down a hill, and about one a month between the eleventh of April and the twenty-fourth of September, and about a fifth of what was brought to that counter could not name a day.

And after the figure, two columns. The first of them with thirty-four lines against it. The second of them headed *carried by*, ruled, and empty, and empty on the thirtieth of June and empty on the thirtieth of September and empty on the thirtieth of December after it, and empty on every quarter-end after that for as long as there is one.

And under the whole of it, in the office's own hand, in the one sentence a clerk is permitted to add, and four words longer than that sentence is supposed to be:

*Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing.*

Agnete Lindqvist was not at that counter and did not come, and did not write, and did not speak about it in the yard, and the hundred of her own who had walked out on the eleventh of April stopped coming in the first week of October, and about nine of them came once each in the way people come once to look at something, and then stopped.

## What He Went Up The Hill To Do

He did not sleep on the night of the twenty-ninth of September and he did not go up the hill to stand under anything, which is the thing he does and has done for about two hundred mornings.

He stayed at the branch off a third court from eleven at night until half past five with a notebook in the inside of his coat and a bin room open behind him and the mop standing in the bucket, and at about four in the morning he wrote one line and ruled a line under it and then did not write anything for about an hour.

*Sentence entered. Thirtieth of September of the third year. Address at the head of every line. Two hundred a week in Cordwain. Cannot be un-entered by anybody including me.*

*And it is not for her, and it is not for the nine in ten, and it is not for the four thousand.*

He wrote one more.

*A threshold is a floor.*

Marguerite Vale had said that at a table off Ferrier Street on the nineteenth of July of the second year, in her own chair, with her own hand on the fourth line of a definition, and she had said it as a warning and had been thanked for it by nobody.

He had laid a threshold under a wall in his own handwriting on that same evening, and a year of standing sheets had gone out at four since.

A column called *carried by* had gone out with it four times with nothing under it. There was a figure off a piece of chalk against a street in Lamplit Row, with a number in it that a woman had written out of her own head at one in the morning.

And there was a man in a delivery coat nine hundred mornings into a lane with nine districts' worth of figures in his head and one condition attached to giving them all away.

And a threshold under a wall is a floor, and a floor is a thing that tells a person in a kitchen at nine o'clock at night what kind of floor they are standing on.

The second line on the wall still said *unrecorded — carried by*, in the office's own hand, at the height of a name on a plate, painted on the eighth of October of the first year and taken down by nothing at all, and about nine thousand people a day walked under it and had begun in the last eleven days to understand that the two words of it had a column, and that the column was empty, and that the empty was correct.

Under the last tile, on the stone, a white tile with his name on it in chalk and no figure whatever under it.

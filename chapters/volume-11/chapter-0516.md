# Classification Wants A Word

The office asked for its definition to be classified on the morning of the thirtieth of March, which is the last working day of the quarter, and the request went out on the paper the Correction Office has because there is one paper and one way of folding it and any person in this city can tell you what it is by the back of it.

The draft it was sent with was twenty-one days old and it had been begun on the ninth of March, and it was four lines long, and three of them had been cut down since the ninth and the fourth had not.

Nothing can use a definition until the definition has been classified.

## What The Office Asked For

"A word," said Yusuf Adeyemi.

"A classification."

"It is the same thing and you know that it is the same thing," said Adeyemi. "In this city a word that a division is going to use has to have a class against it, because a division that uses a word nobody has classified will get its returns read back to it at the end of the quarter by nine clerks who will each have a different idea of what it means,"

"and that has happened four times since the spring and I have read the minutes of all four."

He put the request on the table with his thumb on the date.

"I wrote it out at ten this morning and it goes at four with the sheet unless you want to read it before it does."

"Read it."

"It says the Register of Unrecorded Merit has a definition in draft and requires a classification of it before the office enters anything further, and it names the subject, and it names the Wing." Adeyemi took his thumb off the paper. "It is the first thing this office has ever sent anywhere."

## The Corridor Outside The Door

The Classification Wing is two floors under a part of this city that has no name anybody uses, and the corridor outside it has a rail on it and about nine feet between the rail and the door, and there is a sheet on the door with three names on it for the week.

He stood in the nine feet and read it because a sheet on a door is not a conversation.

The first name is the officer of the day. The second is the officer in the second room.

The third is a name he had not seen on any paper in this city in about two years, and it was on a list of three under a rule about weeks, and he read it and did not stop in front of it and did not think about it in the nine feet, and there was a woman behind a door at the end of that corridor who filed instead of giving and whose standing is gone.

## The Woman In The First Room

The door was open and Alma Quayle was at a desk inside it with her division's own paperwork on her knees, and she had been there since about twenty to nine, and she looked up.

She is fifty-seven and she has spent thirty years arranging to be the person in this city who can say what a thing is, and there is not one other officer in this Wing who can classify a word, and there are two in the building who can and both of them are in Veil-Research and neither of them has come up the iron stair since the summer.

"Sit down," said Alma Quayle.

"Thank you."

"I am not going to be in this," she said. "I have been in this chair since the fourth of December and I have not spoken in a sitting in this building since the twelfth, and I am going to hear what you have and I am not going to say one word about any of it, and you knew that when you came up the stair and you came up the stair anyway."

"I did."

"Then sit down and read it to me."

He read the four lines out.

She listened with her hands flat on the paperwork and did not interrupt and did not write and did not turn a page, and when he had finished she said one sentence.

"That is not a definition," said Alma Quayle. "That is a sentence about what a definition has to do."

"Yes."

"Then it is not mine to classify either," she said. "Mine is the thing that says what a word means and this is a thing that says what a word is for, and the second one is not a classification and it would go round nine divisions for four years and be read nine ways, and the first quarter is already going to have one entry in it."

## Four Hundred And Sixty-Eight Days

"How long have you been out of it."

Alma Quayle looked up. That was the first time she had asked him anything and it took her about two seconds to decide to.

"Four hundred and sixty-eight days," she said. "Eighteen days left in December after the twelfth, and thirty of January, and thirty of February, and thirty of March, and a year of three hundred and sixty on top of that, and that is a year and a hundred and eight days."

"I have had the eleven words since the twenty-second of January of the year before."

"You have had them in a drawer," said Alma Quayle, "and I have known you had them since about the ninth of October, and I have sat in this chair for about four hundred and fifty of those days with a woman outside my door who was not going to use them, and I want to say the thing I have not said, which is that I have been waiting for them and I have not known what for."

He put his hands on his knees.

"Do not," he said.

"I am not going to do anything with them," said Alma Quayle. "You know that a recusal is lifted by the subject and by nobody else, and there is not one sheet in this building for lifting one, and if you say the words in this corridor then I am back on the record in a room where about two hundred people a week come past and not one of them will ever know why."

"I have had that sentence in my own head since the ninth of October and I have not said it to anybody and I am not going to say it to you in this chair."

## What Five Hundred And Fifty-Eight Days Does To A Building

"Nine clerks on this floor route past this door," said Alma Quayle. "That is not a punishment and nobody made it one. It is the standing rule of a division when the officer who classifies is out, and it has been out since the twelfth of December, and it is the correct rule and I would not have it changed."

"What is the cost of it to you."

"Four divisions have been carrying a quarter of my work for eighteen months and doing it at the pace a division does a quarter of an officer's work, which is to say correctly and slowly," said Alma Quayle. "One classification a fortnight has gone past this corridor on average since the spring and every one of them went to a man on a third floor who has been signing his own initials on it for eighteen months and could not tell you this morning what a definition is."

She put a hand flat on the paperwork.

"That is the cost to the city and it is real and it is not the reason I would refuse you and not the reason I would not."

"And the reason you would refuse."

"The reason is in a drawer about nine feet from where we are sitting," said Alma Quayle, "and it has been in that drawer since the fourth of December and I signed the piece of paper that sent it there myself, and nobody in this building is entitled to make me sorry about it, least of all the man it is about."

## Why He Does Not Say Them

Because of a room with no window in this building, and a file that is kept here, and a woman who is not alive in any sense a court would use and who is classified and has never been out of this Wing and will not be.

He did not say that and he was not going to, and it sat in the nine feet of corridor between them the whole time and it was not in his mouth once.

He thought about it in the terms his own trade had given him and his own trade had given him nothing: there is no sentence available that makes this cheap. Anything he said would be a use of her, and everything that had made her worth lifting in the first place was that she had never been used.

## What He Did Instead

He put the four lines back on the table and squared them.

"I am going to take this away," he said.

Alma Quayle nodded.

"You are going to cut the fourth line down."

"It has been cut twice," he said. "It was four lines on the ninth of March and it is four lines now and two of them are shorter, and it is going to be nine arguments in a kitchen and then it is going to come back here, and when it comes back here it will be a classification and not a description, and you will be able to put a class against it."

"Will I."

"It will come back here," he said, "and it will come back to you, because you are the only person in this city who can put a class against it and you have been arranging to be that person for thirty years and you are not going to stop being that person because I did not want to spend you."

He picked the paper up.

"I am not saying the words today. I am not saying them in this corridor and I want you to hear the shape of why, and the shape is that the only door in this city that a definition can come out of is a door that I open, and there is not one other person in Ostara who can open it, and I have been carrying that around since the ninth of March and I do not want it."

Alma Quayle sat with her hands flat on the paperwork for about four seconds and then she said, "That is the first sensible thing anybody has said in this corridor since the autumn."

## What He Took Back Down The Stair

He went down the two flights with the four lines in his coat and a list of three names on a door above him that he had read once and would not read again.

At the bottom of the stair there was a woman on the rail with a coat folded over her arm who had been there since about half past ten and had not come in, and she did not say one word about any of it and neither did he.

Twenty-one days is what the draft had been alive, and the eleventh of March was the last time he had sat down with it and cut anything, and there were eight arguments left in it before it would go into a kitchen off Ferrier Street to be taken apart by nine people who would each say one thing and not answer one another, and the last one of those nine had said her piece on the twenty-first of January on a floor with a lamp on it.

He had eleven words in him that would open the door, and four hundred and sixty-eight days behind them, and he had decided nothing at all on the thirtieth of March, which is the last day a quarter can be decided on and the first day it can be undone.

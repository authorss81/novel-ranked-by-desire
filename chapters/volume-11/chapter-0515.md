# The Ward And The Water

He went up the two flights at about a quarter to eleven at night with a cough in him that had been there for four years and a piece of paper in his coat with nine words on it in his own hand, and the sister on the landing looked at the two of them and went through to the ward and did not ask, and the room off the ward had a cot in it and a chair and a lamp on the floor.

Four people were asleep along the ward and the lamp at the desk at the far end was turned down as far as it goes.

"Sit down," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Then shut the door. The door is the whole of it, and it is on a latch that came up hard in about the third week of November and I have had it oiled once and it has come up hard twice since, and that is the arrangement and it is the arrangement of this room and I did not choose it and I could not choose it."

He shut the door.

## What She Had Left

"I am going to tell you the figures before I ask you for anything," she said, "because you have had a long year and I am not going to make you wait for a person to get to it."

"Go on."

"Four months at a thirtieth on the twelfth of September, which is the day I told you on a trolley and you have not asked me once since what happened to it."

She was not sitting down. "Three of those months went to about two hundred people at a table by the Vestry gate over four days and I have counted them and I know it was three and not two because I took a thirtieth and not a fortieth and I could have taken a fortieth for all of it."

"How much of it is left."

"Nine days," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Nine days at a thirtieth, and about two months of taking a fortieth off anybody who could not get out of a street, which is not a fraction. A fortieth is a garnish."

"It goes on and it does not feed anybody and I have been doing it since the middle of November and I have been doing it because a doctor who says no to a person at half past two in the morning is not being careful, she is being comfortable, and I would rather be one of the two."

She turned her sleeve down over the stippling on her forearm and did it neatly, the way she has done it since the second of July.

"So that is what I am, and you may have it now instead of in about two hours."

## The Woman In The Corridor

"Somebody sat in this corridor for an hour on the seventeenth and did not come in," he said.

"Beatrix Halm sat in this corridor for an hour and forty minutes on the seventeenth and did not come in," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and she had a coat on and she was not cold and she was not waiting for anything, and when I went out at about two she said one sentence to me and went away down the stair, and I have been carrying it for four days and I have not told you because you had not come."

"What did she say."

"That a line went out at four on the thirtieth of December with an address at the head of it and her division has to carry that line back," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Nine divisions, and hers is one of them, and a division that has to carry a line goes and finds out what the line is about, because nobody in this city has ever returned a line they did not understand."

## What That Costs

"You know what I am going to say and I am going to say it quickly."

"Say it."

"A division that goes and looks will find people who did things on those four mornings that were never written down, and being found by a division is not the same as being found by a wall."

She sat down on the chair, which she had not done for the whole of the time he had been in the room. "A wall does not come to your door. A division does. And the cold in a person is not taken out by asking them about it, it is taken out by the asking, because a question with nine people behind it is heavier than a wall with nobody behind it, and that is not a figure and I have never had a figure for it."

"How much of it comes to a room."

"A third of them," said Tessa Okonkwo. "About a third of anybody a division finds in a cold city, and not all of them will know they are cold, and the ones who know they are cold will not come to a clinic."

"They will come to a room off a court with a counter in it, and about four in ten of those will end up on a table in a ward off Cordwain, and that is my figure and I have had it since the seventeenth and it has not improved by being said out loud."

"And you have nine days."

"I have nine days and then I have a fortieth and a garnish and a floor under me in this building that I hit in April of the year before," said Tessa Okonkwo. "And I am not asking you for anything. I am telling you what I am, because you are going to find out about that line in about four months from a sheet and I would rather you found out what it costs first."

## The Second Thing She Said

"You are going to be asked to stop it," she said.

"Who."

"I do not know and I did not ask, and if I had asked I would have had it by the eighteenth and I would rather have it in about four months from somebody who does not know what it is for."

She looked at the door. "You are going to be asked by a person who is being kind to you to stop a line going round nine divisions, and you are going to say no, because you have said no once already and you have not got a second one of those in you. And when you say it, about nine divisions will go out and find cold people, and it will not be your fault, and it will be yours."

He did not answer.

"That is the second thing I have to say tonight," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I am not saying the first one yet."

## Lie Down

"Then say the first one."

"The first one is that you are going to lie down on that cot and I am going to shut the door and we are going to be quiet," she said, "and I have thought about it for two days and I have not been going to ask you and I am asking you."

"How long."

"As long as it takes and not as long as you can do it," she said. "I have been on my feet since six and I have four people in there and one of them has to turn at two and I will know it. That is what I have."

"The door is on a latch and it will go at any hour and if it goes then this stops and I go back in there and you lie on a cot in a clinic and get up and go to work. That is the whole of what I am promising you and I want it said out loud that it can stop."

"Then shut the door."

She shut it, and the latch came up hard, and the room was the size of two ward beds and a chair and a lamp on the floor, and about nine thousand people went under a wall a day in the other city and not one of them could have been in this room.

"I am not going to be a doctor about it," she said. "I have been a doctor about everything since the second of December and I have been a doctor about it with you for thirty-two months and I am tired of it. Lie down."

## What She Wanted

He lay down on the cot in his clothes and she took her coat off and hung it on the back of the door and then took it off the door and put it on the chair, because the door opens outwards and she did not want anything behind it.

She was unhurried in the way of a woman who has been on her feet since six and has decided that being tired is not going to be part of this.

"Slower," she said. "Yes. That one, and that is the one, and you have gone four years without anybody telling you what to do with your hands and so you do it too well and too fast and I am going to leave it about eleven seconds and then I am going to tell you something else while I do it."

He slowed down. She was right about it.

"Here is what I have not said," said Tessa Okonkwo. "On the twenty-eighth of February, at about half past midnight, in a lane, you paid a grain to stand in a line for four hours and a man wrote a nine beside your name on a piece of chalk, and about a hundred and sixty people stood in that lane in the ordinary course of the morning. Ninety-nine of them were standing still for the same reason you were."

He went on with his hands where they were.

"Ninety-nine people out of a hundred and sixty took about a minute each out of themselves for nothing, and the minute came out of the same place mine comes out of, and they did it in the cold with a queue behind them and they have never been told by anybody that it did them anything, and about a third of them knew exactly what they had done."

She moved her weight off him and got a knee on the cot. "A division that goes to their door in about four months is going to find ninety-nine people who gave a minute away for a grain and are about to be told by an instrument that they did it."

"That is what a column is for."

"That is what a column is for," she said, "and you are going to write it, and I am going to be the one who takes what comes off the third of them, and I have nine days, and I have told you the figure before you have asked me for it, which is the only gift I have got and it is not much of one."

## The Middle Of It

She got him over and then got him back and then she was on top of him with both her palms flat on the cot either side of his ribs, and the room went on being a room with a lamp on the floor for about four seconds, and the latch did not lift and neither of them reached for anything at all.

Then she took a breath in and let it go, and her mouth came down on his, and it was not tender and it was not fast and it was four months of a ward at night in it.

She got inside him with two fingers first because that is what she does, and she made him watch her do it, and then she took her fingers out and put herself on top of him and took him all the way in and stopped there with her weight on him and nothing moving at all, and the latch on the door came up about four inches and went back.

She said his name once, in the ordinary voice, and then put her own knuckle between her teeth to hold the rest of it in.

"Do not be careful with me," she said. "I have had four hundred people on that table and I have not had one of them in three months and it is not because I have not wanted anybody, and you know that, and you have known it since about the eleventh of December and you have not come, and I have not asked you, and I am asking you now with my hands on you which is a thing I have never once done in this room."

He was not careful with her.

She took about a minute to find that out, and then she put both hands flat on the cot either side of him and worked out where he had learned it and how long it had been unused, and said nothing about any of it, because a doctor does not narrate a finding to the patient while she is on top of him.

The latch came up about three inches and went back, twice, in about four minutes, and neither of them stopped and neither of them hurried. About eleven seconds after the second time she put her mouth against his ear.

"Still here," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That is the only sentence either of us has said in an hour and I am going to have to stop saying it."

She took it at that pace and made almost no noise for about a minute and a half, and then she began to make noise, and she put her mouth on his shoulder to stop it, and he held the back of her head and did not let her.

It went on longer than the room seemed to be able to hold and neither of them counted a minute, and neither of them counted anything at all.

When she came she got both hands behind his head and held him there and made a noise into his hair that she had plainly not meant to make, and she let it run, and she did not apologise for it and she did not stop.

He came after her with his hands spread between her shoulder blades, and the cough took about nine seconds to arrive, and she said something short into his collarbone, and then the room was a room again for about two minutes.

## Afterwards

She sat on the edge of the cot with her feet on the floor and her elbows on her knees, the way she sat on the edge of the table in September, and he lay where she had left him and the lamp on the floor was burning at the level of the floor.

"Now the thing you are going to ask me for," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"I was not going to ask you for anything."

"You were going to ask me whether I would take another one out of you and you were going to ask it in about nine seconds and I have known you for four years,"

"" she said. "The answer is that I am not going to do it tonight and I have had nine days on the twelfth of September and I am on about the second of them, and I do not take a fraction off a man who has not got one spare for a room that has four people in it."

"That is a reason you made up for me."

"It is a reason I made up for you and it is true," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I am not going to be thanked for it either, because there will be a night in about four months when I do take one and I am not going to have you carrying a debt about it on the day I need you out of a door."

She got up and put the latch up twice to test it, and it came up hard both times, and she wrote the hour on a card on the desk at the far end of the ward in a small level hand and did not improve it.

## The Thing She Needs

At the door she stopped with her hand flat on the frame.

"There is one thing I do need and it is not money and it is not a night."

"Go on."

"When your division goes out — and it will, and it will be a division of nine and hers will be one of them — I need to know what the nine are allowed to say at a door," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Not the words. The words are yours."

"I need to know whether a person can say no and have the no entered, because about a third of them will say no and if the no cannot go anywhere then the third of them will learn to say yes, and a person who has learned to say yes has lost the only thing they had that was theirs."

He looked at her.

"That is a sentence for the kitchen," he said.

"It is a sentence for the kitchen and it is the only one of the nine things I am going to say tonight," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I have said it now because you will not write anything until nine people have tried to break it, and I have read about that, and I am one of the nine, and I have gone first."

The latch came up hard behind her and she went down the two flights to a ward with four people in it and a lamp at the desk, and the clinic kept its hours and nothing that happened in that room off it went on any page at all.

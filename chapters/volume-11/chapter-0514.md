# The Sheet Goes Out At Four

The thirtieth of December is the last working day of the quarter and the standing sheet goes out at four in the afternoon with an address at the head of every line, and any person in this city may read it at a public desk on a working afternoon, and for the second quarter running there was a line at the bottom of it that had not been there in the first quarter at all.

That line said *Register of Unrecorded Merit. Second quarter. Entered: two*, and under the two it said what the office had, which was one act reported by the only person it may ask, and one refusal entered whole with the sentence in the office's own hand and not improved.

She came in at about a quarter to twelve in the morning and she stood in the corridor outside the room for about four minutes before she knocked, which is a thing about her and not about the room.

## Sela Voel

She was a woman who did needlework for four houses on a street off Cordwain and kept a book of who owed her what, and the book had about thirty entries in it in a hand that was her own and had a certificate behind exactly none of them.

She had a folded paper in her hand when she came in and she put it on the table and did not open it.

"I have been coming to this door since the twenty-fourth," said Sela Voel. "I did not come in. I want that said first because I have been four days getting it ready and I am not going to be told I was too slow by the man who would be the one who did it slowest."

"Take the chair."

"I will stand," she said. "I have stood at about nine counters in this city since October and I am not going to be a person who sits down at this one and then cannot get up in it."

## What She Wanted Entered

"In the second month of this year I let a woman have bread for four days," said Sela Voel.

She did not open the paper.

"Four days. She was not with me and she was not my servant and she had not asked me for it and she did not have the price of it and she came to my door at about seven in the morning and she asked me for a loaf and I gave her a loaf,"

"and she came back on the second and the third and the fourth, and on the fifth morning she did not come, and I have not seen her since and I do not know where she is and I did not ask and I am not going to."

"And the deed."

"The deed is that I fed a woman for four days and she has never paid me and is never going to and I am not going to chase her," said Sela Voel. "That is the whole of it. It is a loaf four times. That is all that happened to me in the second month and I would like it on a piece of paper somewhere in this city while I am still able to say it out loud."

## The Reason She Cannot Put On Paper

"The reason," she said, and then she stopped for about four seconds, and then she went on, which is what a person does who has decided in advance to say it once.

"I gave her the bread because she could not put her shoes on."

Lin Hao did not say anything.

"She was standing at the north end of the Reading on the fourth of October and she came to my door at about seven in the morning on the ninth of February having been standing in a cold for about four months, and she could not get her shoes on and she had been trying since the sixth and she had four days of that in her and she did not know what to do with it."

She put one finger on the folded paper and left it there.

"That is the reason. Now I am going to tell you what I cannot do with it," said Sela Voel, "and I have known since the twenty-fourth and that is why I stood in your corridor for four minutes."

## The Book Of Who Owes Her What

"I keep a book," said Sela Voel. "Thirty entries. My hand. No certificates behind a single one of them, and I have had it eleven years and I have never once been asked to show it by anybody, and I am not showing it to you because if you saw it you would want it and you could not use it."

"What is in it that a quarter would want."

"Loaves. Rent. A coat. Nine eggs in the ninth month of last year. A man who came in out of a wet night in the seventh month and slept on my back step and paid me nothing and did not ask me for anything and I have got him at nothing and I have never taken him off the book," said Sela Voel.

"That is what thirty entries are. They are a woman's record of being owed about four hundred times in eleven years by people who are all alive and about eleven of whom will ever say so."

"And none of them can be."

"None of them will come and say so and not one of them is a liar," said Sela Voel.

"You could hold the book in your hand this afternoon and every clerk in this building could read a name off every page of it and not one of them could make a person pay, and that is not because it is wrong. It is because the only instrument anybody has got that takes a debt is a form, and the only people who have got the forms are people who were owed nothing."

## Why The Reason Cannot Go On Paper

"Because it goes out at four," he said, "with an address at the head of every line."

He said it straight, and he did not soften it, because she had come in with a folded paper and had held herself ready to be handled.

"A sentence in your hand saying that you stood at the north end of the Reading on the fourth of October is a sentence that a woman at a public desk can take the number of and put in front of the rest of it. It would read as though you were claiming something about those four days. That is not what you are claiming and I cannot stop it reading like that, and the person it would read like that about is not you and is not here."

"I know what it would read like."

"And there is not one thing this office could do with it if it did go on," he said. "We cannot pay you back. We cannot make the woman pay. We cannot put a word in front of the fourth of October and stop it being on every wall in this city, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that can touch that day, and you have found the one sentence nobody in this city is able to write down and put a heading on."

## What The Clerk Did

The woman who types for that office came in at about one with the day's sheet under her arm and Sela Voel gave her the folded paper and the clerk read it standing up, which is what she does.

It was half of a page and it was in Sela Voel's own hand and it gave the street, the dates, the four days, the loaf, and the fact that nothing had been paid and nothing was going to be.

It did not give a reason.

The clerk read it to the end and then she looked up and she did not say one word, which is not the same as not understanding. She is a clerk and she may only enter and cannot improve what she is given and cannot be asked to hurry, and what she had been given was half a page with a hole in the middle of it, and she entered the half a page in about forty seconds in her own hand and did not add a word.

"That is you?" said Sela Voel.

"That is me," said the clerk. "That is the entry and it is in the book and it is on the sheet and it goes out at four with the rest of it."

"And the other half."

"The other half is not on it," said the clerk, "because it is not on the paper."

## What He Said About It

Sela Voel put the folded paper back inside her coat, and it took her about nine seconds to fold it again because she had opened it out on the table while she was talking and had not put it back the way it was.

"Three years," she said. "That is how long I have had that. Three years of a thing nobody wrote down and nobody can write down and a woman who cannot put her shoes on."

"That is how long I have had a tile with nothing under it," said Lin Hao, "and I am not telling you what I am doing about it because I am not doing anything about it, and you did not come here to be comforted and I am not going to try."

"No," said Sela Voel. "That is why I came."

She got to the door and stopped with her hand on it.

"I am not going to withdraw it," she said. "I want you to understand that. You have not got a form that lets you put a thing back and I have not got an argument that lets me take it away, and if you find one of those in the next year I am going to use it and I would think less of you if I did not."

"You may not need to use it."

"I am going to need to use it," said Sela Voel, "because the office you are running has one entry in it from a man with a piece of chalk, and the office I would come to is one where I could walk in and say what I did and why, and neither of those things is on your sheet today and both of them are going to have to be."

## Four O'Clock

She went at about twenty past one and the corridor took her away and he stood in the room with the sheet on the table and the lamp on and the two chairs, and at about half past three he read the day's page the way he has read one about four times a quarter since the twenty-eighth of January.

The disposition was at the top of the line for his own address and the figure was under it in a clerk's hand and the figure itself is a thing this office carries and does not discuss in public and there is one place in the whole of this city's paperwork where it is set out in figures, and that place is not this.

Halfway down was a street, and under the street a figure and a date and six words in a small level hand with the keeper's own name under them, and no certificate behind any of it, which is what she has written four times a year for thirty years and has never once been asked for by anybody.

A third of the way down was a landlord's quarter return off Lamplit Row with a figure in a column that had been quoted off a chalk in a district four streets away.

And at the bottom, under an address off a third court, two entries and a refusal and, in the office's own hand, a line that had been added that afternoon because a clerk may only enter and a clerk entered it as it was given to her.

*The Register of Unrecorded Merit has entered a deed offered on the thirtieth of December and has not entered the reason for it, and the reason is not on any page of this office and the office has no instrument that would let it be put there.*

At four the sheet went out.

It is the second sheet this office has ever put into the world and the first one with a refusal on it, and it carries a sum of money that is a fraction of the largest thing that has ever happened in this city, and a keeper's six words about a street, and a landlord's figure that came off a piece of chalk, and two entries and a refusal, and one deed with the reason taken out of it by nobody.

About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read it, and not one of them knows that a woman stood in a corridor off a third court for four minutes this morning deciding whether to knock, and that she is the first person to have brought anything to that door that was hers and not a report about a man with a slate.

# Three Years Of Waiting

The counting room is two floors under a sales floor and you go down an iron stair for it, and at about half past four in the afternoon on the twenty-eighth of February of the third year he was let in by a woman who had come to that room on and off for eleven years and had never once been asked a question by anybody.

There is a lamp on a stand. The fifth book is open on the table with a strap across it. Hanneke Ruis is at her end and Sun Meiling is at the far end with a folder she has not opened, and the far end is eleven feet away and there is a chair between them that nobody uses.

Sun Meiling is the research partner of the man whose file is shut, and she waited three years for somebody to ask her the right question, and he has known that for four years and had not come down the stair.

He came down it.

"You have been sitting there for eleven years," said Sun Meiling, "and I have been sitting at this end for nine of them, and you have never asked me anything, and I want to say before you do that I have worked out that the reason is that you do not know what to ask and that this is the correct way to behave."

"I am going to ask you a question about him."

"Then you are going to ask me a question about him and it will be the wrong one," said Sun Meiling, "and I am going to let you, because I have waited three years for one and I would rather have the wrong one than be polite about it for another winter."

## The Wrong Question

He stood between the two ends of the table and did not sit down.

"Was he right."

Three years she had had an answer ready for that. She had it ready on the nineteenth of July of the first year and she had it ready on the thirtieth of September of the second and she had it ready on a night in the ninth month of the year before.

"I cannot give it to you," said Sun Meiling. "Not because of the record and not because of him. Because of the room I am in."

She put her hand flat on the folder.

"I do not see one thing any more. I see every shape at once and they come out in the same hand, and I have spent three years with an answer about a man in a corner of my head where nothing else is, and it has gone soft, and about a fortnight ago I looked at it and what I had was four shapes that had been one answer, and one of them is about a street."

She turned her head about nine degrees, which she has done at two tables and one kitchen and a counter off a third court.

"So the question was wrong and you may have it, and here is the shape of why. You have asked me about a man. You have had four years of men. Ines reads records and she will find you a page. The man in the delivery coat will tell you a fact that is his own and that he has made up in three days and you can check whether he says the same thing twice. Whatever you are carrying, it is not a man's honesty."

"Then what is it."

"It is a shape," said Sun Meiling. "And I am about to give you a shape instead of an answer, and you are going to be angry with me in about nine seconds, and then in about four years somebody is going to go and look at what I have just said and it will turn out to have been right and you will not have it then either."

## The Shape

"He was not working on an account," said Sun Meiling.

"He was."

"He was not. An account has a number and a holder and a direction, and I could see all three of those at any time I wanted to, and they are not what he spent nine months on." She opened the folder, which was a folder of paper with nothing written on the outside of it. "He was working on whether a figure about a street can be wrong."

"That is Hanneke Ruis's column."

"It is Hanneke Ruis's column and it has been hers for thirty years, and he did not know her, and he found out about her in about four days," said Sun Meiling.

"And he went about it the way anybody can only go about it. Assume that a figure written out of one woman's memory with nothing behind it is right, and then spend a year trying to find a second place it could be checked."

"He could not find one. That was not the failure. That was the finding."

Hanneke Ruis turned a page in the fifth book without looking up and without speaking, which is the whole of what she has ever done about him and it is enough.

"And then he did the thing that made him disappear," said Sun Meiling, "which is that he went and looked for a word. Because a figure you cannot check is a figure you cannot argue about, and if there is no word for what the person who cannot be checked is then the person who cannot be checked has no position and there is nowhere for a dispute about a figure to go and it goes nowhere, which is where the column goes."

"What word."

She put her hand flat on the folder again.

"It is two words and it is the heading on the foot of the ranks wall and you have not put it in a column and neither has anybody else in three years," said Sun Meiling. "Unrecorded, carried by. Not *owed*. Not *benefited*. Not *witnessed*. Carried."

Hanneke Ruis stopped turning pages.

"A person who carried something for somebody is not a person who did a deed," said Sun Meiling. "A person who carried something is a servant or a family or a creditor or somebody's employer, and every one of those four has a word this city already has, and not one of the four is yours."

"So the carrying goes in the wall and does not come off it. I have sat at this end of this table for nine years watching a man in a white plate not put it in a column, and the reason is that nobody ever told him what it was for."

## What He Asked Next

"Then why did you never tell me."

"Because you never asked me anything," said Sun Meiling, "and because you were rank one and cleaning three floors of washrooms for four years, and because I could not see which of the four shapes was the one that mattered and I was not going to hand a man a shape in his second year when he had a brother he could not open a file on."

"You could have come up the stair."

"I could have come up the stair," she said, "and I decided nine years of it not to, and I have never once pretended to myself that that was anything other than the flinch of a woman who has spent nine years not being asked and has got used to the not being asked."

## The One She Asked

Hanneke Ruis stood up, which she does once a quarter, and put the strap across the fifth book and squared it, and said that she was going to put the room's one line in for the quarter, and went out, and shut the door, and the stair was two flights and she took them slowly.

Sun Meiling waited until the door was shut.

"Now I am going to ask you one," she said, "and it is three years late and it is not about him."

"Ask it."

"Will you put it in a column."

He did not answer for about nine seconds, and the lamp on the stand made a small noise, and somewhere two floors above them a sale finished.

"Because if you do," said Sun Meiling, "then in about four years there will be a heading on a sheet with an address at the head of every line and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain reading it, and a person who carried something for somebody will have a place in a book."

"And a person who carried something is the only person in this city who has never once been thanked and has never once been paid and has never once been able to say so."

"If they get a column, that column will fill itself faster than anything in the history of this city, and not one grain of it will have been earned by anybody in it."

She put her hands flat on the folder.

"And if you do not," she said, "then you will have spent four years building an office that cannot even say the word, and it will go on being the only thing in this city that measures what nobody measured, and it will measure one thing in nine hundred, and that will be the honest number, and I will not say one word about the return ever again in this room."

## What He Took Up The Stair

He went up the iron stair and out into a sales floor and along it with about nine hundred people in it, and he stood in the street at the top of the steps for about a minute and a half.

Two words on the foot of a ranks wall about nine thousand people a day walk under, in the office's own hand, at the height of a name on a plate, painted on the eighth of October of the first year and taken down by nothing at all.

And a quarter's line at the bottom of a standing sheet that ends in a name, a date, a street and one sentence, and there is no second column on it and there has never been one, and the Register has now been open twenty-four months and has entered two names and not one deed.

He got to the branch at about eleven and the third flight and the bin room, and the notebook was in the inside of his coat where the card had been.

He wrote two lines and one of them is the rule he had made for himself on the second of October and broke on the twenty-fourth and has not broken since.

*Sun Meiling, far table, counting room. Asked her about him and got a shape. Did not get an answer and will not get one and did not know that was coming.*

*Two words off the foot of a wall. She says nobody has ever put them in a column.*

He looked at that for a while.

Then he wrote the third line, which is the one that was going to cost him something in a kitchen in June.

*It is a column or it is nothing. There is no half.*

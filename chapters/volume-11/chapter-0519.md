# A Definition In His Hand

The kitchen off Ferrier Street has one table in it and nine chairs and about four hundred meals a year have been eaten off it, and on the evening of the nineteenth of July there were nine women at it and a man on his feet at the near end of it with his coat still on and four lines of paper in his hand.

He has eaten at that table for four years. He has never once eaten standing up at the end of his own house.

"One thing each," said Marguerite Vale from the broker's chair. "And we do not answer anybody. And somebody goes last and whoever it is goes last."

"It is me," said Agnete Lindqvist, from the doorway, in her coat.

"It was you in October and you were not last in October."

"I am last tonight," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have come to the table about a quarter early and I have been in that doorway for nine minutes, and I am going to stand there for all of it and I am going to say one thing and I am not going to answer anybody, and none of you is going to be helped by any of you."

## What He Read Out

He put the four lines on the table face up and read them out in the flat voice he uses at a counter, and he read them twice, and he did not improve them between the two readings.

*An unrecorded deed is a thing all four of which are true.*

*First. It changed what a person held. Not what they felt. What they held.*
*Second. It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name.*
*Third. Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough that a person reading this in four years would know which person.*
*Fourth. It is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

"And four things it does not take," he said, "and each one of those is a decision and not an oversight."

*Not a thing a person did alone.*
*Not a thing that changed what a person held by about nothing: a night, a morning, four hours in the cold.*
*Not a thing a person can place in a year.*
*Not a thing a person can only put down as a feeling.*

He put his hand flat on the last line.

"That is the whole of it and I wrote it in forty days and the fortieth day was the nineteenth of April, and I have read it out in this kitchen because nine people are going to say one thing each and I have promised them that first."

## Ines

"The third one," said Ines Duarte. She had her sleeves turned up and she had not taken her coat off, the way she never does at that table.

"Go on."

"About four people in nine cannot name anybody." She said it without any weight, which is how she says the worst things. "I have audited about four thousand returns in eleven years and I have read the fourth block of a street in a keeper's hand four times a year for thirty years, and I have never once been able to make a person give me the name of the second person. They cannot. It is not withholding."

"It is that a lot of what happened to people in this city happened while they were the only person in the room."

"That is a fact about this city and not about the sentence."

"It is a fact about the sentence as well," said Ines Duarte. "It takes four in nine out. You have written a definition that throws away the majority of what people will bring you,"

"and I am not going to say that is wrong, because you said you would only write one and I said I would say one thing and it is this. Write the third one tighter and it throws away more. Leave it and it is generous. Those are the two ends of it and you have picked one, and I would have picked the same one, and I want it in the room that I would have picked it."

## Beatrix

Beatrix Halm did not come in off the step.

"You have used the phrase *what a person held*," she said, "and about two people in nine of what my round brings back have ever used that phrase in their lives."

She had her notebook shut under her arm and did not take it out.

"They bring back: he was hard, she was fair, the week was bad, the office was slow. Four hundred lines off a district round and not one figure on any of them and every one of them true, and if I had to put them into your first line about four of them would go in and the other three hundred and ninety-six would not, and I want to say that out loud in a kitchen once and never again."

"It is the correct word."

"I know it is," said Beatrix Halm. "I have got four hundred lines the correct word does not reach, and they are mine, and they are staying mine, and that is my one thing."

## Runa

"The second condition," said Runa Sato, "excludes about half of what happens in a lane."

She said it standing, with her coat on, because she had a round at four.

"Somebody can tell you exactly where they were on a morning and not one day, and it is the same morning every time it happens and they have been doing it for years, and your sentence throws that out because the second line wants a day. And it should, because a thing that cannot be placed in a year cannot be found again by anybody including the person who did it, and a register that takes it is a register of rumours."

"That is what I thought when I cut it."

"You thought it in March," said Runa Sato, "and you have been carrying it since March and you have written it down, and here is the rest of it and then I am going to a round: there are about eleven divisions' worth of people in this city who would be thrown out by that condition and not one of them is a liar."

## Nadia

"I have got nothing," said Nadia Ferrous.

Marguerite Vale did not look up.

"That is allowed," said the broker.

"It is not allowed, it is the truth and I am saying it and I have been not saying it for about nine days." Nadia Ferrous put her glass down on the table, which she had brought without being asked. "He has read out four lines and I have understood three of them and I have been in a lift shaft four nights a week for a year,"

"and if you told me that I had given four minutes to about nine hundred strangers in the cold for nothing then I would understand all four of them and I would not be able to tell you who the second person was."

She was not answered and she did not expect it.

"That is my one thing and it is that I have understood three of your four lines and I am one of the nine people who is going to try to break it, and I would like it noted that I did not get to the part that matters."

## Isolde

From the back of the stove, in a voice that does not carry.

"The fourth one."

"Go on."

"It is not on a page anywhere in this city," said Isolde Marchetti, "and about nine thousand people go under a wall in a day that has a column on it and every number on that wall is on the wall and not on a page, and I have been up a ladder painting the underside of that wall twice since January and there is a box under the step with the sheets in it,"

"and if a number is in a box under a step then it is on a page."

"It is on a page."

"It is on a page in a box under a step and not on anything a person can ask for," said Isolde Marchetti. "So that is a definition and not a rule and I am not complaining, I am telling you what the fourth line is worth, because in about four years that box is going to be cleared out"

"and there will be nothing in this city that can say what the number on a wall was on the morning a person did a thing, and then the fourth line is doing a lot of work."

## Petra

Petra Ilyin had her hands flat on the table in front of her and they were not working and she had not made them work in about a year and it was not mentioned.

"The third one," she said. "Somebody made me a promise once and she was alone in the room and there is not one thing I could enter against that, because there is no second person, and I have had two years to try to turn it into something with a day on it and I have not got it and I am not going to get it."

She looked at the four lines.

"And I am not going to ask you to put it in," said Petra Ilyin. "If you put that in then this is a register of promises and not of deeds, and there are about four of those in this city per person and none of them are worth entering. So the sentence is right about my door, and I am saying that I know it is right about my door and it cost me something, and that is my one thing, and I am not answering anybody."

## Tessa

"January," said Tessa Okonkwo.

She had her coat over the back of her chair and her sleeves down.

"I am not going to say the thing I said in January because I said it once and it was said in a room and it was said by the only person in this city who can take a piece of what a division takes when it goes to a door, and it has been written down in my own book on a night round and it does not need saying twice."

She turned her head about nine degrees.

"I am going to say the other thing and then I am going to stop. If your second line stands, then a person who cannot place a day is not in this register, and about a fifth of the people a division finds in this city cannot place a day either, and they are not lying and they are not stupid, and they are the ones who will get told that they have nothing, because they have nothing that can go in."

"" She put her hand on the table. "Write something on the sheet that says they have nothing in this register and not that they have nothing. That is all. That is my one thing."

## Marguerite

"A threshold is a floor," said Marguerite Vale.

The room went quiet in the way a room does.

"You have known that since about the fourteenth of March of the year before last and you have written it anyway and you have come to this table and told nine women, which is more than most men manage."

She turned the fourth line round and read it again. "In about four years some clerk is going to be asked what the number is in this column and he is going to find a threshold under it with a date on it and a man's handwriting on it and he is going to do exactly what this city has done every time for thirty years, which is use it."

"I know."

"You know and you have written it and you have put your hand under it," said Marguerite Vale, "which is the whole of what a broker understands about a man and I have not got an argument against you and I have got a warning and I am giving it in my own chair and I am not going to be thanked for it."

## The Last One

Agnete Lindqvist came in from the doorway and put her chair out from the table with the back of her leg, and sat down in it for the first time since the seventeenth of October of the year before last, and did not take her coat off.

"There are about four thousand of us and about four thousand of them have never been in a room that could do anything," she said.

"And you have written a sentence that takes four things at once, and about nine in ten of what any of them would bring you does not have all four, and they will bring it anyway, and it will go out at four on a sheet with an address at the head of every line and about two hundred people a week will read it in Cordwain and not one of them will be able to say that a thing was excluded."

"That is what I am going to say at four o'clock."

"You are going to say it at four o'clock and I am going to do the other thing," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I am going to be the first off this table."

"Not the Church, not a street, not a lot out of a roll. Me, at that counter, before the year is out, with one thing. And I am going to do it because if the Church fills that column over nine years then the column is the Church's, and the only way it stops being the Church's is for the first name in it to come out of a kitchen in Ferrier Street and be nobody's."

She was not answered.

"You will not be able to stop me and I would think less of you if you could," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and that is my one thing and I went last."

## What He Had Brought

He had it in the inside of his coat where the letter of the first of February had been for twenty months, and he took it out and put it on the table face up beside Beatrix Halm's notebook, and it was four lines and the fourth one had been cut three times.

"That is the ninth of March version and the eleventh of March version and the one after the eighteenth of April, and I have not kept the ones in between, because there were about thirty of them and they were all the same sentence with a different person taken out of it."

"" He put a finger on the fourth line. "Taking a person out of it is the whole of what I have been doing since February and I have got down to four lines and there is going to be one more of these before the end of the year and it is going to be worse than this one."

Nobody answered him and the rule held, and Agnete Lindqvist did not look up from her own hands.

## The Number He Said

He read the four lines out a third time and then he said the thing he had not said, and he said it without explaining it, and nine people let him say it and then the room said nothing at all for about a minute.

"Forty people have come to that door since the thirtieth of December. Four of them have brought a thing a definition could take, and one of those four is still not on a page and will not be before the year is out. Four out of forty is one in ten, and about nine in ten of what people bring to a counter like that one is a thing that happened to them and changed nothing that anybody can put a holding on."

He put his hand on the four lines.

"Nine in ten. That is the number it excludes and I am not going to explain it tonight and I would rather somebody found a mistake in it in four years than had it explained to them now."

## What The Room Had

They did not improve it. That was the arrangement and it held: nine women said nine things and not one of them said a word about whether the sentence was a good one, and he did not tell them that it was, and he was not thanked and did not want to be.

He could not leave the room. That had been true since the twenty-first of June of the year before last and it was true now, and it was the arrangement he had entered himself with thirty-eight words in it, and he would enter it again and he had said so once and was not going to say it twice in a kitchen.

At about half past ten he went down to the ranks wall with the four lines folded in his inside coat where the letter of the first of February had been, and there were about nine thousand people a day going under it and not one of them reading, and the second line was where it had been since the eighth of October a year and nine months ago, in the office's own hand, at the height of a name on a plate.

*unrecorded — carried by*

And under the last tile, on the stone, a white tile with his name on it in chalk and no figure whatever under it, and nothing of any kind under the name.

He stood there about two minutes.

The second line had a threshold under it now, and the threshold was four lines long and it was in his handwriting and it was going to go out at four in the afternoon in a clerk's hand, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain were going to read it and be able to do nothing at all with it except obey it.

The tile had nothing under it and it still had nothing under it and he still had not asked, and that was the other wall and he was the only man in this city standing in front of both of them.

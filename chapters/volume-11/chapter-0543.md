# A Division Reads A District

Sela Voel had been at the counter off a third court for about four minutes before he came into the room, and she had the day's page in front of her turned round to face her and her finger laid flat on the ninth line down.

She had carried the same folded paper for about three years now and she had not opened it.

"The ninth of July," she said. "I have not known what day of the week that is for three years and I have not cared and I am not going to start. It is the ninth. The street is yours and the number is yours and the hour is eight in the morning."

He put his coat over the back of the chair.

"It is not mine. There are four hundred and nineteen of them and not one of them is mine."

"I did not say it was yours," said Sela Voel. "I said the street is yours and the number is yours. The clerk read me the number out twice and then she read me the hour out twice and then she told me that I could read the rest of it myself if I wanted to, and I have wanted to."

Twenty-three days from the second of August, and from the first of July to this morning is fifty-four, and the ninth of July is in between them.

## What The Page Says

"There are four lines about a street in it," she said, "and the first one is a woman who came to a door and would not give her name and stood on the step for about eleven minutes in the cold and then went down and did not come back, and that is me and it is the twenty-ninth of March of the second year and I will swear to the hour in a room if anybody asks me to."

"That would be the third condition."

"It would be the third condition for me and it would be the third condition for my deed if my deed were a thing I could bring," said Sela Voel. "And I cannot bring my deed here and I never could, and I have known that since the thirtieth of September of the second year and it is the reason there is a name in your book and no deed."

She turned the page round and pushed it two inches towards him.

"Read the fourth line of that entry and then tell me what it is for."

He read it.

It was a division's account of a step at about eight in the morning on a street four streets off the yard end, and it reported what a woman had said in the order it arrived, and the last thing on it was the woman saying that she had carried a piece of paper for a long time and had brought it everywhere and had never been able to hand it to anybody.

"That is what was felt," said Sela Voel. "That is what she said and they wrote it down and it is in a book. And you wrote a sentence in a kitchen in March of the second year and the fourth of the four lines says that it is not on a page anywhere in this city."

"Yes."

"It was on a page at about half past six in the morning on the ninth of July and it is on one now, and it is on one because nine people you asked for went and stood on that step," said Sela Voel. "And I want to know what is left of my half page, and I am not going to be told by a clerk, so I am asking the man."

## What Is Left Of A Half Page

Her half page had three of the four conditions on it and she had carried it for about three years and had been told twice by two different women at two different desks exactly which three.

The first changed what she held. The second happened on a day she can name and in a place she can name. The fourth said it was not on a page anywhere in this city and for three years that was the one that was doing the work, because it was the one that kept a piece of paper in a coat for three years from becoming evidence.

The third wanted another person, and the woman who could not put her shoes on in the second month of the first year had not been found and was never going to be found.

"It is dead," said Lin Hao. "The fourth. It went on the ninth of July and it is not coming back."

"Whose fault."

"Mine."

"I am going to ask you that in about four years and I am not going to get an answer then either," said Sela Voel. "So write it in your own book and I will come and see whether it is in there."

He wrote it in his own book and she watched him do it and did not read it upside down.

"Now," she said. "What is left."

"What is left is a first and a second and a third that cannot be met, and a name you entered three years ago, and that is the whole of it and it was less than that a month ago."

"That is what I came to hear," said Sela Voel. "I did not come to be angry and I am not going to be angry at a desk with a clerk in it. I came because the two of us are the only people in this city who are interested in that piece of paper and neither of us can do one single thing with it."

She put her hand flat on the page and did not take it away.

"It is in two books," she said. "One of them has my name in it. The other one has the step in it. And I am not allowed to put them together and neither are you, and I have gone eleven minutes to this counter to be told a thing I already worked out at about six this morning."

## Nine In A Room Over A Chandler's

They were in there at about half past six in the evening and all nine of them, and it was the first time since the thirtieth of March that the whole division had been in the same room with the round off their own district and over.

Ilma Reese put the district book on the table open at a page with a corner turned, the way you put a thing down when you have been proving for nine years that you never lose a street.

"That one," she said, "has been counted four times in thirty years. A census. A drain inspection after the third winter it flooded. A fire survey. And the roll. Four numbers, four pieces of paper, four different offices, and every one of them was right, and every one of them is a number of households."

"And what has it got now."

"It has got ninety-one lines in a book off a third court and about a hundred and ten people in it who are not a household," said Ilma Reese. "Some of them are two households. One of them is a household that has not existed since the seventh year and there are two people in it. And none of them were asked for by anybody who had come from this room."

She put her hand on the book and did not close it.

"That street is not ours. It is four streets off the yard end and it was read by a division that had never walked it, and I want it understood that I am not complaining, because I asked for the round. I want it understood that I asked for it and I have been sitting here for two hours finding out what it costs and the cost is that I cannot walk a street in this city any more without knowing what a stranger wrote down on it."

## What They Said About It

Nine things, one each, and nobody answered anybody, and that is the rule of the room and has been for two years and is carried and named by nobody.

Tenna Vole had been ill on the last morning and had signed for nothing and would not hear of it being mentioned. Coen Ardry said that a division that goes out on somebody else's street comes back with a sentence about somebody else's street and that this division has four hundred lines about its own and about ninety from the other place and that the ninety are better writing than the four hundred and he would like that recorded as an insult to his own street.

Sef Ossick said the hours on the ninety-one are useless, because the round went out at five in the morning. Everything anybody says to a stranger at five in the morning is said about that hour.

Marget Halle said the opposite and said it twice and Duri Peck let her, and Annis Cray said that two of them together were right about one street and wrong about every other street in this city, and Toller Vine asked her the same question twice about four seconds apart because he cannot hear anything above a certain pitch.

Beatrix Halm had the three pages on the table and did not put her hand on them.

"Four hundred and nineteen," she said. "That is what is in the book and four hundred of it is mine and nineteen of it is off a street I have never walked, and every one of the four hundred and nineteen has an hour against it and not one of them has a figure on it anywhere."

"What do you want done with them."

"Nothing," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the answer and it is the same answer I gave you on a roof in February. They are not mine to do anything with. They are in a book and they are in the order they arrived and they may not be improved afterwards."

"The second condition of your sentence is going to throw out about four in nine of them for the rest of time, and the fourth condition threw out all of them on the ninth of July, and there are two reasons to be pleased about that and I can only find one of them."

"What is the one you can find."

"That they cannot be got at," she said. "Nobody can go and take one of them out and improve it, and nobody can go and use one of them against the woman it is about, because the office that holds it has no money and no instrument and the only thing it can do is a pen, and that is not much of a threat to anybody."

"And the one you cannot find."

She turned her head a little way so that he was on her left.

"That about nine hundred doors was worth it," she said. "I have been a round for nine years and I do not know and I am not going to be told, and if somebody comes to me in four years and asks me whether it was worth it I am going to have to say I do not know, and that is the first thing I have not known in nine years."

*Twenty-fifth of August of the fourth year. A street off the yard end counted four times in thirty years and read once in ninety days.*

*A woman at a counter at four minutes past with a piece of paper she has carried for three years and cannot hand in, and the fourth of my four lines died on the ninth of July because of a satchel I was handed on the eleventh.*

*And I said the fault is mine to a woman who came eleven minutes to be told it, and she is going to come back in four years to ask whether it is in here, and I wrote it in here in front of her.*

*Nine of them in a room and nine things and not one answer, and one of the nine said she cannot find the second reason it was worth it and I have not got the second reason either.*

# Two Names And No Deeds

She came at about twenty past ten on the morning of the thirtieth of December, which is the last working day of the quarter, and she came to the counter off a third court and not to the room, and she had not told him she was coming and he had known since the nineteenth of July of the second year that she would.

He had said at that kitchen that she would be the first off her own table, at that counter, before the year was out.

There are about four thousand of them in the Church of the Quiet Number and she is the chaplain and she had spent four years making sure that no person in that yard is ever looked at by the sort of person who counts faces. She had told a kitchen off Ferrier Street in about nine sentences that she would be one of nine and would go last and would not answer anybody.

She came first.

## What She Brought

"One thing," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I told you I would be the first and I am the first and I have brought the one thing I have, and I am going to say what it is and then the clerk will decide about it, because that is the arrangement you made in a kitchen and a quarter-end is not the place to change it."

She put a sheet of paper on the counter.

It was a copy. That is the first thing about it. It was a copy of something, made by somebody in an office that keeps copies, and it carried a date at the top in the numbering of that office and a file mark and the stamp of the thing.

It was four lines long and it said that the writer declined, on the ninth of October of the first year, to claim for the Church of the Quiet Number the words painted at the foot of the ranks wall, and it said the writer's office, and it said the reason in one sentence: that the words were nobody's and that a body which had four thousand members had no business being the one that claimed a thing nobody had asked them for.

"It is in the record," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have it certified because I did not want a piece of paper. I have wanted it in four places since the ninth of October of last year and the last one is a building in which people take things for granted."

## The Fourth Condition

Wenna Raut had the sheet before the third word of it was out of Agnete Lindqvist's mouth and she read it twice, which is what she does, both times at the same speed.

She put it down and looked up.

"Three of them," she said.

"Read them to me."

"I do not read them out," said Wenna Raut. "I enter them."

She turned the sheet round so that Agnete Lindqvist could see it.

"Your first condition is met. A body that declines to claim something changed what it held, and held is what it kept, and it kept the wall." She put one finger beside the second. "The second is met. There is a day and there is a place and the day is the ninth of October at the foot of the ranks wall."

Her finger did not move for a second.

"The third is met. There were about forty of you standing behind you and every one of them can be named from a roll and I have got the roll in this city somewhere and it is not mine to use. The fourth is not met."

"That is the whole of it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "It is on a page."

"It is on a page," said Wenna Raut, "with a file mark on it and a certification and a stamp, because you wanted it there. The fourth condition says it is not on a page anywhere in this city, and it is on four pages in one building, and you put it there on purpose so that nobody could ever come and say the Church had claimed it."

She looked up again.

"So I cannot enter the deed."

## What The Clerk Was Given

"And what can you enter," said Lin Hao.

"The name." She was already writing. "The date. The street. And one sentence I am permitted to add and could not improve."

She wrote it and read it back at the speed she reads everything, and the sentence was this:

*The Register of Unrecorded Merit has entered the name of a person and has not entered a deed, and the reason is that of four conditions given, three were met, and the fourth was not given. It was not given on purpose, by the person who would have been the one to give it.*

She squared the page.

"That is you and it is in the book and it goes out at four with the rest of it."

## What Agnete Lindqvist Said About It

"That is twice," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"Twice."

"Two names in a book and not one deed. A woman who could not write down why she gave the bread away, and me, because I wrote down why I would not have it."

She put her hand flat on the counter.

"I told a kitchen off Ferrier Street in July that the first four thousand names in a book decide what the book is for. I said that if the Church filled that column over nine years it would be the Church's. I have come here first to find out whether it was going to belong to anybody."

She looked at the sheet going out at four on the wall.

"It is going to be a book about two women who could not write down the interesting half," she said. "That is not a failure of the four lines. The four lines are the correct four lines. I said that in a kitchen and I said it before I knew that the first two people through that door in four years would both fail on the same thing and one of them would fail on purpose."

## The Sentence For Four O'Clock

"You said on the nineteenth of July that you had something for four o'clock," he said.

"I did."

"Then say it to me now and I will tell you whether it is going on."

Agnete Lindqvist turned her head about nine degrees, which she has done at two tables and one kitchen and not once anywhere else in four years.

"The Register of Unrecorded Merit has entered the name of a person and has not entered a deed, and it has been open twenty-three months, and about a thousand people have come to that door."

She was not hurrying.

"The definition went out on the sheet of the thirtieth of June, which is six months ago, and every one of the four lines of it has been off a public desk in this city since then. About four thousand of us have had six months with four conditions and we have all of us understood them."

"Because they were written to be understood by people who are going to be angry about them."

She put her hand down again.

"So the hundred of my own who are going to come are not going to arrive with things they think are good. They are going to arrive having read four conditions and picked the ones that pass."

"And the nine in ten they leave behind them are not going to be left behind by accident. They are going to be read out of the definition by four thousand people who know exactly what the door wants. Every one of those nine is going to hear about itself from somebody in a yard."

"Four lines cannot do that," said Lin Hao. "Four lines do not tell anybody what to bring."

"Four lines tell anybody what to bring," said Agnete Lindqvist. "They are the only instructions this city has ever published about how to be owed nothing, and they are going out at four on a sheet with an address at the head of every line, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read them."

She let that stand in the room for about four seconds.

"That is what I am saying at four o'clock and I am saying it before the quarter turns rather than after, and you may put it on the line if you like and I would think less of you if you improved it."

## Four O'Clock

The thirtieth of December is the last working day of the quarter. The sheet goes out at four in the afternoon, it carries an address at the head of every line, and on any working afternoon of this city a person may stand at a public desk and read it.

At the bottom of it, in a clerk's hand, under an address off a third court, it said *Register of Unrecorded Merit. Eighth quarter. Entered: five.*

Under the five, three lines in the office's own hand and not improved by anybody: what the office had, which was two reports of one act by the only person it may ask and one refusal; what the office had entered, which was two names and not one deed; and, entered at about eleven in the morning by a chaplain who had been told in a kitchen in July that she would be the first and had never once been argued with about it, a third name.

And above it, for the first time in the life of that office, a number about the office itself, and the number was not what anybody expected, and the number was not wrong.

She read the day's page from the top because a page is read from the top, and she came to the Register's line at about half past two and read that twice before she read anything under it, which is what she does.

Under the disposition, in a clerk's hand, sits a figure this office has carried for twenty-three months and has never once discussed in public. She wrote the address at the head of the line and the figure under it in about eleven seconds and did not look at it while she was writing it.

Twelve lines down there was a street with six words under a figure in a small level hand and a keeper's own name under the six words, and there has not been a certificate behind any of it since the fourteenth of March of the year before last, and there is not going to be one.

Four lines below that was a landlord's return off Lamplit Row carrying a figure that had come off a chalk in a lane four streets away, and there was a line under it entered on the thirtieth of November by a clerk who was permitted to add exactly one sentence and could not have improved it.

And then the Register's line, and under it two names that were not deeds, and a quarter's count of five, and at the end of the whole page the fact that a number of about a thousand people had walked past that door in twenty-three months and that the counter on the corridor that records it is in a pool of eleven rooms and gets emptied on the first of the year whether anybody read it or not.

## What Was In The Notebook

He wrote it up at about four in the morning of the first day of the next year at the top of the third flight, in the fourth column, which was one he had ruled the night before and had not told anybody about.

*Two names. Neither a deed. One of them declined on purpose because she wanted it permanent.*

And under it:

*A man in a delivery coat has been writing figures in chalk in a lane for two years because he could not reach this office. I have been told this by four people in two days and every one of them was right, and one of them asked me for it.*

He did not write the notebook entry for Corvin Ashby's wife, whose name he had not been given, and whose thirty days ran out with the door being opened on the first of January by a man who had been right about everything for two years and had had one fact too many in November.

# A Grain To Stand In A Line

He went down the hill at about eight in the evening on the twenty-eighth of February because he had been awake since two and had nothing to do with the hours until eleven, and he took no coat over his coat and no plate out of his coat and told nobody, and the lane off Cordwain was about four feet wide where it is four feet wide and eleven feet wide where the yard wall has gone.

There were seven people in it and three of the five stools were taken and the slate was already up against the wall with its figure on it in four-inch chalk.

It had been open every morning since the evening of the second of October and the first morning was the third. Twenty-eight days left in October including the third, thirty each of November, December and January, and twenty-eight days of February. A hundred and forty-six, and it had not been shut on one morning since the third of October.

The man in the delivery coat put his hand out.

## What A Grain Is

Lin Hao put the edge of the plate down into it.

Not pressed into a socket. A reading is pressed into a socket, and a reading is a thing that happens to a person in a room with a clock on it.

What happened at the wall end of that lane was that a man with chalk on his right thumb took a grain of merit off a metal plate in the open air with his fingers, and chalked the grain onto the back of the hand of a woman about eleven in front of him, and said thank you, and meant it.

Eleven in the mouth. Nothing at the end of a week. That is what a grain weighs, and Emil Brack had told him the true weight of it on the ninth and had not been wrong about that either.

And the price of it, to a man whose plate is white with a name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere, and who has a guaranteed subsistence under him for the rest of his life because he lodged a whole standing at a public counter in a queue with a pen, was nothing whatever.

He thought about that on the pavement for about nine seconds and then stopped thinking about it, because thinking about it was the only thing about that grain that was going to cost anybody anything and it was costing him.

## Nine Other People

He stood at the back and got into it at about a quarter past eight. Behind him a woman came in about ten minutes later with a shawl over her head and stood about four feet off his shoulder and did not speak for an hour and ten minutes.

There was a man with a bicycle. There were two women who had come together and did not stay together. There was a boy who had clearly been sent and was going to be collected. There was a woman with a wrapped parcel that she held against her chest the entire time and who did not look at anybody. There was an old man who came at about ten and knew two of them and said so.

Nine other people, and he made ten, and by eleven the lane held about thirty and the rest of them were standing against the chandler's shutter with their backs to a wall.

Four hours, from about a quarter past eight to about half past midnight. That is the whole of what four hours is in that lane: it is a lane, it is cold, and there is nothing to be at the front of.

At about eleven a lamp came on inside the shutter and stayed on. At about half past eleven a man came down the lane with a jug on a tray and sold what was in it for a coin to about nine people, and did not offer it to him, and did not offer it to the woman in the shawl either, and neither of them said a word about it.

The woman with the parcel went at about eleven. The boy was collected at about half past eleven by a man who would not come into the lane and stood at the mouth of it and said one word, and the word was the boy's name, and the boy went.

At about midnight the cold changed from a cold you notice to a cold you have stopped noticing, and about six people left, and all five stools were taken by people who had been there since about ten.

## What He Thought About For Four Hours

Nothing, most of it. He has checked that twice since and it has not improved.

He thought about the cistern on the second floor, which has not run since the sixth of January, and which he will be able to say has not run to a stranger in about four years, and the stranger will be able to go and look at it, because a cistern is a thing that either runs or does not.

He thought about the strap on the fifth book, and about a plate at the end of a chain that gets scraped and cut from the old one with nothing added to it.

He thought about a woman with a bucket on the second floor of a branch office who has been there every night since the eleventh of October and has never asked him anything.

And about a third of the way through the four hours he worked out what he had actually done, which was that he had walked down a hill and paid a grain and stood in the cold to find out whether a figure nobody can check still gets made.

The answer was yes. Twice a morning, in four-inch letters, in about nine districts now. It had not required him and it never would.

## The Woman In The Shawl

At about ten past nine she said it without turning round.

"You have not asked why anybody is here."

"No."

"You are going to stand here four hours, and then a man is going to write a number on a slate, and it is going to be yours, and after that you cannot say you were not here."

"That is all this is. It is agreeing to it. Not to him. To the being in it. Every person in this lane has agreed to be in it and two of them will tell you it was a good idea and the rest of them will not."

"What is it worth."

"He says what it is worth and he is the only one who knows," said the woman in the shawl.

"He told me when I paid. He said one for standing and one for the hour, and he has never given anybody more than nine. I asked him how a man gets to nine and he said a man is not worth more than nine whatever he has done, and I have been standing in this city for nineteen years and I have never been told anything so short."

"You are not talking about him."

"I am not," she said. "He is talking about himself. He does not think anybody is worth more than nine and he has never once pretended otherwise, and that is the only reason I am standing in a lane at ten at night, and it is not a good enough reason, and here I am."

She was quiet for about eleven minutes after that.

"You are the only one of them who has not asked me what I am doing here," she said. "I have paid a grain in nine lanes in this city over about four months and in six of them somebody asked me inside a week. You are going to ask me. You have not asked me yet and you are going to, and I am telling you now that I do not know, and that the not knowing is the only reason I am still standing in this one."

## Half Past Midnight

At about half past twelve he came along the line with the chalk.

He did not ask anybody anything. He went to the man with the bicycle and wrote a four and a name beside it. He went to the woman with the parcel and wrote a seven and a name. He went to the boy, who had been back an hour with a man at the mouth of the lane, and wrote a two and no name, because none had been given.

"That is fair," he said. "Two for standing and nothing for the hour, because you have not been here for an hour."

He came to the woman in the shawl and wrote an eight and a name, and she watched him do it and did not look pleased about it.

And then he came to Lin Hao and put the chalk against the slate and said, "Name."

"Lin Hao."

The man wrote it in chalk about two inches high beside where the figure goes, which is lower down than the figure and smaller, and he wrote it without any change of pace at all and without looking at the name twice.

There are about two hundred names on that slate in four months and not one of them is a person he is going to know from a word, and one of them has been a woman who runs a bakery.

And then he wrote the figure, and the figure was nine.

## Nine

"That is the top of it," said Emil Brack. "One for standing and one for the hour, and the table stops at nine. You have been here since about a quarter past eight, so you get all of it."

"Is nine right."

"Nine is what my table says a person is worth for four hours of standing still in a cold lane. My table is mine and it is not checkable by anybody including me, and I have never once had anybody argue with it, and I want to be honest with you about why."

"Why."

"Because nobody argues with it, and there is nothing to argue with."

He capped the chalk. "If I put eleven on, you could stand there and say that is too much, and how would either of us know. So I put a number that is small, and I tell everybody standing in this lane that the number is mine, and if it is too low then that is my error and not a lie, and there is a difference between those two things and it is the only difference I have got."

Lin Hao looked at the two-inch-high name in chalk on a slate propped against a yard wall off Cordwain, and it was his name, and no line of any kind under it.

He did not say the figure was wrong. He did not take the grain back. He stood there for another two minutes while the lane emptied, and then he went up the hill, which takes about twenty minutes, and was at the branch on the twenty-eighth at about ten to eleven for a shift that ran until half past five.

## What He Did Not Do About It

There were two things he could have done about the name and he did not do either of them.

He could have told the man in the delivery coat that the figure on the slate was the wrong kind of figure, and not one instrument in this city could have put it right.

The man would have listened to him and agreed with him and written a different number up, and then about a hundred and sixty people who had paid a grain would have found out that the numbers on that slate can be changed by a man who comes up the hill, and the lane would have gone in about four days.

And he could have said the number is mine because I am the man at the wall, which was not true, and which would have turned a lane into something else entirely for about a day.

He did the first thing's opposite and the second thing's opposite.

He went up the hill and did the shift, and he was at rank one in a washroom until half past five with a white plate in his coat and no figure cut into it anywhere. And there was a name in chalk about two inches high on a slate off Cordwain with a nine beside it, and he had done nothing at all about either, and none of it could go in a minute.

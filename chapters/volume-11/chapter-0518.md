# The Woman Who Is Recused

He came up the two flights at about a quarter to ten in the morning on the thirtieth of June with the four lines of the definition in his coat and a slip of paper about the size of a quarter of a page in his hand, and there is not one form in this building for what he came to do and there has never been one and he looked for one for four days.

He stood about nine feet off the door with his back to the rail and nobody in the corridor said anything to him in the whole of the time he was standing there.

Alma Quayle was at the desk inside with her division's paperwork on her knees, and she had been in that chair at the same hour on every working morning for five hundred and fifty-eight days.

"Five hundred and fifty-eight days," she said, before he had said anything. "Eighteen days left in December after the twelfth, thirty each of January, February, March, April, May and June, and a year of three hundred and sixty in the middle of it. Three hundred and sixty and a hundred and ninety-eight. Sit down."

"I am not going to sit down," said Lin Hao. "I have got about ten seconds."

## Four Seconds

He put the slip on the desk face up.

There were eleven words on it in his own hand.

*This recusal is lifted by me now and by nobody else.*

And under the eleven words, his name, and the date, and nothing else, because there is not one sheet in this building for lifting a recusal and a slip with eleven words on it and a man's name is the only shape the act has ever had.

He wrote it in about four seconds. He put the pen down. He did not explain it and he did not ask her to sign anything, because she cannot be asked to sign a thing that lifts her.

## The Two Flights

He came up them on his own account with a slip in his hand and no coat over his coat, and there is a woman on the second landing who has been going up and down them since the spring and who looks at everybody's hands.

At the top he stopped and put the slip flat against the wall for about a second, the way a man straightens a page he is about to hand over, and found that he had read the eleven words four times on the way up and had been holding them the wrong way round, and turned it over so that the words would be the right way up for a person standing on the other side of a desk.

That is the whole of what he did before he said anything. It took about four seconds.

## What She Did With It

Alma Quayle read it twice.

The first time took her about a second and a half and the second took her about two, and then she put it down square on the desk with the words facing up and her hands flat either side of it, and she did not say thank you and she was not going to.

"I want three things said and I am going to say them quickly because you have said that you have ten seconds and you have already used nine."

"Say them."

"One. I did not ask." She did not look up. "Two. For five hundred and fifty-eight days this division has routed anything hard past this door and every clerk on this floor has been told to do it and not one of them has ever asked me whether I wanted to be past it. That ends at about four this afternoon and not one of them will enjoy it."

"And three."

"Three is that you cannot give it back," said Alma Quayle. "There is not one sheet and there is not one form and there is a slip on my desk with a date on it, and you wrote it and nobody can un-write it, including you, including me, and I want that said out loud in this room by both of us before four o'clock this afternoon."

"Neither of us can give it back."

"Then we have both said it." She put the slip into the drawer under her own paperwork, which was where she put everything. "What are your four lines."

## She Read It In The Room

He put them on the desk and she read them standing up, and she read them at the speed of a person who has classified about nine hundred words a week for thirty years, and she got to the end in about eleven seconds.

"That is a definition."

"That is a description of what it is for," he said. "You told me in March that the two are different things and that the second one would go round nine divisions for four years."

"It is still that." She squared the four lines. "It does not say what a word means. It says what the office is for."

"Then take it in the room," said Alma Quayle, "and do not take it out of the room, and here is why and it is the only instruction I am going to give you about a classification in your life: you have had four hundred and sixty-eight days in a corridor deciding whether to spend me and you have not decided, and a definition that waits for a decision is a definition that will be written by whoever is left."

He did not say anything for about four seconds.

"How long."

"Nine days if you go back with it now and do not touch it again," said Alma Quayle, "and a year if you go back to your washrooms and think about it for a month, because a month from now there will be a better sentence and the better sentence will exclude the hard case, and the hard case is the only part of it anybody will need."

## The Cost

She said the rest of it looking at the drawer and not at him.

"You know what this building is," said Alma Quayle. "You have known it since you were nineteen and you have never once said it to me in this room and I have never once said it in this room, and there is a room on this floor with no window in it and there is a file that is kept in it,"

"and I have been able to classify it from about nine feet away for two years without ever being inside the room, and I have not touched it and I am not going to."

He said nothing at all.

"That is what you have just done," she said. "You have put me back on a record in a building where that file is, at fifty-seven, without asking, and I would have said no to a request and I would have said yes to this and there is a difference between those two things and you have taken the one that works on you."

"I know what it costs you."

"You know what it costs me," said Alma Quayle. "You do not know what it costs the nine clerks on this floor from four o'clock this afternoon, and neither do I, and we will find out together and I am not going to be grateful and I am not going to be diminished, and if you come up here in a month and find me ordinary about it you should be more frightened than if you find me like this."

## Nine Days

He went down two flights with nothing in his hand, which was the first time in about eleven years he had come up them and left with less than he brought.

At about four o'clock the chair that Alma Quayle had not sat in for five hundred and fifty-eight days was occupied, and the three names on the door for the week came down and a fourth went up, and in the first four days she classified eleven words belonging to four different divisions and refused four of them, and refused them out loud, at her desk, in front of the clerks who had been routing them past it since December.

He heard about that from a man with a title, on a stair, on the eleventh of July, and what the man with a title said was: "You have handed this city a classifier and you did it in four seconds on a piece of paper, and I have been drafting procedure for nineteen years and I could not have made it smaller."

"I know," said Lin Hao.

## The Room At The End Of The Corridor

He did not go down and get his shift.

He went down to the floor below the Wing and along about forty feet of corridor that he had not walked in about two years, and he stopped at a door with no window in it, and he stood there.

He did not knock and he did not put a hand on it and he did not ask anybody anything, and there is a rule in this city about a man who opens a thing that is kept rather than a thing he is keeping, and he knew the rule in about four seconds and stood still for about a minute and a half.

Somebody inside was moving a chair, once, and then stopped.

He went up to a branch office off a third court at about eleven and cleaned three floors of washrooms until half past five and gave three women on the rota nothing whatever to do and one of them, who has known him for four years and is not a fool, looked at him about nine times and did not ask him one question, which is a thing that woman does.

## What Closed

There is a thing a man can decide once and then be the kind of person who has decided it, and he had decided it on the twenty-first of June of the year before last with thirty-eight words that he entered himself, and he had decided it again on the thirtieth of September of last year with one sentence in a room with two chairs in it.

And on the thirtieth of June of the second year he gave up the last one, and it was the only one left that had cost nothing.

The option of never spending another woman's safety had been open since about the twelfth of December of the year before last, and he had kept it open for five hundred and fifty-eight days, and it had been the one piece of standing a man at rank one had left in a city where everything else he owned had been given away at a counter in ninety seconds.

It shut on a slip the size of a quarter of a page, in about four seconds, in a corridor nine feet wide, with a woman at fifty-seven who had not asked to be on anything and who was on it now and who was not going to thank him and was not going to be diminished and had told him to be frightened if she was ordinary.

And somewhere two floors under a rail, in a room with no window, a file that he had not opened in about two years had acquired a classifier, and that was the price and he had known the price before he picked up the pen.

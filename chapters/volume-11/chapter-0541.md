# The First Morning Of The Sweep

Halvard Senn put a satchel on the counter of the room off a third court at about a quarter to ten on the morning of the eleventh of July and did not take his coat off, and nobody in that room had ever seen the inside of it.

He had been carrying it since the thirtieth of March. Ninety days is three months of thirty days each, and about nine hundred pages, and every one of them up a same stair and on the same two buses and into the same room, and a division that goes to about nine hundred steps and comes back with paper is not a division that has read anything, it is a division that has brought something home.

Ten days from the first of July to this morning.

## What Was In The Satchel

He untied it on the table and put the pages down in four stacks and did not look at any of them.

"Nine hundred and about nine pages," he said. "I have not counted them twice and I am not going to. I counted them about once a fortnight because I did not want to know."

"Where are the divisions' returns."

"On the bottom, in their own hands, nine of them, and eight of the nine have got their officer's name on the top of the sheet and one has not," said Halvard Senn. "That one is Tenna Vole's and it is correct because she was ill on the last morning and her officer signed it for her on the twenty-eighth of June and both of them know that."

He squared the four stacks against the edge of the table, which is not a thing a man does with a thing he cannot hand over.

"The middle two are the accounts."

## Four Hundred And Nineteen

"There were nine hundred doors," he said. "At eight hundred and forty-one of them somebody said something. Four hundred and nineteen of them somebody said a second thing, and that is the number of accounts, and the number of doors is the number of doors, and I have seen nine people get those two numbers into the same sentence and I want it written down that they are not the same number."

"Write it down."

"I have written it down," said Halvard Senn. "On a tally board, at four in the morning, at the bottom, under your name. In my hand this time, because I have got a hand and you are the only man in this city who ever thought of giving the paper to the writer."

He did not sit down.

"An account is a page where a person told a division what they did," he said. "It has an hour against it and no figure on it anywhere. Not one of the four hundred and nineteen has a figure on it. I looked at every one of them over ninety days on trains and on buses and up a stair, which I did because I had nothing else to do and because it seemed like the thing to do with them."

"There is nothing wrong with that."

"There is one thing wrong with it and it is not mine to correct, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go and stand in the corridor," said Halvard Senn. "Four hundred and nineteen people have told nine strangers what they did, in their own words, and every one of those words has gone on a page, and pages are the thing your four conditions are made out of. Not one of those four hundred and nineteen can now satisfy your fourth line."

He stopped.

"I have been the officer of a division for nineteen years and I am not telling you what to do about it. I am telling you that I carried it."

## The Desk

Wenna Raut had the day's page open before he had finished tying the satchel, and she read the top of it, which is what she does.

The office had been open forty-one months and ten days on this morning: forty-one months is a thousand two hundred and thirty days, and a thousand two hundred and forty days is this morning counted off the first of February of the first year, and ten days over.

There are two things on a desk in that room and there have been two things on it since the first of February of the first year. There is a book with a strap across it. And there is a drawer in the desk under the book, and that drawer has been open and empty since the fourth of February of the first year, and it has been opened about four times in three years and nobody has ever put anything in it.

"The rule is what it is," he said. "A clerk may only enter, and cannot improve what she is given, and cannot be asked to hurry."

"Yes."

"And none of those four hundred and nineteen is a deed."

"None of them is a deed," said Wenna Raut. "First condition, some of them. Second, about four in nine, because a person on a step can place a street and a season and very little else. Third, about four in nine again, and for the same reason. Fourth."

She put her finger on the page.

"The fourth is not met by any one of the four hundred and nineteen and it is not met because of what happened this morning at about a quarter to ten."

## What The Office Does With A Return

He read the top of the day's page while she wrote and it was nine o'clock and the room was cold in the way that room is cold.

*Register of Unrecorded Merit. The eleventh of July of the fourth year. Received: nine hundred pages and about nine pages, carried ninety days by the officer of a division, handed over at a quarter to ten.*

*Entered: none.*

He read the second line twice.

"None," he said.

"None is a figure and it is in my hand," said Wenna Raut. "I entered the receipt and I entered the number of pages and I entered what it was and who brought it, and those are the things a clerk is given. The accounts themselves I enter as they go, one at a time, and each one gets a line, and each line says what the person said and the hour they said it at."

"And under a heading that means deeds."

"Under the heading the book has."

"Right."

"That is what this office does with a return," said Wenna Raut. "I am telling you what it does and not what anybody intended. You asked a Bureau to go to about nine hundred steps and read what people did that had never been written down, and the Bureau went and read it, and it came back in a satchel, and there is one instrument in this building and it is me and it is a pen."

She squared the page.

"Nobody in this room intended to put four hundred and nineteen people's own words under a heading that means deeds. That is what happened anyway and it took ninety days and a satchel and me and a pen and I am not going to have it improved."


She got through nine of them before the middle of the morning. She read each one twice at the same speed both times, which is her one habit, and she entered it as it was given, and twice she stopped and asked him one question about a word and both times the word went in unchanged.

The fourth of the nine was about nine lines long and it had a woman in it who has dug a street. The name on that line was not given to the division and the division was not permitted to ask for it, and the line went into the book in nine lines with no name against it.

"Read it to me."

"No," said Wenna Raut.

She squared the page and turned it round so that it was facing him and not her, which is the first time in four years she has done that with anything she had entered.

"I read it twice before I entered it and I entered it exactly as it was given to me, and it has no name on it because I was not given one," said Wenna Raut. "If I put one on it out of my own head then I am a second office and this one has enough in it already."

"You did not ask me that question for yourself."

"You did not ask me that question for yourself," she said. "You asked it because you have read the ninth line down three times since the seventh of July and you are waiting for it to be somebody."

"Who is it."

"I do not know and I am not going to have a way of finding out, and that is not a fault in the book on this table, it is the only thing it has ever been good at."

He did not ask a second time.

## What Cannot Be Undone

Nothing in this city will strike an entry out of a book, which is a thing he has known since the second year and has never had cause to be grateful for.

By the middle of the afternoon there were about ninety lines in it and the figure under the heading had not moved by one, because not one of the four hundred and nineteen is a deed, and there is no heading on that page for a thing a person told a stranger on a step.

He went down the stair at about four and out into Cordwain with nothing in his coat except a plate and a notebook and the piece of paper he had written the last four lines on, and the sky was the colour it goes at that hour in that month, and there were about two hundred people a week in this street reading a page that said *entered: eight hundred and forty-one* and were right about what it meant.

*Eleventh of July of the fourth year. A satchel on the counter at a quarter to ten and nine hundred pages out of it, carried ninety days by a man who was not thanked for it and did not ask.*

*Four hundred and nineteen accounts and eight hundred and forty-one doors and those are not the same number and I have written both.*

*Entered this morning: none. Not one of them is a deed. The fourth line of my own sentence is what stops every one of them and it stops them because of the satchel and not because of anything anybody did at a door.*

*So the round cost that office the one condition the office is built out of. Not one of the people it went to can bring me a deed now, and it did that on the thirtieth of March when I wrote four lines on a piece of a bin-room tally board and did not know it.*

*And there is a drawer under that book that has been open since the fourth of February of the first year and I want something in it that says what a person is supposed to do with nine hundred pages of a thing nobody asked to be written down, and there is nothing in that drawer and there was nothing in it at ten to eleven this morning and there will be nothing in it tomorrow.*

*And I want it more than I wanted any of the four things I have spent in four years, and I have not got one grain to spend it on.*

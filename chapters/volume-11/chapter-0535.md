# Nine In Her Division

She was under the ranks wall at half past four on the morning of the twenty-eighth of January of the fourth year with the paste brush boy gone and the wall about nine feet off the ground and about nine thousand people a day going under it, and she had her notebook shut under her arm and she had her deaf ear to the wall so that she could hear the rest of the city going past.

He came off shift at half past five and she was still there.

"You have been under it an hour."

"I have been under it about an hour and eleven minutes," said Beatrix Halm. "It is the only place in this city where you can stand still and hear the whole of the Ferrier line going past and not one word of anybody in it."

## The Nine

There are nine of them and there have been nine for about two years, and they are not a shift and they are not on any establishment, and they walk a district because somebody has to.

He has heard the division described nine times in four years and had never once seen the nine of them together, and he saw them together on the twenty-eighth of January at about ten past six in a room over a chandler's off Ferrier Street where the first round goes out at five.

Nine of them and a table and eleven chairs and a stove with a hole in it. A signal operator's room is not a room for meetings; it is a room where nine people find out what a street is, once a week, and then write it down, and the writing is what any of them will use to decide what the street is next time.

He counted them twice because it seemed like the sort of number a person would want counted twice.

The oldest had been doing it about nine years. Two of the nine were out on the round that morning. One of them was a man of about forty who had been in the division about four years and who had a way of standing that put his good ear forward and had done it since before she met him.

## What A Division Is, And Is Not

"A division is not an instrument," said Beatrix Halm. "People keep saying it is. It is nine people who walk."

"Can nine people walk to what you walk to."

"Not to the doors. Nothing walks to the doors." She put the notebook on the table and did not open it, which is what she does with it.

"We walk to the middle of a street and we stand in the middle of a street and we find out what the street is this week instead of what it was last week. That is a whole division and it has been one for two years and it is the only machine in this city that has ever found out what about nine hundred people felt on one ordinary morning."

## The Thing She Would Not Move

One of the nine had brought her a page and had been standing with it for about nine minutes.

It was not a page of figures. It had four lines on it with a time beside each one in a small level hand, and the third of the four said something ugly about a woman who kept a stall, and the man who had written it wanted it left out.

"No."

"She will know it is about her."

"She will know it is about her."

"There is no name on it."

"There is a street on it and a time on it and a woman with a stall at the end of that street," said Beatrix Halm. "You did not take it out because you thought it was unkind. You want to take it out because it is about to be on a page and a page is a different length of time than a street."

"Both of those are the same reason."

"They are the same reason and it is the only reason there is."

He did not take it out. He put the page on the table with the other three and it went in the book in the position it was in, which was third, and the fact that it was the third one is the reason she had made him put it there and not anywhere else.

"That happened in the eleventh month of the second year," she said, "and the woman came to this room about nine days afterwards and I sat with her for an hour and a half and she read it to me off the wall of her own stall with her finger, and it was true."

"And she wanted to know what I was going to do about the fact that it was true, and I could not tell her, and she is a tradeswoman and she is still there and she has never spoken to me again and she is entitled not to."

That is what the rule costs when it is applied and it is the reason it is not applied more often than it is about to be.

## What He Asked

He asked it at about half past six with the stove ticking and the nine of them looking at a man in a delivery coat's coat with no chalk on him.

"Can this go to doors."

Not one of the nine said anything at all.

"We can go to about nine hundred doors," said Beatrix Halm. "I have never had a reason to say that number out loud and I have had the addresses in the district book since the autumn. Four streets of them are streets we walk and five are streets we do not, because a street you walk stops being a street you walk after about four years, and about nine hundred is what is left when you take the two lists together."

"That is not a division walking."

"That is nine people and about nine hundred doors," she said, "and I am not going to tell you how long it takes, because you will do it in your head in about four seconds and get it wrong, and it will take what it takes, and you do not get to put a figure on it."

## What She Asked Him For First

"Nobody asks me what a round is for," said Beatrix Halm. "They ask me what I found. That is two different questions and one of them is a request and one of them is an order and everybody in this city gives me the first one."

"Then what is the second one."

"What happens to it when it comes back."

He had nothing for that. He had been on the branch stair for four days with the figure on the sheet of the thirtieth of December and a hundred and twenty-nine people at a door in ninety days, and he had no idea at all.

"What you want is what happens to it," she said. "You have an office with a room in it and a book on a shelf and a sheet that goes out at four with the address across the top of every line, and you have a second column ruled and empty under a heading that came off a wall, and you have just spent a quarter telling four thousand people that where a book has nothing it has not been told anything."

"Yes."

"Write it down and give it to me and I will take it into nine rooms," said Beatrix Halm. "That is what you have. There is nothing else in this building that is worth anything and I have been waiting three years for one of them to say a straight sentence about what it does with a return."

## What He Wrote For Her

He wrote it at the table with a page off the back of the division's own book and gave it to her, and she read it standing up.

*It enters the Register. It goes out at four with an address at the head of every line. No figure on it. A name on it only if a person gives a name. The second column is ruled and it stays empty.*

She read it twice and put it in the notebook under the arm and did not comment on it, which is the only thanks anybody has ever got off her for anything.

"There is one thing I cannot get out of you," she said, "and I am not going to get it out of you tonight, because you have not asked me for anything yet. You have come into this room and asked a division whether it can do a thing, and you have not asked me for a thing, and a man who has not asked has not paid."

"I have given you what happens to a return."

"You have given me a page. That is what a return is worth and you did it before I asked, which means it was cheap, and I know what cheap looks like, and I have been sold cheap things by four different people in four years."

She picked her notebook up off the table.

"You have not asked me for anything. When you do," said Beatrix Halm, "ask me somewhere I can hear you."

## What He Wrote At Four In The Morning

He wrote it up at the top of the third flight with the bin room open behind him and the mop still in the bucket where he had left it an hour and a half before.

*Twenty-eighth of January of the fourth year. The division. Nine of them, two years, a room over a chandler's off Ferrier Street, the first round out at five.*

*They can go to about nine hundred doors. That is the entire capability of the only machine in this city that can find out what people felt and it has never been pointed at a door.*

*She will not do it for nothing and she told me what it is worth and it is a straight sentence about what this office does with a return, and I gave it to her before she had asked, and she called that cheap.*

*I have spent a quarter with a figure that stands still while a hundred and twenty-nine people walk past a door. I have spent four years with no instrument. On the twenty-ninth she asked me not to ask her for anything until I ask her somewhere she can hear me, which is not an arrangement and I have written it down as a sentence because I do not yet know what it is.*

*Twenty-two days to the twentieth of February: two days to the thirtieth of January and twenty of February.*

He read that last line back and it was the first date in the notebook in four years that was not an interval and he could not find a reason for it and he ruled a line under it and left it.

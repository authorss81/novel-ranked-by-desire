# The Night Before The Morning

He came off shift at about a quarter to six on the fourth of October, and the fifth was one of his three nights off, and he went the length of Cordwain and along by the yard end instead of home, and he did not sleep at all in the twenty-eight hours after that, which is a thing his body has not done since the first of October of the fourth year.

## The Room At One In The Morning

He got into the room off a third court at about one in the morning with his own key, which he has, because it is his office in the sense that nobody else's key opens it, and there was nothing in it.

Two chairs and a table and a book with a strap across it and a lamp on the branch and a drawer under the book that has been open since the fourth of February of the first year.

He had come down to that room at about one in the morning on the fifth with a thing he had been carrying for three days and he had not got anywhere with it, and he had come down to see whether there was anything in the drawer, and there was nothing in the drawer, and there has not been anything in the drawer since the fourth of February of the first year.

He sat in one of the two chairs for about a quarter of an hour and then went back up the stairs and out into the street and walked the length of it and went along by the yard end.

He got to the foot of the ranks wall at about half past four in the morning of the fifth.

Five days from the thirtieth of September to this morning.

## The Wall

The paint above the last tile was about four days old and the smell of it was still in the cold under the arcade. There had been a repainting since he last stood here and it had taken nothing with it, as it has taken nothing with it every month for four years.

The second line was on the stone in the office's own hand at the height of a name on a plate, in two words and a dash and two more words, and about nine thousand people go under that wall in a day and not one of them reads it.

*unrecorded — carried by*

And under the last tile, on the stone, a white tile with his name on it in chalk.

Not one figure has been cut into his plate anywhere and there is not going to be one, and the name on that tile is in chalk and it is the same chalk and it is the same hand as the first one.

He has stood under it about two minutes at a time about sixty-five times in the first months of that year and has been doing it less since, and about four hundred people went past him doing it before the end of January of the first year.

That is four hundred people and not four hundred a day, and he has never divided it.

## Four Feet Away

There is a woman about four feet off him on the stone with a short-handled broom and a canvas bag, and she has been cleaning that stretch of pavement every morning since before the first of October of the first year, and he has known she was there for about three years and has not spoken to her.

She was cleaning the four inches of stone under the tile and she had done the whole of that stretch and was coming back to it.

She did not startle. She turned round and looked at him and went back to the four inches.

Then she stopped.

"You could ask me," said the woman.

He did not ask her.


It went on long enough that she looked at him a second time.

"You have been under that tile since before I was on this stretch," she said. "I did not come here for it. Nobody comes for it. It is four inches of stone and I clean it last because it is under the wall and nobody spits there."

"You have been here every morning."

"Every morning for about nine years and I have been here the morning before and the morning after and I have never once seen a person put anything on that tile," she said.

"I have seen it there every morning. It was there the morning I started this stretch. I do not know who writes it and I am not going to stand in a street and tell a man about it, because he is not a man to me, he is a man standing in my four inches at half past four in the morning."

He could have asked. It was one word and he had it.

## Why He Did Not Ask

The reason is not a reason and it took him about nine minutes to get to it, standing on that stone with his hands in his pockets in the cold.

A woman asked him three questions on the sixth of March of the first year, in a corridor with a page open on a table, and none of the three has been answered in four years. What is under that tile. Who put it there. And why he has stood under it sixty-five times without asking.

The first two are a matter of fact and would have taken a sentence.

The third is the one that stopped him, and it had stopped him on a wall for four years before he understood it.

He is the only person this office may ask. That sentence was in a letter on the paper the Correction Office has, dated the first of February of the first year, and it has been true ever since, and nothing in this city has ever distinguished between a question asked by a man walking past a wall and a question asked by the Register of Unrecorded Merit.

If he asked her, then a woman with a broom would have given the Register an answer. And there is a book off a third court with two columns in it, and a clerk who may enter what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry, and a drawer under that book that has been open since the fourth of February of the first year.

Nothing in this city will take an answer back out of that book. Once a thing is in it, it is in, and there is nobody who can go and get it and there is nobody who can say that it ought not to be there.

So the only thing he had left to give her was a silence that was his own, and the silence was the last thing in this city that still belonged to a man and not to an office, and he had been standing on other people's stone for four years because of it.


The woman went back to her four inches and swept them and came back along them.

"I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to do my stretch," she said. "I have told you that I have not seen it put there. That is the whole of what I have got. You are welcome to it."

He did not say thank you.

"You are not going to," she said, "and that is fine, because the ones who thank me are worse. About four hundred people go under this wall in a day and about nine of them stop and look at that tile, and you are the first one in about four years who has stood on my four inches and not asked me a question, and that is a thing I will notice tomorrow and the day after."

She picked up her bag.

"I am going to clean the rest of it and I am going to be here at the same time tomorrow, and if you are here I am going to clean around you."

## What He Took Away

He left at about ten past five and the sky over Cordwain had gone from black to the colour it goes at that hour in that month, and he walked the length of the street and did not go to the branch and did not go home and sat down on the low wall at the yard end for about a quarter of an hour with the cough going and nothing behind it.

The tile was still there. It had not moved and it had not been explained and it was four feet from an answer he had been refused for four years by nobody, because nobody had ever been asked.

*Fifth of October of the fourth year, half past four in the morning, the foot of the ranks wall. I did not sleep and I came here instead of anywhere else and I stood under it.*

*There is a woman four feet off who has cleaned that stretch every morning for about nine years and who told me she has never seen anybody put anything on it, and she offered me that without being asked, and she has never been asked anything by me in three years and I did not ask her tonight.*

*And the reason I did not ask is the only reason I have got left, and it is this: I am the only person this office may ask, and if I had asked her on that stone then the answer would have belonged to that office and would have been in that book, and nothing in this city can take a thing out of that book once it is in.*

*A minute ago I understood that the silence under that tile is the last thing I own that is mine. That is what is under it. There is nothing under the tile and there is a silence under the tile and it is mine and it is the only thing I have got left and I gave it up for about nine seconds tonight and I did not even know I was doing it.*

*In ten days it is the morning. In five days it is the sixth, and there is a room with no door on it off a courtyard in this city where a woman has said she is going to say a thing out loud that has been said in about eleven rooms for four years, and I am going to be in it.*

*And on the fifteenth I am going to put my own name in a column that has been empty for seven quarter-ends, and I have known since the second of August that I am going to, and there is no instrument in this city that can stop a man putting his own name in a book about himself.*

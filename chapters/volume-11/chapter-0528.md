# Half The Lane Believes It

Cordwain split in about eleven days.

On the board at the bottom of the hill, on a working afternoon, a quarter's sheet goes up with an address at the head of every line, and there are about two hundred people a week who stop in front of it. Beside it, forty streets off, five stools stand against a yard wall in a lane and a man chalks a figure in letters about the height of a name on a plate and takes a grain a head off anybody who wants to stand in the line.

By the twelfth of April of the third year there were people in Cordwain who had read both and believed the sheet, and people who had read both and believed the slate, and about nine hundred people in the district who had read neither and were not going to, and the two halves of it did not argue with each other at all, which was the thing nobody expected.

They were both reading the same city.

## The Two Halves

He went and stood at the bottom of the board at about four in the afternoon on the eleventh of April and let the street go past him for about a quarter of an hour.

A woman of about sixty read the Register's line, which was *Entered: five*, and then went and read the columns, and then went and read the second column, which was empty, and then said out loud to a man she had come with, "That is them."

And a man of about forty read the Register's line and said, "That is nobody," and went down the hill and put a grain in a line and stood in it for four hours to be counted by a figure that had been made up in three days by a man in a delivery coat who had never once said the Board took it off him.

Neither of them was stupid. That was what took him eleven days to be sure of.

He had spent four years arguing that the only thing wrong with this city was what it could not see, and it turned out that what it could see was not the problem either. The problem was that a number with nothing behind it had been standing in the open in a public street for two years and about half of a district had believed it, and now there were two of those things and the Board had one of them.

## About A Hundred People

They came down the hill in the last seventeen days of March and the first of April, at the pace of one street, out of a roll taken by lot and in order, because a chaplain had promised in a yard in November of the first year that her own would fill any column the Register ever got before the Bureau did, and she had said that the first four thousand names in a book decide what the book is for.

About a hundred of them came to a counter off a third court in eleven days.

Nine brought something the four lines could take.

A man off the eleventh wrote it up at about four in the morning and it went in his notebook under the third column, which is the one that was going to be the largest:

*Came and said nothing at all.*

One woman of the hundred had told Wenna Raut for about forty minutes about a door on Ferrier Street that had been open to her since the autumn before last and had not been mentioned to anybody. A man of about thirty had wanted to enter that he had gone to his mother's house every day for two years because his father's house was a street away and his mother was afraid, and could not produce a second person, and had been told so in about a minute.

And one woman brought a thing that went in on the fourth of April with all four conditions met, and her name was Rosalind Keck and she did needlework and clean work in four houses off the Vestry and she had fed a boy from a school off Cordwain for nine days in the sixth month of the first year and had paid for it out of her own hands and had not told her family and had not been thanked for it.

She was the first of the nine that the Church's hundred put into that register in eleven days, and the fifteenth thing that had ever gone into it off a street.

## The Lane, At Six In The Evening

He went down at about six on the twenty-seventh of April with the notebook in his coat and about nine hundred people between him and the top of it.

He had been on a night and the cough was in him. He did not pay a grain, because he was not going to stand in that line again and be a person in it, and he did not go down to the wall end and stand with the man in the delivery coat either.

He went to the wall at the side of the lane and stood against it and let the hour go past him.

Emil Brack noticed him at about nine minutes in, the way he notices about nine hundred people a morning without ever appearing to be doing it, and carried on.

There were four people on the five stools and about nine behind them, and the man at the wall end was writing with the heel of his hand and not looking at the slate while he did it.

He had been at this for two years and three days and not one morning of them had been slower than the morning before, and the number had gone from nothing to two hundred and ten in about nine months and had not gone down once since the spring.

Two thirds of the way down the column there was a figure, and beside it in lower letters a name and a date, and a man had paid a grain to be written there in the fourth month of the first year, and it was worth nothing, and nobody in Cordwain will ever attach it to him.

Under the whole column, in half-size chalk, there were two words, and the name of the room they came out of, and there had never been anything behind those two words and there was not going to be.

At about the fifty-minute mark the man in the delivery coat turned round.

## What Brack Said To A Man Who Was Not Paying

"You have stood there fifty minutes for nothing," said Emil Brack.

"I have."

"You are the only man in this city who has ever done that. In two years I have had about nineteen thousand people come through here and not one of them has stood against that wall doing nothing, because why would a person pay a grain and then not stand in the line."

He put the chalk down on the top of the stool and did not sit on it.

"You did it in the fourth month of the first year and you had a name and a nine beside it, and I wrote you down because you asked me to write down what everybody writes down, and you have never once come back, and I have wondered about it every day since."

"I am here now."

"You are here now and you are not paying and you are not standing in it and you are not asking me for anything, and I am going to tell you what I have decided about that, and then I am going to tell you what I want, and then you are going to tell me no and go up the hill, and I would rather do it tonight than in another two years."

## What Brack Wanted

"Every figure I have written is in my head," said Emil Brack. "All of them. I do not keep a book and I never have, because a book behind a figure is a way of being caught, and there is nothing in this city that can check a figure anyway, so there is nothing to be caught out about. I have about nine districts' worth and about eleven of them do not agree with each other and about four of them are the same street twice."

He did not pick the chalk back up.

"I will give you all of them. Not as figures. As a method. I will tell you how I decide what a street is worth and which ones I decided off a line and which ones I decided off nothing at all, and there are about nine of the second kind, and those nine are the ones your four lines would want, because I have never been able to check them either and I have never met anybody who could."

"And what do you want for it."

"My name."

"Under the figures."

"My name on the sheet. Once. In the office's own hand, in the office's line, saying where the figures came from and that a man in a delivery coat supplies them and that he makes them up, and that this is what a figure in this Register is."

He said it without any weight at all, which was how he said everything.

"Not a column of my own. Not a key to the lane. Not the wall. One line, once a quarter, saying that is what this is. And I have thought about it for two years and I have not thought about it for the last nine minutes, and I am saying it now because you are here and I did not want to go up the hill."

## Why He Did Not Answer

He thought about it in about nine feet of lane with a delivery coat in front of him and about eleven people walking away up the hill behind.

The reasons were four and he had them in about eleven seconds.

The first was that a line on a standing sheet with an address at the head of every line cannot be taken off it.

He would be putting his name on a document that goes out four times a year to about two hundred people a week in Cordwain. In about four years a man with a folder would read *carried by* and a street and a name and a figure made up in three days.

There would be one page in Ostara that said so out loud, and it would say it about him.

The second was that it would make the Register a thing that can be asked about a slate, and he had told a clerk on the thirtieth of November of the second year that the day it can be asked about is the day there are two instruments in this city that can be reached, and there is one now.

The third was that a man in a delivery coat who supplies figures to an office is a man who works for it, and a man who works for it can be asked to stop, and Emil Brack had been free for two years and had said so to everybody who stood in the line.

The fourth was a woman in Lamplit Row who had lost a room on the first of January because a number off a piece of chalk was about four times what that room takes anywhere else, and who was owed four loaves in the Register's first month and nothing since.

He did not say any of the four out loud.

"I am not going to say anything tonight," said Lin Hao.

## What Brack Said About That

"That is an answer and it is worse than no," said Emil Brack. "No is a thing a man can plan around and it is a thing I can put in the lane and work beside for two more years. That is a man who has not got to the end of his own reason and will come back when he has."

He picked the chalk up.

"I am at the top of this lane every working morning and the offer does not have a date on it and that is the only advantage I have ever had over anybody in this city. If you want to keep standing against that wall for nothing, do it in the morning and not at six, because at six I cannot tell a person who is watching from a person who is waiting."

## The Hour Afterward

He stayed until about seven.

A woman of about sixty came down the lane and looked at the slate and said to nobody, "That is them," which is what she had said in front of a board a street away that afternoon.

He understood that about half of Cordwain had learned to say a sentence about a number without knowing which number it meant, and the other half had not. Neither half had a word for the other half, and both of them had the same two words on a wall two streets off.

At about nine minutes to seven the man in the delivery coat put the five stools against the yard wall and chalked the column blank for the next morning and went off up the hill with his hands in his pockets, and there was nothing on the slate at all, and about nine thousand people a day walked under a wall that had two words on it and a tile with a name on it and nothing under the name.

## What He Wrote Down

He wrote it up at about four in the morning of the twenty-eighth at the top of the third flight, and the notebook was open on his knee and the bin room was open behind him and the cough took about nine seconds to go.

*Emil Brack. Ninth of about nine districts. He will give every figure he has made up, and the method, and the nine he decided off nothing. He wants his name on the sheet once a quarter, in the office's hand, saying he makes them up.*

*I did not say no. I did not say anything. That is not the same and he knows it is not the same and he said so.*

And then, lower down the page, after a gap, because the rule was that a count is only a count if it is written at the time it is counted:

*Twenty-eight days in October including the third, and thirty each of the twenty-nine months from November to March, and twenty-seven days of April, and that is nine hundred and twenty-five mornings behind that wall and not one of them was me.*

He did not write what the last line of the entry was, because it was still the fourth column, and he had told himself on the nineteenth of April that he was not going to write in it again for a month, and a month was going to be about the thirtieth of May.

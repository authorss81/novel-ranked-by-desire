# Four Hundred Is A Total

The second line went onto the foot of the ranks wall at about six in the morning on the eighth of October of last year, out of a repainting form, in a clerk's hand, and since then the wall has been repainted twelve times in the ordinary course of business and it has taken nothing with it twelve times.

He came off the night at half past five and walked down the hill and stood there, the way he has done about two minutes at a time since the first of January, and it was the ninth month of the second year and it was the exact anniversary of a paint job.

The paint had gone over the top of it each time and the paint had not taken it. Whatever the second line is, it is not on the stone. It is on the stone the way a word is on a slate, which is to say that it is only there while somebody keeps putting it there, and in this city the person keeping it there is a boy with a paste brush who is sixteen and who has never once been asked what the two words are for.

He had wanted to ask him for about four months.

## The Boy

"This is off Lamplit Row, and I have to be at the branch at eleven," the boy said, before Lin Hao had got a word out.

He had a tin and a flat brush and he was working his way along the foot of the wall with about four inches left to do.

"Then do the four inches."

"You standing there is going to put me off."

"Then put me off," said Lin Hao, and the boy did the four inches in about nine seconds and went off up the hill with the tin against his hip.

## Four Hundred Is A Total

About nine thousand people go under that wall in a day, on an ordinary morning, in an ordinary month, and the Board is nine feet of it and everybody sees it.

The tile is under the last tile, on the stone, white, and it has his name on it in chalk about the size of a name on a plate, and nothing whatever under the name. Not a figure. Not a word. A name and a gap.

He has stood under it since the first of January. By the twenty-eighth of January, about four hundred people had gone past him while he was doing it.

Four hundred.

It is a total. It is not a rate and it is not a year and it is not anybody's arithmetic, and it is the only number about this tile that anybody in this city has ever had.

He thought about dividing it by about nine hundred a day, once, in a corridor, in the middle of the afternoon, and then he thought about what it would mean if he did it, which is that a person goes past a tile at a certain rate, and that the rate is a fact about how fast people walk, and that in about four years there would be a page in a building with a rate on it about a tile.

So he did not divide it.

What he had instead was four hundred people between the first and the twenty-eighth of January and nothing at all after that, because he had not counted again, and the not counting was not modesty.

A total tells you how many. A rate tells you what to expect. A man who works out a rate has agreed to be the person who expected it.

## What He Went Down The Corridor For

He went to the room off a third court at about ten to seven in the morning with a night's bundle still in his coat and asked Yusuf Adeyemi a question he should have asked in February and had not thought of until the morning before.

"How many people come to that door."

Adeyemi put his pen down. It was twenty to seven in the morning and the pool of eleven rooms that corridor belongs to is cleaned at six by a man who is not paid enough, and the corridor was empty and echoing.

"How many is a figure I would have to produce," he said.

"Yes."

"Then you want the counter," said Adeyemi.

## The Counter In The Corridor

There is a counter on that corridor because a corridor that eleven rooms come off of has to know who went down it, and it is a wheel on a bracket with a ratchet in it, and it is about four feet up on the wall outside the second door and it has been turning since before the sitting.

Nobody had read it. That was the first thing, and the second thing was that nobody was going to be paid for reading it either, and the third thing was that the ratchet had jammed twice and been freed with a penknife by a man who did not write down that he had done it.

Adeyemi got a stool from the corridor and a sheet of the office's foolscap and read it off in the flat voice of a man who reads things off.

"Nine hundred and eleven," he said. "Give or take nine, which is the difference between what the counter caught when the penknife man freed it and what it lost in about nine days in the spring."

## Twenty-One Months

"Twenty-one months," said Lin Hao. "The first of February to the eighth of October."

"Six hundred and thirty days. Thirty days a month, no thirty-first, twelve months and nine."

"Say it the other way."

"About forty-three people a month," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "About nine hundred and eleven people in twenty-one months, and three things in the drawer, and one of the three is a refusal in this office's own hand which did not come in through that door at all."

He wrote the figure on the sheet and put the sheet flat on the table.

"That is a fact and it is checkable," he said, "and I want you to understand how unusual that is going to make you in about nine months. In this city a fact that can be checked is a fact that somebody can use. I have just given an office a number that any person who can read a counter can go and verify, and that number says that about forty-three people a month walk past a door that has been open twenty-one months."

He did not say anything.

"I am not going to ask you what you are going to do with it," said Adeyemi. "I have found out twice this year what you do with a number and I am not doing it a third time in a corridor before seven in the morning."

## The Ratio He Said Out Loud

He worked it out on the stair between the second floor and the floor the registry is on, and he got it twice and both times it came to the same thing, and then he went back down and put his head round the door of the room and said it once, out loud, to the one man who was in the building.

"About nine hundred and eleven people have come to that door in twenty-one months. Three things are in the book. Two of them came off a sheet I answered and one of those is a sentence I said out loud, and neither of them came in through that door."

"And so."

"So one thing has come in off a street, for every nine hundred people who came to the door to bring one," said Lin Hao. "One in nine hundred. That is the figure and I have not put it anywhere and I am not going to."

Adeyemi looked at him for a moment.

"You have just given this city its second figure that cannot be checked," he said, "and you did it in my corridor, and you did it about three hours after a boy repainted a line you could have asked him about, and I am going to say the rest of it now and then I am going to go and do a quarter."

"Go on."

"The denominator is not the number of people who wanted to tell somebody something," said Yusuf Adeyemi. "That is what nine hundred and eleven counts. It is the number of people who came to a door. It is not the number of people in this city who did a thing nobody wrote down. Those two numbers have never been the same number in any office in the history of this city and if they ever are then something has gone very wrong somewhere and it will not be in here."

He picked up the sheet.

"You have measured how few people talk to a man with no instrument," he said, "and you have dressed it up as a rate of human behaviour, and I am going to let it stand because you said it out loud in front of me and because a thing said out loud in a corridor can be argued with and a thing in a notebook cannot."

## The Thing He Asked For At Eight O'Clock

He had to be at the branch at eleven and it was eight o'clock, and he stood in the corridor of the office that could do nothing at all and asked for one thing.

"Read the counter every week," he said. "Put the figure on the sheet at the bottom in a line of your own and do not send it anywhere, and do not put it in the pigeonhole, and let me have it."

Adeyemi took about four seconds.

"That is a private figure kept in a public office by a Registrar who may only enter," he said.

"Yes."

"It is the worst possible arrangement and there are four hundred ways it could be misused and you know what each of them is and I know what four of them are."

"Yes."

"Then I will do it because the alternative is that a wheel on a bracket in this corridor is read by nobody ever again," said Yusuf Adeyemi, "and because if there is going to be a figure in this office that is not on the sheet, I would rather it was one I was keeping than one you were keeping in your coat. When did you buy the notebook."

Lin Hao did not answer for about four seconds.

"On the sixteenth," he said.

"That is six days before you asked me the question you should have asked in February," said Adeyemi. "You had it before you came down here. I want that noted, because a man who counts afterwards is a man counting, and a man who buys a book first is a man who has already decided what he is going to do with it."

## What He Took Down The Hill

He went down at about eight and a half and the branch did not open until ten and there is nowhere in this city for a man to be at eight and a half who has finished work at half past five.

So he stood on a step off Cordwain for an hour and ten minutes and watched people go into a chandler's.

Nine hundred and eleven people in twenty-one months, and three things in the drawer, and one of them a woman's name and two of them him, and a quarter-end in two months and about a hundred and twenty people expected to walk past that door before it, and four lines of his on the sheet that goes out at four on that day.

And nine thousand people a day going under a wall, and four hundred of them, once, in January, going past a man standing under a tile with his name on it and nothing under that either.

He had spent four years learning how to be unmeasured.

It turned out that being unmeasured was mostly standing on steps.

# Six Words In Her Own Hand

The counting room was two floors under a sales floor and at about half past four on the afternoon of the thirtieth of March Hanneke Ruis had the fifth book open in front of her and the lamp on a stand and a strap across it, and she had a sheet of foolscap on the table beside the book

And she had a sheet of foolscap on the table beside the book with nothing on it yet, because the fourth block is the composition of the street and the street is Cordwain and Cordwain is not a figure and it comes out of her.

Four times a year for thirty years. Four and thirty is one hundred and twenty, and the hundred and twenty-first block was on the foolscap at half past four that afternoon.

She had been doing it since before she took these keys.

## What She Was Writing

She wrote the address first, because that is the part that is easy and has been the part that is easy for thirty years.

Then she wrote the number of persons sleeping under the roofs on that side of the street between the chandler's and the yard wall, which is a composition, and there is a line in the return for it and the line has been in the return since before the Ferrier line was cut.

Then she wrote, in the column that has been in the fourth block since before anybody who is now alive came to work, six words.

*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*

And she put her name under them, which is the one thing she has never once failed to do, and she did not improve them and she did not hurry, and she has written one hundred and twenty of those six-word lines in one hand in this room and not one person in Ostara has ever asked her for a single one of them.

She had got as far as the address when the stair door opened.

Four times a year for thirty years, and the composition of a street is the one line on that foolscap she has never once been able to write without standing up first, because it is about nine hundred premises and the floor has one woman on it and a lamp, and she writes it in the third week of the quarter from what is in her head, and the head is thirty years deep and it is not a number, it is a street.

She writes it in four words and then she looks at it for a while, and then she signs it.

## The Man At The Door

The stair door opened at about twenty past four and it was the man from the lane off Cordwain, in a delivery coat with two fastenings gone and a third one hanging, and he came down the two flights with a slate under his arm that he did not put down.

He had been in four public buildings in four months and this was the first one he had been in that had a lock on the inside of the door.

"Hanneke Ruis," he said.

"You have looked at my name."

"It is on the outside of the door in a small hand and it is the same small hand as the fourth block," said Emil Brack, "and I am not here to take anything and I am going to say that first because I know what rooms like this one think when a man in a delivery coat walks into them."

"Then say it and sit down, because that lamp is not free."

## The Question

He put the slate on the table.

It had on it a figure of merit in four-inch chalk against a premises on the Cordwain side, and against about forty others in a column going down the edge, and at the bottom of the column in small writing the word OPEN and a time.

"Two hundred and sixty-eight mornings," he said. "Twenty-eight days left in October including the third, thirty each of November, December and January, February, March, April and May, and thirty days of June. I do the sum on a card and I have never once got it wrong and I would like you to check it, because you are the first person in four months who has ever been in a position to."

Hanneke Ruis looked at the slate for about nine seconds.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat.

"You have asked me the wrong question," she said. "You have asked me whether your sum is right and you have asked me because you think I am a person who keeps figures, and I am, and I have kept them for thirty years, and there is not one figure in this room that you can check against anything I have written, and I am going to tell you why, and then you can decide whether you have wasted a walk down two flights."

## The Right Answer

She turned the fifth book round and did not offer it.

"I do not know what the composition of Cordwain is," said Hanneke Ruis.

"I have never known. I know it the way you know the name of a street you have walked down every day of your service, which is completely, and I cannot turn it into a figure at a quarter past four in the afternoon on the last day of a quarter without going and counting, and I cannot go and count it because there are about nine hundred premises on that side of that street and this floor has one woman on it and a lamp."

"You did not count the eighth."

"I wrote the eighth for a house off Ferrier Street on the thirtieth of September out of my own memory," said Hanneke Ruis, "and it was correct, and the certificate behind it did not exist and does not exist now and is not going to exist at the end of the quarter, and I wrote it anyway and I put my name under it."

"That is not a skill. That is a woman who has kept a street in her head for thirty years and who is too frightened of being wrong about it to leave a line out."

"And is nine hundred and forty wrong."

"That is a different question and you have brought it into my room and it does not belong in my room." She put her hand flat on the foolscap.

"Nine hundred and forty is what the people at one roof have earned for standing in a lane in the cold, added up every morning and carried forward in chalk."

"How many people sleep under those roofs is a different quantity. It is the one the fourth block takes. Nothing in this return is going to take that number and it is not going to start on my handwriting, because a composition of premises is not a figure about a person and the fourth block has never held one."

## Why He Came Down Two Flights

"I will tell you why I came, and then you can decide whether it was worth the stair," said Emil Brack.

"I did not come to check a sum. I have got about nine sums and about forty people who could have checked them and not one of them would have."

"I came because you write down a figure about a street every quarter and you do it out of your own head, and there are about nine hundred people in this city who have watched me chalk a figure up against a street and has not one of them asked me where I got it."

"And you are the only person I have found who says the same sentence about her figure that I say about mine, and I wanted to hear whether it was the same sentence, or whether I had talked myself into it four months ago with a piece of chalk in my hand."

## What He Wanted

"Then what do you put against a premises like that one."

"A figure and a date," said Hanneke Ruis. "Seven hundred and six on the wall is a figure and a date, and it was nine years old this month and it was right about that morning. And underneath the figure I put what I have put under every figure in that book since before I came, and it is six words, and I will give them to you for nothing because they are not worth anything and because you have come down two flights."

She read them off the page and did not look up.

"And the certificate behind those six words is a certificate I wrote myself, in my own division, in my own hand, under my own name, and it certifies an account that nothing in this city can classify, close or hold, and not one of those certificates has been asked for by anybody in thirty years."

Emil Brack looked at the page for a while.

"Then you are the same as me," he said, "and you have been it for four and thirty years, and I have been it for one hundred and twenty mornings."

"Yes."

## What He Said He Was Going To Do

"I am going to put it on the slate," said Emil Brack.

"No."

"Not the figure. The words." He put his hand flat on the table next to hers, which he did without noticing that he had done it.

"Six words under the whole column, in the same chalk and half the size, and *carried forward, unclosed, no holder*, and then the name of the room they came out of."

"And the figure stays what it is and the chalk stays the chalk and nobody has to believe a word of it, and in about four years there will be a piece of slate propped against a wall in this city with a keeper's six words on it and not one figure on it that anybody alive can check."

"That is the first thing anybody has ever offered to do with my handwriting," said Hanneke Ruis.

She put the foolscap into the drawer and shut it and did not say anything else about it for about four minutes, and the lamp was on the table between them the whole time, and neither of them lied to the other one at any point in the afternoon.

## The Woman At The Far Table

The far table had somebody at it who had not taken her coat off and who had come down the two flights at about four without being asked to and who was standing rather than sitting, with the docket she has carried since the new year under one arm.

She reads standing. She has read standing at that table for about nine years.

She read the page from about a foot behind the keeper's shoulder for a minute and no more, and she put nothing on it and said nothing about it, and Hanneke Ruis kept her eyes on the book, and Emil Brack did not know she was in the room and would have said that a man in a delivery coat is not worth looking up for.

She has a folder under her arm with a docket in it that has been under her arm since the first of January and there is a line of work across the top of her spine where a folder has sat for eleven years.

She has never once been asked about the docket, and on the thirtieth of March a man in a delivery coat stood four feet from her for about ninety seconds and did not know she was there at all, which is the fourth time and the first time it has been by accident.

Not one of them asked her one question.

It is the fourth time, and at about twenty past four she gathered the docket and went up without the two words that would normally come with going up, and Hanneke Ruis noticed the absence of them.

"That is four quarters this year," she said, to the middle of her own table. "Four times a quarter, twice a year, nine years, and I have never asked her one, and I did not decide that this morning, and I have never once found out who did."

## The Sixth Line

At about half past five the man in the delivery coat carried the slate out through a door with a lock on the inside of it and went up two flights and out into a street where about nine hundred people went under a wall every day.

And in a room two floors under a sales floor a keeper turned her lamp off and then back on again, because the room needed it, and the hundred and twenty-first block of the return of the first of January was finished and signed.

Six words of it were in her own hand under her own name with nothing whatever behind them, and one of them is going to end up in chalk on a slate in Cordwain, and neither of the two women who will see it there is going to be told that it came from this table.

# The Man With The Slate

He went down the hill at about seven in the evening on the ninth of February with a plate in his coat and no plan, and the reason he went was about eleven words long and it was this: an office may put one question to one person, and the only thing anybody in Ostara has built since the first of October is a lane, and he had never once been down it in daylight.

The lane is off Cordwain between a shuttered chandler's and a yard wall. It is about four feet wide where it is four feet wide and eleven feet wide where the wall has gone, and at the wide end, against the wall, there were five stools.

He had been up the hill and past it a hundred times in four years and he had looked at it about nine.

## Five Stools And A Slate

The man was at the wall end with his back to it and his coat on, and he was broad through the shoulders in the way of a person who has carried things for about twenty years, and the coat was a delivery coat with the fastenings worked loose at the third one and the fourth one missing entirely, and there was a chalk thumb on the right hand and nothing at all on the left.

Two of the five stools were his. You could tell because they had been under him longer, and one of the two had a piece of sacking tied round the leg where the wood had begun to go, and that is a repair a man makes himself on about the fortieth morning and not on the hundredth.

The other three were for the people in the line.

The slate was propped against the yard wall at the height of a man's chest and it was four feet by three and it had been repainted at some point in a colour that was not the colour it is now, and it had, in chalk, in letters about four inches high, a figure of merit.

Nine thousand, one hundred and forty.

He had read that figure before he had read anything else on the slate, and it took him about four seconds, and it was the fourth figure he had ever seen in this city that was not on a wall with a column down the side of it.

## What The Man Says Before Lin Hao Says Anything

"You have not paid a grain," said the man.

"No."

"Then you are standing in it for nothing and I would rather you stood in it for something," said the man, "and I would rather still you went up the hill, and I have said that to about two hundred people and about forty of them have gone up the hill and about a hundred and sixty of them have paid, and I keep the count in my head and not in a book because a book is a thing somebody can take off you."

He turned round.

"My name is Emil Brack," he said.

"I have been in this lane every morning since the evening of the second of October, and the first morning was the third, and that is a hundred and twenty-seven mornings."

"Twenty-eight days left in October including the third, and thirty each of November, December and January, and nine of February. I have never missed one and I have never once said the Board took my words off me, and if anybody tells you I said it, they have made it up, because I have been waiting four months for that wall to come and say it and it has not come."

## How The Figure Was Made

"You made that up in three days."

"I made that one up in three days in October and I have told every person who has stood in this lane since," said Emil Brack.

"Four, five, six of them at a time in the first week. I stood at the north end of the Reading with a piece of slate and a bit of chalk on the four days and I counted heads, and I counted them on my own, and there is no sheet anywhere in this city with what I counted on it and there never will be."

"And the number."

"Nine thousand one hundred and forty is the fourth day," said Emil Brack. "The first was lower. All four of mine are in chalk at the bottom in small figures so that a person can check them against their own, and I do not take anybody's word for anything and I do not let anybody take mine for anything."

"You are asking nine strangers to check you."

"I am asking the street to check me," said Emil Brack, "and the street will not, and that is the arrangement, and you have come up this hill to work out whether I am lying to anybody and I have not lied to anybody since the second of October."

## The Grain A Head

He sat down on the stool that had the sacking on it and put his hands on his knees, which is what a man does at about a quarter past seven when he has been standing on stone since half past nine.

"A grain," he said. "Off anybody who wants to stand in his line. Not off anybody who does not. I have turned nine or ten people away this month who could not spare it and I have written nothing down about either of them and there is no instrument in this city that can make a person hand me a grain and I have never asked one to."

"And it is a grain of merit."

"It is a grain of merit," said Emil Brack, "and it is the only thing I have ever taken off anybody in my life."

"I know what it weighs because I know what a grain weighs, which is about eleven in the mouth and nothing at all at the end of a week."

"I have had four months to work out a way of doing this that does not cost anybody anything and I have not found one, and I have stopped looking for one, because the looking was worse than the eleven."

He said that last part without any weight on it at all, and it was the first thing he said that sounded like it had been said before to somebody other than Lin Hao.

## What Is On The Slate

Nine thousand one hundred and forty in four-inch letters across the top, and under it, in the same chalk, a second line that was not a figure:

*standing still is a thing a person did and there is nothing on any wall that says so*

Under that, in small figures, four numbers, which were the four days, and under those the smallest writing on the slate of all, which was the word OPEN and a time — about a quarter past nine every morning, without fail, and it had been without fail for a hundred and twenty-seven mornings.

"You could put the hours on it," said Lin Hao.

"I could," said Emil Brack.

"I have thought about it about forty times and I have not done it and the reason is that hours are checkable."

"If I put a figure and an hour on this thing then a person could take it to the Board and ask them to say whether I was there, and if they said no then I would be a man who made a thing up."

"I would rather be a man nobody can check at all than a man who can be caught being wrong, and you may think that is cowardice and I have thought it might be and I have not found the better arrangement."

## The Four Days

He counted them himself on the fourth of October with his own chalk, and he has told it the same way every morning since, and it has not improved once.

He was at the north end of the Reading from about six in the morning on the first, because about nine thousand people came under that wall that morning in the ordinary course of an ordinary day, and he counted them the way you count a queue, which is the wrong way.

"Wrong how."

"You cannot count a crowd," said Emil Brack.

"You can count a line, because a line has an order in it and a crowd does not. I got nine hundred and something on the first morning and I know to this day that I lost about a hundred of them somewhere between the rail and the door, and I have said that out loud to every person who has stood in this lane, and there has not been one person in this city yet who has made me say it twice."

"And four hundred."

"How have you got a figure for people who are standing still. Standing still is not a line. It is a crowd with a reason for being in it."

"I counted the people who stood still on those four days by watching the two of us do it," said Emil Brack, "and a woman with a notebook was watching them too, from the other end, on about the same days, and she has four hundred and something and I have four hundred and something, and neither of us can see the other's sheet, and that is the only thing either of us has that is worth having."

## The Same Two Words

At the bottom of the slate, in a corner where it looks like an afterthought, there were two words and a dash and two more words in the same chalk and about a third the size of everything else.

*unrecorded — carried by.*

"That is the eighth of October," said Emil Brack.

"I chalked that up the same morning the office painted theirs on their wall, and I told about eleven people standing in this lane that it was mine."

"Not that they took it off me."

"Mine."

"I have waited four months for that wall to come and tell me they took it off me and it has not come, and I have stopped expecting it, because they did not take it off me."

"They had it in a box already and a boy put it up. A thing in a box and a thing on a slate are two different things and I have said that to about nine hundred people and not one of them has ever been able to tell me which one is worse."

## What He Does The Rest Of The Day

"You are on that wall from about nine until about one," said Lin Hao. "What happens to the other twenty hours."

"I carry for a chandler off Bellhouse four days and I do a stair on a fifth and I have done the stair since November," said Emil Brack.

"I am not a man who came out of a Bureau and I have never in my life been on a rota and nobody has ever asked me for one."

"I am up at four because that lane is cold at half past nine and I would not have got to it on a wage by then. That is the whole of it. It is not a living and it is not nothing and there is not an arrangement behind any part of it, and if you have come down here to find out who put me in that lane then the answer is that nobody did."

## The Standing Sheet

"You were in a room on the tenth of October."

"I was in a room on the tenth of October," said Emil Brack, "with ninety people in it and a woman who keeps another kind of book saying the best thing anybody has said about this city in my lifetime, and the man at the bench said four things and I heard two of them where I was standing."

He did not say which two and Lin Hao did not ask.

"You were told they would not come."

"Who told me that."

"A woman out of a district division stood at about four feet off me on the eighth of October and told me they were not going to come," said Emil Brack, "and she told me it was not a competition and that I should not want it to be one."

"She was right about both halves of that sentence, and she has not come back once in four months, and I have not gone looking for her, because she told me not to want it to be one and I have tried to do that every morning since and I have very nearly managed it."

"The sheet went out at four the same afternoon," said Emil Brack.

"There is an address at the head of every line and any person in this city can read it at a public desk on a working afternoon, and I read it on a bench at Cordwain on the afternoon of the eleventh with about nine people behind me who were not reading it."

"One of the lines is what you said at that bench, and it says *you cannot check a slate, you can check a wall*, and I have had that on my wall in this room for four months, and it is the only sentence anybody has ever said to me that I have not been able to put a figure on."

## The Question

"It is your own office that put a name on you," said Emil Brack.

"It is a room off a third court with a name on the door and nothing in it."

"A man at rank one is the only person in this city an office can ask a question and not be refused by," said Emil Brack, "because there is nothing anybody can take off you, and whoever wrote that letter knew it about four seconds before they put the pen down, and I would like to know what the question is."

"I do not know what the question is."

"Then you are the only man in Ostara with a title and no idea what it is for," said Emil Brack, "and I have been here a hundred and twenty-seven mornings with a piece of chalk and about nine thousand names I cannot write down, and either of us is waiting for the other, and I would rather we both waited in the same lane than in two buildings a long way apart."

He put his hand out for the grain and did not say anything else about it, and the lane went on being a lane with trams at the end of it and about nine people going through it, and nobody stopped.

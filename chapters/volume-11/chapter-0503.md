# Five Stools And A Lane

Beatrix Halm came down the last of her round at about ten past ten in the morning of the eighteenth of February with a sheet in her hand and forty-one lines on it, and Runa Sato was at the corner of the lane off Cordwain with her own notebook shut, which is what Runa Sato's notebook is shut for.

They had been on the same district since the autumn and had not agreed about anything until about eleven minutes ago, standing about nine feet apart on a pavement with a lane between them, and neither of them had raised her voice and both of them had used the same word three times.

"Line."

A line off Beatrix Halm's round is a date at the head, an hour beside it, and nothing else. There has not been a figure on one since before she took the notebook, and there has not been a name on one since the third of October, and the reason there is not a name is that she has been inside a building where she can see the shelves and has worked out what four years does to a name.

Forty-one of them on the eighteenth. Thirty-nine were people standing in rooms.

## What A Round Is

"A round writes a line when there is something on it," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the rule of my division and it has been the rule since before I took the notebook and it is the only rule anybody gave me when I took it."

"Your round writes a line when there is a person in a room," said Runa Sato. "Mine writes a line when a district has decided something. Those are not the same instrument and they have never been the same instrument and this city has spent thirty years pretending they are."

"I did not say your round was wrong."

"I know you did not," said Runa Sato. "You said the lane was a lane. That is a fact about a piece of ground. I am telling you what the lane is."

## What The Lane Is

And this is Runa Sato's, and it is hers because her whole trade is knowing what a district believes when it is left alone with a thing for a morning, and she has been out before six and in after dark for the better part of nine years, and there is not one division in this city that has ever seen her inside it, and two counters in Cordwain that see her every morning without fail.

"They are not behind him," she said, and it is the same sentence she said on the second of October and has not improved since and was right then.

"Who."

"The people in that lane. They are not behind him." She said it the way a person says a thing they have said once and been told was a figure of speech. "They are in a lane. They happen to be standing behind a man with a stool. That is not the same and the difference is the whole of what I do and it is the difference between a queue and a following."

"A following."

"A man comes along a street and people go where he goes because he is going somewhere," said Runa Sato. "That happened in this city twice in my working life and both times it was a rumour and both times it was over inside four days."

"I have never in nine years seen this city make a line that a rumour did not make, and I have never in nine years seen a line that outstayed the four days by more than about a factor of thirty."

"That is a figure."

"That is a figure of nine years and not a figure about anybody," said Runa Sato, "and you know the difference between those two better than I do, and you have known it since the eighth of October, and you were the one who wrote it on a slate's opposite wall."

## One Hundred And Thirty-Six

"That lane has been open every morning since the evening of the second of October," said Beatrix Halm. "The first morning was the third. Twenty-eight days left in October including the third, thirty each of November, December and January, and eighteen days of February. One hundred and thirty-six."

"Runa."

"I have it on a card," said Beatrix Halm. "And I have carried it about for four months and I have never once known what it was for, and the first time anybody has asked me for it in public I have found out that I would rather give it than explain it, and that is not a good sign in a keeper."

## What Cordwain Believes On The Eighteenth

Runa Sato had two hours that morning and she used them the way she uses every morning, which is by not asking anybody a single thing.

What the district believed, on the morning of the eighteenth of February, about a lane with five stools in it:

That it was a queue. About four in five of the people she put it to used that word without being prompted, and eleven of the nineteen she had a proper conversation with said it before he had got to the end of his sentence.

That the man in the delivery coat was running it. That one was wrong and it was held by people who had looked carefully and thought about it, and they had arrived at it from the stools, because five stools in a row is furniture put out by somebody who is running something.

And that it would end. Every one of the nineteen said so unprompted, and the two who gave a length gave four days and about six weeks.

"That last is the interesting one," said Runa Sato. "Six weeks. A thing a man thinks will last six weeks is not a thing anybody spread. A rumour knows what it is. Somebody stood in that lane every morning for a hundred and thirty-six days, took a grain off about a hundred and sixty people, and told every single one of them it was his line. And there is not one of those people lying to anybody."

## The Disagreement

The lane was about thirty feet away and there were four people in it at half past ten and one of them was a woman with a pram who had come for no reason she could have put on a form, and the five stools were all taken.

Runa Sato did not look at them.

"If I put *a lane off Cordwain, five stools, a grain a head* on my return," she said, "then in about four years a stranger with a folder reads it and sees a queue."

"And he is right. And that is the end of it for ever, because a queue is a thing this city understands, a queue has a shape, a queue has people at the front of it and a reason, and he will look at that line and think he knows what it is."

"And if I put mine."

"You will put the date and the hour and the place," said Beatrix Halm, "and no figure, and he will read that in about four years and see a page about nothing, because a page with no figure on it and a page about nothing look the same on paper and you know they look the same on paper because you have spent nine years being asked for a figure and answering that one does not exist."

"Yes."

"That is the whole of the disagreement and it has been the whole of the disagreement since the autumn," said Beatrix Halm, "and neither of us is going to win it, and I would rather we said so in a lane than in a room."

## What It Costs Runa Sato

"I am going to write on my own return that I cannot report it," said Runa Sato.

Beatrix Halm looked at her.

"Not that there is nothing there. That I cannot report it." She put her thumb on the shut notebook. "Which is a different sentence and which has not been written on one of my returns in nine years, and which will be read in about four years by a man who will not be able to tell the difference between a thing I could not report and a thing I did not look at."

"That is not the same as a hole."

"It is not the same as a hole," said Runa Sato, "and it is going to be read as a hole by everybody in my division inside a fortnight, and one of them is going to say that I have gone soft on a round and I will not be able to correct her without writing something else, and I am going to write the true sentence and let her have it."

She said that without any heat in it at all, which was the first thing about Runa Sato that Beatrix Halm had ever found frightening.

## What It Costs Beatrix Halm

They walked back up as far as the corner and Beatrix Halm said the thing she says about four times a year.

"My rule is that a round writes a line when there is something on it," she said.

"It came out of the third of October. On the second I wrote a line for a woman who came and stood in a doorway and I could not say who she had gone in to see, so I asked her, and she told me, and it was a man with a hundred and six."

"And then I could not write the line at all, because it would have gone into a book in a building, and in about four years a stranger with a folder would have had a page that said a woman stood in a doorway in Cordwain, and the hour, and the man had a hundred and six, and that man is alive, and he did not know, and nobody would ever have asked him."

"So you do not write it."

"So I write the hour and I do not write who," said Beatrix Halm, "and I have thrown away about nine lines in four months and every one of them was a person. A round that throws nine people away a quarter is not a good round. I do it anyway. That is what it costs, and I would do it again, and I am not going to stand here and pretend it is free."

## The Thing Runa Sato Is Not Saying

There was a second half of it and neither of them said it on the pavement.

"If I report that lane, then my division has a thing to work on," said Runa Sato, "and the thing to work on is whether a private person may take payment for a place in a line."

"And if they work on it they will find a rule, because that is what a division does, and the rule they find will say that a grain of merit is merit and that a movement of merit without a seal is not permitted. And that rule will be correct. And it will put that lane out of business inside a fortnight and it will be the best work my division has done this year."

"And you have not reported it."

"I have not reported it," said Runa Sato, "because I read the standing sheet at a public desk on the eleventh of October like everybody else who wanted to, and there is a line on it that says the whole job of the Bureau is to be the thing you can ask. A man with no standing said that in a room. And about nine divisions in this city have spent four months waiting for somebody to hand them a reason that is not his."

## Four Years Is A Position

Lin Hao had been standing at the mouth of the lane since about half past nine and neither of them had made him a part of it, and he said it now anyway.

"Four months," he said. "Not four years. You have been at it a hundred and thirty-six mornings and I have been a hundred and thirty-six mornings from a washroom, and I am the reason it exists and I have not done one thing about it, and I would like somebody to say to me what that is, because I have not been able to name it myself since about November."

Runa Sato turned round and looked at him, and that is the entire sentence and she said it once.

"That is not an absence," she said.

"You know what an absence is."

"An absence is when it is not yours."

"Four months of a man in a delivery coat standing in a lane that came out of a thing he did, with nine thousand people going under a wall about a quarter of a mile away who think it was about them, and not one hour of your hours given to it — that is a position."

"You have been holding it. You have been holding it very carefully and I do not think you have ever once called it anything else in your own head, and I think that is the first thing in four months that has made me understand why the Bureau is still standing."

Then she went back up the hill.

## What Beatrix Halm Put On The Page

The sheet went into her bag with forty-one lines on it and the forty-second was not a person.

*the lane off Cordwain, a hundred and thirty-sixth morning, from half past nine, five stools, all of them taken at half past ten, one woman with a pram and no reason, a grain a head off anybody who stands in it, a figure of merit in four-inch chalk on a slate and nothing whatever behind it.*

No figure of her own anywhere on the page. A time, a place, a count of stools, and a woman with a pram.

She went back up the hill on the other side and did not look at the lane again, and there were four people in it and then five, and not one of them could have said what it was for, and the lane has outlasted the four days by about thirty-four times over, which is the whole of what Runa Sato said at the mouth of it, and she was right, and being right is the sentence that is going to cost her a return.

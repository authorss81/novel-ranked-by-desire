# The Price Of Looking

He did the sum on the stair and he did it twice and both times it came out the same, which is not a thing that happens to him very often.

Eight hundred and forty-one went into that Register in the quarter that ended yesterday, and about a third of anything a division finds in a cold city ends up on a table in a ward.

A third of eight hundred and forty-one is two hundred and eighty, and there are two hundred and eighty of them lying in rooms in this city at this moment, in nine wards and four institutions and about thirty that have no name, and one of them is two flights up off Cordwain with the lamp on the wall turned down.

And about a fifth of the people a division finds cannot place a day at all. A fifth of eight hundred and forty-one is a hundred and sixty-eight, and that is the number of people in this city at this moment who are carrying something they cannot put on a calendar, and they are not lying about it and they are not ashamed of it, and about nine in ten of them have never been asked.

He went up the second flight at about twenty past ten on the evening of the first of July with the arithmetic in his coat and a cough he has had four years.

## The Room Off The Ward

The sister on the landing let him through and did not ask, and she has not asked in four years and she is the only person in this building who has not asked him anything.

There was one lamp on the wall and it was turned down as far as it goes. There were four people asleep along the ward. In the room off it there was a woman on her side with her back to the door and a chair with a coat over the back of it, and she had been there about nine days.

"She is called Mavis Rooke," said Tessa Okonkwo. "She is a big woman with the hands of somebody who has not stopped, and she has been in this room since the twenty-second of June and she has not been out of it and she is not going to be, and I have taken about a fortieth off her for nine days and I have not told her that and she has not asked."

"Nine days."

"I have about four minutes and then I have to go and do the other end of the ward, so I am going to say the thing and then you are going to say whatever it is you came up here to say and it is going to be shorter than either of us wants."

## What She Told Nine Strangers

"A division came to her door on the fourteenth of April," said Tessa Okonkwo. "And then three more times, because she asked them to come back, which I would like you to sit with for a moment. She asked them to come back."

"How many times did they come."

"Four times over about nine days, and about eleven minutes each, and there are nine of them and they went down the whole street on the second visit and came back with about forty pages."

"What did she tell them."

"She dug," said Tessa Okonkwo. "She dug a street for six years in the cold and she was never paid and nobody ever put it on a page."

"And a man in a delivery coat was in the street in the third year of it and chalked the figure and she thought for about two years that the figure was about her. That is the sentence about slates and she has never been near one and she has been carrying it for about two years without anybody ever putting it in front of her."

"And she could not name a day."

"She could not name a day and she could not name a month, and she was not wrong about any of it, and what she said at the fourth visit is what is in the fourth page and it is a paragraph and it has no date in it and it is the correct language."

## What The Cold Minute Was Doing

"She has a cold minute in her chest," said Tessa Okonkwo. "She has had it for about nine years and it came the year she stopped going to the yard because the yard was where the work was and the work stopped and she stopped going and it came into her chest that winter."

"And it has been moving about ever since, and in the winter it is between her shoulders and in the summer it is in the chest, and that is ordinary and I have four beds of it in this room's corridor and eleven in the ward."

"It has moved."

"It moved on the fourteenth of April and it moved about nine days afterwards and it moved every time they came back," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Not because they did anything to her, and not because anybody was unkind to her about it either."

"Because she told four strangers about a year and a half of her life on their own steps and every time she said it out loud to somebody who did not know her it went about half an inch further down, and it has now reached the place where I cannot hold it at a fortieth and I have about nine days of myself and I am not going to tell her that either."

He stood in the room and said nothing for long enough that she turned round and looked at him.

"You asked me for the sentence," said Tessa Okonkwo, "in this room, on the twenty-first of January of the second year, and I gave it to you word for word and you did not do it for a year and a half, and then you printed it on a public sheet on the thirtieth of September of the third year and it cost you a quarter of a counter and about four thousand people and one woman on a board, and none of that was the point of it."

"I know it was not the point of it."

"Read it to her."

## What He Read To A Woman In A Bed

"I am not reading it to her," said Lin Hao. "You are."

"I am going to read it to her because I have known her nine days and you have known her about four minutes and she asked me about eleven minutes ago who was going to pay for the beds, and she asked it of me and not of you, and she has asked me three other things in nine days and has not asked you one."

She took a sheet off the end of the bed and turned it round so that the woman on her side could not see it and so that he could.

"Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing," Tessa Okonkwo read out. "That is the City. That is what the City has printed about itself, in its own hand, on its own page, since the thirtieth of September of the third year, and it went out at four with an address at the head of every line, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read it."

Mavis Rooke turned her head about nine inches.

"There is nothing written down about me."

"There is nothing written down about you," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"Then I have nothing."

## What Was Not Said In That Room

He did not say it.

The sentence is true. It is the truest thing that office has ever printed and it is four words longer than a clerk is permitted to add, and it is the sentence a doctor asked a clerk to say in a room off a ward, on a night in the January of the second year, with the door shut.

It says that where the Register has nothing, it has not been told anything. It says nothing whatever about what she has, and a woman who has never been asked for anything by anybody in this city heard the first half of it and got the rest of it herself, and the rest of it was the thing she already believed about herself before anybody knocked on her door.

Tessa Okonkwo did not correct her.

That is the whole of the price and it is about four seconds long and a doctor with nine days of her own inventory did not spend one of them on it, because correcting a woman who has been right about her own life for nine days is not medicine and she has decided that in this room and not in a corridor and she is not going to be argued with about it.

"I am not grateful for it," she said, to him, not to the bed. "I want that said out loud in this room before you go down those stairs, because you came up them with a figure in your coat and you have been carrying that figure since yesterday afternoon and you have had it in a drawer for a year and a half and then on a sheet for nine months."

"Yes."

"It is the correct sentence and it is a heavy one and I asked you for it and you have given it to me in the worst possible way, which is on a piece of paper, out of a folder, by somebody else reading it."

"Yes."

"I am not going to unask it," said Tessa Okonkwo. "And I am not going to tell you it was worth it, and you are not going to tell me it was worth it."

"And there is a woman in that bed who is going to go home to an address in about four days and is going to be asked at her own door by a neighbour why a stranger from the City came to her door four times in nine days, and there is nothing that any of us can put on a sheet about that."

## What He Wrote At Four In The Morning

He wrote it up at the top of the third flight at about a quarter to five with the bin room open behind him and the corridor counter four feet up and the mop in the bucket.

*First of July of the fourth year. The ward. A woman called Mavis Rooke, forty-one, nine days in the room off the ward, a cold minute in the chest that moved about nine days after a division knocked and has moved about half an inch every time one of them came back.*

*One day after the quarter-end. Eight hundred and forty-one entered. A third of it is two hundred and eighty and a fifth of it is a hundred and sixty-eight, and both of those are hers, and she told me them in a kitchen in July of the second year and I printed them on a sheet and it did not help her and it was not supposed to help her and it did not help her.*

*She asked who was going to pay for the beds and she asked me for it. I have been up these stairs four times in four years and she has asked me one question and she asked it to the doctor.*

*And I read out a sentence in a room and a doctor read it out because I would not, and a woman heard half of it and got the rest of it herself, and the rest of it was what she already believed about herself before anybody knocked on her door, and the sentence is true, and it is mine, and it does not say one word about what she has.*

Then, after a gap:

*I want to be able to put it back. I want the drawer and the sheet and the quarter and the four thousand people back, and I have wanted that for about four minutes and I do not know what I would do with them if they came.

*And that is the second time in four years I have wanted to put something back and the first time I did it I put a figure in the office's book against the instruction of the woman who gave it to me, and it took eleven minutes, and I would do that again.*

*In about ninety-nine days it is the tenth of October and there is a morning in this city that has been named for four years by a woman who has never been thanked for naming it, and I have not got nine days of anything, and I am going to go down the two flights and past the sister on the landing and out into Cordwain at about five in the morning and the round is still going.*

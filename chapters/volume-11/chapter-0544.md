# The Quarter Decides A Number

Two flights down, and the air on the second of them was colder than the air on the first, and the room under the sales floor had one lamp on a stand in it that cost whatever the keeper had decided it cost this quarter.

The fifth book was open on the table with its strap laid across it and Hanneke Ruis was standing at the far side of it. She is on her feet for one line of the quarter and is not for the other three, and he came in on the one.

Thirty-five days from the twenty-fifth of August to this morning: five days of August after the twenty-fifth and thirty days of September.

The foolscap was square in front of her and the pen was lying across it and there was nothing on the paper at all.

"That lamp costs a quarter and that chair costs the other three quarters," she said, "so you are going to stand."

He stood.

## The Fourth Block

The composition of premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight goes in the fourth block of the quarter and has gone in since before she came, and it had had no certificate behind it since the fourteenth of March of the second year.

She wrote the figure, which is eight, and then six words in the column.

*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*

And her own name under them, in her own division, in her own hand, which is the one item on that page she has never once left out in thirty years.

Thirty years is three hundred and sixty days multiplied by ten, and four times a year is four in each of them, and thirty multiplied by four is one hundred and twenty, and there is a hundred and twenty of these in this city somewhere written in one hand and about nine in ten of them are in rooms that have not changed.

"I do not keep the count," she said, as she said it every quarter. "You keep the count. It is the wrong thing for me to keep."

There was a woman at the far table of that room with a folder in front of her who had been there when he came in and had a folder in front of her when he went out, and she has been at that far table on and off for about three years and nobody in that room has asked her anything in any of it, and he has been down two flights for this purpose four times in two years and did not ask her on any of them.

He thought about that for about nine seconds and did not do it.

## The Quarter Turns At Four

At four on the thirtieth of September the sheet went out with an address at the head of every line.

*Register of Unrecorded Merit. Fifteenth quarter. Entered: eight hundred and forty-one.*

It is the same figure as the thirtieth of June. It is the same figure as the eleventh day of July when the satchel came in, and it is the same figure it has been since the first of July, and between the first of July and this afternoon four hundred and nineteen accounts went into that book and not one of them was a deed and not one of them moved the number.

Two hundred people a week in Cordwain read a sheet in which a round of nine divisions over ninety days at about nine hundred steps has produced no entries at all.

And under the figure, two columns. The first with eight hundred and forty-one lines against it. The second headed *carried by*, ruled, and empty, and it has been empty for seven quarter-ends, which is one year, six months and twenty-one days off the ninth of March of the third year when it was ruled, and it has not done one day's work in the whole of that time.

Then the office's own sentence, in the one sentence a clerk is permitted to add, four words longer than that sentence is supposed to be, and it is the only sentence on the page:

*Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing.*

## The Thing That Holds

"I want to ask you about the books," he said.

"You are going to ask me what is holding them."

"I am going to ask you what is holding them and you are going to tell me and I am going to say something after it and you are not going to like it."

She put the pen down and squared the book.

"The books that would take that account meet once in the year on the sixteenth of November at seven in the morning and they did not move," said Hanneke Ruis. "I have told a man in this room the reason for that about twice in four years and I am not going to tell you twice."

"I am not asking for the reason. I am asking what holds it."

"Those six words," said Hanneke Ruis. "Every quarter, under my name, in a chained book, in a room with a lamp in it. And they hold it because there is nobody's name on them but mine and mine is the name of the person who writes them down and not the name of anybody who owns what they describe."

She put one finger on the column.

"That is what *no holder* means and it is the only part of the six that does any work. It does not mean the account is empty, because nobody has ever written in a book that it is empty, and a thing nobody has written down cannot be closed. It means that there is no person in this city who can be pointed at and said to be the one it belongs to. Not me. Not the man who emptied it. Not the nine seals. Nobody."

She put her hand on the page and turned it about nine degrees towards herself, which she has never done with it in his four years of coming down here.

"And the reason you are asking me in the eleventh month of the fourth year, and not in the first month," said Hanneke Ruis, "is that you have found a use for a holder, and you have come down two flights to see whether I can stop you, and I cannot, and I have known since about the ninth of July and I have said nothing to anybody."

She looked at him for about as long as it takes to write a figure.

"You have had eleven days to do it and you have not done it and that is also how I know."

"And what is going to happen in this room in the next fortnight," said Hanneke Ruis, "is that somebody is going to put a name on it."

## What He Is Going To Do

He did not answer for about four seconds.

"On the fifteenth of October I am going to enter a claim against myself," he said. "In the office's own book, in the office's own hand off my hand, in the column headed *carried by*, because that is what the column says and it is the first time in four years that the column has been able to say anything at all."

"That column has been empty for seven quarter-ends."

"It has."

"And you are going to put a name on it."

"My name."

"That is what *no holder* is," said Hanneke Ruis, "and you have just written against it in a book a keeper certifies four times a year in a room two floors under a sales floor, and you have done it in your own name, and there is nobody in this city who can take it out for you, including you."

"I know."

"Then you know that on the sixteenth of November at seven in the morning there will be an entry in a book in Ostara that says where that account went and who sent it and who got it," said Hanneke Ruis.

"And the six words will still be on my page, in my hand, under my name, and there will be one sentence in this city that says it belongs to nobody and one sentence in the same handwriting style four inches below it that says a man decided who it went to."

She squared the book again.

"That is what you have done. It is not a small thing and it is not done out of temper, and I have known for about eleven days that you were going to do it and I have not said one word to anybody, including you, and I would like it understood that I did not say it because I could not stop you and not because I thought of it."

## The One Thing She Did Not Do

"Why did you not ask me," he said.

"Because I have one question in this room and I asked it about eleven years ago and I have not asked a second," said Hanneke Ruis. "And because if I had asked it you would have told me the truth, and the truth is that you have spent everything you had four years ago and there is nothing left in you to spend on me, and I would rather have the column."

She took the nib off the string and put the book away and did not uncork the ink.

"You have ten days," she said. "Ten days of October after the thirtieth of September. It is the morning of the tenth of October that has been named for four years by a woman in a called sitting and it is nobody's morning and it is not mine and it is not yours, and you have written down a thing to put in a book on the fifteenth and I am telling you that you have not decided to do it and that you will."

"I have not."

"You will," said Hanneke Ruis, "and there is not one instrument in this city that can stop you and you have been the whole instrument of an office that has none for four years."

"And I am not going to help you and I am not going to argue, and the book goes on the shelf at a quarter to five and it comes off the shelf on the first of January, and you can come and read it on the second of January and there will be six words under a figure and my name, and that is all any of us will have."

*Thirtieth of September of the fourth year. The counting room, four in the morning off a quarter that has entered nothing for ninety days.*

*Eight hundred and forty-one, the same figure as the last sheet, and four hundred and nineteen accounts in the book, and the second column empty for a fifth quarter.*

*And I am going to put my own name in that column on the fifteenth of October, and the six words that hold the account open are held by there being nobody's name on them, and mine is the only one there has ever been and I am going to make it the wrong one.*

*A keeper told me it will not work and she is right and she told me in a room she has not asked me a question in for about eleven years, and she did not stop me, and I have got ten days.*

*And I have not decided and I have got until the fifteenth, and there is a night before the morning and I know which night it is.*

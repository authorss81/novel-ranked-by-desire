# Agnete Sends Forty Up The Hill

The word came into the branch at about half past six in the morning on the twenty-ninth of November on the back of the night's bundle, in the night man's own hand, because a woman in the yard behind a chapel off Cordwain had come down to the counters at about four in the afternoon of the twenty-eighth and had asked whether anybody in this building would like to know what her forty had done in a lane at a quarter past nine that morning.

The night man had written it on the back of a sheet and put the sheet in the bundle and entered the bundle, which is all he could do with it, and by the time the branch emptied the sheet was in Lin Hao's coat with everything else he keeps in there.

Forty of the Church of the Quiet Number had gone up the hill at a quarter past nine on the morning of the twenty-eighth of November and had paid a grain a head off their own hands at the top of it and had stood in a lane for four hours in a line of nine other people, and not one of them had put his name on anything.

## Forty The First Time

He knew the first forty.

The lane took a grain a head off anybody who wanted to stand in it, and forty people paid forty grains at a quarter past nine in the morning on the twenty-eighth of November, which is about eleven in the mouth each and nothing whatever at the end of a week.

## Forty Grains A Head

"You will want to know what that cost him."

"Forty people paid yesterday at about a quarter past nine," said Agnete Lindqvist. "One of my own was standing in that lane and came back and told me four things in the yard and I have three of them."

"Which three."

"That she had a queue behind her for about an hour and a half and that she was behind a woman who was behind a man from the post," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That the man in the delivery coat took the grain off every one of them in full and did not round it down and did not take a second one off anybody. And that the figure on the slate did not go up by forty when the forty of us came out."

"It did not go up by forty."

"It did not go up by forty, and I asked her twice, because it is the best thing that has ever happened in that lane and it happened because forty women stood in it being dull on my instruction," said Agnete Lindqvist. "He said that a person who pays is not standing still, and that it is his rule and he has never broken it in front of anybody and he was not going to start for me."

She put her hands in her pockets.

"I have wanted that lane shut since the eighth of October of last year and I sent forty of my own into it yesterday and it is the first thing I have ever done that has made it worse, and I did it on purpose, and if you tell me it was foolish I will thank you and go away."

They had come down the hill on the ninth of October with her at their head and had stood behind her under the ranks wall while a clerk's two words and a dash and two more words went into about nine hundred people's heads, and she had turned round afterwards in the street and told them to go back up the hill and be extremely dull about it.

Then she had said the sentence that made it a rule rather than a day: a thing that wants a wall is the thing she had been standing nine feet off for a week.

She had sent forty the first time to make a spectacle of being uninteresting. She had sent forty the second time into a lane that charges a grain a head, and that was a different instruction and took eleven days longer to arrange.

## The Yard Behind The Vestry

The chapel holds two hundred and there were about four thousand of them in the yard, on the steps, in the street at each end of it and in four rooms behind the Vestry that were opened for the purpose and have been opened every working evening since the middle of September.

It was eleven hundred at the end of July of the year before, taken by lot, in the order of a roll.

It is about four thousand now, and she did not say how and neither of them asked, because she had said once that the Church had gone up by people arriving on their own and not one of them had been sent.

## What She Has Not Claimed

"The line on the wall is still there," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"It is."

"I have not claimed it and I am not going to claim it and I want to hear myself say that out loud in front of you one more time, because a woman came to the yard on the twenty-seventh and told two hundred of my people that the Church had a wall and I have spent two days writing to every house she spoke to in."

"Then what are you here about."

"I am here about your office," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and about the fact that you have told a man in a room off a third court that you will not write a sentence until nine people have tried to break it, and that nine of the nine are mine."

He did not answer that.

"Four of them are," she said. "Four of your nine have come to this yard since the middle of September and have taken a thing from me and put it in a kitchen, and I have let them, because what else is a churchyard for."

## What She Decides

Here is what she said, and she said it standing, in a coat, in about eleven sentences, and she did not sit down once.

"In the spring, or at the end of the year, or in four years, your office is going to have a column. It will not have one this year because a line for a room is one sentence and you have told the Registrar you will be late. But it will have one, and the column is the whole of it, because a register without a column is a drawer and this city has four hundred drawers and one of them is yours."

"And when it has one."

"Then the Church of the Quiet Number will fill it before the Bureau does," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Not this year. Over about nine years, at the pace of one street, and I will do it the way we did the roll, which is by lot and in order,"

"and I will not send anybody and I will not ask anybody for anything, and about four thousand people will bring a thing each that they did and that nobody wrote down, because they have been to a room where a person writes things down that nobody wrote down, and they will bring it to a counter off a third court and the woman at that counter will enter it."

She turned her head about nine degrees and looked at the yard.

"That is what I have come to say and I have said it and I am not going to argue anybody out of it. I have thought about it for about three weeks and I want it understood that I have thought about it and not that it arrived."

## What It Costs

"That will make it the Church's," he said.

"It will make it the Church's in about nine years and it will be the Church's in every way that matters from the day after the column opens, because the first four thousand names in a book decide what the book is for," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have not come here to pretend otherwise and I would rather you said it out loud in a kitchen than that nine women spent an evening finding it out."

"Then why do it."

"Because of the other half," she said, "and I would rather you had the whole sentence. This city has a wall with a figure on it that a city believes for thirty years and cannot check, and a lane off Cordwain with a piece of slate, and nine divisions that report what they can count, and not one of them has ever had to put a thing anywhere."

"If the Church fills your column then for nine years every unrecorded thing a person did in this city is a thing a person brought to a counter, and in the tenth year you will find out whether that is a way of counting people or a way of counting the Church."

She put her hands in her pockets.

"And it will not be a way of counting the whole city, and I know that, and I have decided it anyway, and I would have decided it the other way if there had been a column in the spring."

## What She Leaves Him With

She left at about four o'clock and she went out through the yard and not through the front, because about two hundred people were coming in from the street and she had spent twenty years making sure that no person in this yard has ever been looked at by the sort of person who counts faces.

At the gate she stopped and turned round.

"Four of them," she said. "Out of nine. And when the nine sit down I am going to be one of them and I am going to say one thing and I am not going to answer anybody, and you will not be able to stop me and I would think less of you if you could."

"I would not be able to stop you."

"I know," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Then we will be the same on the seventeenth of October and on every working evening after it, which is a sentence I have never had to say to anybody and have said twice in four years."

And that was the twenty-ninth of November, and the yard filled again at about five, and about four thousand people went home past a wall with two words and a dash and two more words on it that the only organised body in this city has never once claimed and has spent a year telling its own people was nobody's.

# He Asks For It Himself

Halvard Senn came at about ten in the morning of the thirtieth of June of the fourth year and sat down in one of the two chairs in the room off a third court and put a satchel on the floor beside him and did not take his coat off.

He is the officer of a Bureau division and he has been for about nineteen years, and he was not in the room on the first of October of the fourth year, and he has been carrying what nine divisions brought in ever since.

"You asked the office for a round," he said.

"I did."

"I am here because you did and not because anybody asked me to be. There is a line about it. I am the officer of the division that carried it out and I could not refuse it, and I did not refuse it, and I want both of those on your side of this table before either of us says anything else."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is a fact, and I want it in the room anyway," said Halvard Senn. "Say nothing back to it."

## What He Said He Was Going To Say

"I have got about nine minutes of it arranged and I am going to say it in order and then I am going to leave, and I am not going to take a question, and if you want to answer me you will have to do it into a room."

He took a hand's width of paper out of the satchel and put it on the table face down.

"Ninety days. Three months of thirty days each from the morning of the thirtieth of March to this morning, which is ninety, and nine divisions went out on that first morning and not one."

"Nine divisions."

"Nine divisions of nine, which is about eighty-one people, and about nine hundred doors between them, and every division that went out did it because a division handed a line has to go and find out what the line is a line about." He turned his head slightly.

"You asked for one. I know what you asked for. I have the paper you wrote on it and it says *one division*, in your hand, on a piece of a tally board, and it is the correct request and it is what happened to me."

## What It Found, In The Terms He Had Written Himself

"Two hundred and nineteen doors by the twenty-fourth of April and about nine hundred by now, and at eight hundred and forty-one of them somebody said something." He did not open the paper. "And I have read what your office has entered this quarter and it says *entered: eight hundred and forty-one* on the same kind of sheet, in the same column, under the same heading, with the same address at the head of every line, as thirty-nine on the thirtieth of March."

"Yes."

"So there is a number on a public page in Cordwain this afternoon that means a thing a person at a desk is owed for a deed she did to somebody else, and a number on the same page that means that eighty-one people stood on about nine hundred steps for ninety days and somebody told them something."

"Yes."

"Your second line," said Halvard Senn. "*A person found at a door is a person somebody has decided was owed something, and finding them is not the same as owing them.* You wrote that at four in the morning on the thirtieth of March and I have read it about nine times and I am a person that you asked for it."

He put a finger on the paper and did not turn it over.

"I have eighty-one people who have spent ninety days finding people, and about a fifth of the people at those doors cannot place a day at all, and they are not wrong, they are the most reliable people in this city and they are telling you something happened and they cannot tell you when, and you knew that, because a doctor told you the same two numbers in a room off a ward two years before you wrote it down."

## What Asking For It Was, From Inside The Machine

"On the ninth of April," he said, "a division knocked at a door in a street off the yard end. A woman answered who had lived there about nine years and said that nobody had ever knocked at that door. She asked them to come back. They went back nine days later and there were four people standing on that landing by the second visit, because a street is about four seconds long and a stranger on a step is the only news anybody has had in nine years."

He picked the paper up off the table and kept it.

"That is what asking for it was. Not the finding. The knock. You have a machine that goes to about nine hundred steps and every step that it arrives at is a place where a thing becomes visible to the four people who can see it."

"And about nine hundred of those places are in a district where nobody had ever been looked at, and a fifth of the people at them cannot say when their own thing happened, and the four people on the landing can see that they were asked."

"I know that part."

"You do not know that part," said Halvard Senn, "because nobody who writes a request on a tally board at four in the morning is told what it is like to have four people on your landing afterwards, and I am the only person in this building who has been on one, and I came here to be on one in front of somebody who has to carry it."

## What He Cannot Put Down

"There is a thing I have not said and I am going to say it and then we are finished."

"Go on."

"A division that goes out on a district round has a return that is a return. A division that goes out to find out what people did has got a thing that comes back which no classification in this city covers, because a classification is about a category and there is no category for a person telling you about a street at half past six in the morning on a date they can name." He did not look at the paper.

"So it does not get carried by a division. It gets carried by the officer of the division. It has been carried by me since the thirtieth of March, in a satchel, on a train and on a bus and up a stair."

"And there are about nine hundred pages of it and I have not been able to give one of them to a clerk and I have not been able to give one of them to a shelf."

"That is a hard thing to carry for ninety days."

"It is a hard thing to carry for one day and I have carried it for ninety," said Halvard Senn. "I am not telling you that to be forgiven and I am not telling you that it is your sentence on the bottom of the tally board, because if a division had gone out on the thirtieth of March on anybody else's order I would be carrying the same satchel and I would have been carrying it since the thirtieth of March either way."

"And that is the part you will not let me answer."

"That is the part you will not let me answer," he said, "because you would answer it with something about how it was difficult for you to write the request, and it was not, it took nine seconds, and I read it off a satchel to tell you that it took nine seconds."

## The Part Where He Was Not Going To Answer

Lin Hao had three answers in him and he got about nine words into the first one.

"You did not have to—"

"No."

"I want to say that the office had no instrument and I knew that when I—"

"No."

"That is not what—"

"I told you before I started that I was not going to take a question," said Halvard Senn. "I have been a Bureau officer for about nineteen years and I have watched about nine hundred men explain themselves to a rank-one officer with no instrument in his coat, and every one of them was doing it for himself and not for the person in front of him, and you are not going to be different, and I have known that since you wrote your name on the bottom of it."

He got up and picked the satchel off the floor.

"You are going to want to tell me that you thought about it first. You thought about it first. I am not interested in that. I am interested in the fact that eighty-one people have been carrying this for ninety days and there is nobody above them."

"Because a division that has to carry a line finds out what the line is about, and that has been true in this city since before either of us was on an establishment, and a man at rank one can put a sentence under that as easily as he can put a sentence on a sheet."

He was at the door.

"I am not going to tell you it was the wrong thing. I have read your sentence. It is a correct sentence and it is a heavy one and it does not do what anybody who reads it is going to think it does, and your own office has printed the same fact on its own sheet in the same quarter, and I am not going to be the man who tells a person at rank one what he did with his one request."

## What Went Out At Four

At four on the thirtieth of June the sheet went out with an address at the head of every line and *Entered: eight hundred and forty-one*.

Thirty-nine on the thirtieth of March. Eight hundred and forty-one on the thirtieth of June. About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read both, and the first is a figure about deeds and the second is a figure about ninety days of about eighty-one people standing on steps, and they are on one page in one hand under one heading.

And after the figure, two columns. The first with eight hundred and forty-one lines against it. The second headed *carried by*, ruled, and empty.

It has been empty since the ninth of March of the third year, when the heading was written on a piece of the office's foolscap and entered in the office's hand. It has been empty through a quarter with nothing in it and a quarter with nine hundred doors in it, and about nine hundred accounts of what people did that was never written down have gone into the first column and not one grain of it has ever landed in the second.

A woman told a clerk in a counting room two floors under a sales floor, in the quarter that ended on the thirtieth of September of the third year, that if that column was ever filled it would fill faster than anything in the history of this city and that not one particle of it would have been earned by anybody in it.

She has been right about it for four quarters and the column is doing nothing at all.

## What He Wrote At Four In The Morning

*Thirtieth of June of the fourth year. A man in one of the two chairs, ninety days, nine divisions, about eighty-one people, eight hundred and forty-one doors with something at them.*

*He did not let me answer and he was right and I could not have said a single one of the three things I had.*

*Sixty-six days from the twenty-fourth of April to this morning: six days of April after the twenty-fourth, and thirty of May, and thirty of June.*

*The second column has been empty for four quarters and about nine hundred things went past it this quarter.*

*What he said and what I have not got an answer to, and he was not going to give me one, and he is the only man in this city who has been on a landing: a street is about four seconds long and a stranger on a step is the only news anybody has had in nine years.

*I put that sentence on a tally board at four in the morning and did not know it was the sentence that mattered, and the sentence I did know was the one about owing.*

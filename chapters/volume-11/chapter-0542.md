# Four Days Of Looking

He went up the arcade at about half past seven in the evening of the second of August, and the arcade was the width of two men and full, and about nine hundred people go up and down it a day past the doors where Nadia Ferrous works, and Nadia Ferrous was on her door and said good evening to him without stopping.

The Vestry is the second building on that side and it has an arcade down the front of it, and the yard behind it holds about eleven thousand people in the ordinary course of an evening, on the ground and on the steps and out into the street at each end of it.

It was about four thousand on the thirtieth of December of the second year.

It is about eleven thousand. She will not say how it went up and he did not ask, because she told him in the eleventh month of the third year that not one of them had ever been sent and that they come in on their own feet or they do not come at all, and she has not repeated it since and she does not need to.

Twenty-one days from the eleventh of July to this evening.

## The Yard Without Forty In It

Forty of them had gone out on the twenty-ninth of July and they came back at about four this afternoon, and a day is four days: one day to the thirtieth of July and two more days of July and one day of August.

So the yard was short of forty all day and nobody could have told you which forty, and she had put them out herself, and she had not gone with them, and she had spent four days in a building with about eleven thousand people in it and forty fewer.

He had heard about it from a woman who keeps a stall at the yard end who has known him since the eighteenth of October of the first year, and the woman's account of it was that the forty went to look at people.

"Which people."

"The ones your divisions found," she said. "The ones on the street where the women went and stood about a fortnight ago and the women on that street have not been right since and I could not tell you one thing they said and I am not going to try."

## Four Days Of It

She was in the room behind the arcade with the door shut and her coat on, and she had been in the room since about half past four.

"Ninety-one addresses," she said. "Out of a list of about four hundred. I did not choose them. Four of my own read the day's page at a public desk the way anybody reads a page and they wrote the ones they wanted and then they came to me and I moved nine of them off and left the rest where they had put them."

"You looked at the list."

"I looked at a list of four hundred and nineteen lines in a book in a room off a third court that any person in this city may stand at a public desk and read, and I want you to understand that I did not have to ask anybody for it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is what your sentence has done. You put four conditions on a public page and about eleven thousand people can work out from four conditions which lines of theirs are about them."

"What did the forty do."

"They went and sat with them."

She turned her head about nine degrees, the way she does at a table, and not once anywhere else in four years that he has been in a room with her.

"They went and sat with a person for about two hours and they did not ask one question. That was the instruction and I wrote it out myself and I gave it to each of them separately so that nobody could pass it on wrongly. Go and sit. Ask nothing. Stay until the person speaks or until it gets dark."

"And they came back with nothing."

"They came back with nothing," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Forty women went to ninety-one doors for four days and asked nothing and stayed until it got dark, and not one of them came back with a thing that could go in your book, and that is not because they did it wrong. It is because nothing that a stranger can sit with you for two hours for is a deed. It is a thing that happened to you."

## Being Useful And Being Owed

"So the two words came apart in my yard," she said, "in four days, and not in a conversation. They came apart because about nine hundred people in this yard have watched forty of their own go out and come back with nothing, and about nine hundred of them have worked out the answer themselves, which is that being useful is how you get to be owed nothing."

She had her hands in her pockets.

"That is what I have prevented for nine years. I made the word *owed* mean a thing you do and not a thing you are, and the whole of this building is that one piece of work, and I have just watched four days of it come undone on a list of ninety-one addresses that I could read for nothing."

"Tell me it was the wrong thing to do and I will go and stand in the yard and say so myself."

"No," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I am not going to say that. I sent them because there are nine hundred people on four hundred lines who have been found and not been paid and not been spoken to since the ninth of April, and nobody in this city has an instrument for any of it, and forty women with two hours and no questions is the only thing anybody has ever come up with."

"Then say what it cost."

"That is the whole of what it cost and it is why the yard has got a new word," she said. "They are calling it *looking*. My own. Nine hundred people, in about four days, out of a yard of eleven thousand. And they are saying it the way they say things when they have understood something about themselves, and they are saying it at me."

## The Thing She Would Not Say In The Yard

She had been in that room since half past four and he had not asked her once in that time whether she was all right, because she has not once in four years needed anybody to.

"There is a woman on the steps," she said. "She got up about a quarter past five and she has said her one thing and she has not answered anybody and she is not going to answer anybody, and there are about nine hundred people in this yard who have heard it and it is going round now and will be round four times by morning."

"What did she say."

"That the column is ours," said Agnete Lindqvist. "That is the whole of what she said. That the office has printed four conditions and a sentence that says where it has nothing it has not been given anything, and it has no money and no instrument, and there are eleven thousand of us and about four thousand of us can read that page, and the column is going to be ours and we are going to fill it and we are not going to wait nine years for anybody's permission."

"And you."

"I did not stop her and I am not going to."

"You could have stood up."

"I could have stood up," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have stood in front of nine hundred people in this yard and told them what they were hearing and I did it twice before and it held both times. It did not hold this time and I would like you to notice the reason, because I have had about three hours with it and I have not got a better one."

"Tell me the reason."

"I have not been to that counter since the first of October of the third year," she said. "I have not written and I have not spoken about it in this yard and I have let about a year go past, and about four hundred people in here worked that out by themselves, and a woman who has not stood in front of them for a year does not get to stand in front of them about the one thing that matters most."

## What She Asks Him For

She came out of her pockets and put one hand flat on the table.

"I am not going to tell the yard anything and I am not going to claim the column for the Church and I am not going to claim the words on that wall, and I wrote that down in a certified copy in an office on the thirtieth of December of the second year and it is in your book with my name on it and three conditions met and one of them not met on purpose."

"I know what is in the book."

"And I am going to say it once in a room that has no door on it," said Agnete Lindqvist, "because there is a woman on my steps at a quarter past five tonight who has told nine hundred people the column is ours, and she is right about the arithmetic and she is wrong about everything else, and I have not got an argument and I have not got anybody to make one for me."

She turned her head.

"Come to the Vestry on the sixth. At about half past nine at night, when this yard has gone home. Not to the counter. Not to the office. Here, and there is no door on this room off a street and about eleven thousand people know where it is and none of them may come in it."

"Why."

"Because everything I say in this room cannot be used against anybody anywhere else in this city, and that has been the price of it for twenty years," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I am not going to pay it twice for the same thing. You will understand what you are being told when I say the rest of it."

*Second of August of the fourth year. The Vestry and the yard, half past seven, and forty of them out since the twenty-ninth of July for four days.*

*About eleven thousand where there were four thousand when I sat at a table in a kitchen and made the nine-year promise that the column would be ours.*

*Ninety-one doors and no questions and nothing came back, and the two words came apart in a yard in four days and not in a conversation.*

*And she has not been to that counter for about a year and did not stop a woman on her own steps and is not going to stand up again, and she says that plainly and she says it as a fact about herself and not as a thing that needs doing.*

*And she has asked me to a room on the sixth that has no door on it, and there is one thing I have been carrying for four years that I have never said out loud in a room where it could not be used against somebody, and she has just told me she is going to say it in there.*

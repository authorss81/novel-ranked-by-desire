# The Flat File

Three thousand six hundred and fourteen was the fifteenth of this month and the twenty-first is day three thousand six hundred and twenty, and three thousand six hundred and twenty less three thousand six hundred and fourteen is six, and twenty-one less fifteen is six by the month count.

## The Flat File

The flat file is on the second floor of a building about four hundred yards off, and it is a steel drawer with a handle and a card holder on the front of it, and it has held that book for nine years, and for eight of them it has never been out of that drawer at all.

About forty quarterly sheets lie on top of it, flat, in a pile, and they are not in a room anybody sits in and they have not been counted this year.

Two people can carry it. One of them takes the bottom edge and one of them takes the strap, and the strap is leather and it goes round the whole bound thing twice, and the binding is a cord that has been done up and undone about nine thousand times.

## A Lift That Is A Hoist And A Hoist That Is Not

The building has one hoist, and the difference between a hoist and a lift is not the cage. A lift has somebody in it who expects to arrive. A hoist has two people in it who are working. It comes down when it is called and goes up when somebody at the bottom sends it up with a lever.

On the days the quarterly sheets come up from the works and go back down it is a lift, because there are nine people on that platform with four hundred sheets and a man shouting, and on every other day of the year it is a hoist and it is empty.

The cage opening is four feet by three and the book is nine inches longer than the opening with the strap round it. So the door comes off its runner, and one of them holds it off its runner with an arm for the whole of the way down, and the other one has the book on his knees with both arms round it.

Nine minutes down and nine minutes up, and four flights of stair after the second floor, and the stair turns twice, at the half landing between the second and third floors.

What the nine minutes is made of, the second time it was done, is about two of them in the hoist and about seven on the stair, and of the seven about four are on the third and fourth floors and about three are carrying the bottom edge the whole way because that is the end that wants to go.

He had done it alone six days before and had very nearly not managed it, at about the fourth of the seven minutes, with the strap over his shoulder and nine years of other people’s words going down through his hip, and he was not going to do it twice in one morning in front of anybody.

## What It Weighs

They put it on a scale on the second floor of that building at about half past nine on that last morning, because a scale is there and it is not there for anything else.

They did not write the figure down and neither of them told the other one what it was and it is not on any page in this city. It is a bound thing about nine years old and it weighs what a bound thing of that age weighs.

The cord that holds it is not a fastening. It has been done up and undone about nine thousand times and it is the only part of that object that anybody in this city has ever touched that was meant to be touched.

## The Test, And Why It Was Set

“I want a person to look at a row in that book beside the fold we sent down on the first of this month to the woman who asked about a drain,” he said on the fourth working morning before, “and I want to find out whether one of them can be checked against the other.”

“Checked by a person.”

“Checked by the two of us. There is nothing else to check it with.”

Wenna Raut put the pen down, which she does about twice a day and both times when something is going wrong.

“You are going to carry that book four hundred yards and up four flights so that two people can look at a piece of paper and a row and find out that they are both true and that being both true is not the same as being one thing.”

“Yes.”

“Why on the last morning of a week.”

“Because there is no other person in that building on the last morning of a week who is allowed to touch it,” said Lin Hao, “and because on the first morning back there would be nine people on the second floor who would see a man in a white plate going down with a bundle and would remember it in about four years.”

## The First Failure

They had it on the table with the door open, and about four people were going up and down the corridor outside it, and they had the fold on the table beside it with the flap gummed shut and the date and the hour on the outside of it and nothing else anywhere on it, exactly as it had gone down four flights on the first.

“Read it out,” he said.

Wenna Raut did not move.

“I cannot.”

“You wrote it.”

“I wrote it on the first of this month at about eleven in the morning and I have not read it back since,” said Wenna Raut, “and if I read it out now I will be reading a row out of that book with the shape of what I wrote on the first in front of me.”

“You will hear it and you will think it is a memory, and it will not be a memory, it will be an inference, and it will be better than nothing and worse than what we have now.”

“So what we have is a folded sheet with four lines on it.”

“What we have is four lines we know were given to a woman, and nothing in this city that ties them to a row in that book.”

## The Second Failure

He put the row beside the fold. He read it out himself.

It had the date at the head and the hour against the date and then the words she had used, in the order she had used them, and the answer under that in a clerk’s hand in the order the clerk had given it, and every word of both was right, and neither of them could be lifted off the paper and put on the fold.

“There,” he said. “They match.”

“They match,” said Wenna Raut. “Now tell me what the office said to her in that room.”

“She asked about a piece of paper she had brought up herself and we told her about the piece of paper.”

“Tell me what the office said in the room, in that voice, with that pause in it, with somebody else in the passage hearing every word of it.”

“I cannot.”

“You cannot,” she said, “and I cannot, and neither of us has said that sentence out loud to anybody since the first of this month, and about four people in that corridor heard it, and none of them can be asked because we do not know which four.”

She turned the row a quarter so the words faced him.

“That book is a record of what was written down,” said Wenna Raut. “It has never been a record of what was said, and it is not going to become one this month, and you have just spent a morning and two pairs of arms to find that out, and the finding does not go on the page.”

“Then what does it do for us.”

“It does what it has always done, which is that in about four years somebody can come up four flights and ask what this office held on a day, and get an answer that is true, in a hand that is not either of ours, about a paper, in the words of a person who has not been found.”

“That is not what I asked either.”

“You asked it as though the two of them could be laid against each other like two sums,” said Wenna Raut, “and they cannot, and we have known that since the first year, and this morning is the first time anybody has spent nine minutes and two pairs of arms to be certain of it.”

She put two fingers on the row and did not turn it.

“And the thing you have not asked is the one that matters,” she said. “The woman who asked that question on the first of this month did not want a copy of what we said. She wanted to know if she could trust it. And there is no arrangement of paper in this city that answers that, and there is not going to be one, and that is not a failure of a morning, it is the shape of the thing.”

## Onto The Table, And Not Opened

He put it on the table at the end of the room at about half past ten and stood looking at it.

That book had never been in that office at all until six days ago, when it went down four flights and across to a reading room, was read for nine minutes by a woman who is not a clerk, and came back up again the same afternoon, and this was the first time it had ever been on a table with a door open and the whole of that stair behind it.

He did not open it.

“Take it down,” said Wenna Raut.

“I am not going to open it.”

“You are standing next to it with your hands down and it is face up on that table and anybody who comes up that stair can see the edge of it,” she said, “and in about four years this room is going to have a thing in it that has been open on a table in front of strangers, and I am not going to have that be how it starts.”

He carried it back down himself at about a quarter to twelve and put it back in the steel drawer on that filing floor, under about forty sheets lying flat, and the card holder on the front of that drawer has never had anything written on it in nine years, and nobody in that building saw him do any part of it, and he is not paid for it.

Going back up the four flights he worked out what the morning had actually cost, and it was not nine minutes and two pairs of arms. It was that the question a woman asks when she comes up those stairs has never been *what did you write down*, and this office has been answering the other one for nine years in the best handwriting in the city.

He had known that since the second year and had never paid to be told it. He had paid for it this morning.

And what a person does with a thing they already knew is a different question from whether it is true, and he had gone four hundred yards down a hill and come back up with only the first half of it answered.

*Three thousand six hundred and twenty days. Less three thousand six hundred and fourteen, the fifteenth of this month, is six, and a book went four floors down and about four hundred yards off and came back up in one morning, and two people found that a row and a fold can both be true and cannot be laid against each other.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is fifty-one, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred days, which is forty months exactly, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it, and it is the fortieth month of it today.*

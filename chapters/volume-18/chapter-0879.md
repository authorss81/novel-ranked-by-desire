# The Ask That Came Afterwards

Three thousand seven hundred and eighty-three was the fourth of this month and the eleventh is day three thousand seven hundred and ninety, and three thousand seven hundred and ninety less three thousand seven hundred and eighty-three is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.

## Her Second Coming

She came up again on the eleventh, seven days after the fourth, slower than before, with a stick she had not carried the first time, and she smiled at Wenna Raut as she came in, as if the room were a place she knew now.

"I have come about something else," she said. "Not what I came for last time. What I asked for afterwards, which I did not know I wanted until I was home."

Lin Hao brought the bench forward for her and she lowered herself onto it carefully, and Wenna Raut poured water and she drank, and the morning light through the high window lay across her thin hands.

"Say it," said Lin Hao.

"I want a copy of the row," she said. "The one about me. With the date and the hour. So I can show people I was here. So the coming leaves something I can hold."

## The Refusal

He closed his eyes for a moment, because of all the things she could have asked for, this was the one he could not give, and it was also the one he most wanted to give, and the two facts together were almost more than he could stand.

"I cannot," said Lin Hao. "Not a copy on a slip and not a copy on anything. A copy is a second place where the thing it is about can be found. And the office burned its only copy of anything in November and will not make another."

She looked at him, and the disappointment in her face was plain and unguarded, and he forced himself to meet it, because looking away would have made the refusal smaller and it was not small.

"It is only my own row," she said. "About me. Who cannot leave a house and did. Why can I not hold what is mine?"

"Because the row above yours is somebody else's," said Lin Hao. "And a copy of yours carries the page it lay on. And from the tenth of June the page is not turned towards anybody, and the answer is read out, and about nine people a month are told so, and you are one of them now."

Wenna Raut stood at the table with her hands twisted together, and she had entered the April row and the August coming and now this, and each entering had cost her, and this one cost most because the woman asking had climbed four flights twice.

## Who Pays For Refusal

The sister nodded slowly, and accepted it the way ill people accept things, without argument, and asked one more question before she stood.

"Who pays for the refusal," she said. "When your office refuses. Who pays."

"You do," said Lin Hao, plainly, because she deserved the plainest sentence in the room. "You came up four flights twice in the cold and you go down with nothing you can hold. And Wenna Raut pays, because she writes the refusal in your words and keeps it. And I pay, because I am the man who wrote the clause that brought you here."

She stood then, with her stick, and he wanted to help her and did not, because she had come without asking anything of anybody and help unasked would shame the asking she had done.

At the door she turned, and her smile was real, though tired.

"I am glad I came," she said. "Both times. Even for nothing I can hold. Because for four years I have been a question in somebody else's mouth, and now I am a woman who came up four flights on her own, and that is mine whatever your book says."

She told them then, standing at the door with her stick, about the house she had not left for months and the room she sleeps in and the window she watches the street from, and Wenna Raut listened with her hands still, and Lin Hao listened the way he listens to rows, without interrupting, because a person describing her own house is entering something no clerk can enter.

"The street looks different from the stair than from the window," she said. "I had forgotten. Four flights changes the shape of everything. I am glad to know it again, even for the price."

She went down, and he listened to the stick on the stair, tap by tap, until it faded, and the grief in him was mixed with something like pride, and the pride made the grief sharper and not less.

## The Stick On The Stair

He stood at the head of that stair long after the tapping faded, and Wenna Raut came to stand beside him, and neither spoke, because the refusal had been entered in her words and the entering did not mend it.

"She will come again," said Wenna Raut at last. "Not for the copy. For the hearing. Because twice now she has been a woman who came up four flights on her own, and that is hers whatever the refusal says."

"I know," said Lin Hao. "And when she comes we will read to her what is hers to hear. And we will not carry it to her. And the difference will have to be said again, and I will say it."

Wenna Raut entered the refusal in the words the sister had used, with the date and the hour, and her hand shook on the first line and was steady by the second, and when she finished she set the pen down and looked at him.

"That is the worst thing this room has done since March," she said.

"I know," said Lin Hao, and went down that hill to the corner where the board goes up, and stood there until dark, thinking about who pays and how often, and about what is left of August.

The board stood empty in the dark, and the paste on the top batten held fast, and he put the back of a knuckle against it the way he had in March, and it gave nothing, because months had passed and the work holds. He thought about the sister's stick tapping down four flights, and about the copy refused, and about how a room that answers honestly had answered her twice with nothing she could hold, and about how the eighteenth would have to end it.

About four people passed him on the path going down, and one nodded, and he nodded back, and the ordinary evening of the hill went on around a man carrying a refusal in his pocket like a stone.

When he reached the bottom he turned and looked up at the four flights of windows, dark now except one, and thought about the eleventh and the fourth and the two comings that belong to her, and about how both would stand in the room on the eighteenth when he said the sentence that ends them.

He walked home slowly through streets going dark, past windows lit and doors shut, thinking about a basket with nine slips in it, and about the certificate one of those women would be holding in seven days, and about the refusal, which stands, and about the eighteenth being seven days off.

At his own stair he stopped and rested, the way she had rested on the third landing, with his forehead near the wall though not against it, and thought about how little separates a man who climbs nightly for wages from a woman who climbed twice for nothing she could hold.

## Six Days Off

The wall was cold through the air before it. He went up, and lay awake the whole of the night with the days of the month arranged in his head the way he arranges everything now, and the arranging did not help, because what he was arranging was not time. It was the shape of a room at twelve o'clock on the eighteenth and the faces in it and the order they would speak in.

He rose before light on the twelfth and came up the four flights and stood with the shut Register on the table and did not open it, because there is nothing in it he does not already know and the carriers take it down at half past five and it belongs to them until then.

Wenna Raut came in at the ordinary hour and put her bag down where she always puts it, and looked at him, and he saw that she had been up those same four flights in the dark, and neither of them said so, because saying so would have made it a thing they were doing together and neither of them had been asked.

"Six days," said Wenna Raut.

"Six days," said Lin Hao. "And here is what I have not said to you and will not say to anybody before that room is full. I have not decided whether the sister is told before the eighteenth or on it."

"Is that a real question."

"It is the only question there is," said Lin Hao. "If I tell her before, she comes up a third time alone and hears it in a room with two of us in it, and she will not have been standing in the room where it is said to everybody else. If I tell her on it, she hears it in front of families she has never met and a young man with plaster on his hands who is going to be angry at somebody on her behalf."

Wenna Raut did not answer, which was how she answered.

"One of those is a kindness and one of those is not," said Lin Hao. "And I do not know which, and nothing in nine years has ever made me choose between two things where one of them is hurting a person I made hurt."

"Then the eighteenth will decide it for you," said Wenna Raut, "the way the tenth of June decided itself, and you will find out which it was afterwards, in a lane, from a woman washing steps."

"No," said Lin Hao. "On the tenth of June there was a woman standing in front of me and I could not unsay anything to her. The eighteenth I am choosing before anybody is in the room. And that is the whole difference, and it is not a small one, and I have not slept on it."

*Three thousand seven hundred and ninety days. Less three thousand seven hundred and eighty-three, the fourth of this month, is seven, and what she asked for afterwards was not what she came for, and the refusal was given to her face, and she pays it and the clerk pays it and he pays it.*

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is forty-one, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and seventy days, which is forty-five months and twenty days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Street In Nine Years

Three thousand nine hundred and two was the third of this one and the tenth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and nine, and three thousand nine hundred and nine less three thousand nine hundred and two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## To Write An Address

He stood at that table after the morning's askings with a blank card in front of him, cut from the back of a superseded requisition book, stiff and grey, and a pencil beside it, and Wenna Raut watching him from the other end without speaking.

To write an address on a card a man has to be able to say where he is. Not the court. Not the building. The street. The name a stranger would need to walk up to a door without being led.

Not one person in that room has said the street out loud in nine years. Carriers say the court. Clerks say the room. The man who is paid nothing says the passage off Cordwain. The street itself has gone unspoken the way a thing goes unspoken when everybody who knows it has agreed without agreeing not to spend it.

Lin Hao looked at the blank card and found he did not know whether he knew it.

## About Eleven Minutes

"Say it," said Wenna Raut.

"I am trying," said Lin Hao.

He closed his eyes and walked it in his head, out of the branch office at night, up the hill, through the arch, across the yard, up four flights, and tried to hear what the lane crier calls at the corner, and what is painted on the kerb board, and what he writes when he directs a carrier with a parcel.

Nothing came. Or rather, two names came, both half right, one the name of the lane off Cordwain where Brack keeps his stools, and one the name of the wider street two turnings over, and neither would bring a stranger to that door.

Wenna Raut set the office clock where he could see it, the way she does when a finding is being timed, and he worked at it for about eleven minutes, going back through nine years of mornings: the first morning he came up those flights, the morning the strap was drawn out of a drawer, the morning a woman read the line above her own, the morning the page was turned away, the morning the book went down at half past five.

At the end of the eleven minutes he said a name, quietly, in the ordinary voice, and Wenna Raut wrote nothing down, because writing it down would have made it a list, and looked at him steadily.

"That is the one I have on the back of my hand," said Lin Hao, "under the hours. I have carried it there for months without knowing I was carrying it. And I am still not certain it is right."

## The Man Who Will Not Say It First

He went down that hill at about four with the blank card in his breast pocket and found the man with the cart where he always is in the afternoon, with a coat and a pair of gloves in a cloth, and asked him outright, because in the fifth month that man had asked about the man who works nights and had been refused the naming of it, and a debt stands there.

"What street is that room on," said Lin Hao. "The room four flights up where you came in the fifth month. Say it out loud if you know it."

The man looked at him for a long moment, and then shook his head, not unkindly.

"You have not asked me for it," he said. "You have not, and the man with the slate asked you for it in the autumn and you told him you could not say it either, and he sent you to a man at a counter off Cordwain, which is not me."

The man turned the cloth over in his hands while he said it, the way a man turns a thing he has decided to go on holding.

"I have brought loads down about four hundred yards twice a month for six years and I could walk it blind, and I have never once said it out loud, and I am not going to be the first mouth in this city it comes out of."

"Then tell me whether I have it right."

And he told him the name he had said in that room, low, so nobody else on that hill heard it, and the man with the cart listened all the way through and then nodded once.

"That is close enough to get a stranger lost," said the man. "One turning off. The true one is the next over, where the washhouse drips onto the kerb. You have lived here nine years and carried every word of it and you do not know where you are. That is not a complaint. That is what an office without an address does to the men inside it."

## The Washhouse On The Kerb

He walked to the next turning with the man, because being told is not the same as being shown, and the washhouse dripped onto the kerb the way the man said it did, steady in the cold, with a dark stain down the stone where nine years of dripping have left their mark.

No name stood over the door of that court. No name stood on the yard gate. Only damp stone, and a stairwell smelling of soap, and four flights of windows with lamplight coming on in them, and a passage where about four thousand people a day go past on a working day without looking up.

"You could walk past it for nine years," said the man with the cart. "I have watched people do it. They ask at the passage, they ask at the rail, they ask in the lane, and every one of them is told a different corner of it, because the whole is nowhere."

Lin Hao stood on that kerb with the blank card in his fist and looked up at the windows of his own room, and felt like a stranger to himself, which is the correct feeling for a man about to put an address on a door.

"And when the card goes up," said the man, "I will still not say it first. But I will not say you are wrong when somebody reads it out. That is as far as a man with a cart goes, and it is further than I have gone for anybody in six years."

Lin Hao stood on that hill with the cold going through his coat and felt shame and relief together, because he had been wrong and the wrongness proved the need, and the need was the thing he had come down to prove.

## What Knowing Costs

He went back up four flights with the true name in his head and wrote nothing down on the way, because he keeps hours on the back of his hand and nothing else, and a street is even less a thing to be written down lightly than an hour.

Wenna Raut looked up when he came in, and he said it again, correctly this time, in the ordinary voice, and she nodded once and did not repeat it, because a thing said once in that room with the door open is a fact and does not need to be said twice.

"Then we can write it," said Wenna Raut. "When the time comes. And not before."

He put the blank card back in his breast pocket, unwritten, and felt the weight of nine years lift a finger's breadth and come down again, because knowing the street and putting it on a door are two different things, and only one of them can be taken back.

## The Clock She Keeps

Wenna Raut asked him how long the finding had taken, not to enter it but to hold the shape of it, and he told her, about eleven minutes, with the office clock where he could see it, and she nodded and put the clock back where it belongs.

"Eleven minutes to learn whether you know where you are," she said. "After nine years. And the answer is that you know it well enough to be wrong by one turning, which is close enough to prove the need and far enough to prove the danger."

"And what will you do with my eleven minutes."

"Keep them the way you keep hours, in memory and nowhere else. About nine people a month come up those flights, and about four of the nine are about a person, and every one of them is lost in a different corner of the same hill. Your eleven minutes are the measure of all of them."

He stood at that table with the true name sounding in his head like water dripping onto a kerb, steady in the cold, and felt afraid of the card in his pocket for the first time, because a card makes being lost curable, and curable is a kind of debt.

## The Door Without A Name

He walked to that door after the carriers had gone down, and stood before it with the blank card in his fist, and looked at the wood where three nails would go, and at the card about washing still pinned beside it, and at the yard gate below with no name on it.

Nine years that door has stood without a name over it, and four flights below it a rail has carried forty-three sheets with six lines at the foot, and about four hundred yards off a flat file has held nine years of other people's words, and none of the three has ever told a stranger how to find the others.

He raised the blank card to the wood and held it there without nails, to see how large three lines look on a door that has held nothing, and the card looked small and final, and his hand did not shake, and he lowered it and put it back in his breast pocket, because the time is the last working day of the year and not today.

That night he carried that book down in the dark with the street name going round in his head like a refrain, and at the bottom he stood in the yard and said it once more to the empty air, quietly, to hear how it sounded outside a room, and it sounded like any other name, which is exactly what an address should sound like.

Three thousand nine hundred and nine days. Less three thousand nine hundred and two, the third of this one, is seven, and to write an address on a card a man has to be able to say where he is, and it took about eleven minutes in that room to find out whether he knew it.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seventy, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and eighty-nine days, which is forty-nine months and nineteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

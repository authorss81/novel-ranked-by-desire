# One Card And Two Yeses

Three thousand nine hundred and nine was the tenth of this one and the seventeenth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and sixteen, and three thousand nine hundred and sixteen less three thousand nine hundred and nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## What A Card Would Do

He laid the blank card on that table after the morning's askings, with the door open and about three people going past outside, and said it plainly to the two people in that room, because a thing that is going to be carried has to be said to everybody together or it becomes a rumour by the end of a fortnight.

"An address on a door," said Lin Hao. "The street, the door, and the hours. Nothing else on it. Not one name. A card a stranger can walk up to without being led, and without asking at a rail, and without finding it by strategy in a lane."

Wenna Raut looked at the card and did not touch it, because she may only enter what she is given and nobody had given her this, and the carriers on the stair below went past with somebody else's load and did not come in.

"It closes nine years of an office that could not be sent to," said Lin Hao. "And it costs what Brack has been saying it will cost since the spring, only sooner. About forty people will not have to be told where it came from. And the bottom two inches under a striking in about four hundred kitchens will go quiet a second time, and the second time they will not come back."

He said it out loud first, before anybody else had to, because the man who writes it ought to know what it does to a lane and to four hundred kitchens and say so.

## The First Yes

The woman with the back said yes before he had finished asking, the way she says most things, with the reason already in her hand.

"I will carry it up and I will carry it down past it," she said. "A door with hours on it does not change the stair. The strap still goes round that book twice because it is the wrong length. The half landing still has no window in it. I am not afraid of a card."

"And if strangers come."

"Strangers come already. About nine a month. About four of the nine about a person. A card will not make more strangers. It will make the same strangers less lost, and less lost is less angry, and I would rather carry past a door that says when we are there than past one that says nothing and lets them knock at midnight."

She picked up the card between two fingers and looked at the blank of it, and put it down again, and he saw that her hand shook a little and that she did not hide it.

## The Second Yes

Wenna Raut said yes after a long quiet, with the pen put down before she said it, which she does when a thing is about herself and not about the room.

"I cannot leave that room while that book is in it," she said. "From a quarter to ten until half past five, five days a week, since the seventh of April. A card on a door does not give me a night. But it gives me mornings when the people who come up know when to come, and that is a smaller room to hold than one where they come at all hours and have to be turned away."

"And the cost."

"The cost is that I will be the woman behind a door with an address on it, and in about four years somebody will ask me why that office stopped being hard to find, and the true answer will be that a man wrote it down. I am willing to be that woman. I have been the woman behind a door with no address for nine years and I am tired of being hard to find for the sake of being hard to find."

He felt gratitude then, sharp as cold air, for two women who had said yes without being managed, and the gratitude made him reckless for a moment, and he mastered it, because recklessness is not what a card needs.

## The One No That Is Right

He took the card down to the laundry and the back room off a street two streets wide, four flights up, with nine sheets under a third table, and laid it on the edge of that table where Perla Quist could see it without touching it.

She listened to the whole of it without interrupting, the way she listens to everything, and then she shook her head, once, finally.

"No," said Perla Quist. "And I will tell you why, because you have come to the only woman in the city who keeps an index and asked her to hold the thing that would make her index complete."

She laid her hand on the nine sheets under the edge of that table without lifting them, and he understood without being told.

"A woman who keeps an index can be given one," she said. "Give me a card with an address on it and in half a year somebody will ask me where else I have been."

"And I will have to say I hold the door's hours in my own rooms, and from there it is one step to holding who comes through it, and I will not take that step. I gave you the only copy once and watched it burn and was right not to thank you, and I am right now not to hold this."

He stood there with the card in his fist and felt the refusal like a door closing that ought to close, because she was correct and her correctness protected them both, and he had wanted her to say yes and was glad she had not.

"You are right," said Lin Hao. "One card and two yeses and one no, and the no is the one that keeps the card honest."

She nodded, and did not thank him, and he did not thank her, and that is about the correct amount of noise a room makes when it stays honest.

## The Laundry After She Says No

He stood a while in that back room off a street two streets wide, with the smell of soap and hot iron in the air, and looked at the nine sheets under the third table without asking to see them, because asking would have been a kind of taking, and he has stopped taking anything in that room.

"You keep an index," said Lin Hao, "and I keep a book, and both of us know what a second place does. A copy is a second place where the thing it is about can be found. A card with hours on it in another woman's rooms is a second door. Either way the thing it is about can be found twice, and found twice is lost."

"And you will put it on the door anyway."

"I will put it on the door. Two yeses carry it. Your no keeps it honest. That is the whole of the arithmetic, and there is no other figure in it."

She looked at him then with something like kindness, which she shows rarely and never names, and he felt the want to have got something off her rise in him and fall again, because there was nothing to get, and because a woman who has just refused him on the right grounds is not owed a warmer face for having done it.

"Go back up your four flights," said Perla Quist. "And when strangers come with the card in their hands, send none of them to me. I am not on it. I am not behind it. I am a woman with nine sheets under a table, and that is all I am going to be."

He went back up that hill with the card still blank in his breast pocket and the two yeses warm in him and the no keeping them straight, and at the top of those four flights he stood a moment and looked at the door that has never had a name over it, and imagined a card on it, and felt afraid, and kept standing there until the fear went into the work where it belongs.

## The Strap On The Stair

The carriers passed him on that stair on their way down, with the strap between them and the evening's weight in it, and the woman with the back stopped long enough to look at his face and read what three people had said to him.

"Two yeses," she said.

"Two yeses," said Lin Hao. "And one no that keeps them honest."

"Then the card is carried," she said. "A thing you only ever carry one way is not being carried, she told you in the autumn, and a card that goes up and never comes down is being stored in a door. Ours goes up two and comes down one. Remember the proportion when you nail it."

He nodded, and they went down with the strap creaking at the turn, and he stood on that landing with no window in it and listened to their steps go out of hearing, and thought about doors that hold and stairs that move, and about how a card on a door is the one thing in nine years that does not have to be carried to stay.

## The Card On The Table

He laid the blank card on that table after they had gone, with the lamplight on it, and Wenna Raut looked at the blank of it for a long time without touching it, because it is not a row and she may only enter what she is given.

"Three lines," said Lin Hao. "The street. The door. The hours. Nothing else. Not one name. When it goes up, that room will be findable by walking instead of by asking, and every asking after that will be a little less afraid."

"And what will you do with being findable."

"Stand at that table five days in seven between a quarter to ten and half past five, and carry that book down in the dark by my own hands, and answer anybody about anything it holds when the person it is about can come up four flights. The card changes where they knock. It does not change what is answered."

She nodded, and put the pen in the tray for the night, and the blank card lay between them like a promise that has not yet learned its cost, and both of them looked at it without flinching.

Three thousand nine hundred and sixteen days. Less three thousand nine hundred and nine, the tenth of this one, is seven, and one card was put to three people and two said yes and one said no, and the one no is right because a woman who keeps an index can be given one.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seventy-seven, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and ninety-six days, which is forty-nine months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Two Rows The Same Shape

Wenna Raut had that page open before the carriers came up, with a strip of grey card laid down the middle of it and her forearm across the lower half, and a woman at the bench with her hands folded round a paper she had brought herself and had not yet been asked to put down.

Lin Hao came up behind the table at a quarter to ten with the cold still in his coat and the strap going out of that room over a carrier's shoulder, and the room full of the noise a room makes when a book has just come up four flights and everybody in it is pretending the weight is not part of the work.

"Ask it in your own words," said Wenna Raut, without looking up. "And do not read it off that paper until you have said it."

The woman said it. A drain behind a washhouse, shared with the house next door, blocked since the end of the month before last, and whether the paper she held said the clearing of it belonged to her side or to both sides together.

Wenna Raut found the row in about nine minutes, with the card moving down that page one line at a time and her finger keeping the place, and Lin Hao stood at the end of that table with his hands behind his back and watched the finding the way a man watches a thing he has paid for and cannot make cheaper.

## The First Of The Two

The row was dated the middle of the month before this one, at about eleven in the morning, in the words a woman uses when she has carried a paper up four flights and is afraid of being told she has wasted a morning.

Under it, in the clerk's hand, an answer of about four lines, saying the paper said both sides together and that the clearing belonged to both houses and not to one.

She read it out in the voice that office reads everything in, with the woman who had asked standing at that bench and the door open and about three people going past outside, and the woman nodded and said that was what the paper said and that she had wanted to be certain.

Lin Hao wrote nothing down. The hour that woman had come up those four flights he kept the way he keeps all of them now, on the back of his own hand in pencil, and nowhere else, and the mark was already there under his cuff before the reading was half done.

The woman went down with the date and the hour in her own hand on a paper of her own, witnessed as her own writing and nothing else, and the room went quiet in the way it does between one asking and the next.

## The Second Of The Two

The next woman came up at about eleven with the same paper in a different cloth, or a paper that looked the same until it was opened, and said the same drain and the same washhouse in almost the same words, and Wenna Raut went still at that table in a way Lin Hao had not seen since the spring.

"Nine days apart," said Wenna Raut, very quietly, so the woman at the bench could not hear it. "Look at the day at the head of it and then look at the words under the day."

He looked. The second row was dated nine days after the first, at about the same hour of the morning, entered by a different carrier's note and in the clerk's same hand, and the words of the question were the same to the shape of them, and the answer under them was the same to the line.

Two rows. The same shape. Entered by two people, in the same words, about the same drain, nine days apart, and the only thing that told them apart was the day at the head of each, and a day is not a person.

He felt the cold come up off that page and into his fingers where they rested on the end of that table, and he understood, the way a man understands a thing he has been carrying without knowing it, that there was nothing in that room that could say whether those two rows were about two drains or about one drain asked about twice.

## What A Day Cannot Do

He asked the second woman for the paper, and she gave it, and he looked at it without reading it the way he does with papers people have already read, and set it on the corner where it could not be confused with anything else.

"Did you bring this up yourself the last time," said Lin Hao.

"I have not been up those flights before this morning," said the woman. "My neighbour was up nine days ago about her side of the same drain. She told me what was said to her and I have come about my side of it."

"And the paper."

"The paper is hers. She lent it me. Ours is lost behind a boiler since the cold began."

Wenna Raut did not lift her forearm off that page. The strip of card covered everything but the one row, and the row under the card was the second one, and above the card, covered, was the first.

"If I read you your answer," said Lin Hao, "I will be reading you words that were written down nine days ago for a woman who is not in this room. And from the middle of the month before last this office reads an answer about a person only with that person in the room."

"This is about a drain."

"It is about a drain that belongs to two houses. And the row above yours is somebody else's, and she is not here, and I cannot turn that page towards you to show you where yours begins and hers ends, because the page has not been turned towards anybody since the early part of the year."

The woman looked at the covered page and then at him, and he saw her decide, the way people decide in that room, that he was refusing her something she had walked up four flights for.

"I am not refusing you the reading," said Lin Hao. "I am telling you I cannot show you which of the two is yours without showing you the other one, and I am not allowed to show you the other one."

## The Price Of Keeping Both

She went down at about half past eleven with nothing in her hands but her neighbour's paper, and the date and the hour of her own asking in pencil on a scrap she had written herself, witnessed, and the promise that the reading would be done again when the neighbour could come up with her.

After she had gone Wenna Raut lifted the strip and looked at the two rows together, uncovered, for about four seconds, and then covered them again.

"They are the same shape," said Wenna Raut. "And they are about two different houses. And if I had read either one sideways, with the door open, I would have read the wrong woman's answer to the right woman."

"And if you had not found the second one."

"Then I would have read the first one to the second woman, and both of them would have gone home certain, and both of them would have been wrong in a way that office could never have got back out of their heads."

Lin Hao stood at that table and felt the weight of that book the way he feels it every morning now, not as paper but as hours, and he thought about the back of his own hand under his cuff, where two figures stood in pencil and nowhere else, and how neither of them could tell him which woman was which.

## What The Carriers Heard

The carriers came up at half past five with the strap and stopped in the doorway when they saw the strip still out on that page, and the woman with the back looked at the covered rows and then at Wenna Raut and understood without being told.

"Two alike," she said.

"Two alike," said Wenna Raut. "Nine days apart. Same drain. Same words. Different houses."

The woman with the back shifted the strap on her shoulder and did not come nearer that table, because carriers do not come near an open page unless they are asked, and nobody had asked.

"Then which one did she get," said the woman with the back, meaning the second woman, gone down half an hour before.

"Neither," said Lin Hao. "She got the date and the hour of her own asking in her own hand, witnessed, and the promise that the reading will be done again when both houses can come up together."

"And if they never come up together."

"Then both rows stand as they are, and the drain stays blocked, and two houses go on sharing water they cannot clear because an office would rather leave a drain blocked than read one woman's answer to another."

The woman with the back nodded, slowly, the way people nod in that room when a thing is hard and correct at the same time, and went out with the other carrier to wait on the stair, and the sound of their boots going down was the sound of a morning ending without anything having been moved.

## The Hour On His Hand

After the room had emptied he stood at the window with his cuff turned back and looked at the back of his own hand in the grey light, where the hours stand in pencil, one under the other, and nowhere else.

He keeps them there because from the twelfth of May he said he would, and because there is nowhere else in the city that will hold them without turning them into a list, and because washing them out would be the end of an arrangement he made with himself about what the number was for.

Two figures. Two mornings. Two women with one drain between them. And neither figure could tell him which row belonged to which house, because an hour tells him when a person came up four flights and nothing about who the person is when the words are the same.

Wenna Raut watched him from that table without speaking, and then she shut that book with the strip still in it to mark the place, and the sound of the cover coming down was louder than it should have been in an empty room.

"You are angry," she said.

"I am angry at the shape," said Lin Hao. "Not at either woman. At two rows that are the same shape and cannot be told apart, and at nine years of work that made a place where such rows can be entered correctly and then cannot be read back to the people they are about."

"And what will you do with the anger."

"Carry it down four flights with the strap round that book, and lay it down at the bottom with the flat file shut, and come back up and sleep on it. It is the only thing I have left to do with any feeling in that room."

That evening he carried that book down four flights and about four hundred yards himself, in the dark, by his own hands, five days in seven, the way he has done since the twenty-fifth of the month before this one, and at the turn he put it on his hip and went round the newel with the shoulder, the way he was taught, and got to the bottom with his fingers still working and stood there a moment in the cold yard.

Three thousand eight hundred and seventy-one days. Less three thousand eight hundred and sixty-four, the twenty-fifth of the month before this one, is seven, and two rows in that book are the same shape nine days apart and the only thing that tells them apart is the day, and a day is not a person.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is thirty-two, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-two of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and fifty-one days, which is forty-eight months and eleven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# He Hears It At The Door

Three thousand six hundred and forty-six was the seventeenth of this month and the twenty-fourth is day three thousand six hundred and fifty-three, and three thousand six hundred and fifty-three less three thousand six hundred and forty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## Half Past Four In The Yard

The light goes off that yard about four o'clock in the last of the third month and he was down it at about half past four with a fold in his hand, the flap gummed shut, the date and the hour on the outside of it and nothing else anywhere on it, which is the way an answer has gone down four flights since the thirtieth of March of last year.

There are about nine answers a month and about nine minutes each way and the carrier writes down whose hand took it and in what order, and there is no place on that sheet for the carrier's name because a carrier's name is a thing that turns a column into a roll.

He had got to the gate of that yard about nine minutes early and the street was empty.

## Four Seconds Before She Asked

The door of that house came open and a woman came out onto her own step about four feet from him and shut it behind her.

“Before you say anything,” she said, “I have to tell you that I am frightened and I do not know why I am frightened and I have been frightened since the spring.”

“There is a form on that fold about a boundary hedge and it came through this door in the first month of this year and there is one row against it.”

“That is not what I am frightened of,” said the woman.

She did not ask him for it.

He gave it to her anyway, out loud, in a street, in daylight, in about four seconds, standing at the gate of a yard with his hands empty and a fold in one of them, and what he said was the first line of the thing inside that fold, word for word, in the voice he reads things in.

And then he heard himself say it.

The first time had been the third of May of last year, in a doorway four hundred yards the other way, to a woman who came out of her own house to ask for a fold that had been refused, and he had carried that fold back up four flights by himself and had had to stand on the second landing with his shoulder blades against the brick for the best part of two minutes before he could go on.

He stood where he was for about four seconds.

The fold was still in his hand. He had given it to her and she had taken it and read it and given it back, and in the four seconds since she had gone in he understood that the thing which had happened was not that he had said a sentence early.

It was that he had heard himself say it.

In nine years he has said a great many things out loud in rooms with the door open on purpose, and every one of them was chosen before it was spoken and was checked against about four people hearing it. There has never been one that came out of him into daylight in a street because his mouth was four seconds ahead of the person in front of him.

There has been one before, in the first week of May of last year, and it cost him a fold, a door, a walk back up four flights and a minute and a half with his back against a wall on the second landing. It cost that one nothing at all, because the other one had a husband in it.

## Four Words From The Woman

She took the fold off him and undid the flap and read all four lines standing on her own step in the last of the light. It took her about nine seconds. She did it the way a person reads a thing they have been dreading, which is quickly and without going back over anything.

Then she gave it back.

“It is not wrong,” she said. “I do not know whether it is right and I have not the least interest and you can see that from here.”

“All right.”

“What I want,” said the woman, “is to be able to stop being frightened.”

She went in and shut the door and he stood in the street with a fold in his hand and the flap open.

That sentence is a thing he has heard before this year, in a room four flights up, in the mouth of a woman who had come up four flights about somebody who could not come up them, and he has not said a word about where he heard it and he is not going to, and there is a row in that book with the date on it and nothing under it and the row is hers and not hers and does not matter either way.

## What She Said About The Four Lines

“You read them standing up.”

“I read them standing up.”

“You could have come inside.”

“I could have,” he said, “and I have not come inside a doorway in about nine years and I am not going to start on a working afternoon with a woman whose husband died in the first month of the year.”

“That is a rule you have made up.”

“It is a rule I made up in the second year and it has cost me about four people knowing things they would not otherwise know and it has saved about nine people knowing things they should not,” said Lin Hao, “and I have never worked out which of those two numbers is larger and I have been asked twice.”

She had not opened her door again and he had not asked her to.

“It is not wrong,” she said, through about four inches of wood.

“I know what it is.”

“No,” said the woman. “You do not know what it is. You know that a clerk in a room wrote four lines down, and that is a different thing from knowing what those four lines are for.”

## The Bottom Of His Own Stair

He wrote down whose hand took it and in what order on the carrier's sheet, in the column of nothing, and put it in his coat and went back up the four flights.

The inside of that coat on the left has been empty since the third of December of last year and there is nothing in either pocket, and the fold went back into that room and onto that table under a date and an hour and was not entered anywhere in the world except the column of nothing, which is not a column and has four lines in it and never had a fifth.

At the bottom of his own stair he stopped, on the step where the stone goes down to the yard, and said the thing out loud to an empty yard because there was not a living soul within about four hundred yards of him at half past four on the twenty-fourth of this month.

“I have done it twice,” he said. “In a doorway four hundred yards the other way in the first week of May of last year, and at a gate about nine minutes ago, and it is ten months and twenty-one days apart and I did not decide to do it either time.”

He put his hand on the stair rail and took it off again.

“A man who says a thing out loud in a doorway four seconds early has no instrument in this city and never had one. I burned the only copy there was in November and I thought that took it out of me, and it did not take a thing out of me.”

“It stopped me finding out that it was still there, and finding out is the whole of what I have been doing for nine years, and I did it again on the second of December and it took me a week to say so out loud and I am saying it now.”

Not one person heard it.

## Six Days

He went up the four flights with nothing in either hand and stood at the end of that table with the door open and did not say anything about any of it for about nine minutes, and then he told Wenna Raut the whole of it in about eleven sentences in the order it had happened, including the part at the bottom of the stair, and she wrote down the date and the hour and the words he used and read it back once.

“That is not going to be a row about a delivery refused at a door.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “The one refused in May of last year is not entered and the reason was said out loud in this room once and is not going to be said again. This one was not refused.”

“Then it goes in as a row about a fold delivered on time to a woman who read four lines and said one sentence.”

“Yes.”

He stood there for a while with the door open and about four people hearing him say nothing at all.

“In six days it is the thirtieth,” he said, “and I am going to stand at the end of this table and say the thing I have been carrying since a woman said four words at a gate, and I have not worked out what it is yet, and that is the second time in nine years I have said something in this room before I have worked out what it is.”

“You have not done that since the second of December of the year before last.”

“No,” he said, “and there is a difference and I have not got to it, and in six days somebody in that room is going to tell me what it is and it is going to be wrong.”

*Three thousand six hundred and fifty-three days. Less three thousand six hundred and forty-six, the seventeenth of this month, is seven, and a man said the first line of an answer out loud in a street about four seconds before a woman asked for it, which he had done once before in the first week of May of last year, and she read all four lines and gave it back and said she wanted to be able to stop being frightened.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and thirty-two, the third of May of last year, is three hundred and twenty-one days, which is ten months and twenty-one days, and on that morning he said a thing at a door four seconds early and carried it back up four flights and stopped on the second landing.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and forty-one, the second of December of last year, is one hundred and twelve days at this morning, which is three months and twenty-two days, and the third room that took it was on the ninth, which was the seventh of them, and not one of the three was told the other two had been told.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is eighty-four, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and thirty-three days, which is forty-one months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

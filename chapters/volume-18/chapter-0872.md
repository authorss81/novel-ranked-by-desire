# The First Dead Person

Three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six was the seventeenth of this month and the twenty-third is day three thousand seven hundred and forty-two, and three thousand seven hundred and forty-two less three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six is six, and twenty-three less seventeen is six by the month count.

## A Man With A Coat Over His Arm

He came up at about ten on the twenty-third with his coat folded over his arm though the morning was cold, and he carried it the way men carry something they do not want to put down on a strange table.

He had the look of somebody who has rehearsed a question for days and is afraid of getting it wrong at the table. He gave his name to the room in a low voice and then stood with his hands on the back of the bench, not sitting, as if sitting would make the asking longer.

"My wife died in the sixth month of this year," he said. "I want to know whether there is anything here about her."

Wenna Raut set the pen down and looked at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao came to the end of that table, because a question about a person is read once with the person it is about in the room and the person who asked in the room, and the person this one was about would not be coming.

"When did she ask here," said Lin Hao.

"She came in the second month," said the man. "She brought a paper about a drain at the back of our house. She was proud of that paper. She had copied it out twice."

"And you want what we said to her."

"I want to know whether there is anything," said the man. "I have her copy of nothing. She told me she came. She told me twice. After she died I went through everything she kept and I found no fold and no paper with a date on the outside. So I came."

## The Long Finding

It took the best part of the morning to find the row, because February had been full and the hand that winter was small and tight, and Wenna Raut went through it line by line with one finger, and the man stood at the bench and watched without moving his coat.

About four people came and went in that passage while it was being done, and the man did not look at any of them, and twice Wenna Raut went back to the start of a page and did it again, and the second time she found it.

She nodded at Lin Hao, and the nod meant one row, dated the second month, in the words a woman had used about a drain, with an answer under it in her hand.

He felt the weight of it press in his chest, because this was the first row in that book about somebody dead, and the book had held about four hundred and seventy rows the last time anybody counted, with no later count made, and every one of them until now had been about a person who would still be coming.

"We have one row," said Lin Hao. "It is dated the second month. It is in her words about a drain. And there is an answer under it."

The man's hands tightened on the back of the bench.

"Read it," he said.

## Read Out And Not Turned

Wenna Raut read it out in the voice that office reads everything in, with the man standing in the room and the person it was about not in it, and the reading was a date and an hour and then a woman's words about water coming under a wall, and then four lines underneath in the clerk's hand.

The man listened the way people listen when they are trying to hear a person inside a paper, and when the reading ended he kept standing with his hands on the bench, and his coat slipped from his arm to the wood and he did not pick it up.

"Again," he said.

She read it again, and this time he closed his eyes for the middle of it, and when she finished he picked up his coat and folded it again, carefully, making the edges meet.

"She did not tell me what you said," he said. "She told me she had been. She said the room was cold and the woman wrote it down. That is all she said about it in four months."

He looked at that Register lying open on the table, and at Wenna Raut's forearm resting beside it, and at Lin Hao at the end of the table.

"Can I see it," he said.

## What Turning Would Mean Now

From the tenth of this month the page is not turned towards anybody, and the answer is read out, and about nine people a month are told so for the rest of this year, and this man was one of them and did not know it yet.

"The page is not turned," said Lin Hao. "That was the promise of the thirtieth of March and it was ended on the tenth of this month. I will read to you whatever is yours to hear. I will not put the page in front of you."

"Because of the other rows on it."

"Because the row above yours is somebody else's," said Lin Hao. "And the person your row is about is not coming and never was going to be again."

The man looked at him for a long time, and Lin Hao met it, because looking away would have been kinder and would have been a lie about what the office is.

"My wife is dead," said the man at last, flatly, as if saying it plainly in a room with the door open were a thing he had not done yet. "She died in the sixth month of this year. She asked here in the second month. Those are the two things I know. And you are telling me the second of them leaves me nothing I can hold."

"I am telling you the row is entered," said Lin Hao. "And that it was answered. And that the answer was read to you twice this morning. And that is the whole of what the office holds that I can give you."

## A Coat Folded Twice

The man nodded, and the nod was not acceptance, and he put his coat over his arm again, and he asked Wenna Raut to write down the date of his coming, and she did, in the words he used, with the hour against it.

"She kept everything," he said at the door. "Every paper any office ever gave her. She had boxes of it. And the house holds no fold of this. So either she lost the fold or you never gave her one."

"We gave folds that winter to people who brought a paper themselves," said Lin Hao. "If she brought the paper herself she went down four flights with a fold with the flap gummed and the date and the hour on the outside. If the question was about the book it went nowhere."

"She brought a paper," said the man. "She would have brought it. She copied it out twice."

"Then look again," said Lin Hao, gently. "Under another name or in another box. It goes down in a fold and folds get put where folds get put."

The man went down the four flights, and his step on the stair was the step of somebody carrying something heavier than a coat, and Lin Hao stood at the head of that stair until the sound of him was gone, and felt anger at the room and at the promise and at himself, all three at once, and all three useless.

Wenna Raut closed that Register and leaned back, and her hands were shaking a little, because the person a row is about had died in the sixth month and the row was dated the second, and both dates were on the page absolutely, and no interval was said aloud about them that morning.

## The Boxes She Kept

He asked the man about the house, and the man told him, standing at the bench with his coat refolded, because talking about the house was easier than talking about the room.

"She kept boxes under the bed," he said. "One for each year we were married. With letters and bills and the papers from every counter. She tied each bundle with string. After she died I opened all of them looking for your fold, and I found bundles for nine years, and the second month of this year holds a string with nothing under it."

Lin Hao pictured the open box and the empty string, and felt the uselessness of an office that writes answers and folds them and hands them over, when a fold can slip behind a box and leave a man with boxes of everything and nothing of one winter morning.

"Keep looking," he said. "Behind the boxes and under the paper at the bottom. Folds travel in coats and get left in pockets. And if it does not come to light, come back, and the reading will be done again, in the voice we read everything in, for as long as you need it."

The man nodded, and something in his face eased, though nothing had been given, because a promise of re-reading is not a page and is not nothing either.

He went down that hill at about three and stood a while where the lane meets the road, thinking about a house where a woman had kept every paper and a man could not find one fold, and about what the office owes a family that has nothing at all, and he did not know, and the not knowing followed him back up the hill.

*Three thousand seven hundred and forty-two days. Less three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six, the seventeenth of this month, is six, and a man asked whether there is anything about his wife who died in the sixth month of this year, and there is one row dated the second month, and the person it is about is not coming and never was going to be again.*

*Less three thousand six hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of March, is eighty-three, and the forty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them, and that rail moves on the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and twenty-two days, which is forty-four months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Hundred And Forty Steps

Three thousand eight hundred and eleven was the second of this one and the ninth of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and eighteen, and three thousand eight hundred and eighteen less three thousand eight hundred and eleven is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## The End Of A Line

The line ends where the city stops pretending, in about two hundred yards of siding, a shed with a stove in it that somebody lights all winter whatever the weather is doing, and a maintenance stair of about a hundred and forty steps going up to the platform.

He had not been to that end of the line in two years and had forgotten how little of it was city.

She was at the top of it at about half past eleven with a hand lamp in her fist and her coat open and the wind coming over the platform off the open ground behind her, and she turned her good side to him before she had got the lamp down, which she has done for nine years and has never once given him a reason for.

"You have not written anything down in about eleven minutes," said Beatrix Halm, "and you have not written anything down in eleven minutes in my hearing since the sixth year. I know because I counted, and I am not going to apologise for counting."

The base of her left thumb has a patch of skin gone hard and shining like a hearthstone, which is what nine years of a controller handle does to a hand, and the middle finger of that hand has been crooked at the second joint since a winter and has never been looked at by anybody including herself.

"I came to ask you something."

"Yes."

He had not got the sentence out and the word had already gone into the wind, and she laughed at that, a short sound, up and away.

"Four seconds," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the fastest anybody has said anything to me in two years and you were not even trying. Come down with me, because I have to chain that gate at the bottom and you are not going up a hundred and forty steps ahead of me for the look of the thing."

## The Lamp At The Turn

The lamp at the turn has been failing since the spring and the authority that owns it does not come, and the four steps under the turn are the last lit steps on that stair.

They went down. The four lit ones, and then the dark, and the whole of the rest of it is dark whatever anybody does about it.

She went first because she knew the way, and he came down behind her with his hand on the cold rail, and somewhere about two thirds of the way down he understood that she had been keeping her left side turned towards him for a hundred and forty steps with a turn in the middle of it, and that he had not noticed her doing it before, because he had never been on a stair with her.

Her right ear has been no use since the Veil. She has said that out loud to a panel on a first of July and to a man on a roof and to a clergyman, and she has never said it on a stair.

At the turn she stopped and put the lamp on the step, and did not light it.

"There is a thing I am going to say here and I am going to say it here on purpose," she said, "and I am not going to tell you why here, and you are not going to ask me why here."

"I am not going to ask you why here."

"Good." She put her back against the wall and looked at him in the dark from about a foot off.

"Because this is where I say things. The top of a stair belongs to the platform and the bottom belongs to the shed and this turn belongs to the dark, and anything said in it has not been said to anybody, and cannot be got back up or carried down, and I have been using this stair for nine years as the one place in this city where I am allowed to be wrong out loud."

## At The Turn

He got his back to the wall beside hers and put his coat down on the step because there was nowhere else for it to go and neither of them could have carried it.

"Take your left side off me for a minute," she said. "I cannot do this with you there and I am not going to pretend I can."

He stepped out of it and put his face into the dark above her and let her work out where he was by his coat against her fingers, and when she found him she took hold of him through the front of his trousers the way a woman takes hold of a thing she has already decided about.

He was hard in about nine seconds and she made a noise about it that she had plainly decided in advance she was not going to keep in.

"There," said Beatrix Halm. "Two years, and it is the same, and you still stand there as if a thing has happened to you that has not happened to anybody else in this stair."

She had his belt open and her cold hand underneath and she was not in any hurry at all, and there was wind coming up the well of that stair from the shed door thirty feet below, and she laughed once more, quietly, at nothing, because she enjoys it, and because he is by a long way the best prospect in Ostara for that.

"Come here," she said, and got him down onto the step beside her, and turned round, and pulled her skirts up over her hips with one hand and held on to the rail with the other and looked back over her shoulder at him in the dark.

"Do not put your face anywhere near the lamp and do not talk to me about four hundred people. Tell me one thing instead."

"Tell you what."

"Tell me which floor we are between," said Beatrix Halm. "And then come and get me."

He told her, because it was the only thing either of them had that was worth having on a stair, and she laughed the third time and pushed her hips back at him, and he got into her in one movement with her fingers spread on the cold wall in front of her, and she was wet and tight and very warm inside, and the sound the wind took off her went all the way down to the shed.

"That is the whole of what I have," she said. "That is all of it. Do not stop."

He did not stop. He got his hand round the front of her thigh and up under the hem where the wool had gone thin, and found her already soaked, and she pushed back against the fingers and made a noise into the wind that she did not try to take back.

Her cunt was tight around him and hot and it moved with her when she moved, and when she set herself against the wall and rode back into him the second time she swore at him, cheerfully, in the ordinary voice of a woman on a platform at midnight.

"Again," she said, before he had finished. "That was the door opening. Do it again."

He did it again with his fingers hooked in her belt at the back and she pushed up into him and counted out loud under her breath, in the way she counts everything, from nothing to about forty, and neither of them was doing arithmetic about anything at all.

She came first with her head back against the wall and her mouth shut hard, which was not what he expected of her, and her whole back went tight under his hand and she held him there afterwards longer than the thing had lasted and would not let go.

Then he spent in her with her name in her good ear, and she took all of it, and afterwards she stayed where she was with her forehead against the brick and her hand still on the rail.

"Nothing about that could go into any book in this city," she said, to the wall. "And that is not a complaint. That is the reason I let you do it on a stair."

## What She Felt In April

They got their breath for a while on that step with the dark going on about them, and then she reached down and found her lamp and did not light it either.

"I felt it on the seventh of April," she said. "At about a quarter to ten in the morning. Going up. In the district where you keep that room. It went up four flights and it came down again at half past five, and I have felt it every working morning since, and it has got heavier and it has got quieter, and the quiet is the part that should have you sitting down."

"Quieter how."

"A weight going up and not being spoken about. It used to feel like a man carrying a sack. It feels now like a man carrying a sack and not breathing. That is the whole of what my ear does, and there will never be a place in this city to put it."

"How many people in this city can feel that."

"I am not going to estimate for you." She turned the lamp in her hand. "If you write down an estimate I will not be on this stair again, and I would not blame you for writing one, and I would still not be here."

"I am not going to write one down."

"Then here is the thing that matters and I am going to say it once." She said it into the dark of the turn.

"It has got out of your room. A weight that used to be one street has become four, and the four are all the same weight, and there are people in this city saying so to each other on pavements and at counters. Your book is the only heavy thing in this city that is carried and not sold, and anybody who has ever carried anything for money knows exactly what that looks like from outside."

"And the office cannot find out who felt it."

"You cannot even find out who *asked* about it, and that is a different thing and it is worse, because a person who asks at least walks past your door on the way." She set the lamp down.

"I have not come here to hand you an instrument. A thing I feel at the end of a line with an empty cab behind me is a thing about a morning, and it is not evidence of you and it is not evidence against you, and the only person in this city who can do anything with it is me, and I am not going to."

## The Gate

At the bottom there is a landing, a gate and the shed, and she chained the gate with the cold of her hands making it take two attempts, and stood against it afterwards with her coat still open.

"You will want to ask me whether it is worse than it was," she said.

"It is not, I gather."

"It is exactly the same." She said it without any weight on it at all. "Every morning the same. Heavier by an amount I could not put in words and I am the only person alive who would know either figure, and that is not a comfort, it is a wall."

He walked back up with her to the shed door where she gets on when the line is running late, and the wind had got into him by then and his chest was doing what it does since the fourth year, and she watched him do it and did not ask, which is the oldest courtesy she has and the one he has not managed to earn a single time.

"Nine years," said Beatrix Halm. "And this is the first time I have put the lamp out at that turn for a person. You should know what that is worth. I have not worked out what it is worth and I am not going to try."

He stood at the shed door while the tram took the empty siding away with her in it, and counted about four hundred and fifty steps from the bottom of that stair to the bottom of his own, because a man who cannot use a thing ought at least to know its length.

Then the wind took the last of the light out of his eyes and he was standing on an empty platform at the end of a line with a chained gate below him and a stair with a dark turn in the middle of it, and what had happened in the dark of that turn was going to stay in the dark of that turn, and he found that he wanted it there.

Three thousand eight hundred and eighteen days. Less three thousand eight hundred and eleven, the second of this one, is seven, and about a hundred and forty steps at the end of a tram line with the light failing at the turn, and a woman who has felt that book go up four flights on every working morning since the seventh of April and will not have one word of it written down.

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is sixty-nine, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and ninety-eight days, which is forty-six months and eighteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

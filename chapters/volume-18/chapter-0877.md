# The Four-Year Clause

Three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine was the twentieth of this month and the twenty-seventh is day three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six, and three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six less three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine is seven, and twenty-seven less twenty is seven by the month count.

## The Sentence Under The Row

It came up on the twenty-seventh because a man asked how long his row would be held, and Wenna Raut gave him the sentence as it was written on the twenty-third of December of last year, in the clerk's hand at the foot of his row.

"Held until you come or until it is about four years old, whichever is first," she read, in the voice that office reads everything in.

The man nodded and went down, and the room was quiet after him, and Lin Hao stood at the end of that table looking at the row with the sentence under it, and felt the floor shift under a thing he had not examined in seven months.

"Bring me that row," he said.

Wenna Raut brought that book round, and he read the sentence twice, and the second time he read it slowly, the way a person reads a thing they wrote themselves and are afraid of.

"I wrote this in a hurry in December," said Lin Hao. "For a sister who could not leave a house. About a row with nothing under it. And I wrote it as though every person can come up four flights eventually."

Wenna Raut said nothing, because she had entered it as given and a clerk enters what she is given, and whether the sentence is right is not a clerk's question.

## Read Back And Wrong

He said it out loud in the room, with the door standing open and about four people in the passage, because a thing said in that room is a decision and a thing said in the ordinary voice is a fact, and he needed this one to be heard as both.

"The clause is wrong," said Lin Hao. "Holding a row until you come or until it is about four years old assumes that everybody can come up four flights eventually. And some people cannot come. And some people are dead. And the only way to keep the sentence for them is to know who is alive."

He stopped, because the rest of it was plain and he did not want to say it, and then he said it, because not saying it would be worse.

"And to know who is alive is a column. Which is a list. Which is the one thing that book refused to be nine years ago and could not be got back from."

Wenna Raut closed the pen into the tray with a soft sound, and the sound was loud in the quiet room.

"It is the only sentence in nine years I cannot keep," said Lin Hao. "Every other promise in that book I have kept or paid for. This one I cannot keep by any work and by any waiting. If I hold the row, I break the waiting. If I let it go at four years, I break the coming."

## The Only Sentence He Cannot Keep

They stood a while without speaking, and about four people went past in the passage, and one of them looked in and saw their faces and went on quickly.

## His Own Hurry

He could see the December morning clearly, the room cold and the light late, Wenna Raut at the table with her hands red, himself writing fast at the end of it because a woman's row had nothing under it and somebody had to promise it would be held.

"I wrote *until you come or until it is about four years old* because four years sounded like care," said Lin Hao. "And because a sister who could not leave a house needed a sentence that left the door open. And I did not ask myself what the sentence does to a man whose wife dies in the sixth month after asking in the second."

Wenna Raut looked up at that, because the twenty-third and the sixth of July were both in the room now though neither family was.

"It does to him what it did to her," she said. "It promises a holding that ends in a reading. And the reading needs the living in the room. And the living cannot all come."

"What will you do," said Wenna Raut at last.

"I will do nothing this morning," said Lin Hao. "The sentence stands as written until the movement closes. And when it closes I will replace it. And the price of replacing it will be paid by people who came up four flights and were told the truth and were given nothing to hold."

He felt anger then, at himself in December writing fast to comfort a room, and the anger was hot and unfamiliar, because he is seldom angry at his own hand, and it shook him more than the clause itself.

"I wrote it to get us through December," he said. "And it got us through December. And now it is July and the dead are in the book and the living cannot all come, and the sentence I wrote to hold one row holds all of them wrong."

Wenna Raut looked at the row a long time, and then she spoke, which she seldom does about a sentence, because sentences are his and entering is hers.

"If you replace it in August, the replacing must be said to everybody together," she said. "Not family by family. Because a practice said to one family in a room is gossip by the time it reaches the lane. Say it once, with witnesses, the way you said the turning-away on the tenth of June."

"I will," said Lin Hao. "Every one of them by word along lanes and stairs. And you will stand with me at the table when I say it, because it is your hand that will have to carry it after."

Wenna Raut entered nothing under the row that morning, because there was nothing given to enter, and the row stood as it was, with the clause under it that is wrong, and the room held it the way a room holds a thing everybody knows about and no hand in the room can move it yet.

## What The Next Month Brings

He went down that hill at about four and walked to the lane where the nine stools stand, though it was not a lane morning, and stood looking at the slate on the nail, thinking about a sentence that assumes legs and breath and years, and about how much of nine years rests on assuming people can come.

A woman washing a doorway nodded to him, and he nodded back, and a child ran past with a hoop, and the ordinary life of the lane went on around a man in a white plate who had just found the one sentence in nine years he cannot keep.

He thought about December and the hurry he had been in, with the year ending and the row empty and a sister behind it who could not leave a house, and he forgave the hurry even as he condemned the sentence, because both were his, and a man who cannot forgive his own December cannot replace it in August either.

A carrier came up with a message from the flat file about the quarterly sheets, and went down again, and the interruption steadied him, because ordinary work goes on around a wrong sentence the way water goes round a stone.

He stood at the window and watched the yard, and thought about the sister behind the row, who had not been named in seven months and would not be named until she came herself, and about how the clause assumed her legs and her breath, and about how little December had known of either.

He asked Wenna Raut to find the row that evening and read the clause a third time by lamplight, slowly, and each word landed heavier. *Until you come.* As if coming were a door and not four flights in the cold. *Or until it is about four years old.* As if years kept promises for him.

He closed the book and strapped it for the morning, and went home through dark streets thinking about August. The month had three weeks left in it, and somewhere in those weeks a sister who cannot leave a house was deciding whether to leave it, and he did not know.

The not knowing was the only honest thing December had left him. At his own door he stopped and looked back at the hill, and thought about how a sentence written in a hurry travels further than any careful one, because haste borrows against years nobody has yet lived.

He slept badly and woke early, and came up the four flights before the carriers, and stood alone in the room with the shut Register on the table, thinking about columns and lists and the one thing that book refused to be nine years ago.

When Wenna Raut came in she found him at the window, and she said nothing, and the two of them began the day the way they begin every day now, with the finding and the reading and the entering, though the clause under one row stands wrong and both of them know it.

August waits with its answer, and the waiting itself has become a kind of work, and the work grows heavier each morning, and neither of them would trade the room for any easier one, and both know the eighteenth will test that.

The day's askings began, and the finding took the best part of every hour, and the room held steady.

By midday the corridor was busy, and the stair sounded with feet going up and down, and the ordinary life of the building went on around the wrong sentence, which waited for August like a stone waits for water.

*Three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six days. Less three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, the twentieth of this month, is seven, and the four-year clause was read back and it is wrong, because he wrote it in a hurry in December, and holding a row until you come assumes everybody can come.*

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty-seven, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and fifty-six days, which is forty-five months and six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Line Saying Dead

Three thousand seven hundred and fifty-five was the sixth of this month and the thirteenth is day three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two, and three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two less three thousand seven hundred and fifty-five is seven, and thirteen less six is seven by the month count.

## A Slip Put On The Wood

She came up at about ten on the thirteenth with a slip of her own paper in her hand, and she laid it on the wood in front of Wenna Raut before she said anything, the way people lay down a thing they have been carrying for days.

"Write it for me," she said. "One line. That my husband is dead. With the date under it. I will pay for the ink."

Wenna Raut did not touch the slip. She looked at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao came to the end of that table, because he knew what the slip was before he had read it, and he knew what the morning would cost.

"What is your name," said Lin Hao, gently.

She told him, and told him the street, and told him her husband's work, and all of it came out fast, as if speed would get her past the asking.

"He died in the spring," she said. "Every office in this city wants a paper that says so before they will do anything. The burial ground wants one. The landlord wants one. And your office writes things down. So write it."

## The Long Refusal

He explained it to her three times, and each time she heard him out and put the slip a little further forward on the wood, as if the paper itself would persuade where words had not.

"If I write that line," said Lin Hao at last, "then this office holds a row saying a person is dead. And that row is the first in nine years anybody could be identified from without a date and an hour already in their hand. After it there will be a second and a third, and in a year the book will hold a column of the dead, which is a list."

"I do not care about your book," she said. "I care about my landlord."

"I know," said Lin Hao. "And I am refusing you to your face and not by letter, because you deserve a person in the room and not a counter with a stamp, and because a refusal that arrives on paper can be thrown away and this one has to be carried down four flights in your own hands."

She wept then, angrily, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand, and Wenna Raut stood up from the table and went to the far end of the room and stood there, because a clerk who watches a refusal is a witness and a clerk who stands aside is only a clerk.

"Other offices write it," she said. "Any counter in this city will write a line for a fee."

"Then let them," said Lin Hao. "And bring me what they write, and I will enter that you brought it, in your words, with the date and the hour. What I will not do is make the line here first."

She stared at him, and he met it, and he felt the familiar pull to give way, and he did not give way, because giving way once would be giving way to every family that comes up four flights after her, including the family of the man who was here on the twenty-third, who has nothing at all and was told so by me myself.

## Said To Them Herself

He told her about them, because a woman who has been refused to her face needs to know she is not refused alone, and he told it plainly and without names, because that room has a rule about names and he keeps it even when it costs him the persuasion.

"A man came up on the twenty-third of June about his wife, who died in the sixth month," said Lin Hao. "And his brother and his sister and his mother came after him on the sixth of July, three of them together. They have no fold and no paper with a date on the outside. I said so to their faces, in that room, that morning. And what I would not write for them I am not going to write for you today."

She looked at the slip on the wood and did not put her hand out towards it, and he let the not doing it stand, because it was the only answer that mattered and she had given it without either of them saying so.

"You would have written it for them in June," she said.

"I would have," said Lin Hao. "And you would have had your line, and I would have had the first one, and the second would have come in a fortnight after that and the tenth by the spring. That is not a reason to refuse you kindly. It is the reason, and I am sorry it is the reason I have."

## What Another Counter Writes

"Listen to me about the landlord," said Lin Hao, changing his ground, because refusal alone would send her down four flights with nothing but the shape of a shut door. "Another counter will write your line for a fee. Any registrar's desk does it daily. Take their paper to him and keep your own copy folded with your husband's things."

"And what is their paper worth that yours is not," she said, bitter.

"Theirs is a certificate and mine would be a column," said Lin Hao. "Theirs stands alone on its own sheet with a stamp. Mine would stand in a bound Register between two neighbours' rows, and the next stranger asking after the dead would learn where to look by where my word is."

She considered this, and he saw her turn it over, and he pressed nothing, because a woman weighing a thing is doing work and work needs quiet.

"My husband brought a paper here four years ago," she said at last, quieter. "About a wall. He did the talking and I stood beside him and said nothing at all, which I have thought about every month since. I do not know whether you hold his words."

"If he brought it himself, the row is his and the reading is his to ask for," said Lin Hao. "Come back with the date if you hold it, or with the paper, and the finding takes the best part of a morning, and the reading takes less than a minute, and both will be done."

She nodded, and the hardness in her face eased by a fraction, because a road forward, even a narrow one, is different from a wall.

"If my husband's row is here from four years ago, I will come with the date," she said. "My sister keeps his papers. She will know it."

"Bring what you hold," said Lin Hao. "And the finding will be done the same morning, and the reading in the same voice, and the entering in the same hand. That much the office promises, and it has never broken that much."

## At The Door

She got up at last, and her hand went towards the slip and stopped an inch off the wood, and she left it where it was, which was the whole of what the morning had cost her and the whole of what it had got her.

"You are sending me down four flights with nothing," she said.

"You are going down four flights with a reason and a date you can take to any counter in this city," said Lin Hao. "That is not nothing. It is only not ours."

She stood at the door and turned back, and her voice was steadier than it had been coming up.

"You refused me kindly," she said. "Which is worse than unkindly, because I cannot be angry at you on the way down."

"I know," said Lin Hao. "And I am sorry for the kindness too."

Wenna Raut picked up the slip the woman had left on the wood and looked at it, one line in a hurried hand asking for a line saying dead, and she held it a moment before putting it in the wastepaper basket, because a clerk may only enter what she is given and a thing refused is not given.

The basket is where refused paper goes when it cannot go in the book, and she has put more paper there this summer than in any summer of the nine, and every slip in it is a person who went down four flights in the cold holding nothing.

He listened to her going, and the stair took her the way the stair takes everybody, which is without hurry and without help.

## After She Had Gone

He stood at the window and looked down at the yard, and his chest ached in the way it does when he has held himself upright for an hour, and his want was simple and could not be met: to have a line to give that would not become a column.

Wenna Raut finished the entering and blotted it, and the two of them stood a while without speaking, and about four people went past in the passage, and the ordinary sound of the building came back, water below and feet above, and the room grew quiet again, though neither of them felt eased by it.

"She will go to another counter," said Wenna Raut at last. "And they will write it for a fee. And her landlord will take it. And the city will go on."

"And this office will hold no line saying dead," said Lin Hao. "Not in the bound Register and not on a slip. And the first row anybody could be identified from will remain unwritten while I stand at this table."

The anger he felt was at the shape of the choice and not at the woman, and he carried it down the hill to walk it off by the water, and the water ran low between the stones, and a woman washing steps nodded to him, and he thought about slips and lines and columns all the way down, and about how refusing kindly costs more than refusing coldly, because kindness keeps the door open in the mind after it has been shut in the room.

When he came back the room was empty and the pen put away, and Wenna Raut had left a cup of water on the table for him, and he drank it standing, and forgave himself nothing, which is his oldest habit and the one that has cost the office most.

## The Basket On The Table

She came in late with the basket in both hands and set it on the wood between them, and there were nine slips in it and none of them was a row.

"Nine this summer," said Wenna Raut. "Nine people went down that stair in the last eight weeks believing this office owed them a line, and every one of them was right about the owing and wrong about the line."

He looked at the slips and did not touch any of them, because a man does not read what a woman has put in a basket, and a basket is a clerk's evidence and not his.

"And in September," said Wenna Raut, "one of those people is going to come back up with a certificate off another counter, paid for, with a stamp on it, and ask this office to enter that she brought it. And you promised her that on the thirteenth, in front of me."

"I did."

"So on the eighteenth, when you say whatever it is you are going to say to everybody together," said Wenna Raut, "that woman will be standing in that room with a paper in her hand that this office would not write for her, and it will be the only thing in the room that anybody can hold."

He said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was not a rule, and rules were what had brought the basket.

*Three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two days. Less three thousand seven hundred and fifty-five, the sixth of this month, is seven, and a woman asked for a line saying dead on a slip and he refused to write it, because the first row anybody could be identified from is a row saying a person is dead.*

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is thirteen, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and forty-two days, which is forty-four months and twenty-two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

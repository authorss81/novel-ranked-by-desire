# Nine Years And One Month

Three thousand eight hundred and seventy-one was the second of this one and the ninth of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight, and three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight less three thousand eight hundred and seventy-one is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## What She Can Read

She came up at about ten with a satchel of ledgers and a letter folded twice, and stood at that table the way people stand who have read rooms for a living and do not expect to be impressed by one.

Lin Hao knew her by sight from the audit floors, where she reads records off the paper itself and has a reputation for saying what a record cannot do in the same breath as what it can.

"I have come about the weight," she said. "Not about a row. About what it costs to move that book and who moves it."

Wenna Raut kept her hands on that book and did not open it, because there was no asking on the table that needed a page, and the room was quiet in the way it is when a person has come to talk and not to ask.

"You carry it down yourself now," said the woman. "Five days in seven. In the dark. Four flights and about four hundred yards."

"Since the twenty-fifth of last month," said Lin Hao.

"And up by two in the morning. At a quarter to ten."

"By two women who both have to be back elsewhere at ten."

She nodded, and opened the letter, and did not read it.

## About Nine Years

"Nine years and one month," she said. "That is what you told a room in the second month of the year, about how long that book has been going down to a flat file and coming back up. And what you told another room in the third month, about how long it has been there for anybody to read."

Lin Hao felt the back of his neck go cold, the way it does when a person who can read brings a figure back to the room it came from.

"And I have spent a fortnight checking it," she said, "because I can read a record and I cannot read you, and I wanted to know which of the two I was doubting. There is nothing on any page that supports the extra month."

She laid the letter flat on that table without pushing it towards him, the way auditors lay things they do not need to enter.

"The office was a room on the second of December of the second year. The flat file has held that book for about nine years. No page in nine years gives you the month on top of the years, and the month is yours and not the record's."

Wenna Raut looked at him, and did not speak, and the carriers on the stair below went past the door with somebody else's boxes and did not come in.

"You are right," said Lin Hao. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that a man who will not let a clerk count rows will let himself count years, and the one is a list and the other feels like memory, and they are the same thing with different coats on."

He stood there with his hands behind his back and felt the truth of it go through him like cold water, because she had priced him exactly and had done it without raising her voice, and there was nothing in him that wanted to argue.

## What It Costs To Move

She asked him then what he had asked himself every evening since the twenty-fifth of last month, going down four flights in the dark with nine years of other people's words against his chest.

"What does it cost," she said, "to move about four hundred and seventy rows that have not been counted since the early summer of the year before last, twice a day, five days in seven, with hands that are already doing four nights of washrooms in a branch office."

"It costs a pair of hands," said Lin Hao. "And about fourteen minutes at the end of a day when two people do it in about nine in the morning. And a clerk who cannot leave that room while that book is in it, from a quarter to ten until half past five, at the same wage."

"And who moves it."

"Two women in the morning who have both got to be elsewhere. And me at night, because the woman with the back told me a thing you only ever carry one way is not being carried, and she was right, and I am the only hands left that are not somebody else's."

She folded the letter again, once, and put it back in the satchel, and he understood that she had not come to enter anything and that nothing she had said would go into that book.

## The Evening Stair

That evening the carriers went down at half past five with that book between them, and he followed them down four flights on foot to watch the turn, because she had asked who moves it and he wanted to be able to answer her if she ever came back.

The stair turns twice at the half landing with no window in it, and the strap groaned at the second turn the way leather groans when it is at its limit, and the woman under the bottom edge took the weight on her forearms without putting a hand out, and the other kept the strap from slipping with her shoulder.

He stood against the wall of that landing to let them past, with his back to the cold brick, and felt the draught from below come up round his ankles, and thought about how many mornings that stair has carried nine years of other people's words without a wage on it, and about how a stair is only ever noticed by the people standing on it.

At the bottom they got that book into the flat file four floors down and about four hundred yards off, and shut the drawer, and wiped their hands on their coats, and neither of them said anything about the extra month, because the extra month was never theirs.

"You have taken a month off your own book," she said, at the door. "That is a finding against yourself, and you took it without being asked, and that is the only reason I came up four flights to hear it."

## The Book Comes Up In The Morning

It was in that flat file all night, and it came out of it at a quarter to ten in the grey before the light, with the strap going round it twice because the strap is the wrong length and was drawn out of a drawer of about nine in the third month, and there is not one line anywhere saying which book goes in which strap.

Two women took it up. One under the bottom edge with both forearms and one at the strap, and the stair turned twice at the half landing between the second and third floors, which has no window in it, and the weight rode the turn the way a boat rides a bend, slow and heavy.

He walked behind them with his hands empty and felt useless, and then felt grateful for the uselessness, because it told him what his evenings are: fourteen minutes alone where two people take nine together, and fingers that do not open at the bottom, and a yard with nobody in it.

## Nothing On Any Page To Correct

At that table with the book open and the day's first asking on it, Wenna Raut put the pen down and pushed it a finger's width towards him without a word about it, which is the way she hands over a morning that is going to be ordinary.

Lin Hao took the pencil from behind his ear and corrected nothing on any page, because there was nothing on any page to correct, and the correction stood in the air of that room where corrections stand when they are about a man and not about a row.

## What He Wants

"Tell me one thing you want," said Wenna Raut, while the last of the morning was still on that table. "Not out of that book. Out of you. In nine years I have not known you to ask me for a thing that was not about a row, and you are not starting with one."

"That is a rule with a hole in it."

"It is what I have got," said Lin Hao. "I want things that will not go on three lines of stiff grey card. And one of them is that somebody in this room will tell me the truth about me once a year without being asked for it, and will do it whether or not I have been right that day."

She looked at him for longer than she usually looks at anybody in that room, and did not answer him, and he understood that the not-answering was the whole of what she had, and that she had given it to him entire.

## What She Leaves Behind

Wenna Raut shut that book at half past five with the strap waited for on the stair, and looked at him across that table with the frankness she has had since the spring, when she began saying what a room costs in the same breath as what it holds.

"She took a month off you," said Wenna Raut. "And you let her. In nine years I have not seen you let anybody take anything off you without arguing for the keeping of it."

"There was nothing to argue for," said Lin Hao. "No page supports it. The month was mine. I added it because nine years felt too round to be true, and one month on top made it feel counted."

"And now."

"Now it is about nine years, and it is round anyway, and I am the man who rounded it twice, once up and once down, and both times in front of witnesses."

She nodded, and put the pen in the tray, and stood with her hands on the edge of that table the way she stands when the day is done and the book is about to go down.

"Then go down with it tonight," she said, "and remember on the second floor that your left arm will go, and on the third that your fingers will not open, and at the bottom that the file drawer sticks in the cold and wants the heel of the hand. Those are true things. The month was not, and now it is gone."

He wanted, badly and all at once, to be a man who had never added a month to anything, and the want shamed him, and he carried it down with him that evening along with the strap and the weight, and at the bottom of those four flights he stood a moment with his arms hanging and let the cold take it out of him.

Three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight days. Less three thousand eight hundred and seventy-one, the second of this one, is seven, and a woman who can read a record told him to his face that his book is about nine years old and not a month more, and he said she was right.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is thirty-nine, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-nine of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and fifty-eight days, which is forty-eight months and eighteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Weight Is Not A Fact

Three thousand eight hundred and twenty-five was the sixteenth of this one and the twenty-third of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two, and three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two less three thousand eight hundred and twenty-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## The Lane With Nine Stools

He came down the hill at about eleven with Wenna Raut, who had asked to come and had said why on the stair and not in the room, which is her way of saying a thing is hers and not the office's.

The lane has nine stools in it and two of them are Emil Brack's and one has sacking tied round the leg, and a slate four feet by three leaning against the wall with a figure of merit on it in four-inch letters, and twelve books tied with a string on a cart behind the third stool.

"A weight is not a fact," said Wenna Raut, before they had got to the end of the lane.

"I know."

"You have not, and I am saying it once and then I am leaving it alone for the rest of this year."

She stopped walking for about four seconds and then started again.

"I have entered a weight twice this month. You entered one and I entered the other and they were both true and neither one can be given to a person. A fact is a thing you can hand somebody across a table."

"A weight is a thing a lane feels and a stair feels and a woman at the end of a tram line feels, and every one of those is right, and every single one of them is past being turned into a sentence that helps a person who comes up four flights with a paper in her hand."

"I have not answered it."

"I know that as well. You are not going to, and I am not going to argue you, and I am going to be standing next to you in about four months when a person says *you felt it too* and expects you to say what you felt."

## What The Lane Has Started Asking

She read the second figure upside down from where she was standing, and then read it again the right way up, and did not say anything about it.

Brack put his own four days of the month in small chalk along the bottom edge where anybody can read them and check them against their own, and under them, that week, in the same small chalk and in his own hand, there was a second figure that had not been there in August.

"About nine a week," he said, "in my line."

"Since when."

"Since the middle of the month before last, and it is not the same nine and it is not the same people twice running. They ask it differently. One asks whether you carry anything. One asks whether the room upstairs is open."

"One asked me on the second morning of the week whether you were the one who comes down at half past five with the light off, and I said I was not paid to know who you are, and he took it well, which is the part I minded."

He wiped the slate end with the side of his hand and did not put the cloth away.

"You have written that on your own slate," said Lin Hao.

"I have written about nine a week on my own slate in my own chalk, in a lane, where about four hundred people a day walk past it, and I am not going to rub it off, and I would think a good deal less of you if you asked me to." He squared the slate against the wall. "And you have not asked me, and that is two questions you have come down here with in about nine weeks and not asked."

## A Certificate In A Line

A woman was standing three stools further down the lane showing a piece of stamped paper to a man who had come out of a doorway to look at it, and the paper had a seal on it the size of a thumbnail and the woman was holding it out at arm's length the way people hold out a thing they have paid money for.

He knew her before she turned round.

"I brought it up four flights in August," she said, "and the clerk wrote down that I brought it, with the date and the hour, in my words. And he would not read it to me and he would not write it for me and he told me to spend the money on a chair with arms."

"I remember."

"I have not spent it on a chair." She got it back off the man in the doorway, who had been very polite about it, and folded it into four and put it inside her coat against her chest. "I have shown it to four people since the spring, and every one of them has understood it, and every one of them has gone away and still not known whether my husband was a good man, and it turns out that is the thing a piece of paper cannot do."

Not one person in that lane said a word to her about it, because nine stools of men who carry things for a living have a rule about a woman and a paper, and she went on up the hill to a house with a lock on it and the four flights of that building were not the four flights this office is on.

## What Brack Will Not Sell

They took two of the stools because Brack offered them, and he is a man who has not offered anybody a stool in nine years and did it that morning because it was cold.

"I have worked it out to the grain and I am not going to give it to you."

"You have not told me what it is yet."

"You know what it is. You have known since the sixteenth of this month." He put the slate flat on his knees. "Somebody in this city can feel that office. I have known it since the summer and I have known it longer than you have and I have not priced it, because the moment I price it, you have a figure, and a figure in this city is a thing you can put in a rent."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the only reason available. I priced a neighbour once, in a counting house on Cordwain, and she said it out loud in front of two rooms, and every woman at a bench in this city has said nothing to her neighbour ever since, and your book went quiet, and it went quiet honestly, and there is nothing left in Ostara that can tell a true quiet book from a careful one." He looked up.

"I did not price your weight, and I would not have, and I would not have thought of it."

## One Sentence More About The Four Years

"You have given me that sentence in June and I have carried it about four months and I am not going to let you off it," said Lin Hao.

"I was not going to." Brack went along the row of stools and counted three of them with his thumb, which is a thing he does at eleven and at four.

"You have not told me what to say when a person in my line asks me who it belongs to. I have worked out the answer on my own, which is that I do not know, and I have said that about nine a week since the middle of the month before last, and one morning in the next six months somebody is not going to accept it, and they will take it out on me, because I am the one standing there with a stool and a slate."

"That is one sentence and it is a different sentence from the one in June."

"It is one sentence and it is the last one for this quarter, and I am not going to argue it and you are not going to answer it, and that is the arrangement and it has held since the middle of the first year." He got up and turned the slate round so the figure faced the lane. "The office not close. Still."

## The Thing He Was Asked For And Could Not Have

"I want one thing off you and it is not a number," said Lin Hao.

"Go on."

"I want to know what street that room is on."

Brack looked at him for about four seconds.

"I have carried a book up four flights into that room about five mornings a week since the seventh of April," he said, "and I could not tell you the street it is on. No name is over the door. No name is on the yard gate. There is a card on the door about washing on Thursdays and that is the only writing in that building a person can read from outside it."

"I have worked there nine years."

"Then you will not be able to say it either." Brack put the cloth in his apron and went to his cart. "Ask the woman on the rail with the counter. He has been standing in the same passage for nine years and I have watched him watch people come out of that door, and if anybody in this city knows, it is a man who is paid nothing to know things."

He walked back up the hill with the two figures on the slate going round and round in his head, the four days and the nine a week, and the want he had carried down that lane came back up it unspent, because the answer had been no and the no had cost a man with a cart eleven in the mouth and had bought him nothing at all.

Three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two days. Less three thousand eight hundred and twenty-five, the sixteenth of this one, is seven, and a clerk said out loud in a lane that a weight is not a fact and he did not answer her, and a man with a slate gave the office one sentence more about four years and did not discharge it, and the street has not been said out loud in nine years.

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is eighty-three, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and twelve days, which is forty-seven months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

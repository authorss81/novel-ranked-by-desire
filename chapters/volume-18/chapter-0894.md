# Eight Hundred Days

Three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four was the fifteenth of this one and the twenty-first of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and ninety, and three thousand eight hundred and ninety less three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four is six, and twenty-one less fifteen is six by the month count.

## A Stranger At The Bench

He came up at about ten with mud on his boots and a parcel manifest folded into his fist, and stood at that bench the way men stand who have carried loads for a living and do not expect a room to make way for them.

"I am not here about a paper," he said. "I am here about a row with a space in the middle of it. A man on my street was up here in the fifth month about the man who works nights on a third court, and he told me what was read to him, and I have come to ask what the space is for."

Wenna Raut kept her hands curled round the edge of that book, because no hand goes down level on anything in that room, and looked at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao came to the end of that table.

The row was there. Dated the twenty-third of August of the ninth year, a date at the head, an hour against the date, and a space in the middle where the words of a man who carried up four flights a piece of pencil paper with nothing on it ought to go.

About eight hundred days old. Old enough to have been asked about by three different strangers, young enough that the man who left it might still walk up those four flights and fill it.

## What The Space Is Not For

"You want to know what it is for," said Lin Hao.

"I want to know why a book that holds about four hundred and seventy rows never counted since the early summer of the year before last keeps an empty place in the middle of a row, and what a man is supposed to do with an empty place when he finds one."

Lin Hao thought about the night that row was entered, which he remembers the way a man remembers a night he did not understand at the time, and about the pencil paper with nothing on it, and about saying no word at a counter, and about how little of that is on any page.

"It is not for holding a name," said Lin Hao. "There is not one name in that book and there has not been one since the office opened, and the day there is a place in it for a person, that book is a list of people."

"It is not for holding the hour, because the hour is against the date at the head of it."

"It is not for holding the words, because the man said no word."

The stranger waited, patiently, the way men who carry loads wait, and Wenna Raut turned that page a little towards the light so the space showed plain, an empty length in the middle of writing, with the pen's track clean on either side of it.

## The Manifest In His Fist

He opened the manifest in his fist to show them he had come honestly, with paper of his own and not empty-handed, and the columns stood in it the way columns stand on manifests, neat and narrow, with weights and houses and names of yards.

"I carry for three streets," he said. "Eleven houses. Twice a month. I write down who gave me what and who took it, and the writing is mine and the keeping is mine, and if I leave a space in the middle of a line my foreman asks me what the space is for before the day is out."

"And what do you tell him."

"That a sack split. That a measure spilled. That a house was shut. There is always something, because a space in my book is a thing that happened and I was there to see it happen."

He folded the manifest again and put it back in his fist, and looked at the space in that row with the respect of a man who keeps books of his own.

"Yours has been there eight hundred days," he said. "And the man who left it was in that room. And the keeping of it is not understood here. That is a different kind of keeping from mine, and I am not saying mine is better. I am saying I do not understand yours, and I came up four flights to say I do not understand it to your faces."

Lin Hao felt affection then, sudden and unwelcome, for a man who carries loads and keeps manifests and expects a space to mean something, and the affection steadied him more than the anger had.

## What He Takes Down

"Then what is it for."

And there was nothing in that room that could answer him. Wenna Raut could not answer, because she may only enter what she is given and she was given nothing. Lin Hao could not answer, because the only honest answer is that the space is where a man who came up four flights left nothing, and nothing is not a thing an office can keep for him.

## The Answer Nobody Can Give

"I do not know," said Lin Hao, at last, out loud, in the ordinary voice, because a thing said in that voice in that room is a fact. "The knowing of it is not in that room. The three of us looked at one another in the fifth month and the row answered nothing, and it answers nothing now."

The stranger nodded, slowly, as if he had expected that and had come up four flights to hear it said plainly rather than to be given a shape.

"Then I will tell the man on my street that I asked," he said. "And that the office said it did not know. And he will have to decide whether that is an answer."

"It is the only one we have," said Wenna Raut, quietly.

After he had gone down, with the date and the hour of his own asking in his own hand on a scrap, witnessed, Lin Hao stood at that table and looked at the space in the middle of that row for longer than the work required, and thought about how many empty places a man can carry in a book before the book is mostly empty places.

## How Long Eight Hundred Days Is

Wenna Raut found the place in the morning's work where the day's askings had stopped, and set the pen in the tray, and spoke without looking at him, the way she speaks when a thing is about the room and not about a person.

"Eight hundred days," she said. "A man comes up four flights with nothing in his fist and says nothing at a counter, and the clerk enters the date and the hour and leaves a space for the words, and the space is there two winters later, and strangers come up to ask what it is for."

"And what do you tell them when I am not at that table."

"That the row is there and the words are not, and that nothing is taken from, so the space stays. And that a space is not an answer, and that an office that keeps one for eight hundred days has made a promise it cannot explain to the person it made it for."

He stood with his hands behind his back and felt anger again, cleaner this time, at the rule and not at the man, and the anger made him want to put a hand on that page and close the space with a line, and he did not, because the rule is the reason that book can come upstairs at all.

## What He Carries Down

He felt anger then, sudden and unwelcome, at the man who had left it, and at himself for keeping it, and at the rule that nothing is taken from, which meant the space could never be closed, and the anger steadied him more than any finding had in weeks.

## The Carriers Going Down

The carriers came at half past five with the strap and found him still at that table with the space open under his eyes, and the woman with the back looked at the empty length in the middle of writing and understood without being told.

"That one," she said.

"That one," said Wenna Raut. "Dated the twenty-third of August of the ninth year. A date and an hour and a space. About eight hundred days old. A stranger came up today to ask what the space is for, and the telling of it could not be done in that room."

The woman with the back shifted the strap on her shoulder and did not come nearer, because carriers do not crowd a page that is hurting somebody.

"Then it goes down with the rest," she said. "Empty and all. Five days in seven. In the dark. By hands that are already tired. That is what keeping means when keeping cannot explain itself."

He got the strap round that book with the buckle on the wrong side, the way he does when he works alone, and went down four flights behind them on foot, listening to their steps find the turn in the dark, and thought about manifests with columns and foremen who ask before the day is out, and about a book that asks nothing and answers nothing and still has to be carried.

That evening, going down four flights in the dark with the strap round that book, he found himself counting his own steps to keep his mind off his hands, and at the half landing with no window in it he stopped and said nothing out loud at all, because there was nothing that belonged there and he knew it.

Three thousand eight hundred and ninety days. Less three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four, the fifteenth of this one, is six, and a row with a space in the middle of it is about eight hundred days old and nobody in that room can say what the space is for.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fifty-one, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-one of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and seventy days, which is forty-nine months and no days over after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Empty Half Landing

Three thousand eight hundred and four was the twenty-fifth of the month before this one and the second of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and eleven, and three thousand eight hundred and eleven less three thousand eight hundred and four is seven, and thirty less twenty-five plus two is seven by the month count.

## Two O'Clock And A Step With Nothing On It

He went down at about two with a lamp in his hand and the half landing between the second and third floors was empty, and he stood on the top step of it for about a minute before he understood that empty was the wrong word for what was in front of him.

The parcel was not there either. That was the part he had not been ready for. Three years of that parcel against his knee at two on a working afternoon, and the step and the wall beside the newel had a worn place in them by now that had nothing to do with him and everything to do with the same hour, over and over, for three years.

"Say good evening," said Wenna Raut from the landing above.

"That is not an afternoon of his and the step is his by that much."

"Say it anyway. I want to see what a room does with it."

He did not say it. He went back up and got on with the morning, which is what a person does when a thing has arrived that has nowhere to be put.

## What The Grammar Of It Is

They found it out together at the table at about half past three with the door open and four people in the passage, which is the way anything gets decided in that room.

"A row begins with the words a person used," said Wenna Raut. "That is the first rule and it has never been bent, and every one of about four hundred and seventy of them began because somebody came up that stair and opened their mouth in this room."

"That is right."

"So say the first line of his."

Lin Hao put his fingers on the edge of the table, which is what he does instead of putting them anywhere else.

"He has not opened his mouth in this room. Not once in three years. He has nodded at me twice and said evening to me once, yesterday, and he said it because I said good evening first, and a row cannot begin with a word a man repeats back at you."

## What Went In And What Did Not

"Nothing goes in," said Lin Hao.

"Nothing goes in," said Wenna Raut, "and not because of your rule about the living and the dead. That rule stops an answer going out. This is a row that could not begin at all, and there is a difference, and the difference is that a dead man's row began with him alive."

He had not thought of it in those terms and it went into him badly for the rest of the afternoon.

"So we cannot even have the fact of him."

"We can have the fact of him in about four heads, the same as anything else said in this room and kept out of it. And in about four years a stranger cannot ask us for him, because a stranger would have no date and no hour to ask with, and that is not a protection. That is a hole."

"I could enter it. You know I could. I could open that book at the back and put a date against today and an hour against the date and write that a man who used to sit on the half landing has stopped coming, and it would be true."

"I know you could."

"Would it be the first row in nine years that arrived without an asking."

"It would be the first row in nine years that no person in it ever spoke a word of," said Wenna Raut, "and there is a great deal of difference between those two things and it took me about four seconds to hear it and it is the whole of why we do not."

He had the drawer of a pencil within reach and he did not open the book, and he understood while his hand was still on the way that he had been about to do the one thing in nine years that would have turned a hole into a column, and that he would have done it for a man on a step.

He put the pencil back and left it there for the rest of the afternoon, and Wenna Raut saw him do it and said nothing about it, because a clerk who comments on a man in a room is a person and a clerk who keeps the room is a clerk.

## Going Down And Asking Four People

He asked four people before the end of the day and it took under nine minutes and it was the worst nine minutes he had spent in that building in a year.

The second carrier said she had once lent him a piece of chalk.

A boy of about ten came up that stair in the spring of last year with a message for a different office and took himself onto the half landing to wait for his own aunt, and the carrier gave him the chalk out of her apron, and he drew on the wall beside him for about four minutes, and she never saw what, and he told her the drawing was not about anything, and she let it go.

A woman who cleans the second-floor landing said he had said one word to her in three years, and it was the word *cold*, about the winter before last, and she had not been able to tell whether he meant the landing or the city.

A porter at the third-floor door who has worked there nineteen years said he had a theory and that theory was that the man waited for something, and that it had not arrived in three years, and that he stopped coming at the end of August, and that he had left the landing swept, which is the part that had bothered the porter.

Three useful answers out of four people and every one of them was about something he had done rather than about the man himself, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor afterwards and understood that he had gone out looking for a person and had come back with three small kindnesses that had been paid without him.

## To The Counter Off Cordwain

The fourth was the one that cost him the afternoon, because it is a passage and a man is paid nothing in it, and the man reads the foot of that standing sheet to whoever stands at the rail.

"He comes down at about three and he goes east," said the counterman, after a silence of about four seconds. "That is all I have. I have watched him go east about four hundred times and I could not tell you one thing about where east ends."

"Does he ever stop."

"He stops at the mouth of the passage about ten yards down, and he stands there, and after a while he goes on. He has done that every day I have been at that counter, and I have watched him for about two years, and I still cannot tell you what he is standing there for."

"Does anyone else stand there with him."

"No," said the counterman. "That is what I have never understood about him. A man who wants to be let alone stands somewhere the rest of us do not go. He stands in the busiest part of that passage, in the middle of everything, with his back to a wall and his parcel on his knees, and every person in this city goes round him."

Lin Hao stood at that rail for another four minutes after that, in that lane he had stood a good many times, and looked at the middle of a passage where about four thousand people went past on a working day, and worked out that he had been doing for three years what the whole city does.

That was the finding and it was not in the book and could not be, and he wrote it on the back of his own hand in pencil, in the middle of the morning's other arithmetic, where a clerk's forearm could not wipe it out in an ordinary movement.

## A Name In A Stairwell

At half past five the two carriers came for the book and took it down the four flights as they do every working day of the year, and going down the woman with the back stopped on that half landing for about four seconds, the way she stops on it.

"I have carried that book five months," she said, "and used that landing as a rest, and he has been sitting on it at about two for three years, and I have never asked him his name. Not once. And I am not going to stand on that step and ask him now, because a man who has stopped coming has said something and I do not know what it was."

There was a pause on the stair of the kind that does not get filled.

"I only know what you would call him," she said, and said the name, once, into a stairwell with no window in it, between the second floor and the third floor, where a thing said belongs to neither of the floors and cannot be fetched back up or carried down.

Lin Hao did not write it, and he was never going to, and the page stayed as it is. But the room above them heard two women say one word, and he heard it, and it is the only name that has ever been spoken in that building and it is not on a page in it.

Then they went down the last flight and across the yard, and he stood at the head of that stair for a while with his hand on the newel, and thought about a man sitting on a step for three years being the one person in that building he had not once thought to find out about, which was either the office's worst failure or its plainest argument, and he could not tell which, and the not telling was the shape of the morning.

Three thousand eight hundred and eleven days. Less three thousand eight hundred and four, the twenty-fifth of the month before this one, is seven, and the half landing between the second and third floors has been empty at about two on every working afternoon since the thirtieth of August, and no page in that room can begin a row about a man who has never opened his mouth in it.

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is sixty-two, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-two of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and ninety-one days, which is forty-six months and eleven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

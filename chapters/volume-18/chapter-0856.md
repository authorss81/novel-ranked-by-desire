# Brack Counts The Weight

Three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven was the twenty-eighth of the month before this one and the fourth of this one is day three thousand six hundred and thirty-three, and three thousand six hundred and thirty-three less three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven is six, and out of a month that begins on the first, thirty less twenty-eight plus four is six.

## Nine Stools And A Slate Face Up

Two streets off Cordwain there is a lane with no name anybody uses and it is about nine feet wide, and it has nine stools down one side and nine districts behind the other, and a slate on a nail at the head of it and twelve books squared beside that are not the district’s.

Emil Brack had the slate face up, which it is on every morning in nine years except the ones when somebody has been down that lane inside four days, and this was not one of those mornings.

He is paid a grain a head for every head that stops, and a grain is nothing, and he takes about nine figures a morning before eleven and has never once been paid for a word he says off the stools and has never once asked to be.

“I have got about forty minutes,” he said, “and you have come down a hill to spend them on weight.”

## What A Lane Feels

“I am going to put a bound book on a table four floors up on a working morning.”

“How often.”

“Every working morning.”

“Then it goes up that stair about four times a day and it comes down about four times a day, and it comes down at about half past five when the light has gone.”

Emil Brack put the slate against his ribs, which is what he does when he is about to say a whole thing in one go, and then he took it off again, which he does not often do.

“Here is what you have asked me. A weight that goes up a stair four times a day in one building is a thing a lane feels. Not this lane. That lane. There are people in it who have counted out how many times a door bangs and people in it who have counted how many times the water comes on and neither of those people is a fool and one of them is a woman who sits at that lane end every morning with a stick.”

“Then what.”

“Then a lane that feels a thing is a lane that will be told about it,” said Brack. “Not by me. I have not got a book coming up a stair and I would not know what to do with one. Told by somebody in about four days, for nothing, the way a thing goes along a row of beds in a fever ward.”

“And about four hundred people in this city already know there is an office in that building and nobody has ever been able to be sent to it, and you are about to make the walking louder.”

## Nine Figures Before Eleven

A woman came down the lane at about ten past ten and took the fourth stool and put her hand out on the board without a word, which is what the fourth stool is for, and Brack gave her a ration out of the middle of the pot and wrote a figure on the slate and did not say the figure out loud to anybody, and she did not ask him to.

“That is the tenth this morning,” he said, after she had gone. “I take about nine before eleven and it is ten today and I have not got a reason and I am not going to invent one to hand to a man in a white plate.”

“It is the fourth of the month.”

“It is the fourth of the month and it has been the fourth of the month about nine times in nine years and it has not meant anything,” said Brack, “and I am telling you the figure because you are going to do something with a number in the next ten minutes and I would rather you had a wrong one from me than a right one you got somewhere else.”

He put the slate back against the wall.

“About four of mine in nine are about a person and about four of yours are about a person, and you have got that ratio the way I have got mine, and neither of us has ever been able to say a name at the other one’s rail. That is nine years of standing about nine feet off each other and it is not an oversight and it is not anybody’s fault. It is two counters in one city with no clerk over either of them.”

## The Month, Given Again, And Not Charged For

“Now the month, because it is mine and I give it when it is given.”

“Nine stools. Nine districts. About four kitchens inside the first week and about nine by the thirtieth, the same two every month, and the sum is taken again from nothing on the first of every month because a man who carries last month’s figure forward has stopped counting.”

“You gave me that on the thirteenth of July of the year before last.”

“I gave you that on the thirteenth of July of the year before last and it has not changed and it is not news and I have not been paid for it since,” said Brack. “It is a month. There is one every month and it does not improve and it does not get worse.”

“Nothing has changed in nine years at this end of it.”

“No, and the whole of what has changed is that you have started asking me things I cannot price,” said Brack, “and I have never once been thanked for a figure and I am not going to be thanked for a thing I cannot price, and you can put that in whatever you are keeping.”

## One Sentence More, And Not A Second

“It takes about four years,” said Emil Brack.

He did not put the slate down and he did not turn it over, and he said the whole of it in one go, which is what he does.

“After that the bottom two inches under that striking are quiet in about four hundred kitchens, and about forty people will have said where it came from, and about four hundred will have heard those forty and believed them, and not one of the four hundred will ever be told the reason. That is the sentence. I have been giving that sentence at this lane for about a year and it is the same sentence and it does not move for you.”

He wiped his hands down the front of his coat.

“And this is the one sentence more and I am not going to give you a second one. From this month there is going to be a book going up a stair four times a day, and I have not worked out what that does to the other half of it, and I am not going to guess at it standing at the head of a lane with a man who has nothing.”

“You could have worked it out.”

“I could have worked it out wrong and got it into about nine heads in a week, and then you would have had my figure instead of your own,” said Brack. “I have nine figures before eleven and I am not going to spend them telling a man in a white plate that I cannot tell him.”

## The One Thing He Asks For

“Say a number.”

“There is no number you want,” said Brack. “You have wanted a number off me four times in a year and every time you wanted it so that you would not have to ask me the next thing. So I am going to ask you for one thing instead and it is not a figure and it is not money and you are not going to be able to do it.”

“All right.”

“Keep that office open.”

“That is not a thing a man in a white plate can do.”

“No it is not,” said Brack. “That is the second half and I have got it right. It is not a door. It has no door, there is nothing on the wood, and none of them could shut it, and if you all die it goes on answering about four hundred and seventy rows, and you cannot close it and I cannot close it and nobody can close it.”

“Then what am I being asked for.”

“I am being asked for a thing I have seen exactly once in nine years at the head of this lane,” said Emil Brack. “Nine districts, about nine people a month, nine stools, and every single thing any of them has ever stopped doing they stopped doing alone in their own kitchen.”

“Every one of them put something down once in nine years, and it was not food and it was not work. It was a question, put to a room, by somebody who could not put it to a person. Nine years, one time, and that is the whole of what I have ever seen anybody put down twice.”

He put the slate under his arm.

“Do not shut it. Do not let it go quiet for a fortnight because it is inconvenient. That is all. I am not asking for it for me and I am not asking for it for the nine and I have not got a figure.”

## Nine People Before The Month Is Out

“Then what do you want.”

“I want you to go back up that hill,” said Emil Brack, “and I want you to be ready for about nine people before this month is out who will come up those four flights and ask who else has come up them.”

“That is not a hard question.”

“It is the hardest question there is and it takes nine minutes and it is not a question about a piece of paper,” said Brack. “It will be asked by people who have not got a fold in their hand and it will be asked by people who have, and it will be asked here.”

“And on the first of next month in that lane there will be a man at this rail who has been told it by nine people and has no instrument in the world to check any of it, and that man is me, and I will say what I always say, which is nothing, and I will be right and it will not help a soul.”

He looked at the four of the nine stools that anybody was sitting on at that hour, which was none of them.

“Go on up the hill,” said Brack. “You have about nine minutes and you will not spend them well.”

*Three thousand six hundred and thirty-three days. Less three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven, the twenty-eight of the month before this one, is six, and a man with a slate priced a weight going up a stair four times a day and would not price it, and gave one sentence more of a four-year sentence he has been giving at that lane for about a year and refused to give a second.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is sixty-four, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and thirteen days, which is forty months and thirteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

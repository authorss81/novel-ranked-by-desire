# Nine Hours With A Book

Three thousand six hundred and twenty was the twenty-first of this month and the twenty-eighth is day three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven, and three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven less three thousand six hundred and twenty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## Nine Hours, And The Same Wage

He put it to her on the morning of the twenty-eighth at the end of that table with the door open, and he gave it to her as a want and not as a decision, because it was a want.

“I want that book on this table on a working morning.”

She put her pen down and did not answer for about nine seconds, and then she gave him four sentences and she gave them in the order she had thought of them.

“If that book is in this room then I do not leave this room, and I am paid the same wage for being in it as I am paid for being in it now, and about four days a month I cannot say what we said and on those four days I have to say it anyway.”

“That door goes onto a yard and anybody in this city may walk up that stair. And a book lying open on a table in a room anybody may walk into is a page that can be read sideways, and that is not a fault you can fix with a good rule. It is a fault with a shape.”

He did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

“That is the best answer I have had to anything in nine years.”

“It is not an answer to a want, it is the price of one, and I have given you the price because you asked me for the want and you did not ask me whether it was a good idea,” said Wenna Raut.

“About nine answers a month go out of this room by hand. If that book is on this table then about nine times a month a person comes up four flights and stands in that room while a clerk looks for their own page in a book of about four hundred and seventy rows with no names on any of them, and I am the clerk.”

“Finding one page is about nine minutes, and getting it wrong is not a thing I can put right at four o’clock.”

## Nine Hours

She worked it out in front of him on the back of a fold.

“The first one comes up at about a quarter past nine and the last one goes down at about half past six, and that is about nine hours of the day in which the room is not empty and I am in it, and it is about nine hours on five days, and the book would be on that table from about a quarter past nine until about half past five, and then half an hour of clearing and signing and putting it back.”

“Two people to carry it.”

“Two people to carry it, and both of them have to be back before the door shuts, and one of them is me,” said Wenna Raut, “which is the part you did not think about and I have been thinking about it since the fourth working morning.”

He looked at the rota page on the wall behind the door, which carries four names, and she watched him look at it and did not help him.

“About nine people a month,” he said.

“About nine people a month, and about four of them come about a piece of paper they brought up themselves and about four of them come about another person, and you know that ratio and I know that ratio and neither of us can change it with a piece of furniture.”

## Four Hundred Yards And Four Narrow Flights

He left at about eleven, went down the hill and along to a street two streets wide, and took four narrow flights to the back of a laundry, and by the second of them his chest had begun, and he stopped on a landing for about a minute with a hand on the rail until it went on being bearable.

Perla Quist had a hand-truck at the near end of about nine long tables and the steam was coming off them and she did not stop working.

“I am going to put that book on a table four hundred yards from here in a room anybody may walk into,” he said, “and I am telling you before I do it and not asking you, because you are not carrying it and you have nothing to do with it.”

“All right.”

“I am telling you because in November you watched me put three pieces of paper into your stove in about nine seconds and did not thank me and were right not to, and I have told three rooms about doing that, and I am about to do something that is the same shape as it and I cannot hear the difference and I would like you to hear it.”

Perla Quist drove the edge of a flat iron along the seam of that table, once, without breaking stride, and did not look up.

“You have carried a thing in the inside of a coat on the left for nine years,” she said, “and you burned it in my stove and told three rooms, and now you are going to keep a book in a room and you have come down four narrow flights to tell a woman who keeps an index about it, and you have not asked me for anything.”

“No.”

“So here is the other half, and it costs you nothing to hear and it costs me nothing to say,” said Perla Quist, “and when you have had it you will not be able to hand it back and I would like you to notice that happening.”

## A Copy Is An Instrument

She came round the end of the table about four feet and stopped.

“A copy is the instrument,” she said. “Not the book. The copy. The book is a record and a record is a thing you find out about afterwards. The copy is the thing you hold.”

“That was the arrangement for nine years.”

“It was the arrangement for nine years and you have had it right and you have just described it to me wrong, and that is worse than getting it wrong on purpose, because you have come down here with your ears full of what you burned in November.”

Perla Quist put the hand-truck down against the end of the table.

“Any second copy of anything this office has ever written is a second instrument,” she said, “and a second instrument is a second place where the thing it is about can be found. That is all a copy is. It is not a spare. It is a place.”

“There is one copy of anything that office has ever written.”

“There is one copy of about nine things it has ever written and one copy of the rest of it is a hole in this city the shape of a stove,” said Perla Quist. “And you are proposing to fill the hole with four hundred and seventy rows of other people in a room with a door onto a yard, and you have not said one word to me about the other people.”

He had no answer to that and he did not make one up.

“I have nine sheets of my own paper under a third table,” she said. “They have never been in that room. They are not going to be in that room. I have never asked you where they are and I am not going to now, and if you ever tell a person in that building that a woman keeps an index then I will know and you will know that I know.”

“I have not told anybody.”

“You have told me, and that is one more than it was.”

## Get It From Where

She went back to her end of the table and picked the hand-truck up off the floor, and then she stopped and put it down again.

“Now tell me what happens when a person asks to see the page their own answer is written on.”

“We turn it in front of them.”

“You have not got it to turn,” said Perla Quist. “You said four floors down and about four hundred yards. So you would have to go and get it.”

“Yes.”

“Get it from where,” said Perla Quist, “and how long, and how do you know which page, and what do you say to the person while you are gone.”

He stood at the near end of about nine long tables in the wet and worked it out in the ordinary way, the way he would have worked out a drain.

It is a flat file on a filing floor about four hundred yards off, and there is a steel drawer and about forty quarterly sheets lying on top of it, and there is no card on the drawer with anything written on it, and there is no list of what is in that drawer anywhere in this city.

Finding one page out of about four hundred and seventy rows is about nine minutes if the person who knows the book is standing in front of it, and considerably longer if she is not.

He had no answer. He had been an hour off the stair by then and he had not got one.

“Say it out loud,” said Perla Quist. “You have been standing there for about nine seconds and you have not said one word, and I have watched you not say it for nine years and I am not going to guess at it this time.”

“There is no answer to that this morning,” said Lin Hao, “and I have thought about it since the fourth working morning and the answer is that a person asks for their own page and we go and fetch it, and it takes about nine minutes each way and it happens twice in about nine months, and if I ever do it in front of them I will tell them exactly how long I stood in front of that drawer and which page I looked at first.”

“That is a practice.”

“That is a practice and not ink and it is the first one I have written out loud in about three years,” he said, “and you are going to hear it said badly about four more times before it is any good, and I am not going to improve it for you.”

Perla Quist picked the hand-truck up and went back to the near end of her table.

“Then say it badly at me once,” she said, “so that I know you have started.”

*Three thousand six hundred and twenty-seven days. Less three thousand six hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of this month, is seven, and a clerk priced a book in that room in four sentences and a woman who keeps an index gave away the other half of it for nothing, and the thing he was asked at the end of the day was get it from where.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is fifty-eight, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and seven days, which is forty months and seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

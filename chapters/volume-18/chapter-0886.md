# The Forty-Third Sheet

Three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two was the twenty-third of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, and three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two is seven, and thirty less twenty-three is seven by the month count.

## An Ordinary Morning Under A Quarter-End

Nine years of quarter-ends have taught him one thing, which is that nothing else about a morning changes, and that is the cruelty of it.

About nine people came up those four flights. A man brought a paper about a water pipe and the boundary ran where the paper said it ran. A woman with a child on the bench asked about a word her husband used in April and got the word read out to her in the voice that office reads everything in and went down four flights with it.

The stair was loud from ten and quiet by one and loud again at two, and Wenna Raut found about four hundred and seventy rows' worth of a morning in it by saying out loud once, at about one, that about nine pages had gone into that book since the thirtieth of March and she was not going to count them, because counting them is a list of the days on which people came.

Somebody asked, halfway through the morning, whether anything was going up on the rail today, and Wenna Raut said that the sheet went up at four and it did not matter what was on it, and he thought for about nine seconds on the way down four flights that the second of those was a sentence worth having and then let it go.

## Four O'Clock In A Yard

The last working day of the quarter has a sound of its own and he has heard it three times before it was one he had made himself.

The forty-second sheet came off that rail at four, face up and going brown at the edges, and the forty-third went onto the nail in its place, and there was nothing new at the foot of it. There never has been. The sixth line of the office hand was entered on the second of December of the year before last, and nothing has gone underneath it since, and nothing is going to.

And the carriers came across that yard at half past four with the strap over one shoulder and stopped in the middle of it because there were four people there and a quarter-end is a thing that gets said in a yard.

"The head is unmoved," said Lin Hao. "Eight hundred and fifty-two, and it has not been touched since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. Twenty-seven sheets behind that number and twenty-six quarters, both of those correct and differing by one, and the arithmetic behind them is three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, which is two thousand three hundred and forty, which is twenty-six times ninety."

He said it in the ordinary voice, with no lift on it at all, because a thing said in the ordinary voice in a yard is a fact.

"That is the third time you have said it this year," said the woman with the back.

"It is the fourth, counting March, and I have said it in a corridor and in a day room and now in a yard, and every time it has been said to a different kind of person, and I am not going to do the other voice here."

The second carrier, who has been on that stair since the first year of it, looked at the new sheet going up the rail for about four seconds and then said the thing the rest of that yard was not going to say.

"That is the one that will still be up when one of us has stopped doing this."

"That is the one," said Lin Hao.

"And there is nobody going to write down which quarter it goes up on."

"No," he said. "And I have thought about that for about four minutes every time for five years and I have not found an instrument that does it without turning the rail into a register of the days this office was noticed in, and a rail that can be dated is a place where a person can be caught."

## About Eleven At A Rail

He went down the hill at half past four and the man who is paid nothing had about eleven people round his rail, which is what a quarter-end carries and not what nine people carry on a normal morning, and about four of the eleven were reading and the rest were letting the man at the counter read to them because it is easier.

"You will read the foot of it," said Lin Hao.

"I read the foot of it four times a year," said the counterman. "Do you want to stand at the rail and be in it or do you want to go up the hill and be out of it."

"Out of it."

He read it. Six lines, five of them instructions this office gave itself in the first year and the last of them a sentence about a copy of that foot that this office may never describe, and about eleven people in that passage heard all six of them go past and about four of them took any of it home.

## What A Woman Said At The Counter

"You want to know who felt it," said the counterman, when the rail had emptied down to about two.

"That is not a question I have asked."

"You have not asked it and you have been in two rooms today with that face on." He squared the quarterly sheet face up on the wood and left it there, because he has done that four times a year since the first year of it. "I will tell you what I have and I will not tell you anything else. About four people have asked me in a fortnight. One of them asked whether you could go in without knocking."

"And the other three."

"One said it was a filing office. One asked what the room was for. And one asked me, at about four o'clock last week, which stair was the one at the back of the court." He looked up. "And I told her."

## The Stair At The Back Of The Court

The yard went very quiet in a way that yards do not.

"You told her."

"I told her," said the counterman.

"I am paid nothing and she asked me a plain question in a plain voice and I am a man who reads the foot of a sheet out loud to whoever stands at my rail, and that is the whole of what I am for. I did not think about it for a second. I have thought about it every second since and I would do it again this afternoon and I am telling you now instead of letting you find it in a lane."

Lin Hao stood at that rail with about two people left in the passage and worked through it in the nine seconds he allowed himself.

A thing said at a rail goes home in about nine heads and it cannot be got back out of any of them. He could have refused it. He could have gone out into the passage and found the woman. He could not find her, and that was the end of that in about nine seconds, and in nine years he has never managed to call a thing back that has been said in a public place to a stranger.

"What did you say to her."

"I said the stair at the back of the court, with the arch under it, and the one the carriers go up," said the counterman. "And she said *thank you*, and went. And I have thought about it every second since and I would do it again this afternoon."

## What Is Left

He walked up that hill for the last time that quarter with the two of them in front of him carrying a book they had carried four hundred times, and he got to the top of it and stood and let them go down into the yard with the strap and the door and did not follow.

Somebody in this city can feel that office. A woman at the end of a tram line feels it every working morning and will not write one word of it down. A lane feels it. A stair feels it. A counter has been asked about it four times in a fortnight by strangers who are not the same strangers twice running.

And this office cannot find out who. It cannot ask, because asking a person whether they felt something is a question about the person and puts them in the open. It cannot enter it, because a row begins with the words a person used. It cannot keep it, because it is not a thing anybody in that room can hold.

That leaves one thing, and he had known it was going to leave one thing since the sixteenth of this month and had not wanted to say it to anybody until a man at a rail told him the truth about a stair in a passage.

"You go on carrying it," said Wenna Raut, from the window behind him.

"I go on carrying it."

"Say the whole of it, because in about four months somebody is going to stand in that room and say *you knew*."

"I go on carrying it," said Lin Hao, "because a book that stops moving is a book in a building, and a book in a building is a thing a man at a rail can describe to a stranger in four seconds, and I cannot stop a man who is paid nothing from saying a true thing."

"So it moves. Tomorrow at a quarter to ten and every working morning after that, and at half past five it goes back down, and that is the whole of what I have left to do and I am going to do it until it stops being the answer."

It was not the answer. It was the thing they had while the answer was being looked for, and he knew the difference and said it anyway, because the yard was emptying and the light was going and there was a quarter-end to get through.

Three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine days. Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-two, the twenty-third of this one, is seven, and the head of that sheet is where it was put on the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, eight hundred and fifty-two, through twenty-seven of these sheets and twenty-six of these quarters, which differ by one, both of them right. And three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand three hundred and forty, which is twenty-six times ninety.

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is ninety, and the forty-second sheet was face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and it came down off that rail at four this afternoon and the forty-third went onto the nail with nothing new at the foot of it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and nineteen days, which is forty-seven months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

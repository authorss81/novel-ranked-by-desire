# Nothing New In Four Inches

Three thousand six hundred and thirty-three was the fourth of this month and the eleventh is day three thousand six hundred and forty, and three thousand six hundred and forty less three thousand six hundred and thirty-three is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.

## Half Past Four And No Light On It

He came down the hill at about half past four on the eleventh of this month, which is three hours after the light has gone off that kerb and seven hours after it went off the wall behind the board, and he had come at that hour because at ten in the morning he would have been able to read the stone and a man who can read it is checking it.

The path off that hill goes down in three turns and comes out at a kerb, and the board stands against that kerb on the far side of it, and about four inches of dressed stone is set into the wall nine feet further in, and there was not enough light left in the third month to make out a letter of anything standing there.

That was the first thing he established and it took him about four seconds.

He had gone down there twice since the beginning of February without wanting anything, and he had come at ten both times, and the light had been on that stone both times, and he had read it both times.

He had not come at ten this time on purpose, and he could not afterwards have said why he had come at half past four instead, except that a man who can read a thing is checking it, and he had wanted to go and look rather than to go and see.

## Fresh On The Top Batten

He could not see the board at all. He put his hand on the kerb and followed it along until his knuckles found the wood, and then he put the back of a knuckle against the top batten, without meaning to, in the dark, the way a hand goes to a thing it already knows the shape of.

It gave about a finger's width.

Cold paste does not do that. Paste put on a wall in this city does not do it any time after the first four minutes, and what his knuckle had found was paste that has been on that batten about four days, in the cold, by somebody's hand.

Nine years and there is one thing at that corner that anybody maintains, and it is not the stone and it is not the form and it is not the two squares. It is the glue on the top batten, and it is put there about once a month by somebody who comes up that hill to do that and nothing else, and the form goes up on the seventh and comes down on the thirtieth.

## What He Knows And What He Cannot Read

He stood about four feet off that wall in the dark and read out of his own head what is in those four inches, and he did it once, and he told himself while he was doing it that he was not looking at it.

About fifty words stand in that stone in about six hands. Five of the six are hands he could name a year for and the sixth is the newest, and the newest one is about a woman.

It says that on the nineteenth of November a woman came up four flights in the cold, stood in a corridor about four feet wide, and went back down again without saying one word to anybody, and that not one person in that building asked her anything.

That is the whole of the sentence.

It stops about four inches in from the left-hand end of that stone, in a gap between two chisel marks that are a finger’s width apart, and behind the words the iron is still clean, and the sentence is not finished and will not be finished this year.

The two words scratched into that stone are not among those six hands. Nothing whatever is known about them here and nothing whatever is going to be said about them.

Under the lower batten, in the place where the paste has gone thin and let the wood take hold of it again, a piece of office paper is kept against that wall, with a date at the top of it and a pencil line ruled straight across the foot of it in her own hand.

It has not been folded. It has not been turned over. It has not been taken, and he did not come near it, and nothing has been sent, and nothing has been asked of anybody, and that ruled line is still the single mark anybody who cannot read has put at that corner in the whole of the nine years anybody has been counting.

The two squares printed at the foot of that month’s form have both been empty since the eighth of October of the first year, and the reason has never moved in nine years.

He could not see one of those things. He was reciting it in the dark off about two years of looking at it, which is the thing he did not want to be doing, and he made himself stop.

## What He Came Down The Hill For

He had been down there twice this month without wanting anything from it, and it was on the second of the two that he worked out what he was doing, and he arrived at it standing still on a wet kerb in the dark.

He was checking that not one person had gone looking for her.

In December, four flights up, he had wanted to find that woman so badly that he got to the bottom of that hill before he stopped himself, and he stopped himself because a man who goes looking for the person who was put off is the man who fetches people.

There is no seventh hand on that stone, and there is not going to be one this year, and the counting of hands is not a thing this office has ever done before and he did it twice in the dark.

Not one person in this city has gone looking for her either. Not one has come up that hill to ask after a woman who stood in a corridor for four minutes in the cold and went back down. She is not in that stone as an explanation. She is in that stone because somebody who did not know her wrote down that she was there, and that is a different thing.

It is four inches of dressed stone nine feet behind a board at the foot of a hill, with fresh paste on the batten above it. About four hundred people go past that kerb in a week and nine or so of them crouch. It is the only account of that morning that exists in this city, and the weather is the only thing keeping it, and one man keeps the glue above it for reasons he has never been asked for.

He went up the hill about four hundred yards and up four flights with nothing in either hand.

## The Question He Took Up The Stair

He carried it to the end of that table and put it there with the door open, which is where he puts things.

“Does anybody know who puts paste on that board.”

Wenna Raut did not look up from the page she was on.

“Define anybody.”

“Anybody in this building. Anybody who has ever gone down that hill on office business. You. Me.”

“I have been up that hill once in nine years and it was the winter a sheet came back wrong,” said Wenna Raut, “and I did not go to the foot of it, and I did not see the wall, and I would not have known there was a stone.”

“So there is nobody.”

“There is somebody, because paste does not keep and his did,” said Wenna Raut, “and that person comes up that hill about once a month to do a piece of work that another person arranged, and about four people a week walk past him on the way and not one of them has ever stopped.”

“Then it is a person.”

“It is a person doing a job,” said Wenna Raut, “and if you go down there and ask him anything then he becomes a person this office asked something about, and in about four years somebody will come up four flights and want to know why, and the answer will be that a man in a white plate went down a slope in the dark to ask a man who keeps a board in repair who he was.”

“I am not going to ask him.”

“I know you are not, and I have not said it to make you say it,” she said, “and I would like it kept in your own head and not on any page, that you noticed it. In nine years you have never once noticed what maintains a thing. You have only ever looked at what people cut into things after the people who cut them had gone.”

She squared the standing sheet on the shelf behind the door.

“Not one person in this city knows that the only maintained thing at the foot of that hill is the glue that holds it up, and neither of us is going to be the one who tells them, and I have said that last part out loud twice this afternoon and I would like you to notice it the way I noticed it.”

“I could not read it this afternoon.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the first honest thing you have put on that table in about four months and I am not going to do anything with it except let it stand there.”

*Three thousand six hundred and forty days. Less three thousand six hundred and thirty-three, the fourth of this month, is seven, and a man went down a slope at half past four in the last of the light and could not read four inches of dressed stone and found fresh paste on the batten above it with the back of a knuckle.*

*And there is not a seventh hand, and a man who keeps that board in repair has come up that hill about once a month for nine years and has never been asked anything by anybody.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is seventy-one, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and twenty days, which is forty months and twenty days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# About Sixty Words In Seven Hands

Three thousand eight hundred and ninety was the twenty-first of this one and the twenty-seventh of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and ninety-six, and three thousand eight hundred and ninety-six less three thousand eight hundred and ninety is six, and twenty-seven less twenty-one is six by the month count.

## Four Inches Of Dressed Stone

He went down that hill at about three in the afternoon with the light already going off the wall, because the corner at the foot of it gets no direct light after midday and he wanted to see it the way a stranger would see it.

The board stood nine feet back from the kerb, with both ruled squares empty and the paste on the top batten about four days old, put there by somebody who comes up that hill about once a month and has done for about nine years without ever being known.

Under the lower batten, held against the wood, her sheet lay unmoved, with a date at the top and a pencil line ruled across the foot of it in her own hand, not folded, not turned over, not taken, and he did not touch it.

Four inches of dressed stone run along the foot of that wall, and in them about sixty words in seven hands, cut at different times by different people with different tools, and the newest hand is the seventh, and it was promised in the spring and it is here now.

## The Seventh Hand

The first six he knew the way a man knows a wall he has visited for months: a word about a pipe, a line about a rent, a sentence about a stair in bad weather that turns round at the top and goes back down, finishing short of the left-hand end with the cutting clean behind it, and that sixth is not about a building, which is what he went down there two months expecting to find out.

The seventh is lower down, near the right-hand end, cut small and hurried, about nine words, in a hand that held the tool the way a person holds a nail when there is no hammer to be had.

It says that a woman came up four flights and was read to and went home, and it stops in the middle, with the cutting clean behind it, the way the sixth stops, the way all of them stop except the ones that trail off into the stone.

He stood there a long time with his hands in his coat pockets and read those about sixty words through twice, and felt the cold come up off the stone into his knees, and thought about how much of his office is in that corner without ever having been written by his office.

Two words are scratched small and hard in that stone, apart from the seven, and they are not among those seven and never going to be, and nothing whatever is asserted about them, and he did not read them twice.

## Her Sheet Under The Batten

He looked once more at her sheet under the lower batten, held against the wood, with a date at the top and a pencil line ruled across the foot of it in her own hand, the only mark a person who cannot read can make, and it was unmoved, not folded, not turned over, not taken.

Nine years it has lain there, through rain and cold and fresh paste about once a month, and no messenger has gone to find the woman whose hand ruled that line, and nothing has been asked of her, and the sheet has outlasted every figure that office ever printed about itself.

He touched the edge of the board beside it with the back of a knuckle, lightly, to feel whether the paste was still tacky, and it was, about four days old, and the tack of it told him more about that hill than any row in that book: somebody comes, somebody pastes, somebody keeps the squares empty, and the keeping of it goes on without a keeper, and the asking of it will not be done.

A woman with a short-handled broom keeps that corner after her own fashion, unnamed and unasked for the seventh running year, and he did not call out for her, and did not look for her, because looking would have been a kind of asking, and he has stopped asking that hill for anything but stone.

## What Stops In The Middle

Wenna Raut was at that table when he got back up, with that book shut and the strap round it for the night, and she looked at his boots and knew where he had been.

"Seven," said Lin Hao.

"The seventh is there."

"It is there. And the sentence stops in the middle with the cutting clean behind it, the way the sixth does. About sixty words in seven hands in four inches, and every one of them stops where the hand gave out and not where the thought gave out."

"And what does the seventh add."

"Nothing that can be entered. A woman came up and was read to and went home. Which woman. Which reading. Which home. The stone does not say, and the hand that cut it is not a hand this office knows, and I am not going to ask whose it is."

She nodded, and put the pen down, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because there was nothing in that room that could hold what was on that wall, and both of them knew it.

## The Paste On The Batten

He told her then what else he had found at that corner, because the corner has two things and not one, and both of them belong to nine years of keeping without knowing.

"Fresh paste on the top batten," said Lin Hao. "About four days old. Put there by somebody who comes up that hill about once a month to do a job with a board and a brush, and not one person in the city knows who it is, and the asking of it has never been done in that office, and will not be done."

"And the board was bare otherwise."

"The board was bare with the paste showing, and both ruled squares empty, and her sheet unmoved under the lower batten with her own pencil line across the foot of it. The woman who keeps that corner with a short-handled broom was not there and is never asked, and the boy with the brush is not on any page now, because neither the seventh of this month nor the seventh of next month is a day anything goes up there."

Wenna Raut listened the way she listens when a thing cannot be entered, with her hands still and her eyes on his face, and he understood that she was keeping his hour of coming up that hill the way he keeps everybody else's, in memory and nowhere else.

## Why He Goes Down There

He thought about the people who cut words into stone at the foot of a hill because paper would not keep them, and about the clerk across from him who enters what she is given and cannot leave that room while that book is in it, and the thinking went on him the way cold goes through a boot, and he was not proud of it and did not stop.

## The Hour He Keeps

Wenna Raut asked him then for the hour he went down that hill, not to enter it but to hold it the way people hold things that will never be on a page, and he told her, about three in the afternoon, with the light already going off the wall, and she nodded and kept it in memory and nowhere else.

"And the seventh," she said. "Say it once more, so I have heard it from you and not only from stone."

He said it, the hurried cut near the right-hand end, about nine words, a woman coming up four flights and being read to and going home, stopping in the middle with the cutting clean behind it, and she listened all the way through without writing, because writing it would have made it hers and it belongs to the wall.

"About sixty words in seven hands," said Wenna Raut. "And the two scratched apart from the seven, not among them and never going to be. That corner holds more of this office than any row we have entered since the spring, and none of it can come upstairs."

He stood at that table with stone dust still on his boots and felt the hill cold in his knees, and thought about hands holding tools like nails for lack of hammers, and about sentences stopping where hands give out, and about how honest a stop can be when the cutting stays clean behind it.

## The Cold On His Boots

That evening the carriers found stone dust on the stair where his boots had left it, and the woman with the back looked at the pale marks on the dark wood and then at him, without reproach, because dust is what hills leave on men who go down them honestly.

"You went to the corner," she said.

"I went to the corner," said Lin Hao. "About sixty words in seven hands. The seventh hurried and small, stopping in the middle with the cutting clean behind it. The two scratched apart from the seven, not among them and never going to be."

"And her sheet."

"Unmoved. With her own pencil line across the foot of it. Not folded. Not turned over. Not taken. Nine years under a batten with paste about once a month, and no sending to find her, and no asking of her."

She nodded, and coiled the strap for the night's going down, and he understood that she was carrying the corner with them the way they carry everything that cannot be entered: without writing it, without counting it, and without leaving it behind.

That evening he went down four flights in the dark with that book against his chest, and at the bottom he stood in the yard and looked up at the four flights of windows without going back to them, and the cold took the smell of paste off his coat.

Three thousand eight hundred and ninety-six days. Less three thousand eight hundred and ninety, the twenty-first of this one, is six, and about sixty words stand in seven hands in four inches of dressed stone, and the newest stops in the middle with the cutting clean behind it.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fifty-seven, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-seven of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and seventy-six days, which is forty-nine months and six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

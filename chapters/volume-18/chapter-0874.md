# Owed To Somebody Dead

Three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine was the thirtieth of the month before this one and the sixth of this one is day three thousand seven hundred and fifty-five, and three thousand seven hundred and fifty-five less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine is six, and thirty less thirty plus six is six by the month count.

## The Family Comes Up Together

They came up at about eleven on the sixth, three of them together, a brother and a sister and an older woman who held the rail all the way, and the brother did the talking because the other two had agreed it would fall to him.

"We are the family of the man who was here on the twenty-third," he said. "About his wife. Who died in the sixth month. Whose row is dated the second month."

Wenna Raut set the pen down and folded her hands, and Lin Hao came to the end of that table, because from the tenth of June the answer is read out and the page is not turned, and about nine people a month are told so, and these three had not been told yet.

"She asked here in the second month about a drain," said Lin Hao. "And the row was found on the twenty-third and read out twice. That is the whole of what the office holds that I can give."

"We do not want the drain," said the brother. "We want what the office can say about somebody dead and what it cannot. The second of those is the one we came up four flights for."

## What Can Be Said

Wenna Raut read the row again in the voice that office reads everything in, a date and an hour and a woman's words about water under a wall and four lines under them, with the three of them standing in the room and the person the row was about not in it.

The older woman wept quietly through the middle of it, and the sister held her arm, and the brother kept his eyes on the wood of the table.

"That is what can be said," said Lin Hao when the reading ended. "A woman came up four flights in the second month. She used certain words about a drain. A clerk wrote them down with the date and the hour. An answer went under them in her hand. All of that is true and all of it was read."

"And what cannot," said the brother.

"What cannot is whether she is dead," said Lin Hao. "The office does not write that word. Not in that book and not on a slip. The difference between the two is about nine words long, and here it is: we can say she asked, and we cannot say she died."

The sister drew in her breath sharply, and the older woman made a small sound, and the brother nodded slowly, as if he had expected the sentence and had hoped it would sound different in a room.

## Why The Word Is Refused

"You have her row," said the brother. "You have her words. And you will not put nine letters after them to say what the whole street knows."

"If I put that word against her date," said Lin Hao, "then the first row anybody could be identified from is a row saying a person is dead. And from that day any stranger can go through that book asking after the dead and learn the living by where the word is not."

He looked at each of them in turn, because they had come up four flights together and they deserved his face and not his voice at the end of the table.

"Your family has nothing at all from that winter," he said. "No fold with the flap gummed and no paper with a date on the outside. I know that because he told me on the twenty-third. And I am saying so to you myself rather than letting you find it out going through boxes."

"She kept everything," said the sister. "Every paper. She had boxes of it. My brother went through all of it."

"Then the fold was lost or was put where folds get put," said Lin Hao. "And I will not make you a copy to stand in for it, because a copy is a second place where the thing it is about can be found, and the office burned its only copy of anything in November and will not make another."

The brother was angry now, and the anger was clean, and Lin Hao was glad of it, because clean anger is easier to carry down four flights than bewilderment.

## What The Older Woman Carried

The older woman had said nothing through all of it, and now she opened the cloth bag she had held on her lap and took out a folded square of linen, and inside the linen lay a brass key and a twist of hair tied with thread.

"She kept these with her papers," said the sister, low. "The key is to the box. The hair is from when she was a girl. Our mother cut it. She asked here about a drain and she told nobody at home what was said. And now the drain is fixed and she is gone, and the fixing and the going have nothing to do with one another."

Lin Hao looked at the small things on the linen, and felt the familiar pressure behind his eyes, because the office holds dates and hours and words used, and a house holds keys and hair, and neither holding reaches the other.

"Keep them together," he said. "With the boxes. And put with them the date she came, which was the second month, and the date she died, which was the sixth. Those two dates are yours. No interval belongs beside them that I can give you."

The brother put his hand over his mother's hand where it rested on the linen, and the older woman folded the corners back over the key, and the gesture was practiced, as if she had folded and unfolded it many mornings.

## At The Door

At the door the brother turned back, and his anger had gone somewhere behind his eyes, and what was left was the plain tiredness of a man carrying for others.

"We will look behind the boxes," he said. "As you said. And if the fold comes to light we will keep it with the key. And if it does not, we have the reading. Twice now. That is more than most houses hold of a winter."

The older woman nodded at that, and the sister did not, because she was looking at Lin Hao instead of at her brother.

"What do we tell people," she said. "When they ask what the office said about her."

"Tell them we read her words back," said Lin Hao. "And that the reading took less than a minute. And that she came up four flights in the cold of the second month and was proud of a paper she had copied out twice. And then tell them the rest is not ours to give, and leave it there, because a street given nothing else will make up something better and be wrong about it as well."

The sister said that was a hard thing to be handed at a door, and thanked him for it in the plain way of people who have run out of words, and the older woman touched his sleeve as she went past and said nothing at all.

## Down Four Flights With Nothing

They went down the four flights together, slowly, because of the older woman, and he stood at the head of that stair and listened to them going until the stair was quiet.

Then he stood in the room with Wenna Raut and the door open and nothing in it, and she entered the morning in the words the brother had used, with the date and the hour, and the pen scratched softly, and the bench where the older woman had been sitting for an hour still had the print of her in the wood.

He felt a heaviness that was not his chest, though the chest was part of it, and he wanted to have given them something to hold, and the want stayed with him through the afternoon, because an office that answers honestly and in the open had answered, and the answer was true, and it was not enough, and it would not be enough in August either.

## The Walk To The Lane

He went down that hill at about three to walk the lane behind the building where the drain runs, because he needed to see water going under a wall with his own eyes after saying so much about it in a room. The water ran low and brown between the stones, and a woman washing steps nodded to him, and he nodded back, and neither spoke.

On the way back he stopped where the road turns and looked up at the four flights of windows, counting them the way he counts everything now, and thought about February and June and the two dates that belong to that family, and about how a row dated one month and mourned in another leaves everybody holding paper that does not meet.

Wenna Raut had the room straight when he returned, and the pen put away, and she looked up as he came in, and the look meant the morning was entered and the cost written down, and he nodded.

The two of them finished the day the way they finish every day now, with the book strapped at half past five and the stair quiet behind it, and the drain running low behind the building whether anybody watches it or not, and the key and the hair folded back in their linen in a house down the lane.

## What The Third Family Will Ask

"That was the second family," said Wenna Raut, closing the pen into the tray. "And there will be a third before this month is out. I have the passage saying so."

"I know."

"And the third will not be about somebody dead. It will be about somebody who is alive and lives on the fourth floor of a building on Cordwain and will not come down, and you have nothing said about that either, and I have watched you tell a man this office says nothing when it says something twice."

He had no answer to that and did not make one, which was a thing he had not done at that end of that table in nine years.

"Then say it again on the thirteenth in the same words," said Wenna Raut, "and do not get better at it as the summer goes on, because every time you get better at it they stop believing you and start believing the office, and the office has not earned that."

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and thirty-five days, which is forty-four months and fifteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

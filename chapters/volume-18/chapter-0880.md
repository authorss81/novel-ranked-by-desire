# The Dead Are Not Excepted

Three thousand seven hundred and ninety was the eleventh of this month and the eighteenth is day three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven, and three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven less three thousand seven hundred and ninety is seven, and eighteen less eleven is seven by the month count.

## Everybody Together

They were all there on the eighteenth, the brother and the sister and the older woman of the sixth of July, and the woman of the thirteenth with her slip folded in her pocket, and the sister with the stick, and about four others who had asked through the summer, and Wenna Raut at the table, and Lin Hao at the end of it.

He had asked them to come, every one, by word passed along lanes and stairs, because a thing said to one family in a room is gossip and a thing said to all of them together is a practice, and what he had to say needed witnesses.

"I have had the book on that table for seventy days and gave it away in the same quarter," he said, "and then for two months I have answered about the dead and about the living who cannot come, and every answer has taught me the same thing."

He looked at each face in turn, because they had all come up four flights and every one of them deserved his eyes and not only his voice.

## The Second Closure

"From this morning an answer about a person is given only if the person is in the room."

That is the plainest sentence in the room, and he said it in the voice he keeps for that end of that table, and then he said it again in the ordinary voice, so it would stand as a fact and not only as a decision.

"With no exception for the dead, and none for the sister, and none for anybody. The man who asked on the twenty-third about his wife was read to and will not be read to again unless the living come. The sister who came up twice will be read to when she comes, and not carried to. The woman who wants a line saying dead will not have it, here or on a slip."

The brother stood up, angry, and the sister with the stick put a hand on his arm, and the older woman wept quietly, and the woman of the thirteenth stared at the wood.

"You are shutting us out," said the brother. "After we came. After she died. After all of it."

"I am," said Lin Hao. "And the price of that sentence is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. And you are that family, and I am saying so to your faces rather than letting you hear it from a lane."

## Each Of Them Heard

The older woman wept without sound, and the sister held her, and the woman of the thirteenth folded her slip smaller and smaller until it would fold no more, and the sister with the stick held herself very straight, and the four others stood along the wall with their faces closed.

"I came in June about my father," said one of the four, a young man with plaster on his hands. "And you read to me. And I went home and told my mother what you said. And now you say that if she had asked instead of me, you would have told her nothing."

"That is what I say," said Lin Hao. "If the row is about you, come, and it will be read with you in the room. If it is about another, bring them, or do without. The row above yours is somebody else's, and the dead cannot come, and the living who cannot come cannot be carried to."

The young man stared, and then nodded once, sharply, and looked at the floor, and Lin Hao saw the refusal land in him the way it had landed in every room since June, with anger first and then the slow weight after.

## What Closes And What It Costs

Wenna Raut stood then, and her voice shook, and she said the thing she had said on the tenth of June, because it needed saying again by somebody who is not him.

"From the tenth of June the page is not turned towards anybody," she said. "And from this morning the answer about a person is given only with the person in the room. Both are practices and neither is ink. And the sixth line under those four sentences, which went in on the second of December of the year before last, is still the last that this office will ever put at the foot of a page of its own."

The brother sank down heavily, and the anger went out of him all at once, and what was left was tiredness, and Lin Hao knew that tiredness because he carries it himself every night up four flights of washrooms.

The sister with the stick spoke last, before they went.

"I came up twice," she said. "And I am glad I came. And if I cannot come again you will not answer about me. And that is right. Because I would rather be unanswered than be a column in your book."

They went down the four flights together, all of them, slowly, with the stick tapping, and he stood at the head of that stair until the last step faded, and Wenna Raut stood beside him, and neither of them spoke.

## Outside The Day Room

He felt the loss of it like a weight lifted and a door shut at once, and his want was to follow them down and take back the sentence, and he did not follow, because a decision that can be taken back is not a decision.

Later he walked to the dispensary in the cold of the evening and stood a while outside the day room where nine beds stand and the window is open, thinking about Tessa Okonkwo and what she had told him about the dead, and about how the office will answer from this morning, and about how much quieter the stair will be now.

The light glowed low in the windows, and a nurse passed with linen, and from inside came the ordinary sounds of a ward growing quiet for the night, water and low voices and a bedstead shifted. He did not go in.

He stood with his hands in his pockets and his breath going white, and thought about the price of the sentence, which is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. And about how the price will be paid again every month by people he will never meet.

A window opened above him and a voice called goodnight to somebody, and he turned up his collar and walked back along the cold street, past the lane with the nine stools and the slate on the nail, past the kerb where the board goes up, up the hill to the building where the book sleeps in its flat file four floors down.

At the door he stopped and looked back at the city lying white under the cold, and felt the sentence lie over it like a lid, and felt, under the grief, a clean hardness, because the office can now say what it will do and do what it says, and that is worth the quiet stair.

Wenna Raut entered the morning last of all, in the words all of them had used, with the date and the hour, and the entering took the best part of an hour, because so many had spoken, and when it was done she blotted it and closed the pen into the tray, and looked at him across the wood.

"It is done," she said. "From this morning an answer about a person is given only with the person in the room. And the page is not turned. And both are practices and neither is ink. And the sixth line under those four sentences is still the last."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And the price is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. Say so on the page" — and she shook her head, because the price is not entered, and they both knew it, and the knowing was the whole of the evening.

They stood together at the window though the carriers do the strapping at half past five, because the evening needed company more than work, and watched the yard empty below. Nine years of other people's words would sleep in the flat file four floors down. Above them the room would stand empty with the door standing open.

He thought about June and the page turned away, and about July and the clause read back wrong, and about August and the sister twice come, and about how the three months have taught one thing: that the office will answer about a person only when the person is in the room.

The arrangement holds, and the stair is quieter now, and that is the movement's answer and its cost in one, and the book goes back to the file, and the morning will bring what it brings.

The room will be ready, with the door standing open and the pen in the tray, and the two of them will meet it together, as they have met every morning of this movement, and as they will meet every morning after, for as long as the book comes up four flights and goes down again at half past five.

Outside the day room the window stands open in the cold, and nine beds stand in a row.

The clerk two floors up writes the names down, and the room below keeps its quiet, and the book sleeps in its file, and the morning will come.

*Three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven days. Less three thousand seven hundred and ninety, the eleventh of this month, is seven, and from this morning an answer about a person is given only if the person is in the room, with no exception for the dead.*

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is forty-eight, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and seventy-seven days, which is forty-five months and twenty-seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

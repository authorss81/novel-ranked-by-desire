# The Dead Are Not Excepted

Three thousand seven hundred and ninety was the eleventh of this month and the eighteenth is day three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven, and three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven less three thousand seven hundred and ninety is seven, and eighteen less eleven is seven by the month count.

## Everybody Together

They were all there on the eighteenth.

The man whose wife died in the sixth month stood by the door with his coat over his arm, because he had asked for this and had not known he was asking. Behind him were his brother and his sister and his mother of the sixth of July, and his sister had hold of the old woman's arm and was not letting go of it.

The woman of the thirteenth stood at the end of the table with a paper in her hand that she had not had on the thirteenth and had gone out and paid for.

The sister with the stick was by the window with the stick against her leg. About four others who had asked through the summer stood along the wall. Wenna Raut was at the table and Lin Hao at the end of it.

He had asked them to come, every one, by word passed along lanes and stairs, because a thing said to one family in a room is gossip and a thing said to all of them together is a practice, and what he had to say needed witnesses.

"I have had the book on that table for seventy days and gave it away in the same quarter," he said, "and then for two months I have answered about the dead and about the living who cannot come, and every answer has taught me the same thing."

He looked at each face in turn, because they had all come up four flights and every one of them deserved his eyes and not only his voice.

## The Second Closure

"From this morning an answer about a person is given only if the person is in the room."

That is the plainest sentence in the room, and he said it in the voice he keeps for that end of that table, and then he said it again in the ordinary voice, so it would stand as a fact and not only as a decision.

"With no exception for the dead, and none for the sister, and none for anybody."

"The man who asked on the twenty-third about his wife is standing in this room and will not be read to again, because his wife cannot be in it. The sister who came up twice will be read to when she comes, and not carried to. The woman who wanted a line saying dead will not have one here, and she has paid another office for a certificate, and I will enter that she brought it, in her words, with the date and the hour."

The brother stood up, angry, and the sister beside him put a hand on his arm, and the older woman wept quietly, and the woman of the thirteenth looked down at the certificate in her hand, and the man at the door did not move at all.

"You are shutting us out," said the brother. "After we came. After she died. After all of it."

"I am," said Lin Hao. "And the price of that sentence is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. And you are that family, and I am saying so to your faces rather than letting you hear it from a lane."

## What The Man At The Door Came For

He had been quiet for all of it, and then he said the one thing in that room that had not been in anybody's plan, and it was not an objection.

"You read it to me twice," he said. "That is twice more than I had anywhere else in this city."

"I am not here to be told I get nothing. I am here because a woman on a street can put my wife's date against a word and go up and down that street with it, and because neither your book nor any stamped certificate in this city stops her, and because the only two people who will ever have stood in a room and said out loud that they will not write it are you and me this morning."

"That is what the office says," said Lin Hao.

"Then say the whole of it," said the man. "Say which of the two I was asking for on the twenty-third, because I have not been able to work it out since and I have had a summer of it."

"The second," said Lin Hao. "You were asking which one to trust, and I gave you neither, and I did not name that at the time, and naming it now does not make up for it."

## Each Of Them Heard

The older woman wept without sound, and the widower's sister held her, and the woman of the thirteenth folded her certificate smaller and smaller until it would fold no more, and the sister with the stick held herself very straight, and the four others stood along the wall with their faces closed.

"I came in June about my father," said one of the four, a young man with plaster on his hands. "And you read to me. And I went home and told my mother what you said. And now you say that if he had asked instead of me, you would have told him nothing."

"That is what I say," said Lin Hao. "If the row is about you, come, and it will be read with you in the room. If it is about another, bring them, or do without. The row above yours is somebody else's, and the dead cannot come, and the living who cannot come cannot be carried to."

The young man stared, and then nodded once, sharply, and looked at the floor, and Lin Hao saw the refusal land in him the way it had landed in every room since June, with anger first and then the slow weight after.

## What Closes And What It Costs

Wenna Raut stood then, and her voice shook, and she said the thing she had said on the tenth of June, because it needed saying again by somebody who is not him.

"From the tenth of June the page is not turned towards anybody," she said. "And from this morning the answer about a person is given only with the person in the room. Both are practices and neither is ink. And the sixth line in that office hand, which went in on the second of December of the year before last, is still the last that this office will ever put at the foot of a page of its own."

The brother sank down heavily, and the anger went out of him all at once, and what was left was tiredness, and Lin Hao knew that tiredness because he carries it himself every night up four flights of washrooms.

The sister with the stick spoke last, before they went.

"I came up twice," she said. "And I am glad I came. And if I cannot come again you will not answer about me. And that is right. Because I would rather be unanswered than be a column in your book."

They went down the four flights together, all of them, slowly, with the stick tapping, and the man from the twenty-third went last, and stopped at the head of that stair a moment longer than the rest of them and said goodnight, and it was the first thing he had said to any of them since June, and Lin Hao stood there long after the last step had faded.

## Outside The Day Room

He felt the loss of it like a weight lifted and a door shut at once, and his want was to follow them down and take back the sentence, and he did not follow, because a decision that can be taken back is not a decision.

Later he walked to the dispensary in the cold of the evening and stood a while outside the day room where nine beds stand and the window is open, thinking about Tessa Okonkwo and what she had told him about the dead, and about how the office will answer from this morning, and about how much quieter the stair will be now.

The light glowed low in the windows, and a nurse passed with linen, and from inside came the ordinary sounds of a ward growing quiet for the night, water and low voices and a bedstead shifted. He did not go in.

He stood with his hands in his pockets and his breath going white, and thought about the price of the sentence, which is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. And about how the price will be paid again every month by people he will never meet.

A window opened above him and a voice called goodnight to somebody, and he turned up his collar and walked back along the cold street, past the lane with the nine stools and the slate on the nail, past the kerb where the board goes up, up the hill to the building where the book sleeps in its flat file four floors down.

At the door he stopped and looked back at the city lying white under the cold, and felt the sentence lie over it like a lid, and felt, under the grief, a clean hardness, because the office can now say what it will do and then do it, and a quiet stair is worth what it costs him at that end of that table every morning of the rest of his working life.

Wenna Raut entered the morning last of all, in the words all of them had used, with the date and the hour, and the entering took the best part of an hour, because so many had spoken, and when it was done she blotted it and closed the pen into the tray, and looked at him across the wood.

"It is done," she said. "From this morning an answer about a person is given only with the person in the room. And the page is not turned. And both are practices and neither is ink. And the sixth line in that office hand is still the last."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And the price is a family in a street who came up four flights and were told the truth and were not given anything to hold. Say so on the page," and she shook her head, because the price is not entered, and they both knew it, and the knowing was the whole of the evening.

They stood together at the window though the carriers do the strapping at half past five, because the evening needed company more than work, and watched the yard empty below. Nine years of other people's words would sleep in the flat file four floors down, and above them the room would stand empty with the door standing open.

## What September Will Ask Of The Practice

"Say it once more to me," said Wenna Raut, "slowly, and tell me what it does to a person who is alive."

"It does to them what it does to a widow," said Lin Hao. "It tells them the office will answer and will not carry, and a person can live with that sentence until the day they cannot come down a stair."

"And then it is not a sentence," said Wenna Raut.

"Then it is a door shut in the face of somebody who was ill. And I have made it the standing rule of a room, and in a year there will be a person on a bed somewhere who has never been able to come up these four flights, and their family will be told this office has nothing for them, and that will be true, and it will be because of me this morning."

"You knew that at the table."

"I knew it at about one o'clock this afternoon while I was finding the thing to say, and I said it anyway, in the voice that keeps a decision a decision, and about nine people were in the room." He put his thumb against the window frame. "That is what a practice costs. Not the money and not the stair. You find out what you have done at one o'clock and you have to go on saying it at twelve, and there is nobody you can hand it to."

Wenna Raut did not argue, and she did not agree either, and after a while she said, "Then it is a practice and not ink, and it is yours, and I will stand at the table with you when the first person comes up in September and finds out."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And the first of them will be somebody who heard about it in a lane, and will not be angry, and that will be the worst of it."

Outside the day room the window stands open in the cold, and nine beds stand in a row.

The clerk two floors up writes the names down, and the room below keeps its quiet, and the book sleeps in its file, and the morning will bring what it brings.

*Three thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven days. Less three thousand seven hundred and ninety, the eleventh of this month, is seven, and from this morning an answer about a person is given only if the person is in the room, with no exception for the dead.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and seventy-seven days, which is forty-five months and twenty-seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Book Goes Down At Half Past Five

Three thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight was the nineteenth of this one and the twenty-fifth of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and sixty-four, and three thousand eight hundred and sixty-four less three thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight is six, and twenty-five less nineteen is six by the month count.

## Said To Everybody Together

He said it at about a quarter to six on the twenty-fifth of this month in that room with the door open, to the two carriers who were there with the strap over one shoulder, and to Wenna Raut, and to about four people in the passage, which is the way anything gets decided in that room, and the practice of the eighteenth of August was said to everybody together because a practice said to one person at a time is a rumour by the end of a fortnight.

"From this evening that book goes down four floors and about four hundred yards at half past five every day, and it goes down in the dark, and it goes down by my own hands, five days in seven, for as long as I am the man who stands at that table."

Four seconds passed and a quarter of a second of them was the stair.

"I am not asking. I have asked three people between the seventh and the thirteenth of this month and two of them said no with the reason given before the sentence was finished, and the third said yes and was right, and I am not using the third because a man who is paid nothing ought not to be the last hands nine years of other people's words goes through every evening of the year."

"What it closes," said Wenna Raut, from the table, "in your own voice. Say the closing in your own voice."

"A book in a room overnight is a book anybody may ask about at two in the morning. From this evening there is no book in that room overnight. So the office does not answer anybody at any hour. It answers about nine times a morning, on working mornings, between a quarter to ten and half past five, and at no other hour, and every question that needs a page waits for the morning."

"And the people whose rows those questions are cannot come in the morning."

"And that is the price of it, and the price is not mine, and I am not going to stand here and tell a clerk it is fair."

## What It Costs

"The cost is your own hands," said Wenna Raut. "Say it out loud and then it is a practice and not a preference."

"My own hands," said Lin Hao. "Five days in seven. Four flights and about four hundred yards. In the dark. About two hundred mornings a year, and I am rank one with a chest that was spent in the fourth year, and four nights a week I am three floors of washrooms in a branch office until half past five, and by the turn of the year I will have gone down that stair five days in seven since the seventh of April without a single morning off it."

"That is the whole of what you have left to spend."

"That is the whole of what I have left to spend."

"A drawer in this room has been shut for forty-eight months and four days and it cannot be opened by anybody including me. There has been nothing in the inside left of this coat since the turn of last year, and there is going to be nothing in it for as long as I am standing at that table."

"I am rank one and I will be rank one at the turn of the year. A band is not coming and a permission is not coming and a standing is not coming, and that is not modesty, it is the arrangement, and four years of this office have made a man out of me who does not expect to be handed anything."

"And there is a woman who cannot leave that room, and there is a man who is paid nothing and read me a stair out loud in a passage, and both of them said no to me inside a fortnight. That is the estate."

He was not thanked, and had not expected to be, and that is about the correct amount of noise a room makes when it gets smaller.

## The Morning And The First Evening

The woman with the back made him say the whole arrangement out loud to the room before he was allowed to pick the strap up, and the half of it he had not said was the half that saved him.

"They still come up at a quarter to ten," she said. "Both of them, five days a week, with the strap between them, because a book goes up two and comes down one, and if you send it up one, a person cannot stand in that corridor and let it come past them." She waited until he had nodded.

"You are not taking the morning away from two women who have both got to be somewhere else at ten. You are taking the evening. And in about nine months somebody is going to ask me whether the morning is safe, and on the day they ask it I am going to say it is the safest hour in the day because it is the hour there are two of us on that stair and one of us in a room with the door open."

He said it to the room, and the room did not argue, and that was the part he had expected and had not got ready for.

The woman with the back put the strap over her shoulder and said, "Then I will teach you how to put it down so that it does not go into your shoulders," and made him do it three times in the middle of that room with a lamp on the table and no book in it, holding the strap and the weight of nothing, and then again with the book, and the third time she did not let him do it at all.

"The hip," she said. "At the turn you put it on your hip and you go round the newel with the shoulder and not the hands, because the hands are what fail at four in the afternoon in the dark in about nine months, and I have watched two people lose the use of a hand for a book and I am not going to watch a third."

He got it up at half past five that evening with the strap round it and the buckle on the wrong side, four flights and about four hundred yards in fourteen minutes, and his left arm was gone by the second floor and he could not get his fingers open at the bottom, and the hip thing worked at the turn and he understood why she had taught it to him.

The man at the rail was still at his rail at ten past six with about four people round him and he came out of the passage and watched the last thirty yards of it.

"Give me that," he said.

"No."

"You cannot carry it up again."

"I did not say anything about carrying it up again."

The man took the other end without being asked a second time and said, "Then I will tell you what I have told nine people this month, which is that you have a book on that stair at four in the afternoon and you have not got anything else anybody can pick up, and I have been standing in that passage since the first year and I would like to be able to say it is true."

He said it without any weight in it at all, and it was the second time in nine days that a man had told him a true thing about himself without making it a speech, and both times it had cost nothing and both times had been worth about nine years.

## In The Yard At Ten Past Six

He stood in that yard with his arms hanging and the strap over one shoulder and could not lift either of them to the height of his own chest.

He worked out that it would come back in about ten minutes and that in about nine months it would not come back in ten minutes at all, and that the whole of what was left to him in the world of this office was the use of two hands, five days a week, in the dark, until the turn of the year and after the turn of the year.

The woman with the back came past about four minutes later going home with her bag over her shoulder, and she stopped when she saw him standing there.

"Two hundred times a year," she said.

"Two hundred times a year."

"Then you will be at the bottom of that stair on the day the hands stop and you will not have laid it down, because you will have been telling yourself every evening for about nine months that you are the man who carries it, and that is how they get it off you." She shifted her bag.

"Lay it down before that. Put it on the rail on the landing, on the step, anywhere on the way. A book that is put down once is a book a person knows how to carry. A book that has never been put down is a thing you were never allowed to put down, and those get taken out of a man's hands by a warden in about four years and by the weather in about nine."

Then she went out through the arch, and he stood in the yard and got his arms back up about a quarter to seven, and went home up the hill with fourteen minutes in his legs and a piece of paper in his breast pocket that had three sentences on it and no date and no hour and nothing whatever to do with a page.

At his own door he could not undo his own coat. That is the whole of what the first evening cost and it took him about four minutes to do it with the wrong hand and it will take him about four seconds in nine months, and the difference between those two figures is the entire price and he was told the price out loud in a room with the door open and he went on saying the sentence at twelve to about four people in a passage anyway.

He stood in his own hall in the dark with his coat open and his hands hanging at his sides and did the arithmetic one more time without wanting to. A pair of hands. Four flights and about four hundred yards. In the dark. From the twenty-fifth of this month to the last working day of the year and after that to a day he cannot see from here.

The first one is behind him and it is the twenty-fifth of October, and he did not look back at the four flights of windows because he has not done it once in nine years and it was not going to start tonight.

Three thousand eight hundred and sixty-four days. Less three thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight, the nineteenth of this one, is six, and from this evening that book goes down four floors and about four hundred yards at half past five every day in the dark by one pair of hands, five days in seven, and what it closes is answering anybody at any hour, and what it costs is his own hands.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is twenty-five, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-five of them, and it does not move again until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and forty-four days, which is forty-eight months and four days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Fifty Months And A Card

Three thousand nine hundred and sixteen was the seventeenth of this one and the twenty-fourth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three, and three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three less three thousand nine hundred and sixteen is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## The Twenty-First

The drawer in that room passed fifty months exactly on the twenty-first of this one, shut by him in a clerk's hand with his name on it, and it can never be opened again by anybody, including him, and no hand goes near it.

He named it once at that table in the morning, with the door open, because a thing that has been shut that long ought to be named when it turns, and then he did not make an event of it, because it is not an event.

Wenna Raut nodded and wrote nothing down, because nothing about that drawer goes into that book, and the carriers went past on the stair with the strap and did not stop, and the morning's askings went on the way mornings do when a thing has been named and left alone.

Fifty months. Four years and two months of a shut drawer in a room anybody may walk into. He felt the length of it in his hands, which have carried that book down four flights in the dark for two months, and thought about how much longer fifty months is than two, and how both feel the same in the shoulders.

## He Writes The Card

He wrote the card at about one, with the room empty except Wenna Raut, who witnessed only the date and the hour and nothing else, because the card is not a row and does not go into that book.

Three lines. The street he learned in about eleven minutes and proved on a hill. The door at the head of four flights, off a third court. The hours between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

Nothing else on it. Not one name. The address, the door, and the hours, and nothing else, because the office's title is the one another man carries and this room will not put a name where a place ought to be.

His hand shook on the second line, where the door is, and he steadied it with the other hand round the wrist, and Wenna Raut watched without helping, because helping would have made it hers and it has to be his.

When it was done he laid the pencil down and looked at the three lines for a long time, and felt the whole of nine years narrow to the width of a piece of stiff grey card, and understood that he had just closed the only protection he ever had that was not a person.

## Told In About Four Sentences

He took it down to the man who is paid nothing at his rail in the passage off Cordwain, late in the afternoon, with about four people round the counter, and waited until the rail had emptied down to two before he laid it face down on the wood.

"I am going to put this on that door," said Lin Hao. "On the last working day of the year. And I have come to tell you first, because you read the foot of that page four times a year to whoever stands there, and after this you will be reading it to people who found the door by card instead of by asking you."

The man looked at the face-down card and did not turn it over, because he has not been asked to and he does not take what he is not given.

"Say what it will do," said the man. "You have told a room. Now tell a rail."

Lin Hao told him in about four sentences, the way he promised himself he would, without dressing it up. That about forty people will not have to be told where it came from. That the bottom two inches under a striking in about four hundred kitchens will go quiet a second time. That the second time they will not come back. That the second quiet comes sooner by years than the man with the slate says it will.

The man listened all the way through without moving, and then he turned the card over with one finger and read the three lines, and turned it face down again.

## What Four Sentences Do In A Lane

About four people had gathered at the ends of the counter while he spoke, the way people gather in that passage when a true thing is being said, and none of them interrupted, because the man at the rail has a way of holding a crowd without lifting his voice.

"You have told a rail," said the man at last. "Now hear what a rail tells you. About four strangers a fortnight ask me where the stair is. All strangers. None twice. Since the autumn they ask it differently, because a woman said the phrase out loud to the passage itself and walked out, and I answered the other three, and the saying of it is out in the city and cannot be got back."

He squared the face-down card on the wood and left his finger on it, lightly, the way he leaves the quarterly sheet face up four times a year.

"When your card goes up, they will stop asking me and start walking. Some will find the door. Some will knock at midnight when the hours say morning. Some will copy the hours wrong and blame the copying on me. That is what four sentences do in a lane when they are true. They do not end the asking. They move it up a hill."

Lin Hao stood at that rail with his hands empty and felt the truth of it go through him, because he had told the cost in four sentences and the cost had been heard, and hearing does not make a cost smaller.

"You were not thanked," said the man at last, "and you will not be thanked for this either, and that is not a complaint. A man who stands at a rail for nine years and is paid nothing knows what thanking is worth."

"Go and put it on the door. And when they come to my rail with the card in their hands instead of a question in their mouths, I will read them the foot of that page and tell them the hours, and that will be the whole of what I am for."

He went back up that hill with the card in his breast pocket and the last week before the quarter-end behind him, a week in which he had told a man at a rail four true sentences about what it would do to a lane, and at the top he stood in the dark of his own hall with his hands hanging and did the arithmetic of hands and flights and yards one more time without wanting to.

## The Card In His Rooms

He laid the card face down on his own table at home, with the three lines hidden and the grey back showing, and looked at the back of it for a long time, because the back is what a door will never show and what he will always know is there.

Three lines. The street. The door. The hours. Nothing else. Not one name. In his own hand, with the pencil's track clean on the second line where his wrist shook and steadied, and Wenna Raut's witnessing of date and hour and nothing else standing behind it like a wall.

He thought about fifty months of a shut drawer, named once that morning and not made an event of, and about nine years of an office nobody could name, and about how both end on the same card: one by staying shut, the other by going up on a door.

He turned the card face up and read the three lines aloud in his own rooms, quietly, to hear how they sounded with the rooms empty round him, and they sounded like any other lines, which is exactly what they should sound like, and he put the card back in his breast pocket and went to bed with his hands aching from the strap.

## The Morning After Writing

The next morning he came up four flights early, before the carriers, and stood outside that door with the card in his breast pocket, unwritten no longer but not yet nailed, and looked at the wood where it will go.

Wenna Raut found him there when she came up at half past nine, and did not ask what he was doing, because she knows what a man does with a thing he has written and not yet placed.

"It is written," said Lin Hao.

"I know," said Wenna Raut. "And it goes up on the last working day of the year, and not before, and until then it lives in your breast pocket and nowhere else, the way hours live on the back of your hand."

"And the drawer."

"Passed fifty months exactly on the twenty-first, named once and not an event. Shut by you in a clerk's hand with your name on it. Never to be opened by anybody. The card does not open it. Nothing opens it."

He nodded, and went in to that table with her, and the morning's askings began, about nine people before midday, with the strip moving down each page and his hands behind his back, and the card waited through every one of them without burning a hole in his pocket, which surprised him, because he had expected it to.

He found himself listening for the card between readings, the way a man listens for a tooth that has stopped aching, and the quiet of it told him more than any finding had in weeks: three lines written and witnessed and not yet nailed can wait, and waiting is part of the writing, and the door will still be there on the last working day of the year.

Three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three days. Less three thousand nine hundred and sixteen, the seventeenth of this one, is seven, and a drawer passed fifty months exactly on the twenty-first, named once and not an event, and he wrote the card with the address and the door and the hours and nothing else on it.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is eighty-four, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and three days, which is fifty months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

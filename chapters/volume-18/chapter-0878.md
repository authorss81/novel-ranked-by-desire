# The Sister Comes Up Four Flights

Three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six was the twenty-seventh of the month before this one and the fourth of this one is day three thousand seven hundred and eighty-three, and three thousand seven hundred and eighty-three less three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six is seven, and thirty less twenty-seven plus four is seven by the month count.

## Four Flights Alone

She came up at about ten on the fourth, alone, in the cold of the morning, having asked nobody for anything, and she climbed all four flights without calling for help, though she had not left her house in months.

She had the look of people who have been ill a long time and are ill still, thin in the wrists and careful with breath, and she held the rail the whole way and rested her forehead once against the wall on the third landing, and then went on.

Lin Hao knew her before she gave her name, because December had put her into a row with nothing under it out of somebody else's mouth, and July had read the clause under that row and found it wrong, and now August had brought her up four flights on her own.

"I am the sister," she said at the door, simply. "Who cannot leave a house. I have left it."

## The Third Landing

She told them afterwards, resting on the bench, how the climb had gone, and the telling took longer than the climb, because she had counted every turn on the way up the way people count things that frighten them.

"The first flight is the worst," she said. "Because the stair knows you are coming and you know it knows. The second is better because your legs forget to be afraid. On the third I put my forehead against the wall and thought about going back, and then I thought about four years of being a question in somebody else's mouth, and I went on."

Wenna Raut poured more water and she drank, and her hands shook against the cup, and Lin Hao stood at the end of that table and felt something break open in his chest that had been shut since December, because the clause he wrote in a hurry had assumed this climb and she had just priced it step by step.

## One Row Out Of Another Mouth

Wenna Raut found the row inside four minutes, because there is only one of its kind in that book, a question with nothing under it and a date and an hour on it, entered in April out of somebody else's mouth about a sister who cannot leave a house.

She read it out in the voice that office reads everything in, with the sister standing in the room and the person who asked in April not in it, and the reading was short, because there was nothing under the question, and the shortness of it was the whole trouble.

The sister listened on her feet, because she had not taken the bench, and her hands hung at her sides, and when the reading ended she nodded once, as if the words were the ones she had expected and expecting them had not made them easier.

"That is what you hold about me," she said. "One row entered in April out of somebody else's mouth. And I must hear it, and I have heard it, and now I am here, and the coming was supposed to be the thing that answered it."

Lin Hao felt his throat close, because she had done the thing the clause assumed everybody could do, and she had done it ill and in the cold and alone, and the doing of it proved the clause wrong rather than right.

## What Coming Cannot Answer

He explained it to her as gently as he could, and every word of it hurt him going out, because she had paid the price of the sentence in breath and in pain and the sentence could not pay her back.

"The row has nothing under it," said Lin Hao. "It was held until you come or until it is about four years old. You have come. And there is still nothing under it, because nothing was ever given to put there. Coming does not make an answer where no answer was entered."

She looked at him, and her eyes were clear and dry, and he understood that she had known this on the second flight and had come on anyway, because not coming would have been worse than coming for nothing.

"I told no person I was coming. I left the house before light and I walked here slowly and I climbed your stair. And what the office holds about me is one row out of somebody else's mouth. And she must hear it. And I have heard it."

Wenna Raut stood at the table with tears on her face and did not wipe them, because a clerk who weeps in a room is a person and a clerk who leaves the room is absent, and she would not be absent for this.

## The Walk Back Alone

The sister stayed a while on the bench, getting her breath back, and Lin Hao brought her water and she drank a little, and about four people went past in the passage, and every one of them went past without looking in, as if the room knew to leave her alone.

She told them, resting, about the walk in, street by street, and where she had rested, and which wall had a ledge at the right height, and Wenna Raut listened as if the route were a row being entered, with the same care, and Lin Hao understood that the listening was the only escort the room could give.

"I will go back now," she said at last, standing. "Before the light goes. I can do it. I did it coming."

He wanted to send somebody with her, and no escort could be spared for the road home, and there may not be a morning in which anybody does what she did coming, because she came without asking anything of anybody and she will go the same way.

She went down the four flights alone, one hand on the rail and one on the wall, and he stood at the head of that stair and listened to every step, and counted them without meaning to, and each one landed in him like a reproach to December.

At the bottom she looked up once, a small pale face in the stairwell, and raised one hand, and then she was gone into the cold of the morning, and he stood there long after the door below had closed, with anger and grief mixed in him so he could not tell them apart.

He went back into the room and stood at the window looking down at the street, and Wenna Raut came to stand beside him, and together they watched the small figure make its way along the cold street, stopping twice to rest, and each stop landed in him like another reproach, and he did not look away until she turned the corner.

"She did it," said Wenna Raut quietly. "On her own. In the cold. Having asked no person for anything. Whatever the clause says and whatever August decides, that climb stands."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And the row still has nothing under it. And coming did not make an answer. And that is the thing I have to say out loud next month, to everybody together, with her in the room if she can come."

They stood a while longer at the window, and the street emptied, and the cold came off the glass, and the room behind them held one row with nothing under it and a clause under that which is wrong, and both would wait for the eighteenth, and waiting is the only thing the office can do rightly now.

He thought about the fourth of August for the rest of the day, between askings, and about a forehead against a wall on a third landing, and about how the climb had priced his December sentence step by step, and the pricing stayed with him through the afternoon like a figure on the back of a hand.

At half past five he stood aside for the carriers as always, and went down himself on foot, counting the four flights the way she must have counted them coming up, and each landing turned him a little, and by the bottom he understood the climb with his legs and not only his head.

In the yard he met one of the carriers coming back with the empty strap over her shoulder, and she nodded to him, and he nodded back, and neither spoke, because the day had said enough.

He walked home through the cold with the count of the four flights in his legs, and thought about how the office holds one row about a sister and nothing under it, and about how tomorrow the row will still have nothing under it, and about how the nothing is now the heaviest thing in the room.

At his own door he stood a while looking back at the hill, and thought about foreheads against walls and sticks on stairs, and about how the heaviest things in this city are not books or slates but sentences, and about how one sentence written in December has now been priced twice.

Once by a climb and once by legs that counted it going down, and the eighteenth will price it a third time, for everybody together, and he dreads it and will not turn from it, and the dread itself is part of the price, and he will pay it gladly if the paying ends the clause.

The street was empty and the cold deepened, and he turned his collar up.

He walked the rest of the way home slowly, counting nothing, and the cold steadied him, and the hill was quiet behind him.

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and sixty-three days, which is forty-five months and thirteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Row With A Space In It

Three thousand seven hundred and one was the twelfth of this month and the nineteenth is day three thousand seven hundred and eight, and three thousand seven hundred and eight less three thousand seven hundred and one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## The Man Who Watched The Stair

He came up at about two in the afternoon and he had nothing in his hands at all, which about two people in a month do, and he had a description.

“I am not here about a paper,” he said. “I want to know whether that office keeps anything about a man who comes up these stairs.”

“What kind of man.”

“The kind you would know at a quarter to ten any morning this month,” said the man. “Ordinary coat, a plate on him and nothing cut into it anywhere, nothing in his hands going in and nothing in his hands coming out, and he does not look at anybody going past and he does not speak on the stair.”

He was not enjoying it. He had a piece of paper folded into eight in one coat pocket, which showed at the knee every time he moved, and he took it out and put it on the table and left it there, and it was a laundry docket and it had nothing to do with anything.

“I am asking about a man because a man has been in my head for a fortnight and I want him out of it,” he said. “I am not asking you to do anything about him. I would not know what to tell you to do about him.”

“I have a laundry in this street and he comes past it at about a quarter to ten four mornings in a week and he has never once looked in a window. That is all I have and I have carried it about long enough.”

“Do you want it entered.”

“I want it stopped,” said the man, “and I have not got the smallest idea which of the two of those that is, and neither have you.”

## The Question He Was Not Sure Was Sensible

“I am not sure this is a sensible thing to ask,” he said. “I have worked it out from about four mornings. I have never seen him go in with a bundle and I have never seen him come out with one. I have watched four flights about nine years and he is the only one on them who goes up in the morning and comes down again.”

“You are sure he works nights.”

“He comes down at about half past five and there is a lamp on that landing at about eleven when I go up in the evening,” said the man. “That is all of it, and I have carried it a fortnight, and I would like it stopped.”

Wenna Raut had her hand off the page and was looking at him.

## One Row

She found it in about four minutes and there was one of them and there was always going to be one of them, because that Register carries no name to search on, and a row that is not about a paper cannot be reached by a person who has brought a paper.

The twenty-third of August of the ninth year. A date. An hour against the date. Then a space where the words go. Then the line under it, which is the line that says the office will answer in writing and will not improve what was asked.

There was nothing in the space at all.

It had never had anything in it, and no hand had gone near it since the day it went in, and it was the only row in nine years that anybody had ever asked about by a description of a man rather than by a piece of paper.

He was standing at the end of that table.

He had been in that room at about nine every morning for nine years and he had never seen that row from the other side of the wood, and he had known it was there since the day it went in, and about four days a month he could not say what he had said the day it went in and this was not one of those days and he had not known that when he walked into the room at about two in the afternoon.

## Nobody In That Room Knows

“What is the space for,” said the man.

The room gave him nothing for about nine seconds.

“I do not know,” said Wenna Raut.

“You keep the book.”

“I keep the book and I have not been given anything to put in it and I am not allowed to take anything out of it either, and I cannot tell you what a space in the middle of a row is for because I have never been told and I have never worked it out.”

“Then it could be anything.”

“It could be anything at all,” said Wenna Raut, “and it is the only space in the middle of a row in that whole book, and I have never counted them and I am not going to, and I have never in nine years told anybody what that space is for because in nine years a person has never once stood at that table and asked me.”

She did not look round while she said it, and he understood exactly what she was doing, because he had done it himself about four times this year at the end of that table with about four people in the corridor, and there is a way of not looking at a person that everybody in the room can see and about four people going past that door cannot.

She did not look at him because she was not going to help him, and not looking was the whole of the help she had to give.

## The Part He Could Have Done And Did Not

He could have filled it.

That is the whole of what happened in that room on the nineteenth of this month between about two and about four, and it took about four seconds to decide and he decided it in about four seconds, and it was a thing no rule in that building stopped him from doing, because a clerk will enter anything she is given and he had been giving her things every working day for nine years and she had entered every one of them.

He did not give her anything.

He said it out loud where about four people going past that doorway would get all of it, that the space was not being filled and that it was going to stay unfilled, and Wenna Raut wrote the date and the hour on the outside of a fold and did not write one word of it into that book.

“Take the fold,” she said, and pushed it across the wood to him rather than to the man, which is what she does when a thing in that room is addressed to one of them and not to the other.

He put it in his coat and it went in on the right this time, because the left has been empty since the third of December and there was nothing in it and there was not going to be anything in it in this quarter.

“You could have told me what was in it and had it written down.”

“Yes,” said Lin Hao.

“Why not.”

“Because the moment I tell her it goes in, and the moment it goes in that space is a sentence and that sentence is a thing a man on a stair can read one morning when the Register is lying open on a table with the door of that room open,” he said. “And you have just told me you have been watching that stair for nine years.”

The man looked at him for about four seconds.

## On The Second Landing

He went down the four flights after him about ten minutes later because he had nothing else to do, and the man was on the second landing waiting for a woman who was not coming yet, and neither of them said anything for about nine seconds.

“That is a man anybody could find,” said the man at last. “A quarter to ten, and nothing in his hands, and a stair that turns. I have had it a fortnight and I have never found a use for it and I do not want one, and I am telling you that I have it, because there is nowhere else in this city I would say this to.”

“I know you have it.”

“You have not asked me what I am going to do with it.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “That is not a question I am entitled to ask and it is not a question anybody in that room is entitled to ask, and if you came up those four flights tomorrow with a different piece of paper you would find this room has never once asked a man where he got a thing.”

The woman came out of a door at the top of that landing and said his name and he went, and Lin Hao stood on the landing about four minutes, in the place where a man had been on it at about two o’clock every working afternoon for about three years, and did not take a seat on it.

A stair turns twice between the second and third floors and a man who goes up it carrying a description of you is not carrying anything that this office entered. He made it up on a laundry counter out of four mornings and a turned stair, and there are about nine of those a day in this city for every one of these, and no instrument in this city could tell a stranger which of the two is which.

He went up the rest of that stair and was in that room with the door open for the last hour of that afternoon and said nothing at all to anybody about any part of it.

*Three thousand seven hundred and eight days.

Less three thousand seven hundred and one, the twelfth of this month, is seven, and a man came up four flights with no paper and a description of a person he had watched for about nine years and asked whether this office kept anything about him, and there is one row, dated the twenty-third of August of the ninth year, with a space in the middle of it, and nobody in that room knows what the space is for, and it was not filled.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-nine, and the forty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and eighty-eight days, which is forty-two months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Woman Who Cannot Hear

Three thousand seven hundred and twenty-nine was the tenth of this month and the seventeenth is day three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six, and three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six less three thousand seven hundred and twenty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## A Chair Turned Toward The Light

She came up at about eleven on the seventeenth with a daughter holding her elbow and a folded paper in the daughter's fist, and the daughter did the talking on all four flights because the mother watches mouths and not doors.

The mother keeps her head turned a little to one side in any room, the way people do when one ear gives them nothing and the other gives them less than they admit to their children. She had a shawl round her shoulders though the corridor was warm, and she kept one hand on the bench the whole time she was lowering herself onto it, as if the wood could tell her what the room was saying.

"She wants to know what you wrote about her husband's brother," said the daughter. "He came up in April and he brought nothing with him and you wrote his words down. She was not here that day and she wants it now."

Wenna Raut looked at Lin Hao and then at the mother, and she lifted the pen out of the tray and set it down again, because the finding takes about nine minutes and the reading takes less than one, and both have to be done before anything is said.

"It will take a little while," said Lin Hao to the daughter. "Tell her we are finding it."

The daughter shaped the words with her mouth exaggerated and slow, and the mother nodded and folded her hands, and the nod was not agreement so much as a way of staying in a room where everybody else knows what is happening.

## The Weight Of That Stair

The corridor had the smell of wet stone in it though it had not rained, and the light through the high window lay in a long bar across the wood, and the mother stopped in it to catch her breath on the third landing, with her daughter's arm firm under hers.

Lin Hao had come down to meet them at the second turn because he had heard the step-slow going on the stair, and he walked up beside them without touching either, saying nothing, because anything said on a stair has to be said twice to a woman who watches mouths.

"How many more," said the daughter.

"Two turns," said Lin Hao, holding up two fingers where the mother could see them, and the mother nodded and went on, and the daughter mouthed *thank you* at him over her mother's head, and he nodded back and kept his pace to theirs.

In the room Wenna Raut had the bench pulled a little forward so the mother could get to it without turning sideways, and a cup of water on the table that the mother did not touch, and that Register lying shut and square with the wood, waiting for the finding that would take the best part of the morning.

## Hands On Wood

Wenna Raut went back through that Register with one finger at the head of each line, and the mother watched the finger and not the page, because the page at two feet is a blur of lines and the finger is a thing moving.

About four people went up and down that passage while it was being done, and one of them stopped to look in and went on, and the daughter stood at the bench with her back half turned so her mother could see her face.

He stood at the end of that table and thought about what the row would be when they found it, and about how the office reads everything in one voice whether the person in front of it can hear that voice or not, and about how April had put a hedge and a boundary and a man's coat into that book and every one of those would fail a woman who listens with her eyes.

The row was there. Wenna Raut brought that book round so the row lay square with the wood, and she nodded at him, and the nod meant it was the right one and that the words under it were in the words the man had used.

## What Reading Sounds Like To Her

Wenna Raut read it out in the voice that office reads everything in, steady and without hurry, a date and an hour and then the words a man had used about a hedge in the eleventh month and an answer under them in her own hand.

The mother watched Wenna Raut's mouth for the first two words and then looked down at her own hands, and by the middle of the reading she was looking at the window, and when the reading ended she looked up at her daughter with the expression of somebody who has been polite through a thing that was not for her.

"She did not get it," said the daughter quietly. "Say it again, slower, and let her watch you."

Wenna Raut read it again, slower, shaping each word, and the mother leaned forward and frowned with effort, and at the end she shook her head once, a small movement, and touched her own ear and then let her hand fall.

"She hears the sound," said the daughter. "She does not hear the words. It is worse with a woman's voice in a room with stone in it. With my father she could manage because she knew what he was going to say."

Lin Hao felt the want rise in him to do something useful, and felt it go nowhere, because there was no slower that would make a row into a thing heard, and no louder that would not shame the woman on the bench.

## Entered In The Words Used

He asked Wenna Raut whether the morning itself went in, and she said it did, because a clerk enters what she is given, and what she had been given was a question in a daughter's mouth and an answer read twice to a mother who could not hear it.

She wrote it down at the table while the two women waited, in the words the daughter had used, and the date and the hour against it, and the hand she wrote it in was the same hand she writes everything in, and the mother watched the pen moving and seemed eased by that, as if a thing written were a thing kept even when it could not be heard.

"Tell her it is entered," said Lin Hao.

The daughter told her, mouthing it large, and the mother nodded and put her hand for a moment on the wood beside that Register, not on the page and not near any row, only on the edge of the table where anybody rests a hand.

"She asks whether her husband's brother will be told," said the daughter. "She does not want him told she came. She does not want trouble between the houses."

"Nothing goes out of that room except in the voice we read in," said Lin Hao. "If he comes up four flights we will read to him what is his. We will not read to him what is hers."

The daughter rendered this into mouth shapes and a few low words close to her mother's good side, and the mother nodded again, and this time the nod reached her shoulders and loosened them.

## What The Daughter Wanted Said

The daughter stayed by the bench after her mother had taken the bench, and her anger came out the way anger comes out in people who have carried somebody up four flights.

"She kept that house for forty years and she knows every boundary on that lane better than the men who argue about them," said the daughter. "She heard your voice through that door in April when her husband's brother came down and told the street he had been heard. She has been asking me since April to bring her. I told her to wait until the weather turned. I should have brought her in April."

"She is here now," said Lin Hao. "And the row is entered. And the reading was done twice."

"It was done twice and she heard neither," said the daughter. "So what does she have that she did not have at the foot of that stair?"

He had no answer that would sound like anything but a rule, and he did not want to give her a rule, because a rule would be true and would not help, and he had given enough of those in nine years to know the shape they leave in a room.

"She has that it was read," said Wenna Raut from the table, not looking up from the pen. "And that it was written. And that her coming was not wasted because she could not hear. The writing does not need hearing."

The daughter stared at her and then looked away, and the hardness went out of her shoulders by a fraction, the way it does when somebody is answered by a person who is working and not by a person who is explaining.

"Tell her that," said Lin Hao.

The daughter mouthed it to her mother, large and slow, and added a few low words at the good side, and the mother reached out and patted her daughter's wrist twice, and kept her hand there.

## Nothing Gained And Nothing Pretended

They went down the four flights with the daughter going first and the mother holding the rail with both hands, one step at a time, and Lin Hao stood at the head of that stair and listened to them going, and Wenna Raut stood at the table with her hands together in front of her.

"She came for the hearing and not the truth," said Wenna Raut after a while. "And she did not get the hearing. And the other woman in April heard and read and took a date down into a yard, and this one heard nothing and takes nothing down, and neither morning is anybody's fault."

He thought about that on the way down that hill at about two, going to stand a while by the kerb where the board goes up, because a morning in which the office does everything it promises and a person leaves with nothing is a morning he had not priced when he promised it on the thirtieth of March.

He wanted, with a sudden sharpness that surprised him, to have been able to put the row into the mother's hands in a way she could hold, and the sharpness stayed with him all the way down the hill, because wanting it did not make it possible and knowing that did not make the wanting go away.

The daughter had left the folded paper on the bench by mistake, and he had it in his pocket, and it was a shopping list in pencil, and he would give it back if they came again, and if they did not come he would keep it in the drawer that is not the one in the wall, with nothing written on it by him.

He stood by that kerb until the light went off the wall, and the anger he felt was not at the daughter or at Wenna Raut or at the room, and it was not useful, and it stayed, and that was the whole of what the seventeenth left him.

*Three thousand seven hundred and thirty-six days. Less three thousand seven hundred and twenty-nine, the tenth of this month, is seven, and a mother who cannot hear a row read out was read to twice in the voice that office reads everything in, and the row was entered in the words her daughter used, and nothing was gained and nothing was pretended.*

*Less three thousand six hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seventy-seven, and the forty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them, and that rail does not move again until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and sixteen days, which is forty-three months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

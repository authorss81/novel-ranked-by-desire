# The Wrong Page

Three thousand six hundred and forty was the eleventh of this month and the seventeenth is day three thousand six hundred and forty-six, and three thousand six hundred and forty-six less three thousand six hundred and forty is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.

## What He Says And What She Says

He had been carrying it since the twenty-fourth of February and he said it on the seventeenth where about four people could hear every word of it, and he had not once in nine years said anything in that room with the door shut.

“I want to say what that book is for and I am going to get it wrong, and I would like somebody to say it after me so that this room has heard the right one.”

Wenna Raut shut the book.

“Say your wrong one.”

“A book in that room is a record of what we said about pieces of paper. It is not a record of a person. It is not evidence. It is not a thing anybody can be checked against, and on the twenty-first of this month you and I spent a morning finding that out at the cost of two pairs of arms.”

“That is right,” said Wenna Raut, “and it is also the reason for the thing, and if that is all you have got then the thing does not happen and I am not going to argue with you about it.”

## She Says It Better

“No it is not,” he said, “and I have not got to the thing. I have got to the reason it is a problem.”

“Then say the reason.”

“Because about four rows in every nine are about a person, and every one of those rows was written by a person who was frightened, and no row of them has ever been looked at again by anybody but you.”

Wenna Raut took the pen off the tray and held it and did not put it in the book.

“A page of that book may be brought to a person who has asked about it, and turned in front of them,” she said. “No row on it is read sideways. No row on it is read out that the person did not ask for. And the person is not left standing at a table while a clerk goes down four flights and about four hundred yards to fetch it.”

“That is it exactly.”

“That is not it exactly, and I know where I got it from and I am telling you so that you cannot take it back,” said Wenna Raut. “I got the shape of it off a man who has been standing at that end of this table for six days saying nothing at anybody, and if you had said the sentence first I would have written down something with less in it.”

“You did not get there first. That is all I am saying and I am not going to say it again.”

“It is a practice.”

“It is a practice and not ink, and nothing goes under the foot of any page this morning, and the sixth line in that office’s hand is still the last one and it is still the second of December of the year before last,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am not entering this as a row.”

“Why not.”

“Because you have not given it to me as something to be entered,” she said. “You have asked me to say a sentence out loud at the end of a table with the door open. That is what you have done and I have done it, and if I put it in that book then a clerk has been given it, and that is a different thing and it is the thing that took nine years and about two thousand people to stop.”

He said nothing about that.

He has been not saying things about that since the second year, and it is the oldest habit he has, and it is the one that has cost this office more than the burning and the drawer and the refusing to watch his own stair, all three put together, and it costs him nothing at all on any morning, and he has never once been able to make himself do it differently.

## The Two Things That Can Go Wrong

“There are two ways that goes wrong and I want them both out loud before I say the other part.”

“Say them.”

“The first is that she finds the wrong page,” said Wenna Raut. “About four hundred and seventy rows, no names, and a woman who has described a hedge and a fortnight in her own words. There will be more than one of those. Not two. More than one. And when she brings it to the table and it is the wrong one, she will know, and this office will have shown a stranger a page of her own life that belongs to a person she has never met.”

“She will not.”

“She will not if she says so,” said Wenna Raut, “and if she does not say so then she takes it away and it never comes back to this room, and about four times a year that is what will happen, and it will not be entered, because the second I put it in that book I have built the thing this office has spent nine years refusing to build.”

“And the second.”

“The second is the row with nothing under it, put in on the twenty-third of December of last year and to be held about four years, and it is not answered and never going to be,” she said.

“And one day a person is going to come up four flights and ask to have their own page turned in front of them, and it will be that one, and there is nothing on it. A date and an hour and a question and no answer under it, and turning that in front of a woman is turning a page at her that has nothing on the other side.”

He had not thought of that one and it took him about nine seconds to get to it.

“Then what do we do when it is that one.”

“We say so,” said Wenna Raut. “Out loud, at the table, with her standing there. And that is the whole of the arrangement and it is the same arrangement that has been standing since the second year and it has never once been written on anything.”

## The Second Step

“On the seventeenth of October,” he said, “you and I entered a decision in that book that a question about a question is not entered, not even its refusal, and I gave you the reason and you gave me a better one, and the reason was that the moment this office starts writing down what people are asking about, it has begun to sort people.”

“Yes.”

“That is the first step. Here is the second one and it arrived about nine minutes ago while you were saying the sentence.”

“Go on.”

“If a page may be turned for a person then somebody in this room has to find it,” said Lin Hao. “There are about four hundred and seventy rows in that book with a date and an hour on every one and not one name in it, and to find one page I have to decide which rows are about you, and that decision is made by one person, standing up, with a finger, in about nine minutes, and about four people hear how long it is taking.”

He put his hands where he could see them.

“And that is the same decision as the one I refused in October, made in the same room, by the same two of us, and the instrument I closed then was the instrument that would have told either of us which rows were about that person. I did not build it. I am about to do the job by hand.”

“How often.”

“About nine times a month, and about four of those nine are about a person,” he said, “and by the end of the quarter about nine people a month will be coming up four flights to ask about the turning as well as about the answer, and about nine a month is what we can carry and it is what we have always carried.”

“And which of the nine has had it before is not a thing one of them could ever find out,” said Wenna Raut, “because there is no record of the turning, and there is not going to be one, because the record of the turning would be a list of the people who have asked to see themselves.”

“No.”

“So the second time somebody asks will be the first time as far as this office is concerned,” she said, “and the ninth time will be the first time, and the hundredth time, and every one of them will get the same eleven minutes of a clerk standing at a table with a finger on a date, and every one of them will get to the end of their own without ever knowing that anybody came before them and got the same.”

“That is the arrangement.”

“That is the arrangement and it is nine years old and it has kept one kind of business off a page for nine years without a single person ever asking to see theirs.”

## Not Today

A man who had come up at about two in the afternoon had been standing at the end of that table for about nine minutes and had been waiting, and he put his hand up about halfway through the last of it, the way people do in a room where none of them has ever been spoken to.

“Is my page one of them.”

Wenna Raut looked at him.

“What is your name.”

“Thomas Isher. I brought a paper up about a lease in the eleventh month and I asked whether there was anything else about me in this office and you said there was not, and I have been standing here since two o’clock working out how to ask whether I could see the page you wrote it on.”

“You may see the page,” she said.

He did not move.

“Not today,” said Wenna Raut. “That book is four floors down and round two streets and there is nobody in this city who is allowed to fetch it, and I am not going to do it with a person standing in the room.”

“Why not today.”

And the room had nothing.

*Three thousand six hundred and forty-six days. Less three thousand six hundred and forty, the eleventh of this month, is six, and a clerk said a sentence at the end of that table that was better than the one the man beside her had gone in there to say, and neither of them entered it anywhere, and a man who had come up at about two in the afternoon asked whether his own page could be turned for him and was told not today.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is seventy-seven, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and twenty-six days, which is forty months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

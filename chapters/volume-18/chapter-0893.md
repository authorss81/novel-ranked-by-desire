# Nine Hundred Sheets On A Line

Three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight was the ninth of this one and the fifteenth of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four, and three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four less three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight is six, and fifteen less nine is six by the month count.

## A Plate Room

The plate room stands two floors below the research wing, long and narrow, with about nine hundred sheets of tracing paper hung on a drying line down the middle of it and one table at the far end under a hooded lamp.

Sun Meiling was at that table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a line of work across the top of her spine where years of bending over plates have left their mark, and no other mark on her at all, and she did not look up when he came in because she had already heard his step on the stone.

"You have come about the two rows," she said.

"I have come because you asked me to come," said Lin Hao.

"Seventy days since you last stood in a room with me and said a word about shapes." She squared a sheet with the edge of that table. "I have been thinking about shapes since that afternoon, and I have got two of them that will not leave me alone."

The line ran the length of that room, sheet after sheet, thin as breath, and the light came through them all and laid their shadows on the floor.

## The Second Trace

She took two traces down off the line, both of the same shape to the eye, both taken off rows in that book in the early part of the year, and laid them side by side on that table, one under each hand.

A junior with a brush at the far end made a small sound and put the brush down, because two traces of one shape are never laid on the same table in that room, and everybody in it knows why: the second is read as the correction of the first, and from the moment they lie together neither can be trusted alone.

"Leave them," said Sun Meiling, without looking round. "And leave us. What I am doing here is forbidden and I am doing it because there is no other way to show it."

The junior went out and shut the door, and the line of sheets moved a little in the draught and then hung still.

"Look," she said.

He looked. Two tracings, each of a question in the words used, each with a date and an hour at the head, each with an answer under it in the clerk's hand, and the shapes of them matched line for line, word for word, to the length of the strokes.

"Tell me which of the two rows in that book is about which person," said Sun Meiling.

He could not. He stood at that table with the hooded lamp making a circle of light round those two sheets and felt the whole of his office narrow to the width of that table, because she had laid out, in paper thin enough to see through, the thing he had found nine days apart in a bound book and had not been able to put a name to.

"I cannot tell," said Lin Hao.

"No," she said. "And neither can I, and I am the only person in the household who sees the same shape in different figures for a living, and I cannot see a single thing clearly here, and that is the finding."

## What She Does With Him

She stood very close to him at that table, with the light making the line of work across her shoulders stand out under the thin stuff of her blouse, and he could smell paste and lamp oil and the soap she washes plates with.

"You cannot pay for what I do," she said, quietly, "and you have a chest that was spent in the fourth year and no permission of any kind and you are rank one on a night shift you asked for, and I have known all of that since before I ever touched you, and I am telling you now because I want you to know I am not owed anything."

He said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not have been a price.

"Four seconds," she said, and laughed, a little, and put her hand to the front of his trousers through the cloth and held him there, firmly, through the stuff, the way a woman holds a thing she has already decided about. "That is how long it takes me. It took me four seconds the first time and it takes me four seconds now."

He was hard under her hand in about nine seconds, and she made a pleased sound and did not take her hand away, and the sheets on the line moved again in the draught from under the door.

She pushed the two traces apart to make room and got up onto the edge of that table among the weights and the brushes, and pulled him between her knees, and opened his belt with quick fingers, and took him in her cold hand and guided him to her, and he felt how wet she was through her skirts before he had even got inside.

"Come here," she said. "And do not talk to me about rows. Talk to me with your hands."

He got into her standing between her knees with her legs wrapped round his hips, and she was tight and hot and very wet inside, and she moved against him at once, hungrily, riding back onto him with her hands on his shoulders and her forehead against his, and her cunt gripped him and let go and gripped again with each movement.

He got his hand up under her blouse from behind and found the line of work across her spine with his fingers and followed it while she moved, and she shuddered and laughed into his mouth and pushed harder against him.

"There," she said. "That is mine. Nobody else in the household has that. You have it now."

She came first with her mouth open against his shoulder and her whole body gone tight round him, and she held him there afterwards, inside, with her legs locked, and would not let him go until the shaking had stopped, and then he spent in her, deep, with her name in her ear, and she took all of it, gladly, and stayed with her forehead against his, breathing hard.

Afterwards she straightened her skirts with quick hands and did not look away from him, and there was nothing reluctant in her at all, and the pleasure had been real and specific and hers as much as his.

"Nothing about that goes into any record in this building," she said. "And that is not a complaint. That is the reason I wanted it on a table with two forbidden sheets on it."

## Which Is Which

They stood a while with the two traces between them, side by side, forbidden, and the lamp making the thin paper glow.

"Nine hundred sheets on that line," said Sun Meiling, "and one table, and the rule of that room is that the second trace corrects the first, and I have broken it today because those two rows correct nothing. They repeat. And a repetition in that book is worse than an error, because an error can be named and a repetition can only be carried."

She lifted one trace and then the other, and let the light through them.

"Which of the two is about which person. I have spent seventy days on it since you last stood in front of me, and my answer is that the shape is the same and the people are two, and no instrument in that room can put one to the other without putting a day where a person ought to be."

He thought about the back of his own hand, where hours stand in pencil and nowhere else, and how little good they had done him with two women and one drain.

"You have given me the thing I came for," said Lin Hao, "and it is not the answer. It is that the answer cannot be had from the shape."

She rolled the two traces and put them back on the line, apart, where they belonged, and the room was a lawful room again, and the thing they had done on that table stayed on that table in a way he understood without being told.

At the door she stopped him with a hand on his arm.

"When you go back up those four flights," she said, "remember that I laid them together and could not tell, and that I will not do it again, and that the next time somebody asks you which row is theirs, you will know that the only honest thing is to say you do not know which shape belongs to whom."

## The Line At Night

He walked back through the lower corridors with the sound of the drying line still in his ears, sheet against sheet in the draught, and thought about the junior who had put the brush down and gone out, and what it costs a room when its own rule is broken for an honest reason.

The rule stands because the second trace corrects the first. Break it once and every trace on that line is a little less certain. Keep it unbroken and two rows that are the same shape can never be laid together and seen for what they are. Either way something is lost, and she had chosen which loss to bear, and he respected her for choosing it out loud.

He went out into the stone corridor with the smell of paste still on his hands and the weight of her still in his body, and the cold of that corridor sobered him the way cold water sobers a man, and he knew he would carry what she had shown him longer than he would carry anything else in that room.

Three thousand eight hundred and eighty-four days. Less three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight, the ninth of this one, is six, and about nine hundred sheets hung on a line with one table, and two traces of one shape laid side by side where no two may lie, and neither of them could tell which row was about which person.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is forty-five, and the forty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-five of them, and it does not move until the last working day of the year.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and sixty-four days, which is forty-eight months and twenty-four days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Two Days And Seven Days

Three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine was the thirtieth of the month before this one and the seventh of this one is day three thousand eight hundred and forty-six, and three thousand eight hundred and forty-six less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine is seven, and seven days out of a month that begins on the first is seven by the month count.

## At The Kerb

The rail changed on the last day of last month and everything that hangs off it changed with it, and the first week under a new sheet has a different sound on that hill, because about four hundred people a day walk past that kerb and most of them look at a board rather than at a rail.

He got down there at about a quarter to ten and the boy was already against the kerb with a board under the left arm and a paste brush clenched in the other fist, and he was doing the thing he does, which is to square the foot of it against the top of the kerb with the side of his boot without looking down while he does it.

The one hundred and eleventh form went against that board on the seventh of this month, and both of the two ruled squares at the foot of it were empty, as they have been every month for nine years, and in nine years nobody has asked him a thing about any of it, and that is the arrangement and it is not going to be broken by either of us.

## Two Days Out Of Seven

"He comes on the seventh and again on the ninth to take the old one down," said Wenna Raut, who had come down the hill with him for the first time in about four months. "So the form is up two days out of every seven and the rest of that week the board is bare with the paste showing, and that is the whole of its calendar and it is on a wall somewhere that is not this room."

"Then where is it."

"Somebody put it there about once a month for nine years and there is a knob of paste on the top batten about four days old that gets a new one on top of it every month, and I have known that since March and I have not asked and neither have you."

The brush went over the back of the paper in one stroke and came away with a soft noise, and the boy put the brush in his pocket, which is the end of that part of the job.

"Every quarter since the spring somebody in this room has had a theory about him," said Wenna Raut, "and every one of the theories ends in a question, and none of the questions has ever been asked, and he has never come to this door and asked us for anything either."

"Theory mine," said Lin Hao.

"Mine as well," said Wenna Raut, "and mine is the same one, and that is the part neither of us has said out loud: that we would rather he stayed on that kerb for nine years longer than be the ones who made him into a person with an age and a wage."

## The Mornings He Stands There

Lin Hao stayed at the kerb for about half an hour, which he has not done since the seventh of April, and watched a man about whom he knows five things.

He knows the brush. He knows the two ruled squares have never had anything in them, and that one of the two has a ruled line in it for a month and the other has one for a name, and both are ruled by whoever prints them and not by him.

He knows the paste on the face of the paper is a morning old and the knob on the top batten is a month old and is put there by somebody else. He knows that man has never carried a paper up those four flights in nine years, and that no quarter-end has ever brought him up with a form somebody wanted filling in.

And he knows that on the ninth of every month he comes back at about the same hour and takes the old one down, and that he comes on the seventh and the ninth and on no other day of the month that either of us has ever noticed, which in nine years has not been a surprise to anybody who thought about it.

"There is a thing I have been putting off for about two months," he said to himself more than to anybody, "and it is not about the boy."

"Tell me what it is about," said Wenna Raut.

"It is a corner of dressed stone at the foot of a hill about nine feet back from that kerb, four inches deep, fifty words in six hands, and the newest of those six is about somebody climbing a stair in bad weather and turning round at the top of it and going back down."

"It finishes about a hand short of the left-hand end, with the chisel clean behind it, and I have not been down there since the eighteenth of March."

## Four Inches Of Stone

They went the nine feet back to the wall together.

There was light on that wall for the first time since he had started going down that hill in the spring, because it was about half past ten in the morning and not half past four in the afternoon, and he had forgotten what four inches of dressed stone looks like when there is light on it at all.

About fifty words in about six hands, and the newest of the six is not about a building.

That was the thing that stopped him, and it had taken him about two months and one turn of the weather to find out. He had gone down to that corner expecting a sentence about a building, because that is what nearly everything in nine years has been about, and the newest hand in that stone is about a hand going up a stair and coming down again empty, and it is the newest because it was cut after the spring.

He stood in the morning light with his own hand an inch off it and read those words four times.

Out of the light, in the part of that wall that has been dressed and re-dressed over nine years, there are two words scratched small and hard, and they are not among those six hands, and they are not about a building either, and nothing whatever can be said about what they are by looking at about four inches of stone at the foot of that hill in October.

## The Thing He Has Been Putting Off

"You have not touched it," said Wenna Raut from behind him.

"I have not touched it."

"You put two fingers against it through a gap in March and you did not touch it then either." She had been looking at the two scratched words for about four seconds. "You will go down there again in the spring."

"I will not."

"You will go down there again in the spring, at about half past four in the afternoon, when the light has gone off that wall entirely, and you will put the back of a knuckle against the top batten and find the paste holds, and you will stand there in the dark and be able to read nothing whatsoever." She turned round.

"And you will come up that hill and tell me the paste is fine, and I will let you, and neither of us will say one word about six hands and about fifty words on a wall we both read every time."

Then she looked up the hill at four floors of windows with the light on them and said, "You have been putting it off for two months and you have been putting it off because you do not want to be the one who goes down there again, and that is not an office reason, and I am not going to allow it."

## Down The Hill Again

They went back up at about eleven and the ordinary afternoon of the building went on around them the way it does.

About four people came up those four flights. A boy of about nine wanted to know whether a thing his mother had signed in the third month was still hers, and the answer was read to him in the voice that office reads everything in, and he wrote his own name on a paper of his own and the clerk witnessed that he wrote it and not that it came from there.

The stair was loud from four and went quiet at six, and the two carriers came up it at half past five with the strap and took the book down into the yard with them, and the boy with the paste brush went past the arch about an hour after that with the old board under his arm and the empty kerb behind him.

"He comes back on the ninth," said Lin Hao, half to himself and half to Wenna Raut.

"He comes back on the ninth," said Wenna Raut, "and he comes back on the ninth whatever the weather is doing, and in nine years there has not been one ninth that he did not come back on, and that is the only fact about that man that anybody in this city has got, and it is not about him at all."

At about eleven he looked out of the window at that wall from the top of the hill, and the light had gone off it, and about fifty words in six hands were not readable at that distance at all, and he stood at that window for longer than the work required, and Wenna Raut let him, because she has known since March that he goes down that hill to look at a thing, and that the looking has cost him a good deal more than the thing does.

Three thousand eight hundred and forty-six days. Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of the month before this one, is seven, and the one hundred and eleventh form went against that kerb at about a quarter to ten with both ruled squares empty, and a man who has not been asked anything stood there about half an hour, and the newest hand in four inches of stone at the foot of that hill is not about a building.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seven, and the forty-third sheet went up on that rail at four on the thirtieth carrying no new line in the office hand, and it is the first count off a new rail in ninety days.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand four hundred and twenty-six days, which is forty-seven months and sixteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

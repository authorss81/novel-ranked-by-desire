# An Address On A Door

Three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three was the twenty-fourth of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, and three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## The Last Working Day

The last working day of the year came up grey and cold, with the yard stones white at the edges and the stairwell dark from the first floor up, and about nine people came up those four flights before midday, because a quarter-end brings people who want to have asked in the old year.

Wenna Raut found every row in about nine minutes each, with the strip moving down each page and her forearm across the lower half, and Lin Hao stood at the end of that table with his hands behind his back and read out every answer in the voice that office reads everything in, with the person it was about in the room, and the page never turned towards anybody.

At about one the room emptied, and the carriers waited on the stair below with the strap coiled between them, waiting for four, and Wenna Raut shut that book for the middle of the day and put the pen in the tray, and neither of them spoke about what was coming, because both knew.

In his breast pocket the card waited, three lines in his own hand, witnessed as to date and hour and nothing else. The street. The door at the head of four flights, off a third court. The hours between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

## At Four The Sheet Moves

At four the forty-third sheet came down off that rail at the counter off Cordwain, face up and going brown at the edges, and the forty-fourth went up in its place, and six lines in an office hand stood above a striking, five instructions and one sentence, the last of them the second of December of the ninth year, and no seventh goes in.

The man who is paid nothing read all six lines to whoever stood at the rail, the way he reads them four times a year, and on the last working day of the year there was a stranger at the end of that counter with a piece of stiff grey card in his hand and the back of it showing.

He had come for the stair at the back of the court. The card gave him the court and not the stair, and he wanted to know which of three doors in a court was the one, and that was a fair question and a card could not answer it.

"Then you want the door," said the man, "and not me."

"I want the door."

"Then go up. Head of the stairs, third door off the landing, and the hours are on it and they are honest ones."

He said it out loud in that passage, in the ordinary voice, to a stranger, and there were about nine people standing in that passage and every one of them heard a door given away.

Lin Hao stood at the back of it with the card still in his fist and understood that nine years of an office nobody could name had just been handed to a room in about four seconds by a man who is paid nothing and has never once been asked whether he is allowed to.

A card nailed to a door would not stop that, because a true thing said in a passage cannot be got back out of the passage. He had been told the price out loud at that rail on the seventeenth and had gone ahead and bought it anyway.

He stood there and felt the whole of nine years stop for the length of four seconds, and then start again, and it started again running.

## Told At His Own Lane

He went to the lane off Cordwain before the light went, with nine stools and nine districts and a slate four feet by three and twelve books, and found Emil Brack at the slate with chalk in his fingers, and stood there until Brack had finished a figure and put the chalk down.

"I have come to tell you what it does," said Lin Hao.

Brack listened without wiping the slate, and Lin Hao told him plainly, without dressing it up, what putting an address on a door does to a lane and to four hundred kitchens. That about forty people will not have to be told where it came from. That the bottom two inches under a striking in about four hundred kitchens will go quiet a second time. That the second time they will not come back.

"And the clock," said Brack.

"The second quiet comes sooner than you say it will. About four years sooner. You have said four years in that lane for nine years and been right about every fact, and on this you are out by four, and I am telling you so at your own lane because you ought to hear it where you keep your books."

Brack looked at the slate and then at him, and said one sentence, and did not say another.

"Then I will rub it out and write it sooner."

He picked up the chalk and did it, in his own small hand where a person can check it, in a public lane, and would not rub it off, and the figure stood there while the light went grey off the slate, and Lin Hao understood that he had just been forgiven by a man who does not forgive, in the only way such a man can.

## The Door

He went back up those four flights for the last time that year with the card in his fist and the two carriers behind him and Wenna Raut at that table with the door open, and stood in front of that door that has never had a name over it, and has no name on the yard gate, and has only a card about washing on it.

"This is mine and it is in ink," said Lin Hao, "and it is the first time in nine years that anything I have done in this city has had a place on it that a stranger can walk up to."

He said what it closes, in his own voice, because a thing said in that voice at that end of that table is a decision. Nine years of an office that could not be sent to. From this evening a person can be sent to that door, with hours on it, and be right to come.

He said what it costs, in the ordinary voice, because a thing said in that voice is a fact. About forty people. The second quiet, four years sooner. His own hands, five days in seven, in the dark, for as long as he stands at that table.

Wenna Raut witnessed the hour, and the carriers witnessed the door, and about four people in that passage witnessed the card, and he nailed it to that door with three nails, straight, the address, the door, and the hours, and nothing else on it, and not one name.

## The Head At The Door

And he turned in that doorway and found the stranger from the rail standing four steps down it with his hat in his hands, waiting to find out whether a card on a door was the whole of it.

"The head is unmoved," said Lin Hao. "Eight hundred and fifty-two, and it has not been touched since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. Twenty-eight sheets behind that number and twenty-seven quarters, both of those correct and differing by one, and the arithmetic behind them is three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, which is two thousand four hundred and thirty, which is twenty-seven times ninety."

He said it in the ordinary voice, because a thing said in the ordinary voice at a door is a fact, and the stranger from the rail heard all of it and did not appear to understand one figure of it.

"That is the fifth time you have said that this year," said the woman with the back, from the table behind him.

"It is the fifth, counting March. I have said it in a corridor and in a day room and in a yard, and I am not going to do the other voice here either, because this is the first of them I have said to a man who came up four flights to find out whether it was true."

The woman with the back looked at the card on the door, and then at the stair, and then at the four steps the stranger was standing on, and said the thing a door does not know about itself.

"That sheet comes down in a year and another goes up. That card does not come down."

"That is the difference," said Lin Hao. "And I am the one who put it there, and it was mine to put there, and I am not going to stand in a doorway being told it was a good thing by people who are only standing in a doorway because of me."

He said it at a door, and there were two of them standing at it, and one of them had got there by walking to it off a counter at about half past four.

## The Last Asking Of The Year

Afterwards, in that room, with the door addressed and the hours on it, Wenna Raut opened that book to the day's last asking and entered it in the words the woman used, and left the space under it empty, because the question was about a person and that person was not in the room and could not be made to come, and keeping it does not make that better.

And beside it on the table lay the scrap the woman had written for herself, with the date and the hour of her own asking on it in her own writing, and the clerk's witness that she wrote it and not that it came from here.

That scrap is going down four floors tonight in a coat pocket and the row is going down in the strap, and not one word of the answer exists anywhere in this city in any hand but those two.

He looked at the page with that row on it and then at the door with the card on it, and felt fear and relief together, and the two did not cancel, and he carried both down with him that evening along with the strap.

He nailed nothing else. He wrote nothing else. The drawer in that room has been shut for fifty months and nine days and cannot be opened by anybody. There has been nothing in the inside left of his coat since the third of December of last year, and there is going to be nothing in it.

At half past five he carried that book down four flights and about four hundred yards himself, in the dark, by his own hands, five days in seven, and at the bottom he stood in the yard and looked back once, for the first time in nine years, at four flights of windows with a card on the door below them, and then he went home up the hill with the street name sounding under his boots like any other name.

He walked slowly, because his hands ached and his left arm had gone on the second floor and come back on the third, and the cold took the ache and left the knowledge: nine years of an office nobody could send to had ended on a piece of card with three lines on it, and the ending was his, and it was in ink, and no stranger after this would ever be lost for want of a door.

Three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine days. Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-three, the twenty-fourth of this one, is six, and the forty-third came down at four and the forty-fourth went up carrying no new line, and the head stands at eight hundred and fifty-two, across twenty-eight sheets and twenty-seven quarters, which differ by one. And three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand four hundred and thirty, which is twenty-seven times ninety.

*Less three thousand eight hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is ninety, and the forty-third sheet was face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and it came down at four this afternoon and the forty-fourth went onto the nail with nothing new at the foot of it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and nine days, which is fifty months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

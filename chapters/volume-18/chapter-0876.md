# She Cannot Say It Again

Three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two was the thirteenth of this month and the twentieth is day three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two is seven, and twenty less thirteen is seven by the month count.

## Her Own Hand

She came up at about eleven on the twentieth with a shawl over her head though the day was warm, and he knew her before she gave her name, because she was the woman who had put a slip on that wood a week before and asked for a line saying dead, and she had not asked for it again, and he understood why she had not.

She asked for the row about her husband by the date she had brought it up, which was four years ago, in the sixth month of the seventh year.

Wenna Raut found it inside four minutes, because the year was an old one and the pages lie easier, and she brought that book round and read the date and the hour aloud, and then stopped, with her finger on the line.

"Read it," said the woman.

Wenna Raut looked at Lin Hao, and there was something in her face he had not seen before, a kind of distress that had nothing to do with the finding.

"I cannot," said Wenna Raut. "Not from the page. And not from memory either."

## A Practice And Its Cost

He took her to the far end of the room, with the woman waiting at the bench, and he asked her quietly what the trouble was, and she told him with her hands twisted together.

"From the sixth of July of last year I do not repeat what I said," said Wenna Raut. "That is the practice of this room. And about four days a month I have to say it anyway, because people come up and ask, and this month it is somebody's dead husband from four years ago in my own hand."

She looked past him at the woman on the bench, and her eyes were wet, and she had been a clerk for nine years and had never asked him for anything except the wage she is paid.

"Four years ago I wrote down a man's words about a wall he had brought up himself," said Wenna Raut. "And the man died in the spring. And now his wife wants the row read, and if I read it I am repeating what I said, and if I do not read it I am refusing a woman her own answer, and either way I am breaking the thing I promised last July."

Lin Hao felt the tiredness come up in him, because the practices of that room were all his or hers and every one of them was a practice and not ink, and every one of them had been made to keep the book from becoming a list, and now two of them were pulling against each other with a widow between them.

## Said Anyway

He went back to the bench with her, and he read the row himself in the voice that office reads everything in, a date and an hour and a man's words from four years ago and an answer under them in the wife's hand, while Wenna Raut stood beside him with her hands together.

The woman listened with her head bowed, and at the end she nodded, and asked one question about a word in the answer, and Lin Hao answered it from the page, carefully, giving only the words and nothing around them.

"Four years ago I came up this stair with my husband," she said, without being asked, because people tell a room more than the room asks for once the reading has moved them. "He did the talking. I stood where you stand. Wenna Raut wrote it down. He died in the spring and I have wondered since what shape my own words were."

"Your own words were not in that row," said Lin Hao. "They were yours. I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that the book holds a thing it does not hold, because you have been up these stairs twice about that already."

She took that without arguing, which told him she had come expecting it.

"They were plain words," he said. "And they were written plainly. And the answer under them was in her hand, and it stands as it stood."

"Thank you," said the woman. "She wrote it down right. I remember now what I said. I had forgotten the shape of it."

She went down the four flights, and the room was quiet after her, and Wenna Raut stood at the table with her back to both doors, and her shoulders shook once and then were still.

At the stair door she had turned back, which she did not do.

"You read his words to me this morning," she said, "and you have not asked me once for the line I came for on the thirteenth. Not once, in three weeks, and I have counted."

"You have been right to wait for it."

"I have been right to wait for it," she said, "because every counter in this city writes it for money and you do not, and I can tell the difference now in about half a second, which is a thing I could not do in the sixth month."

She held the shawl closed at her throat with one hand.

"So I am going to pay a man off Cordwain for a piece of paper, and I am going to bring it up here, and I want to know what you will do with it when I do."

"I will enter that you brought it," said Lin Hao, "in your words, with the date and the hour. And I will read the row to you as often as you come up for it, and I will not put it in your hands, and you should spend the money on a chair that has arms rather than on a stamp."

She looked at him for a moment and said that was the second thing in three weeks he had told her the truth before she had asked for it, and went down.

"That was one of the four days," said Wenna Raut. "And I could not say it, and you said it for me, and that is worse, because now the room has heard my row in your mouth."

He had nothing to offer her except the truth, and the truth was small.

"You entered it four years ago correctly," said Lin Hao. "And it was read correctly this morning. And the practice holds because you did not repeat it and I did. And in about four years, when somebody asks, I will say so."

She nodded, and wiped her eyes with her sleeve, and entered the morning in the words the woman had used, with the date and the hour, and her hand was steady again by the second line, because work is the thing that steadies a clerk when rules do not.

## The Summer Practice

He remembered the sixth of July of last year clearly, because it had been hot and the room had smelled of dust, and Wenna Raut had stood at that same table and said she would not repeat what she said, and had given the reason plainly.

"A clerk who reads back from memory is a second Register," she had said that day. "And a second Register in a clerk's head is a place where the thing it is about can be found without a date and without an hour. I will not carry one."

He had agreed, and the practice had stood a year, and it had cost about four days a month when she had to say things anyway, and every one of those mornings had left her pale and exact and exhausted.

"What happens now," said Lin Hao, "when the practice and the asking pull opposite ways."

"The same as this morning," said Wenna Raut. "You read and I witness. And in about four years, when a stranger puts it to me, I will say that I believe you read it right, against a date and an hour and my word."

## By The Water

He left her at the table and went down that hill to walk by the water for an hour, and the water ran high after rain, and a boy with a pole poled a flat boat along the far bank, and Lin Hao watched him the way people watch work they cannot do, with envy and relief mixed, because the boatman's costs are in his arms and end at dark.

When he came back up the hill the light was going, and Wenna Raut had the room straight and the pen put away, and she nodded to him as he came in, and the nod meant the morning was over and the cost entered, and he nodded back.

At half past five the two carriers strapped that Register and took it down four flights, and he stood aside on the landing to let them pass, the way he does every evening now, and watched them go with the strap that is the wrong one round it.

Wenna Raut walked down with him to the foot of the stair, which she seldom does, and stood a moment in the yard where the stone goes down, looking up at the four flights of windows with an expression he could not read.

"Four years ago I wrote his words and thought nothing of it," she said. "And this morning I could not read them back. That is what a year does to a practice. It finds the morning the practice cannot meet, and stands there, and waits to see what the room is."

He walked her to the corner where their ways part, because the evening was cold and the yard lonely, and neither spoke on the way, and at the corner she nodded goodnight the way she nods everything, once, and went on alone into the dark.

He watched her go, and thought about the two of them, and about how little the wage covers of what she carries, and about how the room stands because she stands.

## What She Brought Back Down

He went home by the long way, past the water, and the evening was cold and clear, and at his own stair he stopped and looked back once, but she had turned the corner, and the street was empty.

He stood there thinking about the woman who had gone down that stair twice, and about the slip in the basket, and about how in four weeks he was going to say something to everybody at once and she would be in the room when he said it, and how the only paper she would be holding would be one this office had refused to write.

*Three thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine days. Less three thousand seven hundred and sixty-two, the thirteenth of this month, is seven, and a clerk could not answer for somebody she entered four years ago, because from the sixth of July of last year she does not repeat what she said.*

*Less three thousand seven hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty, and the forty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand three hundred and forty-nine days, which is forty-four months and twenty-nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Lamp On A Long Table

Three thousand six hundred and seven was the eighth of this month and the fifteenth is day three thousand six hundred and fourteen, and three thousand six hundred and fourteen less three thousand six hundred and seven is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.

## How The Book Got There

He carried it down four floors and about four hundred yards at about nine in the morning and then back up again with it, about nine minutes each way, on the fifth working morning of a week, because not one other person in that building at nine on that morning is allowed to touch it.

It went on a long table of oiled wood in a reading room with a lamp on a stem at each place and shelves of ledgers behind glass along both walls, and about four people were at that table and not one of them looked up.

## The Weight On Every Page

Every page of it was flat and held flat, and the reason is on the table: about nine cast weights, flat-bottomed, one for each place, and a page goes under one of them and stays under it.

Nobody turns a corner there. That is all it is, and it is on no page, and it is because a corner is where a page is lost, and a page that turns up flat and open one day is a page somebody thinks is a whole thing.

The second thing about that room is the same size and less visible. A record read once in there is not read again in there with different people in it, and about four people sit at that table and not one of them has ever watched another of them read.

## Nine Minutes

She read it.

Not the first page fast, which is what he expected. She read the first page slowly, all of it, including somebody else’s letter to this office about a bundle of nails, and then went to the back of the book, found where last month’s rows ended, and worked forward to the first and then forward again from the first.

That is not how you read a book. That is how you find out whether a book is in order.

She kept one finger at the head of each line and touched nothing else, and the corner of each page lay flat under its own weight while she was at the other end of it.

About nine minutes. He counted, because he had nothing else to do with nine minutes, and two of the people at that table finished and left before she was two-thirds of the way down.

“Not one corner,” he said.

“Not one corner,” said Ines Duarte.

## She Turns One On Purpose

At the end of it she put two fingers under the top corner of the last full page and lifted it about two inches, and it came up out of the groove in that table where nine years of pages have lain in the same position, with a soft noise like a boot coming out of a wet ditch.

The two who had not left looked up, both of them, at once.

“That is the whole of what I wanted to find out,” she said, and let it down again, and it did not lie flat. It stood a finger's width up off the wood in the curve where it had been, and the corner would not go back into the groove.

“Can you get it back.”

“It will go back,” said Ines Duarte. “It will not go back flat. It has been held one way for nine years and taken the shape of one way, and I have just told two people in this room a thing about that Register that took them nine years not to learn.”

She got a blade from a drawer and worked the corner down with the flat of it. About eleven seconds, and it went back well enough that you would have to know to look.

“They will not be able to read that book from that page again without knowing somebody turned it,” she said, “and that is what I bought, and I bought it knowing I am not the one who will have to.”

## What Nine Minutes Cost

She put the heel of her right hand on the oiled wood and left it there and looked at it.

“Ink, under two fingers, on the right hand, and it has been there eleven years,” said Ines Duarte. “That is not what I am telling you about, and I have said it in three rooms to three people and in none of the three was it the thing I was telling them.”

“All right.”

“Every record I hold etches. That is what my division is for. I cannot put a record down, and I looked at about four hundred and seventy rows of it nine minutes ago and it is in me and it will not come out.”

“Here is the arithmetic, because you have not asked and not one person in this building ever does.”

“I am holding one more record today than I was holding on the day before and I will hold about nine more by the end of this quarter, and the years do not come back. That is the whole of the price and there is no money in it and no office in this city that will pay it for me.”

“Does any of it show.”

“It does not show on my face and it does not show in my hands,” said Ines Duarte.

“It shows in about nine years, when there is nothing left of me that remembers what a page was for, and by then I will have held about four hundred records and there will be a person alive who was told a true thing by me about a piece of paper and cannot remember being told. I have done that sum out loud twice in my life and both times to a man who did not ask.”

## The Sentence She Says In Her Own Mouth

“You have been standing at that table looking at the middle distance since I started,” she said. “All right. One sentence, and it is not a compliment and it is not an accusation.”

She put her hand on the spine of it.

“This office has never once been wrong about a paper. Not one row in nine years has been wrong about what a piece of paper is, when it came through the door, who brought it, or what was written on it. And it has never once been able to say what a paper is about.”

She let go of the spine.

“Nine years,” said Ines Duarte. “Four hundred and seventy rows. Not one of them has ever been wrong and not one of them has ever been able to answer the question.”

“That is a finding.”

“It is. I filed one on the fifteenth of February two years ago, on about four hundred and thirty of those rows, and it cost me a standing, and I am not going to file the same nothing twice, because the second time it reads as a habit.”

## What Happens When The Person Is Dead

“There is a row in that Register with nothing under it.”

“There is. Tell me what happens when the person your answer is about is dead.”

He had been waiting nine days for somebody to ask him that.

“The rule in that room is that an answer about a person is read out once with the person it is about in the room,” he said. “If the person will not come, there is no answer and the row stands as it is.”

“Then who is the person when it is dead.”

“Not one person comes up four flights who is not alive, so a question about a dead person has to arrive as a question about a piece of paper, and it did, on the first of this month, and the man’s name is on nothing in this city.”

“Right. And you keep a row forever with a date and an hour on it, no name and nothing under it, and that is your answer to a woman about her husband.”

“It is the answer I have. It is not a good one.”

“It is the true one, which is worse,” she said, “and I audit the records of people who have died, and I have about nine hundred of them, and not one of them is in that Register, because not one of them was ever a piece of paper.”

## The Lamp At Her Own Place

The two who had stayed had gone by about half past four and the room had the sound of oiled wood and glass after the last of four people has left it.

She pulled the cord at her own place about two inches and the lamp at that end of the table went down to about half, and she took the edge of it with her heels off the rail and nothing at her back, and she watched him in the glass of the shelves along the far wall.

“Coat,” she said. “Leave it on the chair and do not take the plate out of it, because I read a plate through cloth in about two seconds and there is no figure cut into yours anywhere, and that is the most interesting thing about you and you are rank one.”

“I have nothing.”

“I know exactly what you have and that is why you are the only person in this city I am going to do this with tonight,” said Ines Duarte. “I am not careful with you. I am not going to be careful with you. There is not one instruction in my division that would let me be careful with you and I have looked, and it took me about four seconds to decide and four more to stop wanting to look further.”

“You decided that before I had the sentence out.”

“I decided it before you came down the hill,” said Ines Duarte.

## Sitting Him On The Oil Of It

She made him sit on the edge of that table, which is a thing about eleven years old and about five feet long and made of wood that has had oil into it, and then she stood in front of him with her knees against the front of his thighs and took him out herself, and got her hand round him, and he was already wet at the tip of it and that was the first thing about the afternoon she had not expected.

She did it the way she had handled the first page that morning, without hurry and without any doubt about the order of things, and she got him hard inside about nine seconds and told him the figure when it happened, and he did not know what she meant and did not ask.

“Lean into it and do not put your face in front of that lamp, because I want to see you and not a shadow.”

“There is nothing to lean on.”

“There is a hundred years of oak behind you that has had four men a week leaned on it since the building went up,” said Ines Duarte, “and it is the only thing in this city you are going to be able to do that I am not going to ask you to pay for.”

He got his shoulder against the end of the long table and watched her, and she let him, and about two minutes later she told him what she had decided to do with the next eleven minutes and he said nothing whatever about it.

She got him up inside her wet cunt standing up, with her fingers at the top and her thumb where she wanted it, and it was fast and it was not gentle and she did not enjoy it for the first minute and then she enjoyed it enormously and told him so in one word.

## Reading Him Aloud

Somewhere in the middle of it she started talking, and not to him.

“Your breathing went at a quarter past four,” said Ines Duarte, working down and coming back up, “and it has gone twice more since, and you have not once tried to hide it, which means either you have stopped being able to or you decided about nine years ago that not one person gets to be told and you have been wrong about that ever since.”

He said nothing.

“You can speak,” she said. “Say whatever you like. I am not looking at your face. I am looking at what you do with your hands and you have been gripping the edge of that table hard enough to whiten them since the first minute, and that is the only honest thing you have done this afternoon.”

He said her name.

“Say the other one,” she said, “the one you use when you are saying a decision.”

He said the other one and she laughed, once, in the middle of it, and it went through her and she had to stop for two seconds and start again from the beginning of the stroke.

“Right,” she said. “Now you are going to come, and afterwards you are going to hold me for as long as I want to be held and not one second less, and I have never once had that and I am not going to explain why I have never once had that.”

## Afterwards

He came with his mouth shut and his face against the side of her neck and one hand in her hair where it had come loose, and she took all of it and held it and told him when.

Then she let him hold her, and she shook, and she took longer about it than anybody who has not watched her work would have expected, and afterwards she stayed against him with her face in the side of his neck and did not wipe anything and did not go and get her cuffs and did not hurry.

“There is one more thing and then you can go,” she said, into his neck.

“Say it.”

“Has that office ever been able to tell anybody what it does not hold.”

“No.”

“Then you do not know what you do not have,” said Ines Duarte, “and you have been doing this for nine years, and the person who put those four hundred and seventy rows in that Register and the person who reads them are the only two people in this city who are certain of what is in it, and one of them is you, and you have never once been certain about the other.”

She got off him and got her coat and did not put her hair back.

“I am going to have that page turned again in about four months,” she said, “and it will not be me, because I am not going back into that room for that Register, and you know the reason and I do not have to say it.”

“I know the reason.”

“No,” said Ines Duarte. “You know a piece of the reason. Say the rest of it on the walk back and see whether you get it before you reach your own door.”

*Three thousand six hundred and fourteen days. Less three thousand six hundred and seven, the eighth of this month, is seven, and the bound Register went four floors down and about four hundred yards off and back again for the first time in nine years, and about four hundred and seventy rows were read in about nine minutes by a woman who is not a clerk, and one corner of one page was turned on purpose and would not go back flat.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-five, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-five of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand one hundred and ninety-four days, which is thirty-nine months and twenty-four days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

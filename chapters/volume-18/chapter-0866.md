# Nine Ways To Find A Person

Three thousand six hundred and ninety-four was the fifth of this month and the twelfth is day three thousand seven hundred and one, and three thousand seven hundred and one less three thousand six hundred and ninety-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## A Woman Who Was Angry At Ten Past Eleven

She was angry because she had been told nine minutes on the sixth of this month, and eleven minutes on the ninth, and on this morning she was told that it would not be today.

She had brought nothing with her because she had brought nothing the last two times either, and she had a coat on that was buttoned to the throat at half past eleven in a warm month.

“I have been down those stairs three times to be told about minutes,” said the woman. “I do not care about the minutes. I care that the third time is worse than the first and that the woman at that table keeps saying it will not be today, and today is a word you have had five weeks to get round.”

“It is because that book holds about four hundred and seventy rows and not a name on any of them. That number belongs to the third of June of the year before last, and that was the last day anybody stood over it and counted.”

“There are about nine women a month coming up here about their own rows and about four of them are about somebody who is not in the room, and that has not been said out loud to anybody, and I have been doing sums in my own head since the sixth of this month like a woman waiting for a bus.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair at all and it is certainly accurate, and I have about nine minutes before I have to be at a house up that street.” She went out and stopped in the passage and came back. “Is it ever going to be a number of minutes.”

“I do not know,” said Lin Hao. “And I am going to tell you why, and it is going to take about nine minutes and it is going to be the worst hour either of us has had in this room, and then you can do your own sums about it.”

## The End Of That Table, At Eleven

He stood at the end of that table and made a clerk write nine things down on the back of the standing sheet’s own counterfoil, which is not a page and is not in the Register and goes in the wastepaper basket at about half past five, and he said out loud, with the door open, that he was about to try to find a person in a book that has never carried a name and has never been counted by anybody since that June.

“Nine ways,” he said. “Go through them with me and kill the eight.”

Wenna Raut put the pen down.

“One,” he said. “The paper they brought up themselves.”

“That gets about four in nine and no more, because about four in nine are about a person and the paper is about somebody else’s hedge.”

“Two. The date.”

“Several a day, every day, since the second of December.”

“Three. The words they used.”

“Two women in this city have described the same hedge within about nine weeks and both of them are in that book and both of them used *the wet side of it*.”

“Four. The answer underneath it.”

“The answer does not say who it was about. It says what we said. That is the whole of what an answer in that Register has ever been.”

“Five. Asking them to describe themselves again.”

“That is a figure of a person on the back of a slip that gets carried off in somebody’s hand.”

“Six. Asking them a second question about the first.”

“I can ask. Nothing goes in, since the twentieth of October, and they would both know they were the second one asked, and in about four years somebody would be able to say which two people out of nine were asked twice.”

“Seven. Their own handwriting.”

“They write down what they bring up and about half of them do not bring anything up at all, and the half that do are the half that it does not reach anyway.”

“Eight. A neighbour.”

He stopped.

There was about nine seconds in that room.

“A neighbour,” said Wenna Raut, “is a woman who will be asked a question by this office about a man she eats with, and in about four years that woman will be asked again by a man in a white plate and she will remember which of them she said it to.”

“Nine. Sending somebody to a house.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “And you said no, and it is the oldest thing in that room and it is not about the book.”

## What Is Left

“There are nine ways to find a person in this city and eight of them are finished,” he said. “So the ninth one is the hour.”

“No.”

“The hour is the only thing on a row that is not a word. Two people come up at about a quarter past ten on the fourteenth and one of them is the one whose row it is and the other one is the other one, and out of about nine people in a month the hour is wrong about once, and once in nine is a rate this city would accept for a hedge.”

“That is not a rate that comes out of a person,” said Wenna Raut. “That is a rate that comes out of a column.”

## What It Is, Said By The Person It Is About

“Then say what it is, because I have been carrying it since the twenty-fourth of March and I got it out of a street about four seconds early, and somebody in this room was going to tell me what it was and it was going to be wrong, and you have had seven weeks and it is you.”

Wenna Raut looked at the counterfoil with eight things on it.

“You are a man who would rather not know than hold it,” she said. “You closed the instrument that could have found out who was asking about whom on the twentieth of October and you did it because a question about a question is a list of askers, and you were right, and it has cost this room a clerk who cannot repeat herself on about four days a month, and about nine people a month who are now an hour and a date.”

“That is the reason and not the thing.”

“The thing is that you would rather not know,” said Wenna Raut, “and a column of hours is the only way in this city of not knowing and finding out at the same time, and you have found it, and it is wrong, and I am telling you it is wrong in a room with the door open because that is where things get said and not where they get decided.”

She did not write it down.

## The Thing He Said Back

“Then I am going to do it anyway,” said Lin Hao.

He said it at about eleven and a half with four people in that passage, standing at the end of that table in the voice he has always used there, and it went out through that door into a building in which about nine people a month come up a stair.

“I am going to keep the hour that each person comes up at, on the back of my own hand and nowhere else, and I am going to use it to find rows, and not one piece of paper in this city is going to say that I did it, and neither of us is going to put it in that book.”

“That is the twentieth of October again.”

“No. In October I was frightened of what it would become. This morning I have watched a woman go down four flights three times to be told about minutes and I am not frightened of anything, I am tired, and a tired man builds a column.”

He put the back of his left hand where she could see the skin of it and nothing else, and then turned it over, because the figure was on the other side and he was not ready for her to have it.

“You did not look at it,” she said.

“No.”

“You have had it there since the twenty-eighth of April.”

“Yes.”

“You have carried a figure on your own hand for a fortnight and not told the one person in this city who would have told you what it was for.” She put the counterfoil in her hand and did not tear it. “That is about nine days longer than I would have given you.”

He went down at about four and came back at half past five, and he carried nothing up and nothing down and there is nothing whatever in either pocket of that coat and there has been nothing in the left side of it since the third of December.

The passage outside that door had four people in it when he came out and two of them were gone by the time he went down. One of the two was the woman with the coat buttoned to the throat, who had stood in that passage for the best part of an hour listening to a man describe the end of the only thing he had built in nine years, and who went up four flights afterwards and asked again, and was told again that it would not be today.

The figure on the back of his left hand was wrong by about four and he had not washed it and he was not going to, and he had told the truth about himself out loud in a room with the door open, which was the first thing in nine years he had done that could not be taken back and was not a practice and would not be entered anywhere.

*Three thousand seven hundred and one days. Less three thousand six hundred and ninety-four, the fifth of this month, is seven, and eight ways of finding a person in that book were killed in about nine minutes, and the ninth is the hour, and the size of that book has not been counted by anybody since a June in the year before last.*

*And the ninth way is the hour a person came up those stairs at, and a man said out loud in a room with the door open that he was going to keep it where it could not be read.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-two, and the forty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and eighty-one days, which is forty-two months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# Nine Ways To Find A Person

Three thousand six hundred and ninety-four was the fifth of this month and the twelfth is day three thousand seven hundred and one, and three thousand seven hundred and one less three thousand six hundred and ninety-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## At The End Of The Table, With The Door Open

He made the list himself on the twelfth of this month at about eleven in the morning, and he made it on a sheet of his own paper and not on office paper, and he read it out to about four people going past an open door, because a list that only one person has seen has not been tested.

The subject of the list was how to find a person from what this office holds.

“Not from the Register,” he said. “I am not allowed to use the Register and I never have been and there is no clause anywhere that would let me. From everything else. From everything this city can already reach.”

“Go on.”

“There are about nine ways and I have got eight and I want the ninth and I cannot get it, and I want the eight out loud before I admit the ninth.”

She wrote the eight down on the sheet in front of her while he said them, in her own hand, which is not permitted and which she did anyway, and he saw her do it and said nothing about it,

and there was about a foot of room between that sheet and the bound Register and it was the closest that office has come in nine years to a second copy of anything and she tore it in two at the end of it and both halves went in the basket at the back of that room, which is a different drawer from the one in the wall on the left of that table and has always been a different drawer from that one.

## The Eight He Could Do

“One. The paper a person brought up with. It is the strongest thing in this city and it is in their own kitchen drawer and every one of them has kept it.”

“Two. The carrier’s sheet. Whose hand took an answer, and in what order. There are about nine of those a month going down four flights and four hundred a year and about three thousand and six hundred in the time this office has been open.”

“Three. The foot of the standing sheet, read out loud at a rail in a passage off Cordwain four times a year to about eleven people, which is what a quarter-end carries.”

“Four. The same foot, on an ordinary day, to about nine people a week, at a speed that does not change.”

“Five. A kerb at the foot of a hill. About four hundred people go past it in a week and nine or so of them crouch, and four inches of dressed stone behind a board.”

“Six. A lane off Cordwain with nine stools and nine districts and about nine figures a morning, and a man at the head of it who repeats everything he hears to about four people a day and has never once been asked to stop.”

“Seven. A fold. If the fold has not been burnt, and if the answer went down in one and there are nine a month.”

“Eight. A date. A person will put their own hand to a day unaided.”

“Those are eight,” said Wenna Raut.

“Every one of the eight is somewhere else,” he said. “They have never cost this office anything, and that is not because they are weak. It is because every one of them requires the person to do something. Show the paper. Take the fold.”

“Repeat the date. Stand at the rail. They are all doors with the handle on the other side, and nine years of this office have been built on the assumption that every person who comes up four flights about something can reach a handle.”

“Then the ninth one does not have a handle.”

“No it does not,” said Lin Hao. “The ninth one is a thing that happens to a person while they are thinking about something else.”

## The Ninth

“The ninth is an hour,” he said.

“That is the same thing as eight.”

“No it is not,” said Lin Hao. “A date takes a person's agreement. A date is a thing you cooperate with. An hour does not take anybody's agreement at all. An hour is simply when a person was on a stair, and if somebody in this city knew the hour a person came up these four flights they could put that person on a pavement at that hour on any day they liked, and the person would not have to be in on anything.”

He put the sheet down on the wood.

“That Register carries no name at all. It has no column for a person either. And about nine people a month come up those four flights, and each of them is identified, in this city, by that hour and nothing else.”

## Where The Ninth One Lives

“The hour is the stair,” said Wenna Raut.

“The hour is the stair,” he said. “And nobody has to be asked anything at all. A person goes up four flights at a quarter to ten and a person goes up at eleven minutes past two, and both of those are this building, and the building is the one thing in this city that can put a person on a pavement at a given hour, and it has been able to do it since the day it was built.”

He stopped there and about four people went past the door.

“You closed it,” said Wenna Raut.

## What He Closed, And When

“On the nineteenth of November of last year,” said Lin Hao.

“There was a man on the half landing between the second and third floors at about two o’clock on a working afternoon and I went and stood about four feet off him for about four minutes and asked him nothing, and I came back up those flights and told you at the end of that table that I would not watch who comes up my own stair, and I asked you not to write it down anywhere, and you did not write it down anywhere.”

“That is right.”

“It was a good reason and it is still a good reason,” he said. “And I gave it to myself in one afternoon, on a stair, for a man I knew nothing about, and it is the only instrument in this city that would have found out who is asking about whom, and I shut it in about four minutes without asking anybody to help me.”

## The Decision, Which Is A Refusal To Un-Refuse

“I could go and stand on that landing tomorrow afternoon and I would learn nothing at all, because that man comes at about two o'clock and anybody watching a landing at about two o'clock learns that somebody is on the landing.”

“To learn the hour anybody comes up I would have to be on it every working morning from about a quarter to eight until about half past six, for years, in a corridor about four feet wide, and about nine people a month would see me doing it.”

“That is an office.”

“It is a watch,” said Lin Hao. “And the day this office becomes a watch is the day the thing somebody came up four flights to ask about turns into the thing somebody watches them come up four flights for, and I do not know which way round that goes and I am not going to find out in nine years by doing it to nine people a month.”

He did not open it. That was the whole of that decision and it was made in about forty seconds and it cost him the ninth of the nine and it will cost him whatever comes after the twelfth of this month.

Wenna Raut went on writing for about nine seconds after he had stopped and then turned her wrist over and looked at the nib, and then she said the thing she had been holding since the middle of last week.

“You did not say the reason out loud with the door open that time.”

“No.”

“You have said every other one of them out loud with the door open since the second year.”

“That one is different.”

“It is different because the reason was that you did not want to,” said Wenna Raut, “and everything else you have said out loud since the second year has also been because you did not want to, and that is why about four people in this city could put your decisions together and have, and have not used them.”

He did not answer that.

## About Half Past Two, And A Counter Under A Window

At about half past two he was standing in a passage off Cordwain, at a rail under a window, on the far side of about four hundred yards of street, in front of a man who is paid nothing to be behind a counter.

He asked whether the foot of that sheet had ever let anybody find anybody.

The counterman went on with what he was doing for about nine seconds, which he does, and then he said:

“Yes.”

Lin Hao said nothing.

“Once,” said the counterman. “And I am not going to say which sheet and I am not going to say which person and you cannot ask me for either of them, and if you were to stand here until the light went I would not tell you and I would be right not to tell you.”

“Nobody can ask you.”

“Nobody has ever asked me for anything,” said the counterman, “and the reason that has been true for nine years is that the two of us have never once stood here and been the same kind of person. You are a man who comes up four flights and I am a man behind a glass box, and the day either of us wants the other one’s job is the day this rail stops being worth anything to anybody in this city.”

He squared the sheet on the rail with his thumb and went back to his counter.

*Three thousand seven hundred and one days. Less three thousand six hundred and ninety-four, the fifth of this month, is seven, and a man read out nine ways of finding a person out of what this office holds, and eight of them are in the hands of this city already, and the ninth was an hour and an hour is a stair, and he shut that stair himself in about four minutes on the nineteenth of November of last year and has not opened it.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-two, and the forty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand two hundred and eighty-one days, which is forty-two months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

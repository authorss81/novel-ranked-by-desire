# The Book Holds No Names

Three thousand six hundred was the first of this month and the eighth is day three thousand six hundred and seven, and three thousand six hundred and seven less three thousand six hundred is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

## What She Asked A Second Time

She came up at about a quarter to eleven on the eighth and she had the same sheet and she had not folded it this time, which put it in a worse condition by the end of the morning than the first visit had.

“You told me on the first of this month that you have about four hundred and seventy rows.”

“There are about four hundred and seventy rows in it, which is the count as at the third of June of the year before last and the last day anybody counted them, and rows have gone in since and I have not counted those, and about four hundred and thirty was the count on the first of February of that year, which is the other figure and the older one.”

“Then read me the ones about him.”

Wenna Raut squared the book to the edge of the table.

“I can find you a row,” she said. “I want you to understand what finding one is before I do it, because in about nine minutes you are going to be angry and it will not be with me.”

“Go on.”

“A row is a piece of paper with a date at the head of it and an hour against the date and then the words somebody used, in the order they used them,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the whole of what a row is, and not one of them in nine years has carried a name on it, and not one of them ever will.”

“And when people say to me that this office has a name in it, they mean a woman in a column on a quarterly sheet that goes out to about two thousand counters four times a year. That name is on that sheet and not in here, and the sheet is a page and this is a book, and you have brought me a piece of paper.”

“So the answer is that there are no names in it at all.”

“There is not one name in that book,” she said, “and there has not been one since the office opened, and that is not an oversight and it is not a defect and it is the only reason the book is a book.”

## The Reason The Book Is A Book

“Why is it the only reason.”

“Because the day there is a column in that book for a person, that book is a list of people,” said Wenna Raut. “And it was a list about nine years ago and it was the best list in this city, and it could not be got back. Not slowly. There was no month in which anybody could have got it back.”

He took the chair at the end of the table, which he does about four times a year.

“I know what it was.”

“You know what it was and you have spent nine years refusing to build the thing that would put a name against a row,” she said, “and you did it on purpose and it was the right decision and I entered nothing about it because you did not give me anything to enter.”

“And now a woman has come up four flights twice and the only question she has is whether a man is alive, and this office cannot tell her, and that is not an oversight either. That is the price of the arrangement.”

“You could tell her there is nothing about him.”

“I could tell her that there is nothing in that book about him by name, and she would hear it as *nothing*, and in about four years she would be standing at a rail saying that the office told her there was nothing about her husband.”

She pulled the book round to her own side of the wood, squared the head of the page against the edge with the side of her hand, and then pushed it back to where it had been, which took about four seconds and which she did not do for him and did not explain.

“So here is what we can give. That on the first of this month a piece of paper came through that door and a question was put about it and an answer was given, and that the row for it is at a date and an hour, and that the man it is about is not named on the row and never was.”

## The Question She Actually Asked

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “You asked whether that book can tell anybody anything about a person. And that is a question about the Register and not about a piece of paper, and since the twentieth of October, nothing that is a question about a question goes in. Not the answer. Not the refusal. Nothing.”

“So this morning is not going to be anywhere.”

“This morning is going to be in a room with the door open and about four people hearing all of it, and it is not going to be anywhere at all,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the second time you have asked me for something and got the true answer and I would like you to notice that both of them were given to you in the same voice.”

“You have been doing that since the twentieth of October.”

“I have.”

“For how long.”

“Since the twentieth of October, which is not quite four months,” she said, “and I have never once counted how many times I have done it, and I am not going to start, and you can ask me at the end of every quarter for the rest of my working life and I will tell you the same thing.”

“Stop not counting.”

Wenna Raut put the pen into the tray and squared it.

“I have not been able to,” she said. “That is the honest answer and it took me about four seconds. The moment I count them they become a queue and a queue is a place where things get added up, and I have watched that happen to two other rooms in this city and neither of them has got it back.”

The woman took the sheet back off the table and put it in her coat without folding it.

“You will find out who it was,” she said. “In about four years. Somebody will come up those stairs with the name of the man who asked.”

“Perhaps.”

“Not perhaps,” said the woman. “That is what a list is for. You have built a thing that cannot answer me and you have built it so that in about four years a stranger can say his name in this room and everybody in it will know exactly where to look.”

Then she went down the four flights, and Wenna Raut did not enter that either.

## What He Wanted, In Seven Days

He did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

“In seven days I have understood one thing,” he said, “and it is the thing I have been refusing to build since the second year, and I would like to hear how it sounds in somebody else’s mouth.”

“Say it.”

“If this office could say whether a man is alive, then this office could say it about anybody,” said Lin Hao, “and the second it could do that it would have to know who everybody is, and that is a column, and I have known that for seven years and it has never once cost anything, because not one person has ever come up four flights about somebody who is dead.”

“And now one has.”

“Now one has, and she will come back in about a month, and she is not angry with you and she is not going to shout, and she is going to put the same sheet on that table and wait.”

Wenna Raut did not answer that. She had the pen in the tray and both hands on the wood and she was looking at the door, which was open, which it is.

## Four Lines Under Two Printed Words

At about half past two he told her in front of the open door that he was going down to that rail himself and read the foot of the page with his own eyes.

“You forbade that on the second of December.”

“I did,” he said. “I forbade it in a room with the door open and I said out loud that I would not do it, and I meant it that morning and I have meant it every morning since, and I am going to do it at half past two this afternoon and I am telling you at the end of this table before I go.”

“Why.”

“Because a man’s memory of a woman’s handwriting looks right and is worse than nothing,” said Lin Hao. “That is still true and it is the reason I gave then. It is a different reason now and I want to be exact about the difference, because the first one was about protecting a page and this one is about a woman who has just told us that in about four years a stranger will be able to say a name in this room.”

He got it back at about a quarter past four and it had been off that rail since four o’clock the previous afternoon.

There are four lines in that column under two printed words that say *carried by*, which are a works department’s routing line and are not this office’s and have never once been this office’s.

The first three carry a date and a place. The fourth carries a date, a place and a woman’s name, and no figure on it, and she has been paid once for exactly that since the third of December, and she is paid the same wage for it now, and she is not paid twice.

He stood in a corridor about four feet wide with the sheet in his hand for about nine minutes and read those four lines twice.

He did not add a fifth. He did not take a fifth out of his own head and he put nothing in that column at all, which took him about four seconds and which was the easiest refusal he has ever made.

“There is a woman in the fourth of those lines and I have just read her name off a page about four hundred yards from where she is standing this evening.”

“There is,” said Wenna Raut, “and you are not going to go and tell her that you read it, and if you do I will know about it inside about four minutes, and she has never once asked me for anything and you are not going to start a habit with her either.”

He said the thing at about half past two and she said the word *again* and he said the word *yes*.

*Three thousand six hundred and seven days. Less three thousand six hundred, the first of this month, is seven, and a question about the book was answered in that room and was not entered anywhere, not even as a refusal, and a woman went down four flights with the true answer and nothing else, and four lines under two printed words stood on a sheet at the end of a corridor about four feet wide and no fifth line went in.*

*Less three thousand five hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is thirty-eight, and the fortieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand one hundred and eighty-seven days, which is thirty-nine months and seventeen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

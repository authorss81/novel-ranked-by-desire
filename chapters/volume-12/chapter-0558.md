# Nine Hundred Out Of Eleven Thousand

Three days from the thirteenth of January to this morning, and this morning is one thousand four hundred and twenty-five days off the first of February of the first year and the thirteenth was one thousand four hundred and twenty-two.

The woman who keeps the list came and got him out of the corridor at about eleven in the morning.

She had done it properly. She had come to the branch at about ten with a note in her own hand and given it to Nadia Ferrous at the counter, and Nadia had brought it down the corridor at about a quarter to eleven and stood at the end of it and waited, and she had not read it, and she said afterwards that she had not read it on purpose.

The note said four lines. It said that the Register could not take her list and that everybody in that yard needed to hear it from a man in an office rather than from a woman who was somewhere past sixty with a pencil, and that it would take about four minutes.

He had not gone up that hill once in four years and had said out loud on the street that he was not going to.

He went up it at about a quarter to eleven on the sixteenth of January because somebody had asked him to.

## About Nine Hundred Of Eleven

There were about nine hundred of them in that yard in daylight, which is a different number from eleven thousand in the evening, and about nine hundred is also the number that had come apart in the same yard in the second month of the fourth year when forty women went out for three days to ninety-one doors and came back with nothing.

He knew that number because a woman had told it to him at a table in a room behind the arcade. He did not know anybody in that yard.

The one doing the sums was about sixty and had been in the yard for nine years and did not work for the Church and had not been to the Vestry door in nine years, and when he came through the arcade she looked at him for about two seconds and then went back to her arithmetic, out loud, at the bottom of the steps, to nobody.

"You have come up here," she said. "That is new."

"Somebody asked me to."

"Then say it quickly and then stand where you are and let me do this, because I have been at it since about ten and there are nine hundred people in this yard and about four of them can count past a hundred in their head." She said it to the yard and not to him. "Seventy-one. Seventy-two. That one is about sixty-one and he will tell you he is fifty and he has told eleven people that this morning."

## The Two Hundreds

"You are going to want to say something about August," said Ruta Mielke, "and I am going to tell you the part of it that is yours and leave the rest alone."

"In August of the fourth year forty women went out to ninety-one doors and sat with people and asked them nothing and came home with nothing, and about nine hundred of us watched the whole of that and worked out what it meant out loud on the steps. Being useful is how you get to be owed nothing. We got there ourselves in about three days and nobody in this building said one word of it to us."

"That is nine hundred. That is what I am standing here doing."

She turned round and looked at about four hundred of the yard and then at the rest of them.

"And in January about nine hundred of us worked out a different thing, and we worked it out on our own in about a week off a board, and it is that those four lines are a way of being asked for. Not a way of being owed. A way of being asked for. You put your name at the foot of a board and somebody comes and asks you a question."

"About nine hundred again. Same yard, same number, same fortnight of work in it."

She put her hand flat against the wall of the Vestry, which she did without looking down at her hand.

"Now you tell me, because you are the man from the office and you are the only one who can. Are those the same nine hundred."

## The Difference

He stood on the bottom step and worked it out and got it wrong twice.

"They were not the same nine hundred."

He had known the August nine hundred only as a figure, and the figure had come to him through a woman's mouth at a table, and every one of the four hundred and eighteen things in a room with a window that a woman behind a stove had counted was somebody's own words, and about nine hundred of those words in a yard said that being useful is how you get to be owed nothing.

"And the January nine hundred were people who had read four lines on a board and had worked out that you could ask for things."

The first nine hundred were people who had done something and been given nothing for it. The second nine hundred were people who had wanted something and had found out how to say so out loud in a yard.

"Those are not the same people and about half of the difference is one word, and the word is what a person says at the bottom of a step when the week has gone badly."

He said that out loud, on the step, in front of about nine hundred people, and it was the longest thing he had said in public in four years, and about nine hundred people heard it and one woman at the back said *that is a load of it* and about forty of them agreed with her out loud.

## What Nine In Ten Leaves Out

"And the other half of it," said Ruta Mielke, "which is the half that matters and which you did not say, because you cannot say it and you are an office."

"Those four lines will take in a man who changed what he held, on a day he can name, in a place he can name, and who can say who else was there. That is four lines and it is the right size for a counter and a book."

"I have watched about ninety people put something at the foot of that board since the ninth of January. About nine of them can do all four of your things. Not one of the other eighty-one can name the day."

She turned back to her arithmetic.

"So that is what the four lines leave out. It leaves out anybody who was helped on a day they cannot remember, by somebody they cannot describe, in a street they have moved out of."

"That is not about nine in ten of what people walk into a room with. That is about nine in ten of it, and it is the same nine in ten every single time. It is the same nine in ten for a person who dug a street for six years and for a woman who fed a boy from a school for nine days."

"You made a rule that takes in a person who can set it out in order. That is all it takes in. I am not saying it is a bad rule. I am saying it is a rule, and rules leave things out, and everybody who has ever been left out of one has always been told it was their own fault for being hard to describe."

## Taken Down And Kept

He got his four minutes in and he gave them to her in about forty seconds.

The Register of Unrecorded Merit cannot take a list. It has no instrument for taking a list, it has no column for it, it has no money and no fund and no schedule and no hearing and no seal.

The only instrument in the building it is in is a pen and a clerk who may only enter what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry. There is no form in this city for entering the name of a person who has brought something to the foot of a shutter. He had looked.

"Then will you say it," said Ruta Mielke. "Say it up there, now, where nine hundred people are, so that they stop waiting for you to say yes."

He said it up there.

Nobody believed him and about two hundred of them argued about it for ten minutes and about four hundred did not care either way because they had worked out in January that the four lines were a way of being asked for and being asked for is what they were doing, and the office being unable to help is not a thing that stops a yard doing anything.

And then she said the thing.

"I have been in this yard for nine years," said Ruta Mielke, "and for eight of those years I have been waiting for somebody to print something I agree with, and I have stopped twice, and it is the only thing I have ever wanted off this hill."

"That is the first thing anybody has printed in nine years that agrees with me, and I am aware that what it agrees with me about is a set of ways of leaving about nine in ten of us out of a room."

"That is why there is not one of us going to take that board down. We took the four lines down off it in our own heads the night after they went up, and we have had them in about nine hundred of us ever since, and that board can stay there as long as the paint holds."

She went back to her sums and got to where she had left off.

"Ninety-four," she said, to nobody. "Ninety-five."

*Sixteenth of January of year five. Three days from the thirteenth. The yard, about a quarter past eleven in the morning, about nine hundred people.*

*I went up the hill because a woman who was somewhere past sixty wrote me four lines and I have not gone up it once in four years and I have said out loud on a street that I would not.*

*And the two nine hundreds are not the same nine hundred and about half of the difference is one word, and the other half of it is that about nine in ten of what a person walks in with cannot say the day, and that has been true every time anybody ever measured it.*

*And she said out loud in front of nine hundred people that it is the first thing anybody has printed in nine years that agrees with her, and that is why the board stays up.*

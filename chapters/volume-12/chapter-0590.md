# Five Years Of The Morning

He came in to the branch at eleven at night on the ninth of October and worked the floors, and at about a quarter to four in the morning on the tenth he was on the second floor with the bucket and the mop and about an hour and a half left, and he had been counting down to it for two days and had told a man with a piece of chalk that there was nothing coming.

It took about nine seconds.

He was on the landing when it happened, or rather it did not happen on the landing, and the whole of what he had been waiting five years for was a corridor at about a quarter to four in the morning in a branch office off a third court with a bin room at the end of it and about nine people in the building, none of whom knew what day it was.

There was no sound. Nothing came. No clerk arrived with anything and no man with a slate stood at the end of a lane and no door opened that has been shut since the year before the first.

He had checked. He would like it on the page that he had checked, because he had checked for five years and it is the only part of this that is his and nobody else in Ostara would have bothered.

He checked the corridor. He checked the stair. He put his ear against the bin room door, which is a thing he has never done in four years of nights and did for about two seconds on the morning of the tenth of October of the fifth year. He checked the yard door at the back of the branch, which is painted shut and has been since the second year.

And then he stood on a landing in a branch office off a third court and understood that he had spent four seconds a day for five years checking a wall, and that as of a quarter to four on this morning there was nothing left to check.

He stood on the landing with the mop in his hand for about half a minute and then he went and did the rest of the second floor and the bin room and the stairwell, because he is rank one and rank one gets assigned work, and he asked for this shift.

## The Lamp At The End Of The Corridor

The night man came up the stair at about ten to four with his lamp and did what he does at that hour, which is put the light flat against the fire door and look through the glass to see whether anybody is standing in the stairwell.

“You are early,” he said.

“I have got the second floor.”

“You have got the second floor and you have been on it since eleven o’clock at night and it is twenty to four in the morning.”

“That is the shift.”

The night man looked at the mop and then at the stair he had come up out of, and he was quiet long enough that Lin Hao understood he was deciding whether to ask. Then he asked it.

“What is today.”

“It is the tenth of October.”

“I know what day of the week it is,” said the night man. “I asked you what today is.”

“That is what today is.”

The man set the lamp down on the sill and left it standing there.

“It is a quarter to four in the morning of the tenth of October,” he said, “and there is not one other man in this building who has said that date out loud today, and I have been on that stair since eleven o’clock last night listening for somebody to.”

Lin Hao did not answer straight away.

“I said it out loud at a quarter to seven two mornings ago, to a man with a piece of chalk, and that was the whole of what I had to say and it took about nine seconds, and then I came in here and worked. It is the only morning in five years where there is nothing left to find out.”

“That is not a question,” said the night man.

“It is not. It is a thing a man says at four in the morning.”

The night man picked the lamp up off the sill. “Then go and say it to somebody who wants it,” he said, “and do not tell me what it is about, because in about four years somebody is going to ask me and I will not have anything to hand.”

He went down the stair with the lamp and did not ask him again. It was the first time in four years that somebody had put a question to Lin Hao about himself and been given nothing at all back, which is a thing that happens to him about four times a year.

## The Thing That Was Lit Once

Ines Duarte was at the end table with an hour sheet and a pencil at about four o’clock and she did not look up and she did not say anything about it, and neither did he.

He wanted to. He came down that stair with a date in his mouth and a woman forty feet away who is the only person in this city who has ever put a question in a book, and he stood in the corridor about four seconds and then went back up.

And the morning came due once. It came due on the tenth of October of the fourth year to a woman who came up two flights without a hand on the rail with a sheet folded in four in her coat.

What came due to her was six years of a street that had never been written down. Nobody alive picked that day and nobody alive can move it, and there is no office in Ostara that keeps a list of the mornings it has decided not to do anything about.

There is no second time, because a thing with no holder does not come round again. After that it is an ordinary morning in a city that has forgotten it, and the most expensive thing anybody did for this city in thirty years was free and went out of a called sitting and nobody afterwards asked.

About two and a half hours later he took his bucket out into Cordwain and there was a tram going under the ranks wall with about nine thousand people under it over the course of a day and not one of them awake.

## The Four Inches Of Stone

He had about twenty minutes before half past five and a wall about two hundred yards away that he had been asked about by the only person in this city who has ever asked him anything, twenty-six days ago, on a clear table, in an audit room, at half past one in the morning.

Three of those four questions were asked to him about that wall on the sixth of March of the first year in a corridor with a page open on a table, and she told him at the counter that she was not going to ask again, and she broke that about four times over four years and asked nothing again until the fourteenth of September, and the fourth one she asked that night had never been asked before in her life.

And this morning he stood on the tile and none of it was answered and none of it needed to be.

About four inches of stone sits under a white tile with his name on it in chalk and nothing at all under that. A woman with a short-handled broom and a canvas bag cleans it every morning, last, because it is under the wall and nobody spits there, and she told him once, unasked, that in about nine years she has never seen a person put anything on it, and then she stopped saying anything.

Nobody has said who put it there. Not in five years and not this morning at about a quarter to five with the street empty and his own name in chalk under a wall nobody can read.

He stood about two feet off the four inches so as not to be on them, because they are hers and not his, and there was nothing under the tile and nothing on the stone and nothing came.

He went to work at about ten past five, and half past five came at half past five, and the morning got light at about twenty past six off Cordwain in the way that it does.

There were twenty days left in that month, because thirty less ten is twenty, and in six days it would be the sixteenth of October, and there is a man in this city who has had a thing standing on a table since the twenty-seventh of April of the third year and who carries it about with him at a wall in the morning, and he has not said it out loud yet this quarter and it is going to be said in October.

*The tenth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine days. Less one thousand six hundred and eighty-seven, the eighth, is two. Less one thousand three hundred and twenty-nine, which is the tenth of October of the fourth year, is three hundred and sixty, and three hundred and sixty is one year.*

*Nothing happened. He said out loud two days early that nothing was going to, and then nothing went, and a man who is owed six years of a street is still owed six years of a street, and about four in every thousand people who read a page ever get to the line about her.*

*Twenty days left in the month. Thirty less ten is twenty, and in six days it is the sixteenth of October.*

# Every Figure He Has Invented

He came down to the lane at a quarter to seven on the twenty-first of October of the fifth year with his hands still rough from a night of stairs and a piece of folded paper in his coat, and Brack was already at the slate with two stubs of chalk and a wet rag and a board with three columns ruled on it in pencil.

Five days had gone since the sixteenth. One thousand seven hundred less one thousand six hundred and ninety-five is five, and twenty-one less sixteen is five by the month count, and he had spent the five days carrying the offer about with him on shift the way a man carries a stone in a shoe.

He wanted the method. That was the whole of what he wanted that morning, and he wanted it written where he could see it and not said to him over a line of waiting men.

Brack did not greet him. He turned the board round so the ruled side faced out and tapped the first column with the chalk.

“Three kinds,” Brack said. “The first kind is what a man is worth standing in a line. I read that off shoulders and hands and the way a man holds his grain. The second kind is what a man is worth when nobody is waiting for him. I read that off the same man an hour later when he walks past without stopping. The two disagree and I put both down.”

“And the third kind,” Lin Hao said.

“The third kind is off nothing,” Brack said. “About nine a morning over three years. I decide them because a line needs a figure at its head and the street needs something to look at while it waits. I have never hidden one of them and I have never sold one of them as anything else when I was asked.”

Lin Hao took the folded paper out of his coat and did not unfold it.

“How many asked,” he said.

“About eleven in three years asked me straight out whether a figure was off nothing, and I told all eleven yes, and about four of them paid anyway, and about seven walked away, and none of the eleven came back the next morning to argue.”

The line formed behind them and Brack wrote without looking down, nine figures in chalk about four inches high, each under the last, and the men paid a grain a head and went to work. Lin Hao watched the chalk the way an auditor watches a pen, for the hesitation that gives away the invented ones, and there was no hesitation anywhere in it.

Anger came up in him again, clean and sudden. It was not at Brack. It was at the neatness of the board, at three columns that told the truth about lying.

“You keep a count of the invented ones,” he said.

“I keep every one,” Brack said. “About nine a morning is about two thousand seven hundred a year, and three years of that is about eight thousand, and I have every one of them in books in my rooms, with the date and the weather and what the line looked like that morning.”

Lin Hao unfolded the paper. It was blank on both sides.

“I brought something to write on,” he said. “Show me.”

Brack wiped the slate with the rag and ruled three short lines on it and wrote three figures, fast, in the good hand the tiled end had taught him to recognise.

“The first I read off a carter with a split lip,” Brack said. “The second I read off the same carter when he came back for a second grain because the first line had moved too slow. The third I made up while the second man was paying, because a boy at the back was getting restless and the line needed a head.”

He put the chalk down.

“All three went out the same morning and all three were paid for. The carter is still working off Cordwain and the boy is on a wharf now. None of the three did anybody any harm and one of them kept a line from breaking.”

Lin Hao looked at the three figures until they stopped being figures and became chalk.

“You are right about the disagreement,” he said. “One street is worth two things at its two ends and a city that reads one of them is reading half a lane.”

“I am right about that the way a man with a slate is right,” Brack said. “I am not right the way your office is right. Your office has two words in paint at the height of a name on a plate and about nine thousand people go under them in a day because the trams go under them, and not one of those nine thousand can say what they mean.”

Lin Hao folded the blank paper and put it back in his coat. His want had changed shape while he stood there, from wanting the method to wanting the man behind it to be wrong about one thing, and the man was not wrong.

“What do you do with the books,” he said.

“I keep them,” Brack said. “A man who makes up figures and does not keep them is a cheat. A man who makes them up and keeps them and shows them to anybody who asks is something else, and I do not have a name for it, and that is why I have had a thing on a table since the twenty-seventh of April of the third year.”

A boy at the back of the line called out and Brack turned and answered him by name and told him the wait was about four minutes, and the boy nodded and stayed. Lin Hao watched that exchange and felt something in him shift that he did not want to name yet.

He had come to find a reason to refuse. He had found about eight thousand reasons to answer.

## The Printer's Proof

He walked to the shop off Cordwain after the line cleared, because a compositor called Aurel Stenner had the four lines in metal and had struck his own name off his own plate in March, and Lin Hao wanted to see what a man who sets type for nineteen years does with a figure somebody admits is off nothing.

Stenner had a proof on the stone, nine figures in a row, with two ringed in grease pencil. He read them upside down without touching them, the way compositors read, and tapped the two ringed ones.

“The door and the list,” Stenner said. “You cannot get through a door by holding the list. Your man at the lane end knows it and your office is about to learn it.”

“He is a man with a slate who is right,” Lin Hao said. “He keeps eleven books with the date and the weather and what the line looked like.”

“I have set returns for nine divisions for nineteen years and I have never set anything that honest,” Stenner said. “About eight thousand figures and about a quarter ringed, and every ringed one with a reason. Ink and hot metal smell in here and the stone stands between you and the door, and still you came, because you want the method written where you can see it.”

Lin Hao felt his body read the narrow room, and breathed through it. No danger threatened him, and still the press and the stone and the single way out put his shoulders up.

“What happens when his sentence goes out in the hand of the office,” Stenner said.

“Then every party with a real claim stands beside a man who invents his, in the same hand, and the city has no instrument to tell them apart,” Lin Hao said. “And I am the one who will have given that away.”

Stenner wiped his hands on a rag.

“Then give it away exactly,” Stenner said. “One line. Once a quarter. His name under it. And the two words beside it with their meaning plain, or the notice is lying.”

“I will come again,” he said.

“Come on the twenty-seventh,” Brack said. “I will have the books out.”

Lin Hao walked the wall end to end before his shift, eleven minutes each way, and counted the tiles at the north end without meaning to, about four hundred in eleven courses under a rail, and the chalk figure under the lowest rank was fresh that morning.

# The Offer Again In October

He got to the wall end of the lane at about a quarter to seven on the sixteenth of October of the fifth year with a bucket in his hand and about forty minutes before he had to be off Cordwain, and the slate was already out and the line was already forming and the man in the delivery coat was writing with his sleeve pulled back so the chalk would not mark the cuff.

Six days had gone since the tenth. One thousand six hundred and ninety-five less one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine is six, and thirty less ten is six by the month count, and he had spent all six of them on floors and stairs and a bin room and had not gone near the lane in the morning once.

He wanted one thing that morning and it was small and it was his own. He wanted to hear what was going to be said to him before the quarter turned without having to answer it on the spot.

Anger was in him already and he knew where it had come from. It had come from relief. Relief had left him exposed in a way fear never had, and a man who has been braced for five years does not know what to do with his hands when there is nothing left to brace against.

Brack finished the figure and stepped back and looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has made and is willing to stand beside.

“Nine this morning,” Brack said. “Nine figures and about nine of them off nothing. You want the count.”

“I want the morning first,” Lin Hao said. “Write your line and let them through.”

The line went through one at a time and each man put down a grain and each man looked at the slate and none of them looked at the wall behind it. About nine thousand people would go under that wall before dark because it was an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams went under it, and about nine of them would stop at the lane end and the rest would walk past with their shoulders down.

When the line thinned Brack wiped his hands on his coat and put the chalk down on the edge of the slate and looked at him directly for the first time that morning.

“It has been on the table since the twenty-seventh of April of the third year,” Brack said. “Every figure I have invented and the way I did it and about nine I decided off nothing, for one line in the hand of that office, once a quarter, saying that I make them up.”

Lin Hao put the bucket down.

“You have said it before,” he said.

“I have said it to you four times and to other people about eleven times and I am saying it again in October because the quarter turns at the end of December and a quarter here is three months of thirty days, which is ninety, and ninety after the thirtieth of September is the thirtieth of December.”

“That is the calendar,” Lin Hao said. “It is not a reason.”

“It is the reason I am saying it now and not in January. A line that goes out once a quarter has to be written before the quarter goes out, and the hand that writes it does not hurry, and I have watched that hand for three years.”

A tram went under the wall and the sound of it covered the lane for about nine seconds and neither of them spoke through it. Lin Hao felt the specific dread he had felt in a room off a third court in December of the fourth year, the dread of a man about to find out what his intentions weigh when they are read by strangers.

“What would the line say,” he said.

“That a figure in Ostara may be a figure somebody made up on purpose,” Brack said. “In the hand of the office itself. With my name under it.”

Lin Hao picked the bucket up and put it down again because his hand had gone tight on the handle.

“You are right about every fact you say,” he said. “You profit nowhere a person loses. You have written about nine a morning for three years and not one of them has been about a day something happened on.”

“I know what I am,” Brack said. “I am asking for what I am to be written where people read.”

“And if it is written?”

“Then a man who stands in a line knows which of the two instruments he is standing in. Yours in paint and mine in chalk, side by side, disagreeing, and both of them telling the truth about what they are.”

Lin Hao looked at the slate and then at the wall behind it. The office had two words at the foot of that wall in paint at the height of a name on a plate, and Brack had a figure in chalk on the stone beside it, and neither covered the other, and a city that read one of them was reading half a lane.

He understood the offer perfectly. He also understood that it would cost him the one thing he had told himself was not his to give.

“You are not asking me to lie,” he said.

“No.”

“You are asking me to write that you invent, in the same hand that writes that other people carried, and to put the two where about two thousand people a day cannot walk past without reading them.”

“Yes.”

“And you have carried that about with you at a wall in the morning for more than two years.”

“I have carried it since April of the third year,” Brack said. “One thousand six hundred and ninety-five less the April day is more than two years and I have not shortened it by wanting it.”

Lin Hao felt anger move in him and then move past him, the way weather moves past a wall. The man in front of him was not cruel and was not wrong and could not be refused on any ground that would survive being said out loud.

“I am not answering it this morning,” he said.

“I did not ask you to answer it this morning,” Brack said. “I asked you to hear it in October, with seventy-five days to the quarter, because seventy-five is thirty less sixteen with sixty after it, and a man who hears a thing in October cannot say in December that he never heard it.”

“Seventy-five,” Lin Hao said. “One thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand six hundred and ninety-five is seventy-four, and you said seventy-five.”

“Then I am wrong about one fact,” Brack said. “Count it against me and keep the rest.”

Lin Hao laughed before he could stop himself, and the laugh surprised both of them, and a man at the end of the forming line looked over and then looked away.

“You are wrong about the subtraction and right about the quarter,” Lin Hao said. “The quarter goes out on the thirtieth of December and the sheet that goes out that afternoon is the twentieth, and the nineteenth went out on the thirtieth of September with eight hundred and fifty-two lines and two in the second column, and nothing has come in since the eleventh of March.”

Brack picked the chalk up.

“You keep your count,” he said. “I keep mine. Mine is nine a morning and about a quarter of them off nothing, and I will show you every one of them before December if you come at a quarter to seven.”

“I will come,” Lin Hao said, and hated how quickly he had said it, and said it again because it was true. “I will come.”

## The Night After

He worked the branch that night, eleven at night until half past five, with the rota page on its nail and four names on it, and Nadia Ferrous on the counter said the market off Cordwain was repeating the two words wrong, half *carried by* and half something else, and he told her she was right to notice and did not tell her what to do about it.

Petra Ilyin stopped him at the corridor board where three conditions hung with a drawing pin through the corner and the fourth cut off with a blade. About thirty people a day walked past it and about two stopped, and one of the two that night was a division clerk who read the three and asked where the fourth had gone.

“It was cut off with a blade,” Lin Hao said. “A copy lost it in January and the loss stayed.”

“And the loss is the door,” the clerk said. “Three lines are a list. Four are a test.”

He had no answer that would survive being entered, so he gave none, and the clerk went away with the three lines in his head. Petra watched him go and then looked at Lin Hao.

“You are carrying something again,” Petra said. “All four names on that rota can tell. None of us will ask.”

That mercy nearly broke him. He went up to the third floor with the bucket and worked water until his shoulders burned, and the burning steadied him. He was rank one and rank one gets assigned work, and he had asked for that shift, and the shift asked nothing back except his hands.

At half past two Isolde Marchetti found him on the stair and told him a compositor off Cordwain had the two words in metal and a yard had them in chalk, and that a street with two instruments in it cannot be handed a third without a notice at the top saying which is the office’s. He thanked her by nodding and did not explain, because explanation was now the most expensive thing available to him.

He left the bucket where it was and walked the eleven minutes to the tiled end and back, because he had started doing that in August and had not stopped, and the chalk figure at the tiled end was still there in a good hand on the stone under the lowest rank, and no painter worked that end of the wall.

At the lane end Brack was writing again and the line was moving and the morning was ordinary in every direction. Lin Hao stood about nine feet off and watched a man put down a grain for a figure somebody had made up on purpose, and the man paid it gladly, and that was the whole of the argument and it could not be answered by refusing to look at it.

*The sixteenth of October, one thousand six hundred and ninety-five days. Less one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, the tenth, is six. Thirty less ten is six by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine, is sixteen. Less the eighth, one thousand six hundred and eighty-seven, is eight. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand six hundred and ninety-five is seventy-four.*

# Twenty-Six Years Of A Cold Room

Eleven days from the seventeenth of February to this morning, and this morning is one thousand four hundred and sixty-seven days off the first of February of the first year. One thousand four hundred and forty was the first of February of year five, and twenty-seven days into that month is one thousand four hundred and sixty-seven, and one thousand four hundred and sixty-seven less one thousand four hundred and fifty-six is eleven. There is no thirty-first and the twenty-eighth is the last day of February.

He came down the stair to that floor at about ten past four in the afternoon with nothing in his coat, for the fourth time in three years.

## What Was Different

The book was on the shelf with the strap across it and the chain through the keeper's lock, and it had been there since the first of January, and it comes off on the first of April, and there was nothing he could have read if he had stood there all night.

The lamp was on its stand and turned down about a finger's width, and the far table was empty.

Four times in three years and that was the first time the far table had been empty, and she did not say a word about it, and neither did he, and there was no arrangement in this building by which a man who is not a keeper asks a woman at a far table why she is not in the room.

## Twenty-Six Years

There are two flights over this room and no window in it and no fire in it, and it has had neither for twenty-six years, which is a quarter of a century of a building standing in the way of a shaft that was never cut through it. The cold in here is worse than the cold on the stair above it at every hour and in every month, and the only thing anybody has ever put in the room to do anything about it is a lamp.

"You have asked me nothing since the second of January," said Hanneke Ruis.

"I have not."

"That is not a complaint. It is a tally, and I keep tallies." She had her hand flat on the strap and she was not turning the book round.

"Twenty-six years of that. I have been in this room thirty. Somebody put a fire in it for the four years before I came and then they took it out and there was no form for asking for it back, and I have not asked, and there is a reason and the reason is that I asked about something else once and it cost me the only thing I had that nobody could argue about."

"I was not going to ask you about the fire."

"I know. That is why I am telling you."

## The Question

He had come down two flights with one question and had been holding it since the second of January and had practised it, which he has not done for anything else in four years.

"How many times have you written the six words."

She stopped with her hand on the strap.

"That is the question," she said. "I have had a question ready for about eleven years and you have not asked it and I have just heard the wrong one."

"The six words. *Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.* Under your own name, four times a year."

"That is not the question. That is a number." She turned round and looked at him for the first time in three weeks, which is about nine seconds. "I have said every quarter for thirty years that I do not keep it. You keep it. It is the wrong thing for me to keep."

## Thirty Multiplied By Four

"I have worked it out," said Lin Hao.

"You have not."

"I have. Thirty years is thirty lots of a year and a year is four times, because it is four blocks and the blocks are the quarters, and thirty multiplied by four is a hundred and twenty." He had it in his coat and had not known it was in his coat until he said it.

"There are a hundred and twenty of those blocks in this city with your handwriting in them and nobody's name on them but yours, and nearly all of them are in rooms that have not been changed inside since the year they were ruled."

She put two fingers on the strap and left them there.

"That is the first figure anybody has spoken in this room in about nine years," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is not entered anywhere and it is not on a page and it is not certified and it cannot be certified, because a thing that is not on a page cannot be a composition, and there is a whole book two flights above this one that I am not allowed to keep and do not keep."

"Nobody has ever said it out loud."

"It has never been said out loud. It has been written a hundred and twenty times and not once said, and that is the arrangement, and the arrangement is thirty years old and it was not designed by me. I found it."

## Why She Does Not Keep It

"You have never counted them yourself."

"I have counted them twice," said Hanneke Ruis. "Once in the eleventh year and once in the twenty-fourth, in my own head, standing at that table, at about four in the morning because I could not sleep in it, and both times I got the same figure and both times I stopped."

"Why."

"Because the moment a keeper keeps a count of her own compositions, that count is the only thing about the premises that anybody can check, and it is in her head, and a head is not a place a figure can live."

She said it with no weight in it at all, the way a woman gets when a sentence has been round the room in her own head every quarter for thirty years and this is not the first time it has come out of her mouth.

"And it is not in any form either. There is no form for a keeper's count and there is not going to be one, and a thing with no form cannot be produced in a room with anybody in it."

"So it is thirty years of a number that only exists at four in the morning."

"It is thirty years of a number that exists," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I am not going to say it again in this room, and I have said it once and you may write it down and you may not come back and read it off the page, because a page of mine that is not one of mine is a thing I do not have."

## Down The Stair

He wrote it in his own notebook at the top of the stair with the door open behind him, and there is no place in this city that may read that notebook, and there never has been, and he did it in about four seconds and then sat down on the step for a while.

There is one woman in this city he can give a number to.

She came to the counter of that building off a third court at about half past three in the morning on the thirteenth of January with a sheet she had ruled herself and eighty-one names in her own good hand, and she had asked whether she was allowed, and he had said yes, and he had told her he was not the person who could have said no, and she had written that on the bottom of it with the date and her own name against it.

She came in on the evening of the twenty-eighth of February at about half past six, through the door with no sign on it, and she did not sit down, and she had the list.

She is a woman who keeps a list. She has been in that yard for nine years and she does not work for the Church and she does not work for the Bureau and she does not work for anybody at all, and that is why she is the only person in this city he can hand a figure to without it becoming a thing the office did.

## The Last Column

The sheet had four columns on it now instead of three, because she had ruled a fourth herself about a week ago, and the fourth column was not names. It was the condition each name could not meet.

"How many are on it."

"One hundred and forty-one," she said. "And I have about two hundred names I have taken off people and not written down because I ran out of paper, and I know that is the same as saying there are about two hundred, and I have stopped pretending it is not."

She put her hand flat on the table, which she does, and she did not look down at it.

"And there is nothing I can do with any of them. That is what I have come to say and I have come twice and the other time I asked a different question." She looked up. "How many times has she written them."

"A hundred and twenty."

"She has never said."

"She said it to me about an hour ago on a stair and she says it is the first figure spoken in that room in nine years."

Ruta Mielke did not write it down at once. She read the number off him twice, the way she read sums off a slate, and then she turned her sheet over on the back of it, at the bottom, under the last of the fourth column, and wrote it there in her own hand, and wrote the date against it, and wrote her own name against it.

"A hundred and twenty," she said. "Thirty multiplied by four. That is the first thing anybody has ever given me that is not a person."

"What will you do with it."

"I do not know," said Ruta Mielke. "I will count the column under it. I have about nine in every ten who cannot name the day and I have been putting them in one column and I have had no idea what it was a column of."

"Now I have a thing it is a fraction of and I can say it out loud on those steps in about a fortnight, and about nine hundred people will hear a sentence they have not heard before, and none of them will be able to do one single thing about it."

"That is not what it is for."

"I know exactly what it is for," she said. "I have known since the sixteenth of January and I said so in front of nine hundred people and I am not going to say it again to you, and you have not asked me, and that is the only reason I have said anything at all."

## What He Did Not Say

She went out at about a quarter to seven. He sat on the step with the door open behind him and worked out what he had done and it took him about nine seconds.

A figure about a premises has been in one woman's head for thirty years and it has not been on a page and it could not have been on a page.

He has carried it up two flights and out of a room off Cordwain and put it in a notebook nobody may read and then given it to a woman who works for nobody, and there are now two copies of a hundred and twenty in this city and neither of them is certified and one of them is in a coat.

He could have left it. It was thirty years of one woman's professional pride and she had counted it twice in nine years and stopped both times and told him not to come back and read it off a page.

And the reason he had not left it is that he had a woman in a corridor who had run out of things to count, and he wanted to see what she would do with it, and he had wanted that before he came down the stair.

*Twenty-eighth of February of year five, the top of a stair off Cordwain, about a quarter to seven. Eleven days since the seventeenth and a hundred and twenty blocks on a plate somewhere in a room two floors down.*

*A room with no window and no fire in it for twenty-six years and a woman in it for thirty, and she counted her own compositions twice in thirty years and stopped both times, and she is right, and a head is not a place a figure can live.*

*And I carried the figure up two flights and gave it to a woman who keeps a list and works for nobody, and she wrote it on the back of her own sheet with the date and her own name against it, and she has about two hundred names she has not written down because she ran out of paper.*

*And I wanted to see what she would do with it. I wanted that before I came down the stair and I did not want it for the room.*

*There are now two copies of a hundred and twenty in this city and neither of them is certified and one of them is in a coat.*

# The Repainting Form

No days at all since the drawer was shut, and the drawer was shut this afternoon: both of them are the first of May, and the first of May is one thousand five hundred and thirty days off the first of February of the first year. One thousand five hundred and thirty less one thousand five hundred and thirty is nothing at all.

One thousand five hundred and thirty less one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine, which is the thirtieth of April, is one, and that one went by in the night before a woman who keeps a list stood in a doorway and said the one thing she was going to say.

And it is ninety-one days from the thirtieth of January, which is one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine, and one thousand five hundred and thirty less one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine is ninety-one: thirty, thirty, thirty and one, counted out of the four months behind today and not forward out of this one.

He had been in the room off a third court at about a quarter past five and a woman who keeps a list had stood in the doorway and said the one thing she was going to say and had gone home. The drawer was shut. The page with about a hundred and thirty-nine names in it was in the drawer. The book was on the table above it with two lines in the second column, and one of them was his.

He put his coat on and went out to look at a wall before his shift, because there was a thing he had not looked at properly in about four months and it was on the way to nothing.

## The Drawer Shut

Wenna Raut had locked nothing, because there is nothing in that room with a lock on it except the keeper's lock two floors under a sales floor, which is a different building. She had squared the book on the table and put her pen down flat, which she does about twice a day, and she had looked at the second column for about nine seconds longer than she looks at anything.

“Two lines,” she had said. “It was ruled on the ninth of March of the third year and it was empty on seven quarter-ends and it had one line in it since the fifteenth of October. Now it has two.”

Lin Hao had stood by the door with the notebook in his coat and his hands empty. He is rank one throughout, on a branch night shift he asked for, eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off, with a white plate in his coat with his name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere.

“The eighteenth sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of June,” he had said. “Her name will be on it.”

Wenna Raut had nodded once and had not written that down, because it was not a thing to be entered. A clerk may only enter what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry, and there is no form for striking a line a person entered against themselves in their own hand.

He had gone out into the third court with the door shut behind him and the drawer shut under the book, and the first name on the yard's board now in a column headed carried by, against himself, in his own hand. He had not asked her anything and he had never seen her, and he cannot strike it.

He felt tired in the particular way a man feels when he has done a thing in ink and has not yet been told what it costs. He wanted to see the repainting form while there was still nothing in the boxes, because once the paint went on he would not be able to say he had seen it blank.

## The Foot Of The Wall

The ranks wall comes down to the stone in the ordinary course of business every month, and the office that paints it had it down that morning. Two men had the quarter's wash off it and a third had a paste brush, and the stone was wet and dark and showed every mark the paint had covered.

The repainting form goes up with the wall every month and every quarter. It is a sheet of the office's paper, pinned at the height of a man's chest beside the last tile, with the quarter and the address at the top and two ruled boxes at the foot of it.

He had walked past those two boxes about sixty times in four years. The last time he had put them in frame on purpose was the thirtieth of December, when the sixteenth sheet went out at four and a shutter went up at the yard end the same evening.

Both boxes were blank. One of them holds a figure and one of them holds a standing, and both have been blank since the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, when a clerk was handed a sheet and a form and no instruction and typed two words into one of them because the box was there and the words were there, and a boy painted what was in the box.

The paste boy looked at him and looked away. The older man with the wash water wrung his cloth out into the bucket and said the wall would be dry by midday and the paint would go on after it.

Lin Hao stood at about nine feet and read the form the way a man reads a sheet in a corridor. The quarter at the top was the first quarter of that year, misaligned by about half an inch where the pin had gone through twice. The address was the one that has gone out at four every quarter since the first of February of the first year, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read it.

A woman with a bundle of returns stopped at about thirty feet and read the wall the way people read it when the paint is off, top to bottom and then away. About nine thousand people go under that wall in a day because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it. About two hundred of them stop at the last line on a quarter morning and cannot finish it.

He watched her stop and not finish and go on. He had worked out at sixty feet and at forty feet how many stop on the afternoon a sheet goes out and how many stop the day after, and he had never found an answer and never found one person who could give him one.

## What The Painter Asked

The man with the wash water straightened up and put his hands on his back and looked at the form and then at Lin Hao's coat, which had a white plate in it with his name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere.

“You are the office at that address,” the man said. It was not a question.

“I am the man with the bucket at that address,” Lin Hao said. “The office is a room with two chairs in it.”

“The division that printed this wants to know what goes in the two at the bottom,” the man said. “They asked us because we put it up. We put it up and we take it down and we have never once been told.”

Lin Hao looked at the two boxes for about nine seconds. They were ruled off a straight edge in the same hand as the quarter above them, and the left one was about half an inch wider than the right one, and neither had ever held anything.

“The left one holds a figure,” he said. “The right one holds a standing. That is what the ruling says, and it has said so since the eighth of October of the first year.”

“And what figure,” the man said. “And what standing.”

That was the question four years had not asked him. He had stood at forty feet and at sixty feet and at thirty feet and had worked out how many people stop at the last line, and nobody in four years had asked him what the boxes at the foot of a repainting form are for, because there is no form in this city for asking what a box is for.

He did not answer it standing at the foot of a wet wall with a bucket in his hand. He said he worked nights at a branch off a third court, eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off, and that the rota page there carries four names and no band and no figure, and that he would come back when the paint was dry.

The painter nodded as if that were an answer, which it was not, and wrung his cloth out again. The paste boy pinned the corner of the form down tighter with his thumb and did not look up.

He went down Cordwain with about six hours before his shift and the drawer shut behind him with a name in it he had put there without asking, and the form blank at the foot of a wall about nine thousand people go under in a day because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it.

The nine boards had gone up that morning in nine corridors with his four lines on them and no name at the top, and about thirty people a day walk past each.

*The first of May, one thousand five hundred and thirty days. The same day as the nine boards and the same afternoon as the drawer, nothing at all since the drawer was shut, one day from the thirtieth of April and ninety-one from the thirtieth of January.*

*The repainting form is up at the foot of the wall with two boxes at the foot of it, both blank, one for a figure and one for a standing. A man who puts the form up has asked what goes in them, and he has not answered yet.*

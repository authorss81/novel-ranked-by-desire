# The Woman Who Reads Everything Twice

Nine days from the eighth of April to this afternoon, and this afternoon is one thousand five hundred and sixteen days off the first of February of the first year. The first of April was one thousand five hundred, and the seventeenth is sixteen days on, and one thousand five hundred and sixteen less one thousand five hundred and seven is nine.

And this afternoon is three hundred and sixty days after the seventeenth of April of the fourth year, which is one thousand one hundred and fifty-six, and three hundred and sixty days is a year.

So it is a year since she opened the second book in the back room of a house in Lamp Street and wrote the first line of it, in her own hand, at about nine in the evening.

## The House In Lamp Street

She kept a lodging house of thirty-one rooms over two floors and a stair that anybody who has used it remembers, and she had taken it from a man who took it off a man, and there is no form for any of that either and she has never asked for one.

Her name is Ilva Vask and she is not a clerk, she is on no roll in this city of anybody at all, and she has never in her life been anybody's employee, and the only list she has ever kept is her own.

She read the sheet four times. She reads everything twice. It is a habit and not a party trick: every docket that comes into that house is read twice before it is entered, and every name is entered twice, and about a hundred and forty of the names in the second book got entered three times because she went back over them.

"You read them twice because you got one wrong," said Lin Hao.

"I got one wrong in the sixth year and a man went out of a room he had paid for to the end of that month and he did not find another one for about nine days, and he came back and told me about it on the ninth day." She put her hand flat on the counter of her own desk, which is a thing she does with her own counter and not with anyone else's. "That is the whole reason and it is not a virtue."

## The Second Book

It had three hundred and something pages and it was not a book anybody could read as a book. It was a list of people who had stayed in that house over about nine years and had not been able to pay what they owed, and whom she had not put out.

Four columns, in her own hand. The name. The street. How long.

And then, from the seventeenth of April of the fourth year, a fifth column she ruled herself with a straight edge off the counter: what the person had done for the house that year and what they had been given for it.

"There is no column for that in any book in this city," she said. "I went looking for about a fortnight and there is no column for it and there is no form and there is no office that keeps one, and I have been a person who puts people in rooms for nine years and I have never once wanted a column for it before."

## Why She Did Not Put It Down

"I have not put it down for a year," she said, "and I have thought about it every day of that year, and the reason is not that there is nowhere."

"Then what is it."

"That a list is a thing you do to people who cannot pay." She turned the second book round and did not open it.

"That is what a list is. In this city a list of names with a street against each one is a thing a man reads when he wants to know who in a street has nothing, and I have read one. Everybody who keeps one reads one. And I sat here for a year with three hundred pages and I could not put it anywhere, because anywhere I put it stops being a thing I did and becomes a thing that is kept."

"Who would keep it."

"I do not know and that is not the point," said Ilva Vask. "The point is that the moment it is in a hand other than mine it is a list. And I have had it a year and about a hundred and forty people in the second book have not been in a list, and I have never asked any of them for anything, and that is the only thing I have."

## Why She Has Decided

"What changed."

"The board," she said.

He had not asked her about the board. She had not seen him since the tenth of January.

"The board at the top of the yard has about a hundred and forty names on it in chalk going down the wall in the yard's own hand, and about nine hundred people read the four lines at the top of it in a fortnight, and about a hundred and forty of them cannot read."

"How do you know you cannot read."

"Because half of them came to me." She took a sheet out of a drawer and put it on her own counter. It was an eight-hundred-copy sheet with a printer's mark in the corner, and it had been folded and unfolded about forty times and the folds had gone through.

"Since the ninth of March people who had put their names at the foot of that board have come to this house and asked me whether I would write them a copy of their own name off the board, because they can pay for a bed and they cannot read chalk. About ninety of them. They stood in that doorway and said their name out loud and I wrote it down and they said thank you and they went."

"Ninety people have said their names to you and you have written them down."

"Ninety, and about forty of them are in my second book, and there are about a hundred and forty on that board, and it is the same hundred and forty people twice over, and I have got it in a drawer in this house in two places."

## The Brother

"My brother is in this," she said, before he asked. "He is not going to be told I said so and he will not bring you anything and he has been at the foot of those steps every evening since the twenty-second of March with a book in his pocket, and he has about a hundred and eleven yeses in it, and I have read that book twice because I read everything twice."

"You have read it."

"I have read it four times," said Ilva Vask. "It is his. It is in the room at the back. And it is the same hundred and forty people again, and about a hundred and eleven of them are in my second book, and his are the ones who can name the day."

She put her hand on the sheet with the folds in it.

"And I am not going to hand it to you and neither is he, and I want to say why before you ask, because about nine people have asked in a fortnight and I am tired of it."

"Go on."

"Because a clerk at a counter may only enter what she is handed," said Ilva Vask. "You have told me that yourself and I believe it, and I have worked out what it means."

It means that the day my book goes through that door it stops being a thing I did. It becomes a thing that is held, and a thing that is held is a thing somebody can ask to see.

"In about four years there will be a room somewhere in this city with a man in a coat who can say *is there a page*, and there will be a page, and it will have a hundred and forty names on it, and somebody who cannot produce a deed will have not proved anything."

"That is the fourth condition."

"That is the fourth condition and I read it on a sheet of paper four times and I worked out in about nine seconds that I was not going to be able to use it against anybody."

## What She Did With It

She picked the sheet up and turned it over.

On the back of it, in her own hand, ruled off the counter with a straight edge, were four columns and a hundred and thirty-nine names.

The name. The street. How long. What was given.

She had done it in one night. She said so, and she said that she had done it in one night on purpose, because it took her the best part of a night and she wanted it to have cost her something, because a thing you spend a night on is not a thing you did in a minute at a counter.

"How are you going to get it there."

"It is not going to be me." She put the sheet down flat on her own counter, which she does, and she did not look down at it.

"It is going to be the woman who keeps the list. She is going to carry it to a building off a third court with no sign on the door, and the address is at the head of a sheet that has gone out at four o'clock every quarter since the first of February of the first year, and every one of the two hundred people a week who read that page has the address in front of them, and I have read it four times."

"She will ask whether she is allowed."

"She will ask you," said Ilva Vask. "And whatever you say to her is going to be true, and it is going to be a sentence about a counter and a pen, and it is not going to stop about a hundred and forty people from being on a piece of paper in a building where there is nobody in the room but a clerk and a man with a bucket, and that is the correct outcome and I have known it since the ninth of March."

## What He Wants

He stood in that room for about eleven minutes and he wanted to say one thing and he could not find a way to say it and there is not one instrument in this city that would take one.

"What do you want me to do," he said.

"Nothing," said Ilva Vask. "And you have said that to about nine hundred people in a yard and to a man at a coal wharf in the last ten weeks and you said it twice both times, and I have known what the true answer is since the ninth of March and I would like it understood that I did not come here to be told it."

"Then why did you come."

"Because you are the only man in this city whose sentence is on the front of that sheet," she said, "and because in about four years there is going to be a room with a magistrate in it, and in that room somebody is going to say *is there a page*, and I have wanted you to have heard a woman say no to that in a house with a stair, in daylight, before it happens, and not after."

She put the sheet in an envelope and put the envelope inside her coat.

"And my brother is going to be furious with me and he has no business being furious and I am not going to sit here and be talked out of it by a man who works nights."

*Seventeenth of April of year five, a house in Lamp Street, about eleven at night. Nine days after the eighth and exactly a year after the seventeenth of April of the fourth year, and a year is three hundred and sixty days and this is three hundred and sixty.*

*A second book, three hundred pages, opened a year ago tonight at about nine, and about a hundred and forty names in it, and four columns and a fifth she ruled herself.*

*And about ninety people cannot read the chalk at the top of that hill and they came to a boarding house and said their names out loud in a doorway and she wrote them down and they said thank you and went.*

*And her brother has about a hundred and eleven in a book in the room at the back and she has read it four times.*

*And she has put a hundred and thirty-nine names on the back of a printed sheet in her own hand in one night on purpose, because a thing you spend a night on is not a thing you did in a minute at a counter.*

*And she is going to hand it to a woman who keeps a list and a woman who keeps a list works for nobody.*

*And she said: I wanted you to have heard a woman say no to that in a house with a stair, in daylight, before it happens, and not after.*

*And I had nothing to say in that room and I have had nothing to say in eleven of them this quarter.*

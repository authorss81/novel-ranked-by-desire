# A Quarter Of Them Are His

He went up to the rooms behind the lane on the twenty-seventh of October of the fifth year at about ten in the morning, after the line had cleared, because Brack had told him the books would be out and he had not been able to stay away.

Six days had gone since the twenty-first. One thousand seven hundred and six less one thousand seven hundred is six, and twenty-seven less twenty-one is six by the month count, and he had worked four nights of the six on the branch rota, eleven at night until half past five, with Nadia Ferrous on the counter and Petra Ilyin reading at the far end of the corridor.

He wanted a number he could hold. That was all he wanted now, and he wanted it badly enough that his hands were tight before he reached the stair.

The rooms were two small ones over a shop with a glass front, and the books were stacked on a table by the window, eleven of them, bound in cloth, with dates on the spines in Brack’s own hand. Brack stood beside them the way a man stands beside something he has carried a long way.

“About eight thousand figures in three years,” Brack said. “About a quarter of them off nothing. About two thousand decided, the rest read. I did the sum twice last night and it came out the same both times.”

Lin Hao put his hand flat on the top book and took it away again.

“Show me a quarter,” he said.

Brack opened the third book from the top and turned to a morning in the second month of the fourth year. Nine figures ran down the leaf in chalk-dust grey ink, and two of the nine were ringed, and a note in the margin said *off nothing, line restless, boy at back.*

“Two that morning,” Brack said. “Some mornings three. Some mornings one. Over three years it runs at about a quarter, and a quarter is the figure I will stand behind if your office asks me for one.”

Lin Hao turned five leaves forward and five leaves back. Every leaf had nine figures and every leaf had two or three ringed, and every ringed one had a reason in the margin in plain words. *Line slow. Man drunk. Rain coming. No time to read.*

He felt a cold admiration that was close to fear. A man who invents and writes down that he invented, morning after morning, for three years, had built a better record than most offices he knew.

“You have been honest about being dishonest for longer than most people are honest,” Lin Hao said.

“I have been exact,” Brack said. “Honest is your office’s word and it has two words in paint nobody can read. Exact is a slate and a book and a date.”

Lin Hao sat down at the table because his legs had gone strange under him. The want in him had become a weight, and the weight was the knowledge that the answer was going to cost exactly what Brack said it would cost and could not be haggled down.

“What happens to the ringed ones when the line pays them,” he said.

“They go into the street the same as the read ones,” Brack said. “A grain is a grain and a morning is a morning. About four of eleven who asked walked away and the rest paid. Nobody who paid came back to say the figure had harmed them, and I have the names of every one who paid and I have asked after about forty of them.”

“You asked after them.”

“I carry a slate in a street where people live,” Brack said. “A man who does that and does not ask after them is a landlord.”

Lin Hao laughed, short, and then stopped, because the laugh had anger in it and the anger was at himself. He had spent four years telling himself that the office was the only honest instrument in Ostara, and it had never been written anywhere, and it had been true until that table.

He understood it all at once.

He would have to give that away.

“You will have your answer before the quarter goes out,” he said. “I am not giving it on a stair and I am not giving it in a lane. It will be in the hand of the office itself, on the standing sheet, once a quarter, with your name under it, or it will not be given at all.”

Brack closed the book.

“I will wait for the quarter,” he said. “I have waited since April of the third year and I can wait thirty-three days more, because thirty less twenty-seven is three and thirty in November with thirty in December is sixty-three, and three with sixty is sixty-three.”

“Sixty-three,” Lin Hao said. “One thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and six is sixty-three.”

## The Rota Holds

He worked the next two nights on the branch rota and told none of the three women what the books held, because a man on a rota does not say out loud what he is carrying, and all three knew it anyway.

Nadia Ferrous left a copy of the standing sheet on the counter with the foot turned up, so the two empty spaces showed. Petra Ilyin read at the far end and did not look up, and Isolde Marchetti mopped the stair without being asked, and the building held them the way a cold room holds people who know its rules.

On the second night a man from a division came to the counter at about one in the morning and asked whether the office would write him a paper saying his rent was paid, and Nadia told him the office writes nothing for anybody except what it is given, and the man went away angry, and Lin Hao watched that anger and felt his own future in it.

A paper that says one man invents will be used by every man who wants a paper. That was plain. It was also unavoidable, because refusal had become a worse lie than admission.

He stood on the landing at about four in the morning with the mop in his hand and understood that the reckless part of him had already decided, and that the rest of him was only waiting for the ward.

He went down the stair and out into Cordwain with the eleven books behind him and the lane ahead of him, and the wall stood at the end of it with two words in paint nobody could read and a figure in chalk everybody could, and for the first time in five years he saw both of them as one thing.

*The twenty-seventh of October, one thousand seven hundred and six days. Less one thousand seven hundred, the twenty-first, is six. Twenty-seven less twenty-one is six by the month count.*

*Less the sixteenth, one thousand six hundred and ninety-five, is eleven. Less the thirtieth of September, one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine, is twenty-seven. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and six is sixty-three.*

# The Book Comes Off The Shelf

Two flights down, and the cold down there is worse than the cold a landing above it, and it has been worse for every year this city has had a room with a lamp in it.

One day from the thirtieth of December to this morning, and the thirtieth of December was the last day of that year.

Four years is three hundred and sixty days multiplied by four, which is one thousand four hundred and forty, and the first of February of year five is one thousand four hundred and forty, and this morning is one thousand four hundred and ten, and thirty days off the first of February is the thirty of January and there is no thirty-first.

Three hundred and sixty days multiplied by three is a thousand and eighty, and a year runs from the first of February to the thirtieth of January, and the thirty days of January that close it are three hundred and thirty, and a thousand and eighty and three hundred and thirty is one thousand four hundred and ten, which is this morning.

He had nothing to do on the first of January. He had four nights and three off in every seven and this was the first of the three and there was not one thing in any of it that wanted doing.

## What The Room Is

A lamp on a stand, a table, one chair, a shelf, a chain, and the fifth book.

The lamp costs a quarter of the quarter and the chair costs the other three quarters, so a man stands. He had been standing in that room four times in two years and he had not been told to and had worked it out.

There is a strap across the fifth book and a nib on a string and an ink that is uncorked and re-corked and uncorked again in the course of a morning.

At the far table there is a woman with a folder in front of her. She had the folder when he came in and she had the folder when he went out.

There is no window and there never has been one, and the room is colder than the stair above it at every hour of every day of every year, and a keeper works in it four times a quarter for thirty years and has never once been asked whether she would rather be somewhere else.

He has been down two flights four times and he could not tell you now what the temperature in that room is, and he has thought about it perhaps twice.

She has been at that far table on and off for about three years and nobody in that room has ever asked her anything in any of it. He had been down two flights four times and had not asked her on any of them and had thought about not asking her for about nine seconds each time.

## What Went Out Yesterday

One woman at a public desk in Cordwain had read the whole of it yesterday afternoon, top to bottom, twice, and had said out loud to a man beside her that it was the address of the thing and not a count of anything.

She was right. About four in every thousand of the two hundred people a week stop at the bottom of that page, and he has no way of finding out which four and no instrument that would let him ask.

The figure on it has not moved through two whole quarters now, and a quarter is three months of thirty days and that is ninety. Below the figure, about four hundred and nineteen lines, one of which has his name at the head of it.

And in the second column, one line, his, four lines long.

And underneath everything, the office's own sentence, four words longer than that sentence is supposed to be, a sentence he did not write and the only thing on the page that tells anybody what the page is.

## The Rule About The First Block

There is a rule about the first block of a year and she does not tell it to him because he is not a person it is told to.

The last block of a year is written with the room open and anybody standing in it may read over her shoulder while it is being written. She is on her feet for that one and not for the other three.

The first block of a year is written and closed and chained. The book goes on the shelf at a quarter to five and it comes off the shelf on the first of January and it may be read on the second, by one person.

That is the whole of it. It is not a courtesy. A keeper who is on her feet in a room with a page open is a keeper who can be spoken to, and what she is certifying on that page is what a premises is made of, and a sentence spoken into a room while a page is open belongs to the room.

He had known the rule for four years and had never once had it apply to him, because he had never had anything to read in that room.

He had one.

## What She Wrote

She wrote the figure, which is eight, and under it six words in the column.

*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*

Then her own name under them, in her own division, in her own hand. She has never once left that off.

Thirty years is three hundred and sixty days multiplied by ten. Four times a year in each of them. Thirty multiplied by four is a hundred and twenty, and there is a hundred and twenty of these written in one hand somewhere in this city, and nine in ten of them are in rooms that have not changed.

"I do not keep the count," she said, as she said it every quarter. "You keep the count. It is the wrong thing for me to keep."

The composition of premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight goes in the fourth block of every quarter and has gone in since before she came, and it has had no certificate behind it since the fourteenth of March of the second year, and there is no form for a certificate and there is not going to be one.

## Two Hours In A Cold Room

He stood in that room for about two hours.

What he wanted, in order, was to be allowed to read the page; to be allowed to ask her what was on it; and to be told.

None of the three was available to him. Not because she was refusing him — she had not looked up from the page once in two hours — but because there was no arrangement in this city, in this building or in this room, by which a man who is not a keeper gets to read a keeper's page on the day the keeper writes it.

He had gone down two flights with nothing in his coat. He had nothing to bring her. And the sentence that governs him says the only person this office may ask is him.

He had been unable to ask a question three times in four years. Twice it had been a corridor with a page open on a table and a woman who had put three questions to him on the sixth of March of the first year and had never had one of them answered. And once it had been a chair and a lamp and a woman who had known since about the ninth of July and said nothing to anybody at all.

This was the first time it had been by arrangement.

At about the middle of the second hour he took the notebook out of his coat and put it on the table on the side away from her, and he wrote nine or ten lines, and he read them back, and he did not ask her one question about any of them, and she did not look at the page.

He wrote that down too: *I sat in this room with a book open and a woman behind it and I wrote in my own book about what I was going to do about a thing that is on the page in front of me, and I did not ask her a single question, and she did not offer me one, and neither of us broke anything.*

It is the only arrangement either of them has ever had that worked.

## What She Did At The End

She squared the book, which is a thing she does, and she put it on the shelf, and she took the nib off the string and uncorked the ink and drew the chain across it, and the chain went into a keeper's lock and not into any other kind.

"It is a quarter to five," said Hanneke Ruis. "It goes on now and it comes off tomorrow and one person reads it tomorrow and it is not you."

"Then who."

"That is not a thing you get to ask me," she said, "and you knew that when you came down the stair, and you came down the stair anyway, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about you this afternoon."

The woman at the far table had a folder in front of her and put the folder under her arm and went out, and nobody in the room asked her anything, and it was the fifth occasion on which nothing in that room had asked that woman anything.

He went up the two flights. The air on the first of them was warmer than the air on the second and it always is.

*First of January of year five, the counting room, two flights under a sales floor. One thousand four hundred and ten days off the first of February and one day after the thirtieth of December, which was the last day of the year before.*

*Eight hundred and forty-one went out yesterday afternoon for the third quarter running, with one line in the second column that is mine, and a sentence underneath it which I did not write and which is the only one on the page that says what the page is.*

*And I stood in a cold room for two hours today and asked a woman nothing, for the third time in four years, and this time I had arranged it myself.*

*She has written her block and chained it and told me the time out loud and she has not told me one word about what is on it, and I go up those stairs at a quarter to five and I do not come down again until tomorrow.*

# A Yard Decides On Its Own

Three days from the tenth of January to this morning, and this morning is one thousand four hundred and twenty-two days off the first of February of the first year and the tenth was one thousand four hundred and nineteen.

The man who shuts doors off the arcade came into that branch at about one in the morning because the corridor is warm and because he has been going up the hill at about eleven for eleven years and he had come down.

He has shut the shutters of nine buildings off that arcade for eleven years and he does them in the order he has done them in for eleven years, and he had done the Vestry last for about four of those years, which was not the order.

## Thirteen Days

"It went up on the evening of the thirtieth," he said. "The same evening. There was about four hundred in the yard and a woman with a piece of slate off a shutter and a bit of chalk and she did it in about nine minutes and about thirty people watched her do it and about nine of them said the words out loud as she wrote them."

"How long has it been up."

He worked it out on his fingers, slowly, the way a man does a sum he has to be sure of.

"Thirteen days," he said. "Thirtyth of December to tonight. Thirteen."

## The Two Words

"You have written two words under it," said Lin Hao.

"Carried by."

"Where did you get them."

The man looked at the floor of the corridor for a while.

"I do not know where I got it. My father said it and his father said it. It is what you call out to a man carrying something, like *mind yourself*. I have said it every night for eleven years at the foot of those steps to about nine hundred people and I have never once thought about the two words and I have never once looked up afterwards."

"And on the ninth of January a woman up there with a slate read the four lines out to the yard, and when she came to the end of the fourth one somebody in the crowd said *carried by* out loud, the way you say it, and about four hundred of us said it together, and I said it with them like a man says a thing."

He shifted the strap of his bag.

"That is where I got it. From four hundred people. And I have not told anybody that and you are the first one I have said it to and I am saying it to you because you are a rank one and you are in here every night."

## The New Word

They were in the little room at the end of the ground-floor corridor where the branch keeps the day's returns, which is not his room and which has a radiator in it that works, and it was about a quarter to two in the morning and the two of them had been in there about half an hour.

"Have they decided it is a rule," he said.

"They decided it on the second night and they took four minutes about it." The man turned his cap over in his hands. "I am on that hill every night and I can tell you exactly what happened, and nobody asked me to, and nobody up there knows I have been saying those two words at them for eleven years."

"The first night it was a notice. A thing on a board that said four lines and where they came from. That is a notice. Everybody knew it was a notice."

"And the second night a woman stood on the step at the yard end and read them out loud in the yard's own words."

"And then she said — and I remember it exactly, because it was the same words a woman in a chapel used to say about nine years ago — she said: this is not what he wants to know. He wants to know what it is for. And the yard is going to use it."

"And that is it. That is the whole of it. Four minutes and about nine hundred people agreed and the yard has had a rule since the second of January."

He put the cap back on.

"It is not a quotation any more," he said. "There is a word they have started using and it is not *owed* and it is not *useful* and it is not *looking*."

"What is it."

"*Asked for*," said the man who shuts doors. "A woman said on the steps that anybody who wanted to be asked for could put their name down and about ninety of them did it before dark and there were more on the ninth and more again last night."

## What That Means Here

A question on a pavement is now an act of this office.

He had that in his own writing in his own hand, written at half past four in the morning of the fifth of October of the fourth year on four inches of stone at the foot of the ranks wall, which a woman with a short-handled broom cleans last because nobody spits under a wall.

*If I asked her there the answer is the office's. It goes in the book. And a thing in that book is a thing in that book for ever.*

So there is a line in the second column of a book off a third court with his name at the bottom of it.

Everything he asks this month is going to be entered somewhere. He cannot ask a question in a corridor off Cordwain without it being a thing this office did, and the only instrument in the building is a pen, and he is the whole of it.

He had not asked one.

## The Thing At The Foot Of The Board

"There is an address at the foot of it," said the man who shuts doors. "Nineteen in a yard, four off an arcade, and that is the one they are bringing them to. They are not bringing them to the Vestry and nobody has told the Vestry."

"How many."

"About a hundred since the ninth. They put it down at the bottom of the step under the board and they stand back from it."

"What are they bringing."

"The sort of thing you bring," he said. "I have not looked. I am not going to look. There were women up there on the ninth who had the two words in their mouths standing in a queue behind about nine other people, and that is not a thing you stand in a queue behind without being asked for."

He got his bag up off the floor.

"That is all I have got and I have said all of it and I have said it in here and not up there, because I am the man that says it and I am not going to be anything else this month."

Then he stopped in the doorway and turned round, because there was one thing he had been told by about nine different people on that hill in eleven years and had never once believed.

"Does he actually come," said the man who shuts doors. "The one on the wall. Does a person like that ever come up."

He considered it, standing at the end of a corridor off a third court at about a quarter to two in the morning with the returns rack at his back.

"I have no idea," he said. "I do not know anybody. That is not a thing I am saying to be modest, it is the truest sentence I have said in about four years, and I have said nothing at all for eleven years to about nine hundred people and you are the first one who has asked me a question about it."

He went out and the door of the returns room swung a long way before it stopped.

He stood and listened to it swing, and thought about a man who has made himself the only person an office can ask and has never once been able to ask anybody anything at all.

## The First List Made For Nothing

At about half past three that morning a woman came to the office of that building off a third court, which has no sign on the door and is not on any of the pages, and asked for a sheet of paper and a pencil.

She had been keeping a list since the second of January. She had eighty-one names on it and what each person had brought and the evening they brought it, in her own hand, and it was a good hand, and she had ruled it herself with the straight edge off the counter.

She did not give it to him. She asked whether it was going in the Register and he said that it was not, because a person may only enter what she is given and there was nothing on the desk to give her.

She said she had not brought it to be put in the Register. She said the yard had asked people to put their names down at the foot of a board and she was writing them down because there was about ninety names in a yard and about nine hundred people in a yard and no paper, and because a name that is written down is a name and a name that is not written down is a rumour.

She asked him one thing and it was the only thing she asked in four days.

"Am I allowed."

He said yes, and he was not the person who could have said no, and he told her so, and she wrote that on the bottom of it as well, with the date, and her own name against it.

About a hundred people are in it. Not one of them can bring this office a deed. The second condition keeps out anybody who cannot name the day. The third wants a second person who can be named, and there is not one in this city who has been found.

And the fourth condition said the thing it describes was not on a page anywhere in this city, and it has been on one at a public desk since the thirtieth of December, and it is now in four-inch chalk at the top of a hill and on a notice board in a corridor off a third court, and four conditions that were written to keep unrecorded people inside an office have become a rule with a queue at the bottom of it.

*The first list in four years that anybody has made for nothing.*

*Thirteenth of January of year five. Three days from the tenth. The branch, about half past three in the morning.*

*A board up thirteen days. A yard decided on the second night that it was a rule and not a notice, and the word is asked for, and about a hundred people have carried something to the foot of it and stood back from it.*

*And a woman who keeps a list came to this office with a sheet she ruled herself and eighty-one names on it in her own good hand and asked me whether she was allowed, and I said yes, and I am not the person who could have said no.*

# About Two Hundred People A Week

Five days from the second of January to tonight, and this evening is one thousand four hundred and sixteen days off the first of February of the first year and the second of January is one thousand four hundred and eleven, and five off one is six and sixteen is a thousand four hundred and sixteen.

He was inside the branch at about ten to eleven with a bucket under his arm that had nothing in it, and the plate on his coat is white with his name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere.

Eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off. The page that says so is the size of a folded hand and it carries four names on it, and no band and no figure and no note against any of them of what they are owed.

## Thirty A Day

About thirty people a day walk past the notice board in that corridor, and thirty is one for every day of the month, and he has never counted them and is not going to.

About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read a standing sheet somewhere. That has been about two hundred for four years and the number has never once been the number of people who agreed with what was on it.

He works eleven at night until half past five. So he is awake for the people who read a sheet at seven in the evening and asleep for the people who read it at seven in the morning, and about five of the two hundred a week have ever come through this door.

## Three Lines On A Board

There was a sheet of paper on the notice board at the head of the corridor with four lines on it and a bit of drawing pin through the top left corner, and it was not his paper and it was not the office's paper and it had been cut off something larger with a blade, because one edge was straight and three were not.

He read it standing up on the way in.

Three lines.

Not four. Four lines were on the sheet of the thirtieth of December in a public desk in Cordwain, under an address, under a figure, under two columns, and about two hundred people a week have read it since. Somebody had taken that page to a lamp somewhere and copied three of the four lines off it and brought the copy here and put a drawing pin through it.

The three that were there were the first, the second and the third.

The one that was missing said that the thing it describes was not on a page anywhere in this city.

He read it three times standing in the corridor at about a quarter to eleven at night in a building where thirty people a day walk past that board, and what he worked out on the third reading was the hand.

He could not name a person. There are perhaps nine people in this city whose handwriting he knows well enough to be wrong about, and it was none of them, and the letters were even and the lines were straight in a way nobody in this building writes.

He stood and looked at it for about a minute and a half and then went and did the first floor.

There is no instrument in this building that is not a pen, and a pen is not a thing that can be used on a sheet of paper pinned to a corridor wall, and the person who put it up will not be asked about it by anybody in this building tonight.

He did not go and take it down either, because taking it down would be the act of a man who has been told about something by a page, and there are about two hundred people a week in Cordwain who will do the same thing to a page if somebody in an office shows them that it can be done.

## The Counter

Nadia Ferrous was on the counter because it was one of her two nights and she came off at about three, and she had been there since eleven, and she did the thing she does with her hands at the counter which is to put them flat and leave them there.

"You have read it," she said.

"I have read a copy of three lines of it."

"Then that is the one you have read," said Nadia Ferrous, "and I did not put it up and I am not going to tell you who did, because I do not know and because I would not, and because I have been in this building for about four years and I have never once put anything on that board."

"Somebody did it with a blade."

"Somebody did it with a blade and a lamp and about nine minutes," she said. "I know that because I have watched four people do it this week to things and there is a way people copy things and it is not the way anybody in this building writes."

## The First Kind Thing

Isolde Marchetti came in at about one from the bellhouse stage, went to the far end of the corridor, sat down and read there until about four. She has not said in three years what she is reading and nobody in that building is going to ask.

At about half past three she spoke without looking up, from about thirty feet, in the voice she uses in a kitchen from behind a stove.

"You have got a thing on the notice board."

"I have."

"It has got three lines on it."

"It has."

"That is not what I want to say and I am going to say it anyway, because I have had the shift from one to four and about nine hundred people have walked past that office and not one of them came in here for anything at all."

She turned a page.

"The second column is the one I have been looking at for three years. It says *carried by*. It takes the name of the person who carries something, and for three years there has not been one thing in it in this building or in any other, and about nine hundred people a week walk past it."

"And in the middle of October there was one line in it, and nothing about that line says you carried anything. It says you decided where something went. That is a different sentence and a worse one to have on a page, and it is the only sentence in that book this month that anybody in this city will be able to say out loud without somebody asking them a question about it."

She turned another page.

"That is not a compliment and I am not saying it is one. It is the first useful thing I have heard about that column, and it has been there since the middle of October, and about four weeks of this quarter have gone past with nobody stopping at it."

He did not go and stand at the end of the corridor. He went and did the second floor and the stairwell and the bin room.

He did not thank her and he did not try to, because there was nothing in it for him to be thanked for, and because she had not said a word about him at all — she had said a word about a column, on a shift, at thirty feet, to a room, and it was the first thing anybody in this building had said about the sheet that went out on the thirtieth, and it had been a kind thing.

## On The Way Out

At half past five he came down the corridor with the mop in the bucket and the bin room open behind him and he stopped at the notice board, because there is a thing you do not do at ten to eleven in the evening and you do do at half past five, which is to look at it properly.

Three lines.

*It changed what a person held.*

*It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name.*

*Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough that a person reading this in four years would know which person.*

He could not do one single thing about it.

There is no form in this city for asking that a sheet be taken down, because a sheet on a wall in a corridor of a building is not a document. There is no form for asking a person to copy four lines instead of three, and there is no instrument in this building that is not a pen.

And that was not the part that stopped him.

The part that stopped him was that the three lines on that board are a rule, and that the rule is in about two hundred people's hands and about a yard on a hill.

The first three are all about the person doing the deed. The fourth is the only one that was ever about anybody else, and it is the one that got left off.

There is no version of his four conditions in this city tonight that has it in.

Being readable is not the same as being agreed with, and he had known that for four years, and the difference between the two things turned out to be one line of a sentence, cut off with a blade and pinned up at the head of a corridor by a person who did not leave their name on it, in a building where about thirty people a day walk past and about five of them come in.

*Seventh of January, the fifth night since the counting room, the corridor at the branch.*

*Five days since the counting room and a woman who asked me a question she had carried for eleven years and I could not answer it.*

*The sheet has been out for five days and there is a copy of three lines of it in my own corridor with a drawing pin in it, and the missing one is the only one of the four that was ever about anybody except the man doing it.*

*There is nothing in this city I can do about it and there is nothing in this city I could have done about it in the first place, which is the whole of what the definition was.*

# Nine Divisions And A Printer

Ten days from the ninth of March to this evening, and this evening is one thousand four hundred and eighty-eight days off the first of February of the first year. The ninth of March was one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight, and one thousand four hundred and eighty-eight less one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight is ten.

And the fourth of February was one thousand four hundred and forty-three, and forty-five days is twenty-four to the last day of February, and three more to the first of March, because the first of March is the day after the thirtieth of February and not a day of it, and then eighteen days of this month. Twenty-four and three and eighteen is forty-five.

He went down Cordwain at about four in the afternoon on the nineteenth of March to a shop with a glass front and about nine people in it and stopped a compositor on his way out, which he does not have any right to do, and which he did.

## What A Compositor Is

The shop does handbills and trade circulars and the weekly returns for nine divisions of the Bureau, and the man who sets the type is called Stenner and has been setting type for nineteen years and could not read a newspaper properly if one was put in front of him at nine at night.

He had been standing at a stone a foot high with a stick in his hand reading the same four lines upside down for about four minutes.

"I have set these four lines four hundred times," he said.

"That is Ashby's demand."

"That is the landlord's demand and I set it on the twenty-eighth of January and delivered it on the second of February, and I did not think about one line of it, because you do not think about a line you have been handed." He put the stick down. "You think about them when you have set them twice and the metal is still warm."

"You set them twice."

"I set them again on the ninth of March for a man with a plate he wanted cheap and I read them the whole way through with the stick in my hand the first time, because by the ninth of March about four people in this district had come in and told me a thing about them and I wanted to know what they were talking about."

## The Fourth One

"They are a gate," said Stenner. "That is all they are. I have been setting them since the twenty-eighth of January and I did not know that until the ninth of March, and I have been a compositor for nineteen years and I can tell you what a gate looks like in metal."

"They are four conditions."

"They are a gate and a list. Those are two different things and a man who prints them together is printing a thing that says *here is the door, here is what you must be holding to get through it*, and every person in this city who reads those four lines is going to try to satisfy the list first and never get to the door." He picked the stick up again and put it down.

"The fourth one is the door. The other three are the list. And you cannot get through a door by holding the list."

He said it entirely without any idea that he was saying anything that had cost anybody anything.

"Who told you that."

"Nobody. I set type. It is the only thing I have ever been able to do and I do it about nine hours a day and most of what I know I know because it is in front of me upside down."

## Four Hundred And One Price

"What I want to talk to you about is the plate," he said.

"You gave four hundred to Ashby and charged him the price of one hundred."

"I charged him the price of one because the paper is the money in a job like that and the plate is mine. A plate is not a paper."

"I have one plate and it has those four lines on it in the same type as about nine thousand demands and findings and letters to landlords that this shop has set since the fourth of February."

He put both hands flat on the stone and left them there, which is what a man does with his hands about nine seconds before he is told he is wrong and is not.

"I can print four hundred of anything on it and I can print four hundred again the next night, and the price is the same at four hundred as it is at four. That is not a trick and I am not a careful man. I am a man with one plate and a press."

"And nobody has asked you to print them again."

"About nine people have asked me to print them again. I have been counting since the ninth of March and it is nine, and one of them asked for four and gave me the money for one, and I took it."

## The Plate Is Not His

"That is the answer then."

"No, that is the problem," said Aurel Stenner. "I am not going to print them for nothing and I am not going to print them without asking the man who wrote them, and there is no way in this city for me to ask the man who wrote them, and I have been standing at this stone for about four minutes working out what to do about it, and you are the man from the office and you have been standing in my shop for about nine of them."

"You have his name on the plate."

"I have a name on the plate because I put it there in February off a landlord's copy. That name is worth nothing to me and it is worth a great deal to about four hundred people in this district who cannot write their own deed down and have to have somebody else hold it for them, and if the plate goes out with a name on it then those four hundred people are sending their paper to a name instead of sending it nowhere, and that is worse."

He said it the way he said everything, as though it were a fact about metal.

"Four hundred people who cannot write their own deed down."

"Whatever they are. I do not know what they are. I set type." He stopped. "That is the whole of my objection and I am not going to put a better one on it."

## What He Wants

"You said you had a question," said Lin Hao. "You have had it for four minutes and you have not asked it."

"I have," said Stenner. "Here it is, and I am asking it of the man from the office and not of the man in the coat, because the man in the coat cannot answer it and you have been here for nine minutes and you have not told me anything either."

"Ask it."

"Does the office want them printed?"

"No."

"Then who wants them printed." He turned the stone about ninety degrees. "That is the whole of it and it is a nine-second question and you have not answered it, and you have been in this shop for about nine minutes, and I have watched you work out three different ways to say something that is not an answer."

## The Answer

"Nine divisions in this city," said Lin Hao. "Nine divisions went out on the thirtieth of March of the fourth year and were finished on the thirtieth of June, and about nine hundred doors."

"The returns for those nine divisions come to this shop every week and they go out at four o'clock on a fixed morning, and the plate they go on is the plate and your presses are the presses. If the four lines go on instead of them for four nights then nine divisions of this city's Bureau do not get their returns, and that is not a thing I may decide."

"That is not a decision, that is an excuse with a date on it," said Stenner. "You have had four days to think of it. I can tell because you have not said the word plate for four minutes."

"You are right. It is the second thing I have thought of. The first one was worse."

"Go on."

"The first one was that I wanted to see the plate," said Lin Hao.

The compositor put the stick down and did not pick it up again for about nine seconds.

"That is more honest than anything that has happened in this shop this month," he said. "Well. Then that is the answer to my question and it took you ten minutes and it is the first time anybody has answered one of mine straight in nineteen years."

## Four Nights

He did not print them that week. He printed them on the four nights after, between the last of the trade circulars and the middle of the night, because the plate is his and the paper is the money and the paper came in on the seventh, and the nine divisions' returns went out at four on the fixed morning with a plate that had about nine thousand other things on it.

Four hundred on the first night. Four hundred on the second. And then there was not enough paper for a third, because the paper came in on the seventh of February and what is left of it goes to the trades.

He wrote on the first of them, at the top, in the same typeface and the same weight as the four lines and in the same place the landlord had put the address of the premises: nothing at all.

He had taken the name off.

"You did not ask me about that," said Lin Hao.

"I took it off because a sheet with a name at the top of it is an advertisement and a sheet without one is a rule," said Aurel Stenner.

"I have been a compositor for nineteen years and I have set about nine thousand advertisements in this city and I know exactly what a name at the top of a sheet does. It makes a person stop and decide whether the thing is about them. And I do not want anybody stopping. I want four hundred people to be able to pin a thing like that up in a corridor and have nobody in the corridor stop."

## What Is On It Now

There were about eight hundred of them in the city by the twentieth of March and about eight hundred people had read one by the twenty-fourth, and about one in four of them had never seen the four lines before, because the four lines had been on a shutter in four-inch chalk since the thirtieth of December and about half the people in that district cannot read chalk at forty feet from a ground.

And the fourth of the four lines was on all of them.

"There is a copy in your own corridor with a drawing pin through the corner of it," said Stenner, "and somebody cut the last one off it with a blade, and I have had the whole thing in the metal since the twenty-eighth of January and the last one has been in there the entire time."

"Somebody took it off on purpose."

"Somebody took it off because it is the one that does the work and it does not read like the other three." He set the stone about half an inch back where it had been.

"The other three are things you can do. The fourth one is a thing that is true or is not true, and it is not on a page anywhere in this city, and a man who cannot show a page has not proved anything. I have set that sentence eight hundred times this month and I did not write it and I have never been able to work out who wants it printed and why it is doing so much more than the other three."

## The Ninth Division

They went out into the district in bundles, and they went into three shops and two tea rooms and the back of a tram shelter and onto a shutter at the top of a hill where somebody put one up over the chalk, which is not a copy of the chalk and is not by the same hand.

On the morning of the twenty-second of March Stenner counted, because he counts, and he told Lin Hao the figure in the doorway at about eleven.

"There are nine divisions of the Bureau in this city," he said. "Every one of them has a board in a corridor where they put up returns and notices and things a man has left about the weather. Every one of those nine boards has about thirty people a day walking past it, and there is no form in this city that says a board in a corridor has to have a name at the top of it, and nobody has ever asked me for one for a notice."

"Somebody will."

"Somebody will," said Aurel Stenner, "and I have one plate, and I am going to be able to say yes to all nine of them in about nine minutes, and there is not one thing in this city that a man with a bucket and a plate with no figure cut into it can do about that, and you have been in my shop twice now and you have not told me once that there is."

*Twenty-second of March of year five, in the doorway of a printer's off Cordwain, about eleven in the morning. The chapter is the nineteenth and I was there on the nineteenth and this is the twenty-second and I have been there three times in four days.*

*About eight hundred of them and about eight hundred people have read one and one in four had never seen the four lines before, because half the people in that district cannot read chalk at forty feet from a ground.*

*And the fourth line is on all of them. Somebody cut it off my own corridor with a blade and it has been in the metal since the twenty-eighth of January.*

*And a man who sets type for nineteen years read the four lines upside down on the ninth of March and worked out that the fourth one is the door and the other three are the list, and nobody asked him.*

*And nine divisions of the Bureau have a board in a corridor each, and I am not able to say no to any of them.*

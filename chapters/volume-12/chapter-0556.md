# Four Lines On A Yard Board

Three days from the seventh of January to the tenth, and this afternoon is one thousand four hundred and nineteen days off the first of February of the first year and the seventh is one thousand four hundred and sixteen, and three off six is sixteen and a thousand is nineteen.

Nadia Ferrous had told him at about a quarter to three on the counter four days ago that there was a board at the yard end with his lines on it, and had said it once and had gone back to her hands, and he had not asked her a second question about it because there was no second question about it that was not a request.

He went up Cordwain at about two in the afternoon on the tenth of January and turned in at the end and stopped where the ground begins to go up.

About eleven thousand people were in that yard and it was about four thousand of them when a woman first sat at a table and promised them a column. She has never told him how the other seven thousand arrived and he has never asked.

The light was the light it gets off the ridge in the second week of January, which is not much light, and the whole of it had the particular flat look of a place where about eleven thousand people have agreed about a thing without a meeting.

He had seen it four times in his life. The first was in the third year, the second was in the fourth, the third was in the fourth again, and the fourth was ten minutes ago, and every single time it was a place where about nine hundred people were standing a long way off from a building waiting to find out what a woman said.

They were not waiting now. That was the thing he had not been ready for.

There was nowhere in that yard for a person to stand and watch. Everybody in it was doing something.

## What Is On It

A shutter, whitewashed at some point in a decade nobody in the yard can name, propped against the wall that backs on to the Vestry, with a hand that came up fast and did not stop to correct itself.

Four lines in about four-inch chalk.

He read them at forty feet on the thirtieth of December and he read them again now from about ninety, and they were the same four lines in the same order, and they were the four he drafted from the ninth of March to the nineteenth of April of the second year and read out at a kitchen off Ferrier Street on the nineteenth of July, and that a woman at a counter off a fourth court put in a box after.

And above them, in the same chalk, in a smaller hand that was not the yard's and was not his:

*A register off a third court. Printed by the Bureau for the public. Fourth quarter of the fourth year, the thirtieth of December.*

The notice is correct.

It is correct in every part of it, including the part about the fourth quarter and the thirtieth of December, and it was not written by anybody in that yard, and he could not see anybody who would have written it, and it does not matter.

A notice that says where four lines came from is not an accusation and is not a claim. It is a true sentence that a person wrote at the top of a true sentence so that people who came after would know which one they were looking at.

He had spent four years wanting a city that could read the office's hand.

## What It Is Doing

The yard is right that those four lines are the rule.

They are not a description. They are not a complaint and they are not a sentence anybody made about anybody. They are a test, and a test is a thing you apply, and about eleven thousand people were applying it on the ground and on the steps and for two hundred and thirty yards either side of this one.

And there was a second copy of three of them on a notice board in a corridor off a third court with a drawing pin through it that he reads every night on his way in, and three of them is a rule too, and there are people in this city who are now working from the three.

A definition that took in a street and a loaf of bread was supposed to be too narrow. It is not too narrow. It is exactly the right size for one room and one counter and one man who may ask one question.

It is the wrong size for a yard.

## The Reason He Did Not Go Up

The woman at the stall at the yard end had known him since the eighteenth of October of the first year and she was not packing anything away in January, and she watched him stand there for about four minutes and then she came out from behind the trestle.

"You are going up," she said.

"No."

"You have been up that hill about nine times in four years and every one of them was somebody else's afternoon."

"That is not why."

"Then it is the door." She nodded at the Vestry. "She is in there and she has not been at the front of them since the first of October of the third year and she is not going to be today either, and I am not going to tell a woman like that what to say to eleven thousand people about her own yard."

"I have not come up here for her."

"Then say the reason out loud," said the woman at the stall, "because you have it and you are standing on my side of the street with it."

He said it out loud, because it was cheaper that way than carrying it up the hill and putting it down somewhere.

"The last time I went up this hill uninvited I sat in a room with two chairs in it and said that I would not take another man's two words off a slate for standing on the wrong surface. I said it about the wall and about the man with the chalk and about two words in a hand a city cannot read, and I was right about every part of it. It is the only sentence I have ever written down that I would put my name to twice."

"And a man who has said that does not get to arrive at a yard and tell a yard what to do with a board. He cannot un-say it."

"If I go up those steps and I stand in front of about eleven thousand people and tell them what those four lines are for, then I am a man who said a thing about that wall four years ago and has come to tell them a different thing about it. Every person on those steps would hear both, and they would be right to hear both."

The woman at the stall put her hands on the trestle.

"So you are going to stand down there."

"I am going to stand down here and read it and go to work."

"That is the first honest thing anybody from that office has said up this hill in about four years," she said, "and it is worth exactly nothing, and you are going to find out how much it is worth over the next month and I am sorry for you."

## The Word Under The Fourth Line

He read the four lines a fourth time from about sixty feet and then he read what was under them, which he had not seen on the thirtieth because he had been at forty and there had been a crowd and he had not looked at the bottom of the board.

Somebody had come along with chalk and written one line under the fourth of them, in the yard's own hand, the same fast hand, smaller than the four, and the same chalk.

*carried by*

Two words and no dash.

He stood and looked at it for a while.

Those two words were on the foot of a wall in this city since the eighth of October of the first year, at the height of a name on a plate, in the office's own hand.

About nine thousand people go under that wall in a day and have gone under it every day since, and not one of them can read it, and a boy with a paste brush has put paint over the top of it about once a month for four years and it has never taken it.

Nobody in that yard had ever seen it.

Nobody in that yard could have invented it. It was not a phrase anybody says, it is not a word in any trade in this city, and it is not what the wall says, because the wall says it with a dash in the middle and in the office's hand and about twice the size of what is on this shutter.

Somebody carried it up a hill.

He did not find out who for another three days. When he did, it was from a man who has shut nine buildings off that arcade at eleven at night for eleven years, who does them in an order he does not change, and who had done the Vestry last for about four of those years, which was not the order.

That man had not known what he was carrying. He had been saying the same two words at people in a yard every night since before there was a board there, and he had not once looked up afterwards.

He went down the hill at about ten to three and went to work at eleven.

*Tenth of January of year five, the foot of the yard, about ten to three. Three days from the seventh.*

*Four lines in four-inch chalk on a shutter and a true notice at the top of them saying whose they are, and about eleven thousand people applying a rule that was written in one room for one counter.*

*And under the fourth line somebody has written two words off the bottom of a wall that nine thousand people a day cannot read, and carried them up a hill in their mouth without knowing it, and I stood at sixty feet and read it.*

*And I did not go up the hill and I said out loud on the street why I was not going to, to a woman who has known me since the eighteenth of October of the first year.*

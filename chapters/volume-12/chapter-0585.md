# The Woman Who Was Not Told

He went to Lamp Street on the twenty-first of September at about half past ten in the morning with the notebook in his coat.

There is one person in this city who put a hundred and thirty-nine names on the back of a printed sheet in her own hand in one night and could not read a single line of the paper she wrote them on, and the first name on that sheet had been entered in his own hand in a column headed *carried by*, against himself, on the first of May, and it goes out on the thirtieth at four.

She keeps a lodging house of thirty-one rooms over two floors and she is on no roll in this city of anybody and she is not a clerk and she works for nobody, and she reads everything twice.

He had been at her door before and she had refused him twice and had handed him over to a woman who keeps a list in a yard instead, and she had been right to both refusals, and he had no instrument, and he had come anyway, which is the thing he had sworn he would not do in the first week of January and had done on the first of May.

## Thirty-One Rooms

She opened the door with her sleeves down and did not ask him in and did not shut it either.

“I have read the last one that went out,” she said. “That is the eighteenth sheet, which went out at four on the thirtieth of June, and you were on it, and the first name on the list was on it, and the first name on the list had your name under it.”

“Yes.”

“You have not been in this building since the seventeenth of April and you have come at half past ten on a morning with a notebook, which means you want an address.”

“I want one address.”

She read that back to him before she did anything else with it.

“One address,” she said, “out of a hundred and thirty-nine, and you have not asked me for one name out of a hundred and thirty-nine on any of the three times you have been on this step.”

“I am asking you for one address and not for a name. The name is already on a page. I want to know where the woman is who is on it.”

“That is a worse sentence and not a better one,” said Ilva Vask, “and I am going to give you the address anyway, and I want you to understand exactly why, because you will do something stupid with it and I would rather you did it knowing.”

She looked at him for about nine seconds and then she read the notebook, because he turned it round and put it on the step between them and left his hand on it, and she reads everything twice and she read that one twice before she gave it back.

“It is a door at the top of a stair off this street, and the stair is dark and the door is the second one on the landing,” she said. “I have not got it written down. She came to my counter in the fourth month of the third year and she said where she was going when she left, and she could not say her own door number and she was not ashamed of that and I have thought about it a good deal since.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not,” said Ilva Vask. “If you go up that stair with a notebook in your hand you are the office, and you know that better than I do, and if you go up it and do not say anything then you have come here for nothing and you have told me what you are going to do and then not done it, and that is worse, because now I know.”

He stood on the step.

“I am not going to say anything to her.”

“Then you have not needed me and you have got what you came for.”

“I know that.”

“You do not, and you will, and I am not going to wait for it, because I have thirty-one rooms and a stair and I have been down that stair twice this morning.” She shut the door to about four inches and then opened it again.

“She was in my doorway in the fourth month of the third year and she could not read what I wrote, and I read it back to her, and she made me read it twice, and the second time she said, that is me, is it, and then she went off up the street.”

“And in five years since then nobody has ever come to my door to ask me where she went.”

She shut it properly this time and he stood on the step and let it be shut on him.

## Four Minutes At The Foot Of A Stair

The stair was off Lamp Street about two hundred yards down and it was dark at the bottom and there was a lamp at the top with a switch that had not worked since the spring. The second door on the landing was painted a green that had been a different green about nine years ago.

He stood at the foot of it for about four minutes.

He worked it out on the stair and he worked it out again on the step and it came out the same both times, and the sum was short and it had been short since the first of May.

A man at that door with a notebook is an instrument. An instrument cannot stand on a step and say nothing, because a woman who has spent nine years being asked to produce things will hear a man not saying something exactly as loud as a man saying it.

In about four years there would be a page in a building off a third court with the shape of that silence on it, and there is no instrument in this city that can take a line back out of that book.

If he went up and told her, then she would be a woman who had been told. And told by an office, at her own door, on a working morning, in a city where a name in the second column cannot be read by the person whose name it is and cannot be got back out afterwards.

So he stood at the foot of a dark stair in Lamp Street and did not go up it, and the address is in his notebook now, in his own hand, with the date against it, and there is nothing whatever in this city that takes an address back out of a man’s notebook.

He had come out of the building with his coat on and he stood there in it for about nine minutes in a street two hundred yards from the door, in the way a man stands who has brought something a long way and has not got a use for it yet, and what he had got a use for came to him on the pavement and it was this.

She is going to be found out. She is going to be found out at a desk in Cordwain by a stranger who will read her a line off a public page because about four people in every thousand who read a page get to the bottom of one, and about a hundred and sixty of those people a week go past the desks, and in about four years one of them will be her.

He can move that forward. That is the whole of what he can do with a door at the top of a dark stair in Lamp Street and it is the only move in the world he has got, and moving it forward means a man at her own door on a working morning saying out loud a sentence that becomes a line in a book off a third court before he gets to the bottom of the stairs.

## Nine Days

On the way back down Lamp Street he did the sum, because he was that sort of man and because it took a minute and a half and the street was there.

The nineteenth sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of September, and the thirtieth is nine days from the twenty-first, and thirty less twenty-one is nine. On it will be eight hundred and fifty-two lines in the first column with an hour against about four hundred and nineteen of them and no figure on any of them, and the second column will have two lines in it.

One of them is his. One of them is a woman who dug a street for six years in the cold and was never paid for any of it, and whose name was put on a sheet in Lamp Street by a stranger, and who gave him nothing and cannot read a page.

And he stood on a step four feet from her door on the morning of the twenty-first of September and did not tell her, and he has not told her, and there is a form in this city that carries her name over his name under a heading a city reads every quarter, and there is nothing at all in this city that would tell her it has happened.

*The twenty-first of September, one thousand six hundred and seventy days. Less one thousand six hundred and sixty-three, the fourteenth, is seven. Less one thousand six hundred and fifty-five, the sixth, is fifteen. And thirty less twenty-one is nine days to the thirtieth, which is a quarter-end and the nineteenth sheet.*

*He has got a woman's address in a notebook in his own hand and has not gone up the stair, and the name is in a column against his, and in nine days it goes out at four.*

# A City Reading Two Sentences

He walked Cordwain on the twenty-third of November of the fifth year from about ten in the morning with no bucket and no shift behind him, because it was the second of his three days off, and everywhere he went the standing sheet was out with its two sentences at the foot of it.

Ten days had gone since the thirteenth. One thousand seven hundred and thirty-two less one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two is ten, and twenty-three less thirteen is ten by the month count, and in the ten days the two sentences had gone from a sheet at one desk to nine division corridors and a yard shutter and a lane slate.

He wanted to know what a city does with a thing it can finally read. That want had replaced fear in him, and it felt like hunger, and he followed it the way he used to follow a figure.

At the first division corridor off Cordwain about thirty people a day walked past a board with the four conditions on it and no name at the top, and somebody had pinned a copy of the two sentences underneath with a drawing pin through the corner. About two of thirty stopped. One of the two read the foot and shook his head and said to nobody in particular that an office that admits one man invents cannot be trusted about four hundred and nineteen accounts, and walked on.

He felt that sentence go into him like a nail. It was the cost he had agreed to on the ninth, stated back to him by a stranger, and it was true, and there was no answer to it that would survive being said out loud.

In the yard off the Vestry about eleven thousand people had the two sentences in chalk on the shutter under the four lines, in the yard’s own hand, and a woman he had never met was reading them out to about ninety people at the foot of the steps. She read the invented-figure sentence and then the second-line sentence, and the ninety were quiet in the way a crowd is quiet when it is thinking.

A man near the back said the office had finally told the truth about something, and a woman near the front said the office had finally told a lie about everything, and neither was shouted down, and the woman with the slate waited until both had spoken and then read the two sentences again.

Lin Hao stood at sixty feet and did not go up the hill, because he had said on a street in January that he would not come up that hill and say one word about a board, and a man who has said that does not get to arrive and explain his own footnotes.

Tenderness came up in him instead of dread, sudden and unwelcome. These people were doing with his sentence exactly what they had done with his definition, which was using it for their own purposes, rightly, without asking him.

At the lane end Brack had chalked nothing new. The slate carried nine figures as always, and under the lowest rank the office’s two words stood in paint, and beside them in chalk a copy of the foot of the standing sheet in another man’s good hand. Chalk beside paint. Neither covering the other.

Brack saw him and nodded and went on writing, and the line went through, grain a head, and about nine of the morning’s figures were ringed in Brack’s books before the men who paid them reached the corner.

“You are right about the disagreement,” Lin Hao said when the line thinned. “A street takes two words off a wall and after about four days has two words and a half, and now it has two sentences and it will have four versions by December.”

“Let it,” Brack said. “A figure nobody re-computes is the figure that is wrong, and a sentence nobody argues with is a rule nobody chose.”

Lin Hao felt anger and affection together, which was new, and did not try to separate them.

“You have not been diminished by any of it,” he said.

“I have been written down,” Brack said. “That is different, and it is what I asked for, and I asked for it since April of the third year, and I am not going to dress it up as anything else.”

## The Divisions Read

He went on to three division corridors before dark, because about thirty people a day walk past each board, and at each one somebody had pinned the two sentences under the four lines, and at each one a small crowd stood reading.

At the second corridor a clerk asked him whether the invented-figure sentence meant her division’s nine lines from June were invented too, and he told her the nine lines were entered exactly as given and the sentence was about a slate and not about her book, and she did not believe him, and he did not blame her, because the same hand had written both and the hand could not be separated from itself.

That disbelief was the cost, stated back to him a third time that day, and each stating cut deeper, because each came from a person who was right to feel it.

At the third corridor a woman thanked the office for printing something plain at last, and said her sister had paid a grain to a slate for two years and had always known some figures were decided, and that knowing it officially was better than guessing it alone. That gratitude cut deepest of all, because it was the one response he had not prepared for.

He walked home through streets that were reading his sentence without him, and the relief of that was worse than the fear had been, the way it had been in December of the fourth year.

*The twenty-third of November, one thousand seven hundred and thirty-two days. Less one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, the thirteenth, is ten. Twenty-three less thirteen is ten by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is fourteen. Less the tenth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, is forty-three. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine.*

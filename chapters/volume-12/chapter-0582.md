# The Fifth Year Of A Line

The two words went onto the ranks wall on the eighth of October of the first year, in the office's own hand, at the height of a name on a plate, under the last tile, and there is no date on the stone anywhere under them and no man alive who knows it except the man who did it and the man with the brush.

This morning is the sixth of September and the line is in its fifth year. One thousand six hundred and fifty-five days, less two hundred and forty-seven, is one thousand four hundred and eight.

Three years of three hundred and sixty is a thousand and eighty, and one thousand four hundred and eight less a thousand and eighty is three hundred and twenty-two, which is ten months of thirty days and twenty-eight days, and a year is three hundred and sixty, so there are thirty-two days to go in the fifth year.

He said the two words out loud at about a quarter to seven for the sixth morning running.

## The Man On The Way To A Shift

There is a man who goes under that wall every morning at about a quarter to seven on his way to a shift at a foundry off the Vestry side, and he has gone under it for about nineteen years, and on the sixth morning of September he stopped.

He had a bag over his shoulder and he stopped at about four feet off him and stood there and waited, the way a man waits who has worked out that a person is going to finish saying something.

“Say that again,” he said.

Lin Hao said it again.

“That is what it says?”

“That is what it says.”

The man looked at the stone for a while. About nine thousand people go under this wall in a day because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it, and this one had stopped doing it.

“Nineteen years,” he said. “I have gone under that wall every morning for nineteen years and I thought it was the painters going over old paint.”

“It is the painters going over old paint.”

“That is what I just said,” said the man, and went to work, and did not say anything else, and did not say thank you, and was gone round the corner before Lin Hao had worked out what to do with his face.

## What He Could Not Say In One Sentence

He stood at the wall for about four minutes afterwards and went over it, and the going over took about nine seconds, because a rule has no example in it and he had just tried to give a man one and had run out of room in the middle of it.

The two words are a rule. They mean that when a deed is not written down anywhere in this city, the column headed *carried by* takes the name of the person who decided where it went, and that this is the only instrument in Ostara for a thing nobody measured.

He has known that since the ninth of March of the third year, when the column was ruled, and he has said it out loud in a yard in front of about eleven thousand people and to a compositor off Cordwain and to a man in a coal queue at the foot of a hill.

It has never once come out in one sentence, because it is a rule, and a rule has no example in it.

A man on his way to a shift at a quarter to seven in the morning cannot be given a rule.

He tried two other ways while the foundry man was still round the corner. He said that it was the heading of a column in a book, which is a true sentence and does not tell anybody anything. He said that it was the only instrument this city had for a thing nobody wrote down, which is a true sentence and takes four minutes to say and has an exception in it every time he has ever said it out loud.

What he said instead was that the office at that address keeps a book and that there is a column in it and that nothing goes in that column except a name somebody chose to write there.

That is about nineteen words and it is the best a man can do at a wall at a quarter to seven in the morning, and it is not what the two words mean.

The man was already round the corner. He said it into about nine feet of empty lane to a street that was waking up, and it was not wrong, and it was not the sentence, and he knew while he was saying it that he had spent the best instrument he had left on the one audience in this city that could have carried the whole of it.

## Six Mornings

He did it again on the seventh and on the eighth and on the ninth and on the tenth and on the eleventh.

On the ninth a woman asked him what the words were and he said them and she said carried by back to him and went on, and he stood there and found that he had been waiting four days for somebody to say them back to him and had not thought about why.

On the tenth a boy of about fourteen said the second word before he did, and then asked what the first one meant.

Lin Hao stood at about nine feet off the stone in a lane off Cordwain and found out that he could not put the first one into words that fit in a boy’s head at a quarter to seven in the morning, because the first one means that a deed which was never written down is still a deed and this city has no column for it.

He said that. All of it, in about nineteen words, standing at nine feet off a wall.

The boy said, “Oh,” in the way that means nothing at all, and went off up the lane, and that was better and worse than the foundry man, because the foundry man had asked for it twice and this one had heard the whole of it and given nothing back.

On the tenth the branch office had him on the same shift and Nadia Ferrous came through the corridor at about half past two and stopped dead and said, without any preamble at all, *are you stood outside saying that at people*.

He said that he was.

She looked at him for about four seconds the way she looks at a reading she has already taken twice and does not believe.

“Four nights on this floor and a woman from the market comes in at three in the morning asking me whether the man who writes at the lane has been writing at my wall,” she said. “So it is going round.”

“It is not going round. It is two men.”

“That is what going round is, at the beginning,” said Nadia Ferrous, and went back down the corridor with a bucket that was not full.

On the eleventh nothing happened and the line formed at about half past six at the lane end and Emil Brack chalked a figure at the wall end and one at the tiled end about half an hour later, and neither of the two men who were watching from the arcade said one word about the two words, because neither of them had heard them.

That was the day he found out that the sentence had three audiences and that one of them was asleep.

He went in to his shift on the twelfth with a sore throat, which was his own fault and entirely his own business, and mopped three floors and a stairwell and a bin room, and at about two in the morning he worked out the only reason he had started: that in four years the sentence had been safe because no city could read it, and a man who cannot read a thing has never once been disagreed with about it.

In six mornings he had made it readable and disagreed with in three directions by strangers, and one of the three had been a boy of about fourteen who had not even wanted it.

*One man stopped on the sixth morning of a stretch of six mornings, and asked for it twice, and went to work without saying anything else about it.*

*The sixth of September, one thousand six hundred and fifty-five days. Less one thousand six hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of August, is six. Less one thousand six hundred and twenty-seven, the eighth of August, is twenty-eight, and twenty-two of August after the eighth and six of September. Less two hundred and forty-seven, the eighth of October of the first year, is one thousand four hundred and eight, of which a thousand and eighty is three years and three hundred and twenty-two is the rest.*

*The two words are in their fifth year and have been said out loud under their own wall six mornings running and there is now one man in this city who has heard them and nobody at all who has understood them.*

# A Sentence A City Can Read

He came into the room off a third court on the thirtieth of December of the fifth year at about ten in the morning with the bucket left in the corridor, because the quarter goes out at four in the afternoon and the morning belongs to the sheet.

Ten days had gone since the twentieth. One thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine is ten, and thirty less twenty is ten by the month count, and he had spent the ten days the way he spends every run-up to a quarter, working nights and walking walls and saying nothing.

He wanted the sheet to go out exactly as it is. That want was the oldest he had, older than the lane, older than the wall, and it sat in him beside fear the way a second person sits beside a first on a bench.

Wenna Raut had the bundle open on the table and Yusuf Adeyemi stood at the end of it with his coat on. The tray beside them was empty, because nothing at all had come into the address since the eleventh of March, and Adeyemi had looked at the tray four times that morning for a reason he could not put to anybody.

“The head stands at eight hundred and fifty-two,” Wenna said. “Unmoved since the thirtieth of March. The second column has two lines in it. The foot has the two sentences.”

Adeyemi nodded.

“Read the headings,” he said.

She read them in order, in her level voice. The sixteenth went out on the thirtieth of December of the fourth year with his name at the head of one line.

The seventeenth went out on the thirtieth of March with eight hundred and fifty-two. The eighteenth went out on the thirtieth of June with eight hundred and fifty-two unmoved. The nineteenth went out on the thirtieth of September with eight hundred and fifty-two for the third quarter running.

This one is the twentieth, and ninety days have gone since the nineteenth, because one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine is ninety, and thirty with thirty with thirty is ninety.

Lin Hao listened with his hands flat on the table edge. One year had gone since the sheet with his name on it went out, because one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand four hundred and nine is three hundred and sixty, and three hundred and sixty is one year. Half a year had gone since the eighteenth, because one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand five hundred and eighty-nine is one hundred and eighty.

He understood those figures perfectly.

He also understood that they were going to outlive him.

At four in the afternoon the twentieth sheet went out with everything on it exactly as it is: eight hundred and fifty-two lines with an address at the head of every line, about four hundred and nineteen accounts with an hour against each and no figure on any of them, two lines in the second column, and at the foot, in the hand of the office itself, the sentence about invented figures with a name under it, and the two words off the wall with their meaning plain beside them.

About two hundred people a week read one somewhere in Cordwain and about four in every thousand get to the bottom. About nine thousand people go under that wall in a day because it is an ordinary afternoon of an ordinary month and the trams go under it. About two thousand a day cannot walk past the foot of the standing sheet without reading it.

He stood at the foot of the ranks wall at about ten to five with the December form above him, both boxes at the foot of it blank, one for a figure and one for a standing, blank since the eighth of October of the first year, and watched the city read.

A woman read the invented-figure sentence and laughed, short, and then read it again and did not laugh. A man read the second-line sentence and said to his companion that where the Register has nothing it has not been given anything, and that a blank is not a claim that there was nothing, and his companion nodded. A boy stood on tiptoe to see the name under the first sentence and sounded it out under his breath.

The office is fifty-eight months and twenty-nine days old on that afternoon, because one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine is thirty multiplied by fifty-eight with twenty-nine after it. The lane is one thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight mornings old on that afternoon, because the first morning was the third of October of the year before the first, which is day minus one hundred and eighteen, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less minus one hundred and eighteen with one after it is one thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight.

He wrote his verdict in his own notebook on the wall end of the lane, standing, with the bucket at his feet, in plain words.

The Register has twenty quarter-ends on sheets and a standing sheet that says a man makes up figures and his name under it, and a wall a city can read, and a lane with nine districts in it, and a yard of about eleven thousand people with a rule on a board that he put there.

Rank one on a plate in his coat with no figure cut into it anywhere. Four names on a rota. Eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off, which he asked for and chose.

That is what there is. It is not what he hoped for in the fourth year and it is what he made, and no one in Cordwain is going to hear him say it was a good thing, because none of them thinks it was one.

## The Chained Month

He went back to the room off the third court at about six, because Wenna had left a copy of the twentieth sheet squared on the table for the night shift to see first, the way the thirtieth of December gets read first by people who work nights.

Nadia Ferrous read the foot out loud, slow, and Petra Ilyin listened with her book open and her pencil still, and Isolde Marchetti stood at the doorway with her hands in her apron, and none of the three said whether the office was right, and none needed to.

“What comes in January,” Nadia said.

“The book comes off the shelf on the first and one person may read it on the second,” Lin Hao said. “The stools go out in the lane whatever the weather. The wall gets repainted in the ordinary course of business and takes nothing with it. That is the whole of what I know.”

He left them with the sheet and took the bucket up to the third floor, and worked water until the building quieted, and his want narrowed to the next hour, which was all he had ever been able to hold anyway.

Brack came down the lane with the slate under his arm and stopped beside him and looked at the wall and then at the notebook, and neither asked the other for anything.

“The quarter is out,” Brack said.

“It is out,” Lin Hao said. “Nine a morning tomorrow. About a quarter off nothing. Keep them in books with the date and the weather.”

“I will,” Brack said, and went on up the lane toward Cordwain, and the frost showed on the rails, and the trams went under the wall, and the white tile with his name on it stood in chalk with nothing under it, and nobody said who put it there.

He picked up the bucket and went to work, because half past five comes at half past five even on quarter days, and behind him the city kept reading two sentences it had never had before, and ahead of him January lay shut like a chained book, and what comes off the shelf on the first of it he does not know.

*The thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine days. Less one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine, the twentieth, is ten. Thirty less twenty is ten by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine, is ninety, and thirty with thirty with thirty is ninety. Less the thirtieth of December of the fourth year, one thousand four hundred and nine, is three hundred and sixty, and three hundred and sixty is one year. Less the thirtieth of June, one thousand five hundred and eighty-nine, is one hundred and eighty.*

# Two Figures And One Street

Seven mornings, the seventh to the thirteenth of September, and on the seventh morning the two words started going round a street in more than one version, and by the thirteenth there were three.

The first version was the true one, unrecorded, carried by, and it is on three of the four conditions pinned to the corridor board at the branch with a drawing pin through the corner, and about thirty people a day walk past that board and about two of them stop, and the fourth line is missing off it because somebody cut it off with a blade, and the fourth line is the one that was ever about anybody but the man doing it.

The second version was carried by.

The third version was carried over, and it came out of a yard off the Vestry where a man who shuts nine shutters off that arcade has said two words at the foot of his own steps every night for eleven years without once knowing what they were.

A woman who keeps a list has written them down twice and cannot read what she wrote, and a printer off Cordwain has them in metal, and about eight hundred sheets went out over two nights with no name at the top of them.

He had seen all three of the sources in the four days and he had not joined any of them up. What joined them up was a boy of about fourteen on the tenth of September at about a quarter to seven in the morning, at the wall end of a lane off Cordwain, who said the second word before he did.

And on the eleventh of September somebody put the two words in chalk on the lane wall about eleven feet from a figure in a delivery coat's hand.

## Two Instruments, One Morning

Emil Brack did not rub the chalk out.

“They are wrong,” he said. “One of them has got the wrong word in it.”

“They came off a wall.”

“They came off a wall and into a street where people cannot read the wall,” said Brack. “That is what a street does. A street takes two words off a wall and after about four days it has got two words and a half.”

He was at the wall end with the slate and the line behind him, and about nine people by half past six, and he wrote the lane figure without looking down and said out loud, as he says to anybody who asks and to anybody who does not, that he makes them up.

“Who wrote that on the wall.”

“Different man every night, near enough. Somebody reads them off yours and writes them eleven feet from mine, and he gets one of the two right most nights and the other one right the rest.”

“Whose is right.”

“Yours is right,” said Brack. “His is the one that is in about four hundred places at once and mine is the one that gets said out loud by a man, and in about four years a stranger with a folder is going to find all three of them and he is not going to be able to tell which of them this city actually said.”

He put the chalk down and looked at the two of them side by side on the same stone, one in paint and one in a good chalk hand, both at the height of a name on a plate.

“The wall says one thing and you are writing two,” Lin Hao said.

“The wall says one thing in a hand about nine thousand people a day walk under and have not read in five years,” said Brack.

“You have been putting it into the street by hand for six mornings. If you wanted it in the street you should have learned what the street does with a sentence, which is that it does not carry it. It carries a piece of it and throws the rest away.”

“That is not a fault in you. It is the only thing a sentence can do.”

## What The Chalk Figure Says

“The one at the tiled end is bigger than nine.”

“The one at the tiled end is what a man is worth when nobody is waiting for him,” Brack said. “The one here is what a man is worth when he is standing in a line. That is not a mistake and it is not two figures. It is one street and it is worth two things at its two ends, and a city that reads one of them is reading half a street.”

“Nobody reads yours.”

“About nine thousand of them do, and about forty stop, and I say out loud that I make them up, and that is the whole of why it works.” He put the chalk down and picked up the slate.

“Your two words have been said out loud six mornings and the street has three of them and no notice at the top of any of them saying where they came from, and you did that and I did not. I would like you to notice the difference between us before you decide what to do next.”

## What He Decided

He went into the branch at about a quarter to eleven and up to the corridor board and stood in front of it and read the three lines with the drawing pin through the corner.

They were the true ones. They were the only true copy of the four conditions in that building and they were on a corridor board with about thirty people a day walking past it, and the other two versions of the office's two words were in the lane and in a yard and in about eight hundred sheets of paper with no name at the top of them, and there was nothing on any page in that city that said which one was the one.

He had spent his own voice on it in a street at a quarter to seven in the morning and what he had bought was a street with two words and a half in it.

There was one thing he could have done about that and he worked it out standing in front of the corridor board at about a quarter to eleven in the morning, and the thing was this.

A page of his office goes out at four on the last working day of the quarter with an address at the head of every line, and about two hundred people a week in Cordwain read one, and the fourth of the conditions has been on about eight hundred of those sheets since the twenty-second of March with no name at the top of any of them.

So the four lines are already in a street in about eight hundred places. What is not in a street is the two words.

And a street that has got two words and a half in it cannot be handed the true one now without a notice going up at the top saying which of the three is the office’s, and there is no form in this city that says a notice has to have a name at the top of it, and none that says a notice has to come down again.

He put his hand flat on the pin in the corner of the board and took it away without touching anything.

He stopped saying them. That was all there was to decide and he decided it on the thirteenth of September at about a quarter to eleven in the morning in a corridor off a third court, and the deciding cost him the one thing he had left that nobody could take, which was the fact that he had said them, and it is still true that he said them.

Then he went to find one person in this city who had asked him three questions on the sixth of March and had never been told one word of anything.

*The thirteenth of September, one thousand six hundred and sixty-two days. Less one thousand six hundred and fifty-five, the sixth of September, is seven. Less one thousand six hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of August, is thirteen. Less one thousand six hundred and twenty-seven, the eighth of August, is thirty-five, and twenty-two of August after the eighth and thirteen of September.*

*A sentence has been put into a street by a man reading it out loud at his own wall and it is in the street now in three versions, and the only true one of the three is on a corridor board about thirty people a day walk past, and he has stopped saying it.*

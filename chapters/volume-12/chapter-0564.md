# The Yard She Cannot Stand In Front Of

Eleven days from the twenty-eighth of February to this morning. One thousand four hundred and sixty-seven was the twenty-eighth of February of year five and this morning is one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight, and the ninth of March is the first day of the second month of that year, which is thirty days on from the first of February, and one thousand four hundred and forty plus thirty plus eight is one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight.

It is thirty-nine days from the thirtieth of January, which is one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine, and one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight less one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine is thirty-nine.

It is sixty-seven days from the second of January, which is one thousand four hundred and eleven, when a woman two floors under a sales floor spent a question she had carried for eleven years on a man who could not answer it.

And it is five hundred and eighteen days from the first of October of the third year, which is nine hundred and sixty, and one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight less nine hundred and sixty is five hundred and eighteen — three hundred and sixty for the year, and five months of thirty days, which is a hundred and fifty, and eight days.

That is the last time she stood in front of them.

## The Note

The note came to the branch at about ten in the morning and it came to him and not to the counter, which it has never done, because a piece of paper addressed to a building is addressed to a building and this one had a man's name on it.

Four lines.

*I have one thing to say and I cannot say it in the room, because the room is where I say it, and I have been not saying it for a year and four months and I have worked out why this morning.*

*Come at about eleven. Do not come up the yard. Come in at the Vestry door at the bottom and do not stop in the arcade and do not speak to anybody.*

*I am not asking you for anything. I have not asked you for anything in four years and I am not starting.*

*— A. L.*

He read it twice in a corridor with a bucket under his arm and then he went and did the first floor and came back at a quarter to eleven.

He was going up Cordwain at ten to eleven and he had said out loud on a street on the tenth of January that he would not come up that hill and say one word about that board.

He was not going to say a word about it. He was going to a door at the bottom of it because a woman had written his name on a piece of paper, and the sentence he had said on that street was about arriving at a yard uninvited and telling a yard what to do, and a man who has been invited is not that.

## The Room With No Door On A Street

She had the lamp on the bracket turned down and her coat over the back of the chair, and she was broad-shouldered and heavy and forty-five years old, and her hair was white at the front of it and wet at the part, because she had washed it that morning in a building where a woman washes her hair because there is nothing else to be done with a morning.

She did not get up.

"Close the door," she said. "Not the street door. The other one, in the corner, that comes off the yard."

"There are two doors."

"There have been two doors for twenty years and about four hundred people in this city have only ever known about one of them." She waited until it was shut. "Listen."

## What She Heard

There were about eleven thousand people in that yard on an ordinary evening and about nine hundred of them in daylight, and in the four days since the thirtieth of January something had happened in it that nobody had thought to send word about.

"They have started doing it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "The rule. The one in this room."

"Which rule."

"It belongs to somebody else and I did not build it, and I have told you that before. One thing each, and the last one says hers last, and nobody answers anybody. It is the rule of a kitchen off Ferrier Street. I have never moved it. It has been in this building for twenty years and nobody has ever been inside this room while it was being used."

"And now they are using it out there."

"Now they are using it on the steps," she said. "For four days. A woman gets up and says one thing and nobody answers her, and then another one gets up, and that is all, and about nine hundred people stand there and it is very orderly and I have never seen a yard in this city so quiet."

She turned her head about nine degrees.

"They are extremely good at it. They are better at it than about four thousand of the ones in the arcade ever were. And I taught them."

"You did not teach them anything."

"I put them in a room with a rule and I never let anybody out of it," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And now there are about nine hundred people on my steps who have learned that the way to speak in front of a crowd is to say a thing into a room in which nobody answers anybody, and they have got it from twenty years of a chaplain refusing to answer questions in a building four hundred people could not get into."

## What It Costs A Rule

"You built it so that nobody could use anything said in there," he said.

"I built it so that nobody could take anything out of this room," she said. "There is a difference and it took me twenty years and one year of silence to find out which one it was, and I found it out about four hundred people at a quarter past five on the first of August of the third year, in a yard, in about four seconds, and nothing in this city puts a name back on a person for a silence."

She got up then, which she did not do.

"I am going to stand on those steps at about eleven this morning and say one thing and go last and not be answered, and I have thought about it since about five and I am going to do it, and I am telling you the reason and the reason is not the argument."

"What is it."

"It is that I have not been in front of them since the first of October of the third year and about eleven thousand people have decided what I am without me, and what they have decided is *she went quiet*, and they are not entirely wrong." She put her hand on the back of the chair.

"And there is one way left to be wrong out loud in front of people and I am going to take it, and if the rule swallows it then it swallows it and I will have said it."

## What She Wants Him To Do

"I want you to be in this room," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"You have never asked me for anything."

"I have not and I am not now." She came and stood about four feet off him, which is closer than about nine hundred people have ever got to her. "You are going to stand about here, where I can see you, and you are not going to say anything, and when I go out of the door into that yard you are going to be in this room listening to about eleven thousand people not answer their chaplain."

"Give me a reason I would understand."

"Because there is one person left in this city who has ever told me that he heard something I said," she said, "and it is you, and it was in this room, at half past ten on the evening of the sixth of October, in a room where nothing could be used against anybody. And you are going to be the only person in this city who knows whether it came back through the door."

## Through The Corner Door

She went out at about eleven and the corner door shut behind her and he was in a cold room with a lamp on a bracket and about eleven thousand people on the other side of two doors.

He could hear it. Not the words — the yard is too big for the words — but the shape of it, which he has heard four times in four years and has never once been able to explain to anybody.

One voice. Then a silence. Then another voice, from a different part of the yard. Then a silence.

It took about twenty minutes and there were about thirty of them.

At about eleven-twenty somebody in the crowd said one thing at enough volume that he caught the shape of it, and there was no answer, and then about nine hundred people made a noise that was not a noise and went back to standing.

At about eleven-thirty she said it. He knew the shape of it because she had said the same four lines twice in four years and both times in a room, and this time it was on the steps at the top of a yard with about eleven thousand people in it.

It took about forty seconds.

Nobody answered her.

Not one voice. There were about eleven thousand people in that yard and she had been the only person in this city who was ever right in public for twenty years, and four hundred of them had stopped believing her on her own word in the eleventh month of the third year, and about nine hundred people in that yard had worked out on their own in the last four days that the way to be heard by a crowd is not to be answered but to be unheard past.

They waited for her to finish. That was the part he had not been ready for. They waited the way a room waits for the next thing, and then somebody at the back said one thing about a room on Lamp Street, and that was the end of her.

## What Came Back Through The Door

She came in at about ten to twelve, shut the corner door behind her, and stood with her back against it.

She was not angry. He had braced for angry for four days and what came through the door was a woman who looked about as tired as she had looked in four years, which is not a thing she shows at the front of anything.

"Say it," she said. "You were standing there and I want it said by somebody who heard it and not by me."

"It did not come back through the door."

"No."

"There was no answer. Not one, out of eleven thousand."

"No," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And I knew there would not be, and I went out there anyway, and I want you to understand that I am not here about that, because I went out knowing."

## What She Lost

"Then what."

She stood with her back against the corner door for about nine seconds.

"I lost the room," she said.

"The room is here."

"It is here and it is the only room in this city where what is said cannot be used against anybody," she said, "and I have said that out loud to four people in twenty years and I have never once said it to about eleven thousand, because they cannot come in and that was the entire point and the price of it for twenty years."

She wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist.

"What I lost is that I said one thing out loud this morning on my own steps and about eleven thousand people treated it the way this room treats a thing. That is what I said it for. That is what the rule is for."

"And it works. It works beautifully. It is the best mechanism I have ever been inside of and it has just been used on me by nine hundred people who learned it from me, and I built it, and I would build it again."

"Agnete—"

"You will not say it was the right thing," she said. "I know what you are going to say and it is the sentence from the sixth of October and I have had about four months to get used to it and I have not, and I am not going to be comforted by it this morning."

## What She Is Not Going To Do

"There is something you could ask me for."

"I can guess what there is."

She said it quickly, the way a woman says a thing she has been asked eleven times.

"And no. I am not going to claim the column and I am not going to claim the two words at the foot of that wall, and I said so in a kitchen in the second year and I said it to a clerk with a certified copy in her hand on the thirtieth of December of that year, and I said it again in this room in October of the fourth year."

"I have not changed my mind in any of the four years and I am not going to change it now because I have lost a yard."

"Nobody is going to ask you to."

"Good," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Because if anybody in this city asks me to, I will stand there and say no out loud, and I have not got the voice for it this morning."

## Her Terms

She sat down, and put her hands on her knees, and her hands do not come up to about the second joint and have not been warm in a cold room for twenty years, which is a state and not a mark and which she stopped apologising for somewhere around the twentieth year.

"Now I am going to tell you what I want," she said, "and I am going to tell you all of it before any of it happens, because I have not got this afternoon for a man working out what a woman wants, and I did not get it last October either."

"Go on."

"I have been on my feet in a yard in every month of twenty years. I have not been wanted by anybody in about four years and I have not told one person in this building that, and I am telling it to a man with a bucket because he has come up a hill this morning on a piece of paper with four lines on it."

She put her hand flat on the arm of the chair, and the chair took it.

"I want this. I have wanted it since the sixth of October of the fourth year, and I did not do anything about it then because a man came to my steps and I did not want the last day of that year to be that. I have been not doing anything about it for four months."

"Yes."

"That is the whole of my terms and I am giving them to you before anything has happened. That is the rule in this room and nobody has ever watched me keep it for anybody."

She stood up, and she is broad-shouldered and heavy, and she came at him the way a woman who has decided something comes. "I am going to be very ordinary about it, the same as I was in October, and I am saying so first, because you have had three of these in four years and one of the three was ordinary."

"I remember what you were in October."

"You remember it because I told you and not because you noticed." She got her hand into the front of his coat. "So. Come here. You will do what I tell you and you will not ask me whether I am sure, and every question you ask me this afternoon I am going to answer, including the ones you have not thought of yet. I am about four years out of practice and I am not going to apologise for that either."

## Nothing Was Persuaded

He came to her.

She had him against the side of the lamp bracket with one arm and got her own dress up over her hips with the other hand and neither of the two operations took her attention off his face, which was where she had put it, and she looked at him.

"Right," she said. "Say it."

"I do not have to."

"I know you do not. I am asking you to say it. That is not the same request as asking you whether you would, and you are going to find out which of the two this is."

She got her thumb under the front of his shirt and pushed. "And you are not going to be careful with me either. That is the other term and it is the one that matters. There are about eleven thousand people outside that door and not one of them has been inside it and I have not been wanted by anybody in four years, and if you are careful with me I will know you are doing it out of manners and I will stop."

"I am not going to be careful with you."

"I know," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have been watching you not be careful with me since the seventh of January and I have not known what to do about it."

She got his coat off his shoulders without letting go of him with the other hand, and then his shirt, and then the rest of it, and she did all of that in about forty seconds and without any particular display, the way a woman takes off her own coat in her own hall.

She is a big woman of forty-five who has been on her feet in a yard in every month of twenty years. He got his hands on her and found that everything about her was exactly as solid as she looked and none of it gave, and she let him take her weight and then took it back.

"Now," she said. "Here. Mouth. And do not be quick about it, because the last one of these was four months ago and it lasted about nine minutes and I have been thinking about it at the bottom of about eleven steps every week since."

## Nine Minutes In October

"You have been thinking about it."

"I have been thinking about the end of it, which is the part nobody thinks about at the time."

She had her hand in his hair already and was not using it. "In October you finished and then you held still and I laid along the front of you with my chin on your chest and my hands went from cold to not cold in about a minute and a half, and I have done nothing else with my hands since the seventh of January. So that is the part I have been thinking about at the bottom of the steps."

"That is a very stupid thing to have been thinking about."

"It is a very stupid thing," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have been thinking about nothing else, and I would have come down that hill in October for it and I did not, and I have not had anybody to give it to since, and I am going to have you now and you are going to take your time."

She took him into her mouth and worked at him with her mouth and her hand, and she was not fast, and she was not slow either, and she was listening to him the entire time with about half of her attention, which was more than she had given anything in twenty years.

After a while she took him out of her mouth and looked up at him along the length of him and said, "You are going to last longer than you did in October."

"I am rank one with a chest that has been spent for four years."

"Yes," she said. "That is what I said in October and I am saying it again and it is still the only medical opinion anybody has given me about you. Come here."

## The Order Of It

She got him over the bench and then had him up off it again and then put him back, and the bench went an inch across the boards on its old feet and stopped, and it made a noise the second time and she laughed about two seconds at that and told him to be quiet and then did not require it.

She told him what she wanted next in one flat sentence and he did it, and she told him what she wanted after that in another one and he did that too, and neither of them asked the other about being sure at any point in it, and there was no part of the whole of it in which either of them was performing anything.

"There," she said, at one point, when he had got his mouth to the side of her neck and his hand where she had put it. "That. Twenty years of a yard and about four years of nobody and that is the first thing in four years I have not had to decide whether to want."

She was not quiet about any of it. She had told him in October that she was not going to be and she was not.

## The Term She Breaks

Then she stopped him with two fingers on his chest, which is a thing she does, and which is not a refusal and which he took as one for about a second and she told him he was not to do that.

"What have I said to you about the rule in this room."

"One thing each. And the last one says hers last. And nobody answers anybody."

"That is not the whole of it. Listen to the order of it." She got up off him and sat on the edge of the bench with her feet on the floor and did not put her clothes back on. "I have said my one thing three times in four years and I went last three times. That is the rule and the rule holds and it is going to hold out there on the steps tomorrow as well."

"And?"

"And I am going to change one of the three in this room, and I am telling you while I have still got my dress on, because a rule you break in your underwear is a different thing from a rule you break in your clothes."

She put her hand flat on her own thigh. "I am going first. Before I ask you anything. I am going to say the thing I want, out loud, in this room, and then I am going to take what I want, and that is a breach and it is the only one and I have thought about it for four months."

"You do not have to tell me the order."

"No," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Nobody tells anybody the order. I have told you the order because there is a second rule in this room that nobody has ever heard and it is the one I am about to break the other half of, and you will not understand a word I say after this unless you have it."

She got him up off the bench and onto his feet with one hand on the back of his neck.

"So I am going first," she said. "I am a big woman of forty-five and I have not been wanted by anybody in four years and I am going to say it in the plainest words there are and then I am going to have you, and you are going to say yes, and you are not going to be asked twice."

"Yes," he said.

"Good," said Agnete Lindqvist. "Now come and take your coat off the back of that chair and put it on the floor, because I am not having you cold in my building at the end of it. You are a man who has a chest that has been spent for four years and there is not one thing in this city anybody could hand you for it, and I am not going to be the reason you cough your way down two flights in March."

## The Rest Of It

He put the coat on the floor and she had him back against her and took the rest of her own clothes off in about nine seconds, and she was not cold anywhere, and he was, and she noticed that about four times and said nothing about any of it except the first time.

"Over the arm of the chair," she said. "Put your hands there. There is nothing on that arm and I have been meaning to have it cleared for about four years."

He put his hands there and she took him into her mouth with her hand closed round the base of him.

She worked at it with her mouth and her hand and no hurry at any point in it, and she had done this before and knew how much of her jaw was going to be available for it. When it began to go she did not slow down: she sat back on her heels, wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist, and started again lower down, with her hand and the flat of her tongue and about nine deliberate passes of it before she came back up.

She looked up at him along the length of him and said, "You have not asked me once whether I want you and I am telling you that I do, and I told you inside four minutes of you getting here, and I would have told you faster if you had not looked so careful about it."

He came back into her mouth and she held him there and worked him with her tongue against the flat of her tongue, and then she pushed him back off her, stood up, and took the rest of it in her own hands with her back against the cold wall.

She put him where she wanted him and made him hold still while she got her breath and then told him to move, and he moved, and she came with him and was louder about it than she had been about anything else all afternoon, and neither of them counted how long it took because there was no clock in this room and there has not been one since before she came.

She got her hand flat against his chest and left it there for about a minute afterwards, and his heart was under it and hers was not, and he said he was sorry about that and she told him not to be.

"You do not know what that is," she said. "I have had it for thirty years and it has never once been any use to me and it is not going to improve because a man is in the building. That is a thing you are going to find out in about four years and it will not be a shock."

He asked her about the yard and she answered him. He asked her whether she was frightened and she answered him that too, and what she said was that she was frightened of being the only person in this city who was ever right in public for another four years while eleven thousand people learned to be quiet from her, and that having him in the building had done nothing whatever about that and had made it a great deal easier to carry.

Then she made him go again, slowly, and put him face down over the bench and came down onto him from behind with both her hands flat on his shoulder blades and worked her hips into him until the bench went across the boards again and stopped.

"Twice," she said. "In twenty years. Same bench."

She was not quiet about that either.

## The One Thing She Wants Asked

She lay along the front of him afterwards with her chin on his chest and her hands, which had been cold for the first minute and had not been cold since, flat on his ribs until they were not cold.

"Ask me something," she said. "I know what I said and I have about four minutes before I put my shoes on, and there is one question in this room and it is yours."

"You said you had spent yours in October."

"I spent a thing in October. I did not spend a question and I have been careful with what is left." She lifted her head about nine degrees. "Ask me whether I am glad you came."

He asked her whether she was glad he came.

"I am," said Agnete Lindqvist. "And there is no version of this room in which that is a small thing, and you are the only person in Ostara who is going to find out that it was not one."

She put her hand flat against the side of his face, which she has never done.

## After

She drank three glasses of water out of a jug on a shelf and put the jug back where it had been.

"I am not going to tell you that was the last one," she said, "and I am not going to tell you it was the first of anything. I lost the attention of about eleven thousand people this morning and I had it back inside a quarter of an hour, and those are not the same thing, and I am not going to spend an afternoon pretending they are."

"You will not be last on anything else."

"I will be last," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I have been last three times and I am going to be last a fourth, and whether the four of you ought to be sitting at that table at all is a thing I have decided to leave where I found it."

She put her coat on and stood in front of the corner door with her hand on the latch and did not open it.

"One thing, and then you go out of the other door and down the arcade and do not stop, because I mean the do not stop and I will be able to tell."

"Go on."

"The rule worked this morning," she said. "It worked on about nine hundred people who have never once been in this room and they used it on me as though they had been in it for twenty years, and they are right to use it, and I am not going to spend another four years being the only person in this city who is ever right in public about it." She turned her head about nine degrees.

"I am going to stand on those steps once a month and say one thing and be unanswered, and about eleven thousand people are going to hear me and not hear me at the same time, and that is what is left."

"Nobody will answer you."

"Then they will have to want me to say it twice," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and that is a thing that has not happened to me in twenty years and I am going to make it happen."

*Ninth of March of year five, a room with no door on a street, about twenty to one in the afternoon. Thirty-nine days after the yard, sixty-seven days after the second of January, and five hundred and eighteen days after the last time she stood in front of them.*

*She sent for me. Four lines with my name on the top of them and a letter at the bottom, and she has not asked me for anything in four years and she has not asked me for anything today.*

*And the rule that was in this room for twenty years is out on those steps being used on her by about nine hundred people who learned it from her, and it worked, and she went out knowing it would.*

*And she lost the room. Not the room. The room is here. She lost the only thing the room was for, which was that a thing said in front of about eleven thousand people comes back through a door into a room where it cannot be used.*

*And she is going to stand on those steps once a month and say one thing and be unanswered, and I am not going to be able to help her with that and she has not asked.*

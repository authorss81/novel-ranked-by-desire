# The First Line In The Office's Hand

He came into the room off a third court on the ninth of November of the fifth year at about ten in the morning with the bucket left in the corridor and his hands clean, and Yusuf Adeyemi was already at the table with the standing sheet laid out between them and Wenna Raut at the counter with a pen.

Six days had gone since the third. One thousand seven hundred and eighteen less one thousand seven hundred and twelve is six, and nine less three is six by the month count, and he had not slept the night before, and Tessa Okonkwo’s taking sat in him like a wound that had been cleaned.

He wanted to write it before he could stop himself. That was the whole of his want, and it frightened him, and he held it the way a man holds a hot tool, by the one end that does not burn.

The standing sheet lay face up with its headings typed at a desk off a fourth court. At the foot of it, under the last rank, there was a space the width of a thumb where a line could go in the hand of the office itself, once a quarter, and the space had been empty on every sheet since the first of February of the first year.

Adeyemi looked at him without sitting down.

“You have carried something for twenty-three days,” Adeyemi said. “Since the sixteenth of October. Say what it is or write what it is, but do not carry it past the quarter, because the quarter goes out on the thirtieth of December and a line that goes out once a quarter has to be written before December is out.”

Lin Hao put his hand flat on the table beside the sheet and took it away again.

“A man in a delivery coat has had a thing on a table since the twenty-seventh of April of the third year,” Lin Hao said. “Every figure he has invented and the way he did it and about a quarter decided off nothing, for one line in the hand of that office, once a quarter, saying that he makes them up.”

Wenna Raut looked up from the counter.

“Is he right about the figures,” she said.

“He is right about every fact he says,” Lin Hao said. “He profits nowhere a person loses. He keeps eleven books with the date and the weather and what the line looked like, and about two thousand of about eight thousand are ringed, and every ringed one has a reason in the margin in plain words.”

Adeyemi was quiet for a long time.

“And you have seen the books,” Adeyemi said.

“I have seen them,” Lin Hao said. “I went up the stair behind the lane on the twenty-seventh of October and turned the leaves myself. Five forward and five back. Every leaf nine figures and two or three ringed.”

He felt the dread again, and under the dread a reckless clarity he recognised from the thirtieth of January, when he had said yes in a yard in front of about eleven thousand people. He had one thing left that was his own, which was a sentence nobody could quote, and he was about to spend it.

Short sentences now. He owed himself that much.

The office was the only honest instrument in Ostara. It had never been written anywhere. It had been true until that morning.

He picked up the pen.

“What are you writing,” Adeyemi said.

“A sentence no document in the history of Ostara has ever carried,” Lin Hao said. “That a figure in Ostara may be a figure somebody made up on purpose. That this standing sheet is printed by the office itself. And here is his name under it.”

He wrote it in the hand of the office itself, in the space at the foot of the standing sheet, in plain words, with the name Emil Brack under it in the same hand. His hand did not shake. He had expected it to shake and it did not, and that steadiness frightened him more than shaking would have.

Wenna Raut asked the one question she is permitted to ask, which was whether the words were the words he had written, and he said yes, and she entered them exactly as given, and told him the price of saying it out loud was eleven words in a book about nine people a week will ever look at.

“And the second line,” Lin Hao said.

Adeyemi looked at him sharply.

“Say it,” Adeyemi said.

“A notice that says a figure in Ostara may be invented and does not say what the second line means is a notice that is lying,” Lin Hao said. “The two words at the foot of the ranks wall go on the same sheet, in a hand about two thousand people a day cannot walk past without reading, with the meaning plain beside them: that where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything.”

He wrote that too, under the first line, in the same hand, and the sheet had two sentences at its foot that it had never had before.

He put the pen down.

It was done. It could not be undone, because a clerk may only enter what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry, and there is no form for striking a line a person entered against themselves in their own hand.

“What it closes,” Adeyemi said slowly, “is the option of that office being the only honest instrument in Ostara.”

“Yes,” Lin Hao said.

“What it costs is that every party with a real claim now stands on a standing sheet beside a man who has admitted in the same hand that he invents his, and Ostara has no instrument to tell them apart, and you are the one who gave that away.”

“Yes,” Lin Hao said.

Brack was not in the room and did not thank him, and no word of thanks came into the room from anywhere. The man in the delivery coat would hear of it from a stranger at a desk in Cordwain, and what he would say about it was his own, and Lin Hao would not be there to hear it.

## The Walk Down

He walked out into the third court with empty hands and a hollow chest, and the November light lay flat on the stone, and he understood that he had broken the office in order to keep it honest.

He went to the lane end because he could not stay still, and the slate was out with nine figures, and Brack was not there, and a boy minding the slate told him Brack had gone up to his rooms to rule the morning’s ringed ones into the book before the ink of memory dried.

Lin Hao stood nine feet off and watched men pay a grain a head to figures, some read and some decided, and felt the weight of what he had written come down onto that small exchange like a hand onto a shoulder.

A woman at a stall asked him whether the office had really printed that a figure may be invented, and whether her rent figure was one of the invented ones, and he told her the office had printed what it printed and that her lease was between her and her landlord, and she called him a coward without heat, and he accepted it, because she was right to want more than he could give.

He walked the wall end to end, eleven minutes each way, past the tiled end with about four hundred tiles in eleven courses and the chalk figure fresh under the lowest rank, and back to the lane end, and his legs were tired and his chest ached the way it has ached since the spring of the third year.

Tessa’s taking held him upright. Without it he would have sat down on the stone and put his head in his hands. With it he could stand and look at what he had done.

*The ninth of November, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen days. Less one thousand seven hundred and twelve, the third, is six. Nine less three is six by the month count.*

*Less the twenty-seventh of October, one thousand seven hundred and six, is twelve. Less the tenth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, is twenty-nine. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and eighteen is fifty-one.*

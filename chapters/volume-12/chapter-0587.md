# A Woman Two Flights Up

The nineteenth sheet went out at four on the thirtieth of September and on the first of October it was lying out on a public desk in the ground-floor passage of a building off Cordwain, face up, at the height of a man's chest, with the address at the head of every line showing.

It was not collected. It was not the way a sheet is handled, which is that a person reads it on a working afternoon and the desk takes it back at the end of the day and it goes into a bundle and is burned or is not.

It had been left where it landed.

He went and stood at about nine feet from it at about half past nine in the morning and he wanted one thing out of that morning, which was to find out how many people stop at a desk where a page is lying out, because about thirty people a day walk past a corridor board in his own building and about two of them stop, and he has never had a figure for the other case.

## The Man At The Desk

The desk is against the wall of the passage under a window and it belongs to the building and not to the office, and there is a man behind it who has run public desks for about nine years and who is paid by the building and not by anybody who prints anything.

He was reading the sheet when Lin Hao came up and he finished the line he was on before he looked up, which is a thing a man does who has been on a desk nine years and has learned that a person standing at the other side of it is a person who is going to ask.

“You are the office at that address,” the man said.

“I am the man with the bucket at that address.”

“Same thing to nine thousand people a day,” said the man, “and you will find that out before I have finished this cup of something.”

“It came out yesterday at four,” he said.

“You have not collected it.”

“I have not collected it since the first of March of the second year.” He put his thumb on the top of the sheet. “I read about nine thousand lines off those things in nine years and nothing in them ever got better. The figures do not move. The names do not get anything next to them. There is one line at the bottom in the same words every quarter for four years and I could set it in type.”

“Why do you leave them out?”

“Because a page that is put away at four has been read by whoever got there first and by nobody else for the rest of the week,” he said. “And because in about four years a stranger with a folder is going to open one of these and he is going to want to know who had it, and I am not going to be able to tell him, because I never did.”

“How many sheets have you left out.”

“Thirty-one sheets and two bundles and a fact I have never told anybody, which is that this desk has been in this passage for nine years and it was not my desk until the second year, and the man who had it before me put them away at four, and I thought he was being tidy.”

He put his cup down out of the way of the paper.

“I have a stair to go up at half past six and a woman who reads to the bottom does it standing at that end of it,” he said, “and she has never once said she was here when one of those was lying out, and I have never once asked, and I would not now.”

## What He Counted

He stood at about nine feet for about an hour and a half and he counted, and he had counted things at walls before and knew what he was doing.

Of about thirty people who came through that passage between a quarter to ten and a quarter to eleven, nine stopped at the desk. Of the nine, one read the sheet the whole way down to the last line, and one more read to the second column and stopped there, and the other seven looked at the address at the head of a line and went on.

One in nine reads to the bottom. He has heard that from a desk in Cordwain before and it is the same desk and the same man, and it is not a figure anybody in the office has ever printed.

He had sat on the clear table in an audit room at half past one in the morning three days earlier and been told that a page that is open does not get put down for anything, and it turns out that a page lying out in a passage does not get picked up by the office either.

Nine stops out of thirty, and one of the nine to the bottom, and that one person a morning in a passage is the entire reach of a quarter’s page in this building.

The bottom of that sheet is the second column, and under the second column is the office's own sentence, and above the second column on the ninth line down is a street and six years in the cold and no figure against it and no name against it.

## The Ninth Line Down

It was entered on the eleventh of July in a clerk’s hand, and the division was not permitted to ask her for a name and did not ask her, and there is no column on the page that would take one if it had asked.

He read it standing up, the way he has read it four or five times in four years.

*A woman told a division on the ninth of April that she dug a street for six years in the cold, that nobody wrote it down, and that she had believed for two years that a figure on a slate in a lane was about her.*

Mavis Rooke is two flights up off Cordwain and she came up those stairs on the tenth of October of the fourth year without a hand on the rail, with a sheet folded in four in her coat, and she said finding is one thing and being caught is another thing and that she cannot tell which, and that is still true, and there is no instrument in this city that can make anybody write down that she is owed six years of a street.

And her sheet is lying out on a desk in the passage under her own stair, at the height of a man’s chest, in the morning.

He wanted to go up. There were two flights and a dark stair and a second door on the landing with paint on it that had been a different colour about nine years ago, and he had not gone up one of them on the twenty-first of September and he was not going to go up one now.

So he stood at about nine feet off the desk and understood the thing the morning had actually been about, which was that a page belonging to an office with a room off a third court and no door onto a street has come to rest about eleven inches, vertically, from a woman who has a creditor and no name against it, and that the office cannot recall it, cannot move it, cannot take it back, and has no way whatever of telling her it is there.

Nobody is owed anything by that. She cannot read it.

*The first of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty days. One day after the thirtieth of September, which is one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine. Less one thousand four hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December, is two hundred and seventy-one, and two hundred and seventy-one is nine months of thirty days and one day.*

*The nineteenth sheet is lying out on a public desk in a passage in Cordwain and has been since the first of March of the second year, and the ninth line down on it is the line of a woman who is two flights up off that same passage, and about one person in that building in a week reads far enough down to know it.*

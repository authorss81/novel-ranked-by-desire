# The Page In Her Building

The sheet was still lying out on the sixth of October, on the sixth day, in the same place at the same height with the same address showing at the head of every line, and about four feet of it had been rubbed round at the corner where shoulders go past a desk in a narrow passage.

He had come to it at about ten with two things he wanted. The first was to see whether it was still there, which it was. The second was to see whether anybody had written on it, because a page that has been lying out on a public desk for six days in a city where about two hundred people a week read one is a page somebody in about four years is going to want to know the history of.

Nobody had written on it.

In four years, and in about nine years of desks in this city before that, he had never seen a mark on one of these sheets. Not a name, not a figure, not a scratch, not a thumb at the bottom. A person reads one, gets to the bottom or does not get to the bottom, and puts it back, and the page goes into a bundle at the end of the day.

The man at the desk had taken it out of that in the second year and had not put it back in a bundle since, and that was the entire history of the sheet and it was four days old and it was going to be a month old.

He had not expected that and it had taken the whole of the walk across Cordwain to get it into his head. He had come here on the first with one thing to find out and he had found it and then had come back on the sixth without a reason he could put in a sentence, and a thing you cannot put in a sentence at ten past nine in the morning is usually the one you came for.

There is no person in this city who can make a sheet be collected. There is no form for it and there is no clerk who may be told to do it and there is no office that the man at that desk answers to except the owner of the building, and the owner of the building has been paid for a desk.

## What Was On It

He read it standing up again, the whole of it, because on the sixth day he wanted to know what a page of his office actually is now that it had been left in one place for six days.

At the top, the figure. Eight hundred and fifty-two, and the third quarter running without moving.

Under it, the address at the head of every line, which is the only way anybody in this city finds a room off a third court with two chairs in it and a drawer under the book that has been shut since the thirtieth of April.

Under the address, about eight hundred and fifty-two lines. Four hundred and nineteen of them are a woman's account of six years of cold with an hour against each and no figure on any of them, and the other four hundred and thirty-odd are other people's words and are not his.

Then the second column. Two lines. One is his, entered by him on the fifteenth of October in the fourth year against himself. One is a woman he entered on the first of May without asking her one question and has never seen.

Then the sentence at the foot, which has been at the foot of every one of these since the first of February of the first year and which about four people in every thousand who read a page ever get to.

And above all of it, on the ninth line down, a street dug for six years in the cold with nobody’s name against it, which is the line of a woman called Mavis Rooke who is owed it and has no way of being told.

## The Thing He Could Have Done

There was a pencil in the man’s desk and the man did not mind a pencil, because a man who has run public desks for nine years has had pencils taken off him by people who could not read and by people who could.

He stood there with it for about four minutes and he worked out the whole of it, because it is a short sum and he is a man who does short sums.

If he wrote anything on that page then the page would carry a second hand. A line of his own, in pencil, in the margin beside the ninth line, saying that the six years were owed and had never been paid for — and there is no instrument in this city that could move it, and no form that could strike it, and about four people in a thousand who get to the bottom of a sheet would get to a hand that was not the office’s hand.

And if he wrote on it then the sentence at the foot of that page would be a lie by about four in a thousand, because that sentence says that where this Register has nothing it has not been given anything, and it would stop being true the moment the man who runs the Register put a hand on a public desk in a passage off Cordwain.

He put the pencil back in the desk.

“You are not going to write on it,” the man said.

“No.”

“Nobody writes on them,” said the man. “That is the thing about them. You can leave one out for six months and nobody writes on it, and I have never worked out whether that is because they cannot read or because they do not believe it is theirs to write on.”

“That is the whole of it.”

He went down the passage and out into Cordwain at about a quarter to eleven with two days in front of him and a quarter-end behind him and a sheet lying out above a woman’s stair that nobody had read to the bottom.

And about four feet of it had been rubbed round at the corner where shoulders go past a desk in a narrow passage, which meant that in six days about a hundred people had pushed by it, and every one of them had had a hand on the paper, and not one of them had got to the ninth line.

He went up the lane instead, because it was on the way and because there was nothing else on the way, and at the wall end a man in a delivery coat was on the tiles with a ladder he did not own and a chalk in his teeth.

“The eighth is the eighth,” Brack said, before he was asked. “Five years tomorrow. I do not chalk it and neither should you.”

“What should I not do.”

“Say it out loud under my figure at a quarter to seven in the morning so that nine thousand people hear an anniversary,” said Brack. “They would hear a sentence on a wall about itself and they would hear it for about nine seconds and then they would be at work, and in four years there would be a page in a building that says a man stood under a wall on the eighth of October and said his own sentence, and nobody would be able to tell why.”

He came down the ladder.

“It is a wall. It is not a page. That is the whole of what it is for and you have been asking it to be a page for five years.”

*In two days it will be five years since the second line went onto the wall.*

*The sixth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-five days. Less one thousand six hundred and eighty, the first of October, is five. Less one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is six. Less one thousand six hundred and sixty-three, the fourteenth, is twenty-two.*

*A page of this office has been lying out on a public desk in the passage of a building in Cordwain for six days, and it is the page that carries the name of Mavis Rooke in the ninth line down, and she is two flights up that same passage.*

*He had a pencil in his hand for about four minutes and did not use it, and there is nothing in this city that will now take the page back in, and in two days the line will have been up five years.*

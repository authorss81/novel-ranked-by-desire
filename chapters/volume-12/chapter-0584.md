# Three Questions She Never Finished

The audit room is at the end of the ground-floor corridor at the branch off a third court, and it has two tables in it and a lamp on a bracket, and one of the two tables has had a bundle of returns and a pencil and an hour sheet on it every working night since the first of October of the third year, and the other one has had nothing on it in all that time.

She was at the first table at half past one in the morning on the fourteenth of September with her sleeves down and her cuffs buttoned, and she did not look up when he came in with the bucket, and she went on reading for about four minutes, because a page that is open does not get put down for anything.

He had been in the building since eleven. Three floors and a stairwell and a bin room, and the bin room first, because the bin room is the one nobody opens and he opens it at eleven and not at half past, and the third floor last before this because the third floor is where the water is heaviest.

He put the bucket down outside the door where it would not stand up in the night man’s way and came in and sat on the clear table with his hands on his knees, and the lamp was over the far side of the room and he could see her and she could see the paper.

She audits this building. She has audited it since the first of October of the third year, four years, and she is not paid to stand in this room at half past one in the morning and she is there four nights in seven.

She has read the same ground-floor corridor off the same sheet every working night of that time without once saying anything about it to anybody, including him, including the night man at the far end who carries a lamp and has never once asked her why she is here.

There is a rule and the rule has been in this job for nine years and it came with the job rather than with her. It is a rule about a page and not about her and it has exactly three parts, and she can say all three of them without stopping to think, and she has never once broken any of them.

And the fourth thing about it, which is not in the rule and is hers, is that she does not stop to be spoken to while a page is open.

“Three questions,” she said, at the end of the line she was on.

He did not say anything.

“What is under that tile. Who put it there. And why have you stood under it about sixty-five times without asking.”

She did not say it in the voice she audits in, which is level and gives nothing away. She said it the way she says things at half past one in the morning when the hour on the page is the only thing that has changed.

“I asked you those on the sixth of March in a corridor with a page open on a table, and I told you at the counter that I was not going to ask you again, and I have broken that about four times since, and I am not going to apologise for it.”

“I know.”

“I am going to keep them open. All three. There is a page in this city where a thing that has been asked twice by an auditor is a rule, and I have never once in eleven years asked anybody anything twice, so you may have the whole of the benefit of it.”

He put his hands flat on the clear table.

## She Put The Page Face Down

She read to the end of the line she was on, and wrote the hour against it, and squared the sheet to the edge of the table, and then she turned it over.

That was all she did. She did not get up first and she did not say anything about where they were or how long they had, and she put the page face down on a bundle of returns in a room where she has never in eleven years put a page down for anything at all, and then she came round the table.

Her cuffs came off herself, one button and then the other, and there is ink at the base of the first two nails of her right hand that has been there for about as long as she has been in this building and that she stopped trying to lift a long time ago, and there is a grey at her left temple with the etches in it that has not moved since the summer of the second year and does not move now either.

Her hands were warm. She had been over paper for an hour and paper is the only warm thing in that room, and he had come in off three floors with a bucket and a stair in him and his hands were not, and she took his right hand and turned it palm up and put it against her cheek and left it there for about two seconds, which is the only kind thing she did and she did it without looking at him.

Then she took the lamp off the bracket with its flex and carried it four feet and put it on the clear table and turned it so that it lit the two of them and not the page, which is the exact opposite of what she had spent the hour doing, and he understood what that cost an auditor who reads in order.

She got his coat off him over the shoulders the way a woman gets a coat off a man who has been carrying water for four hours, one sleeve at a time and then the other, and hung it on the back of the second chair. Then she got her own cuffs back down and did not do them up again.

She put her palm flat on his chest once, the way a woman checks a thing she already knows the answer to, and took it away again, and she was not careful and she was not slow either, and whatever she had come down the corridor for she had said it by putting a page face down.

He got his shirt open and she got it off him over his head, because his arms had been up a hill of stairs since eleven o'clock, and she did it in one movement without stopping to look.

She got her own dress up over her hips with one hand and took none of it off, because there is a corridor ninety feet long on the other side of a shut door and a bucket standing outside it, and a woman who has been auditing a building for four years does not undress in a room with a door on it.

Her mouth went onto his throat and then onto the knot under his jaw that three floors of carrying water puts there by about one in the morning, and she got her fingers on it in about two seconds and pressed, and neither of them said one word.

There was nothing in that room making a noise. The corridor was dark at that hour and the night man was on the far end of it with a lamp he carries, about ninety feet off, and a door went down on its hinge somewhere off to the left and nobody came through it, and the building makes a sound like a building and neither of them listened to it.

She worked her mouth down the line of his throat and back up it and he put a hand round the back of her neck and she let him keep it there, and she pushed his head back a little with her own hand over his so that he was looking at the lamp instead of at the ceiling, which was deliberate and was not for him.

Then she got him out of the rest of it and pushed him back against the edge of the clear table with two fingers on his hip, and she went down in front of him on her knees on the boards of a room that has not had a fire in it since the first of February of the first year and never will have one, because the building is a branch office and a branch office does not have a fire in an audit room.

She took the cock in her mouth and there was nothing careful about it. She did the two things she knew and she did them until she had done the second one properly, her tongue going over the length of him and coming back and doing it again, with a hand flat on the front of his thigh and her fingers tight at the root of him, and she looked up at him once in the middle of it to see his face and then stopped looking and went on.

He came into her throat and the sound he made went up the corridor about ninety feet and the night man did not come back, because a man who carries a lamp at the end of a corridor at half past one in the morning has heard worse than that and is paid to be about ninety feet away.

She took all of it and swallowed it and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and did not comment on any part of it.

"Now me," she said, and pulled him off the table by the hip and turned him and put him into her herself, standing, with her back against the clear table and her hands on the edge of it behind her.

She was wet already and she was tight for about a second and then she was not, and she watched his face while that happened and did not look away from it while the rest of it happened either.

He went into her slowly, the way he does everything that nobody is timing him on.

At about half past one she put both her hands flat on the table behind her and said his whole name and then said, do that again slower, which is the opposite of what she had said the first time, and he stopped.

Her cunt closed on him in the middle of each stroke and she rode him up against the edge of a table in a room with a lamp on it, and her knees came apart until she had to shift her feet to get the second of them, and she did that without a sound and without hurrying and without once asking him for anything.

The lamp hummed. She made a noise about four times in nine minutes and twice she shut it by turning her face into the sleeve of his shirt, and she left a mark on the cloth that was still there at half past five when he took the coat off the back of the second chair.

She had her fingers in his hair at the back and she took him over in her own time and in no hurry at all, and she set the pace and changed it twice and watched his face each time she changed it, and when she came she shut her mouth and shook for about four seconds and held on to him with both arms and put her face into his collarbone and stayed there and did not make a sound.

He came about nine seconds after her and she took all of that as well and did not comment on the interval between them and neither did he.

Then she let go, and stepped back, and got her cuffs done, and squared her cuffs against each other, and walked round the table and picked the lamp up and carried it back to the bracket and turned it to where it had been, and turned the page the right way up, and put her finger on the line she had been on before she came off this table.

## The Fourth Question

He was at the door with the bucket when she spoke.

“One more and then I am going back to this.”

He stopped.

“You asked nobody why I have been auditing this building for four years.” She did not look up.

“I have read the same corridor every working night since the first of October of the third year and I have never once said anything about it out loud, and that is a rule of this room and not a rule of me. The job says a page is read in order, the date first, the hour with it, nothing skipped, and an auditor does not stop to be spoken to while a page is open.”

“I have kept that rule for eleven years and I have never broken half of it.”

“You have broken half of it tonight.”

“I broke half of it tonight about nine minutes ago and I am going to put that in my own private note about myself and nowhere else, and it will not be in a page, and it will not be in a building.” She wrote an hour against a line. “And there is no form in this city that asks me whether I have a private note about myself and I am not going to look for one.”

He stood in the doorway.

“What would you have to do to make it stop,” said Ines Duarte.

He did not come back into the room.

“To make it stop I would have to ask somebody something,” he said.

“A woman with a broom has been cleaning four inches of stone under that tile every morning for about nine years, and she told me once, unasked, that she has never seen a person put anything on it, and I did not ask her a single question and I am not going to.”

“I am the only person the office may ask, and there is no way in this city to take an answer back out of a book once it is in.”

“So it does not stop.”

“No.”

“That is four questions then,” she said, “and none of them has been answered, and I am not going to ask any of them again, and that is not a promise, it is a record of what I have done.”

She wrote for about ninety seconds before she spoke again and she did not look up.

“You will want to know what it cost,” she said. “It cost me about ninety seconds and the corner of a bundle of returns, because that bundle was squared before I turned the page over and it is not squared now, and in about four years somebody is going to open this room and find a bundle that is out of square and there is no form in this city that explains a bundle.”

“I am sorry.”

“Do not be,” said Ines Duarte. “You have been sorry about six times in eleven years and every one of them has been about something that could not be helped. I do not want that. I want the ninety seconds and I have had them and I would take them again in the morning.”

He stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

“In four years somebody opens this and finds a bundle out of square,” he said.

“Or they do not. That is the whole of what an auditor has ever been able to leave behind and it is not a small thing to be handed, and I have handed about nine of them in eleven years and I know what each of them cost.” She squared the bundle. “It is squared now. It cost me thirty seconds and it will cost me another thirty on the first of January when I find it and do it again and know why I have done it.”

She picked the pencil up and put it down flat on the table, which is what a clerk at a counter does and which she does about twice a day.

“Go and do your floors,” she said. “You have the bin room still.”

He took the bucket and went out along the corridor with the lamp still burning at the end table and about two and a quarter hours left, and he heard the pencil go back on the paper before he was round the stair, and the pencil did not stop while he was on the stair and did not stop while he was on the second floor, and that is what the rule is for.

*Three questions asked on the sixth of March of the first year, and a fourth asked on the fourteenth of September of the fifth, and four unanswered, and a page put face down about nine minutes on a clear table in an audit room off a third court, which goes in a private note and in no page at all.*

*The fourteenth of September, one thousand six hundred and sixty-three days. Less one thousand six hundred and sixty-two, the thirteenth, is one. Less one thousand six hundred and fifty-five, the sixth, is eight. Less one thousand five hundred and seventy, the eleventh of June, is ninety-three days, and there has been nothing between those two rooms since a woman read a second column aloud off a proof she keeps face up. Less one thousand one hundred and fifteen, which is the sixth of March of the fourth year and the last time she asked him anything, is five hundred and forty-eight.*

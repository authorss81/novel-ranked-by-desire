# Two Ends Of One Wall

The August form came off the ranks wall on the last working day of the month with both boxes empty, and the September form goes up on the first with both boxes empty again, because they have been empty since the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, and a form that a city reads every morning has had nothing in either of them for one thousand four hundred and two days.

That is three years of three hundred and sixty multiplied by three, which is a thousand and eighty, and one thousand four hundred and two less a thousand and eighty is three hundred and twenty-two. He did the subtraction at the foot of a wall and then did it again on the other side of it in case the first one had been done in a hurry.

He had never once walked it.

## The Other End

The ranks wall is not short and it is not a straight thing, and it runs from the tiled end at the north, where the tiles go up in ranks under a rail and the rail is nine feet off the pavement, down past a shuttered arcade and a public stair with no rail on it, to the lane end, where the lane off Cordwain comes up against the stone and about sixty feet later there is a branch office with a stair and a bin room.

He had stood at about nine feet off the lane end about sixty-five times since the first of January of the fourth year and had never once gone the other way. He went the other way on the thirtieth of August at about ten minutes past six in the morning, with a bucket in his hand, and it took him about eleven minutes.

At the tiled end the wall is a different wall. There is no paint on it and there has not been paint on it for years, because the tiles go on that part and the tiles are put up by a different set of men who own different ladders, and the stone underneath them is dry and pale and has a rail cut into it about nine feet up with the slots worn round.

The ranks go up from about waist height to the rail, about four hundred of them in eleven courses, and the slot at the top of them is a different colour from every tile under it. He has stood under that slot about nine times in nine years, the last of them with a woman who reads that wall at half past four every morning in the dark, and she was not at the north end that morning and he did not go and find out whether she had been.

About nine thousand people go under this wall in a day because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it, and every one of them goes along this stone at some point in the ordinary course of the day, and not one in ten of them looks up.

There was a figure in chalk at the foot of it, about four inches high, on the stone under the lowest rank of tiles.

It was done in a good hand. That was the thing that stopped him. It was not scrawl and it was not a mark a man makes with the side of a piece of chalk because he has nine minutes before the queue starts; it was a figure made by a man who has written about nine hundred of them and has never had to hurry one, and it stood on clean dry stone in the shade of the ranks with nothing over the top of it.

There is no painter at this end of the wall. The tiles go up here and the tiles are put up by men with ladders who own the ladders, and what they do to the stone underneath is their own business and nobody has ever written it down anywhere.

## A Figure At Each End

He went back down the wall with the bucket in his hand and a figure on the stone at the tiled end that nobody had told him about, and Emil Brack was at the lane end with the slate under his arm and the line behind him, about nine people by half past six.

“You have been to the north end,” Brack said, without looking up.

“There is a figure at the north end.”

“There is.”

“There is one here.”

“There is one here as well,” said Brack, and finished the figure, and stepped back from the slate the way a man steps back from a thing he made up on purpose.

The two figures were not the same and they did not agree. The one at the lane end was nine. The one at the tiled end was larger than nine and not round and not a number a man could count into, and it had been on that stone since before six o'clock and it would be under about nine thousand pairs of eyes by eight.

## Right About The Disagreement

“One is what a man is worth when he is standing in a line,” Brack said. “One is what a man is worth when nobody is waiting for him. That is not a mistake and it is not a different figure. It is one street and it is worth two things at its two ends, and a city that reads one of them is reading half a street.”

“You write them at both ends because people walk at the tiled end.”

“I write them at both ends because people walk at both ends and there is nowhere else in this city I can stand with a piece of chalk and be read in the morning.”

He took the first grain out of the line and wrote the lane figure without looking down, and said out loud, as he says to anybody who asks and to anybody who does not, that he makes them up, and the line did not move, because it has not moved in three years.

Lin Hao stood at about nine feet and looked at a wall that has carried the office's own hand at one end and a made-up figure at both, and understood that in five years the sentence had been read aloud at its own wall by nobody at all and a number had been said out loud by a man every morning for three years, and that this was not a disagreement about anything.

It was a disagreement about whether anybody was listening.

He felt anger, and then underneath the anger a thing he did not want, which was that the man with the chalk was right and had been right since the winter and would go on being right after this morning, and that there was nothing on that wall he could put next to it.

The office's own hand is better than the chalk. He knows that and has known it for four years, because it is a form hand and it is the same hand as the book and the sheet and every line a clerk has ever entered for this office, and it is a better hand than a man who writes at a quarter to seven in a lane with a queue behind him.

A better hand in a place where nobody stops is worth nothing at all. He had known that for four years too, and it had never once cost him anything to know it, because it had never once been in front of him at both ends of the same wall on the same morning before.

“That is your fifth year,” Brack said.

“It went up on the eighth of October of the first year.”

“Next month,” said Brack, and went back to his slate, and did not look up again, and did not say anything else, and that was the whole of the conversation.

## What He Did About It

He went down Cordwain with about four hours before his shift and stopped at the wall end because a man who has looked at both ends of a thing for five years and has not done anything about either of them ought at least to stand at one of them.

The two words were under the last tile at about the height of a name on a plate, in the office's hand, and the paint had gone over the top of them every month in the ordinary course of business and had taken nothing with it every time.

He stood at about nine feet from them for about two minutes, the way he has stood at about nine feet from them about sixty-five times, and then he did the thing he had come down the street to do, and there was nobody in the lane at all to hear it do it.

He said the two words out loud, once, in his own voice, at the height of a name on a plate.

Nobody heard it. It went about nine feet and came back off the stone, and he stood there afterwards and understood that he had just spent the only thing in this city he had left that was his own, on a sentence that cannot be heard from nine feet in a street with trams in it.

He did it again at the same hour the next morning. He did it again on the third and the fourth.

On the fourth morning a man at a window four floors up put his head out and asked him what he was doing, and he told him, and the man said he had been going to that window every working morning for eleven years and had never once known there was writing on that end of the wall, and then the window shut.

So there are two now. A man who asked for it twice and went to work, and a man who came to a window for eleven years about forty feet from it and had not known, and both of them said the same thing, which was that they had thought it was paint going over old paint.

He slept about three hours that night and thought about two men.

*Nothing happened. A man said two words under a wall on the fourth morning of a stretch of mornings and nothing happened, and the line formed behind him at about half past six and about nine thousand people went under it because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it.*

*The thirtieth of August, one thousand six hundred and forty-nine days. Less one thousand six hundred and twenty-seven, which is the eighth of August, is twenty-two, and thirty less the eighth is twenty-two, and the eighth month has thirty days and no thirty-first.*

*The second line went up on the eighth of October of the first year, which is two hundred and forty-seven, and one thousand six hundred and forty-nine less two hundred and forty-seven is one thousand four hundred and two, and a thousand and eighty of those are three years and three hundred and twenty-two are the rest.*

# It Goes Out At Four

He came into the room off a third court on the thirteenth of November of the fifth year at about half past three in the afternoon with his coat buttoned wrong and his name still ringing in his ears from the morning, and the standing sheet was already squared on the table with its two new sentences at the foot of it.

Four days had gone since the ninth. One thousand seven hundred and twenty-two less one thousand seven hundred and eighteen is four, and thirteen less nine is four by the month count, and he had worked two of the four nights on the branch rota and had not told Nadia Ferrous or Petra Ilyin or Isolde Marchetti what he had written.

He wanted it to go out clean. That was his want, small and fierce, and it sat beside his fear like a second person in the room.

Wenna Raut read the sheet twice top to bottom at one speed, the way she reads everything, and Adeyemi stood at the end of the table with his coat on and did not sit down.

“The figure at the head stands at eight hundred and fifty-two,” Wenna said. “Unmoved since the thirtieth of March. The second column has two lines in it. The foot has two sentences it has never had before, in the hand of the office itself.”

“Read the foot,” Adeyemi said.

She read both sentences out loud without hurrying and without emphasis, and the room changed shape around the sound of them. A figure in Ostara may be a figure somebody made up on purpose, printed by the office itself, with a name under it. And the two words at the foot of the ranks wall with their meaning plain beside them, in a hand about two thousand people a day cannot walk past without reading.

Lin Hao stood with his back to the counter and listened to his own sentence in another person’s mouth, and felt sick and steady at once.

“Is there any instrument that can stop it,” he said.

“No,” Adeyemi said. “If a sheet can be read, it goes out. That is the whole of the rule and you knew it in December of the fourth year.”

At four in the afternoon Wenna carried the sheet to the public desk in Cordwain herself, because the ordinary carrier was down with a cough, and Lin Hao walked beside her with his hands in his pockets and said nothing the whole way. The desk man took the sheet and laid it out under the window and weighted its corners with four smooth stones, and about thirty people came through the passage in the next hour and about nine stopped.

Lin Hao stood across the passage with his shoulder against the wall and watched a woman read the head and then the ranks and then the foot, and saw her stop at the foot and read the two sentences twice, and saw her look up at the desk man.

“Is that his name,” she said, pointing at the name under the first sentence.

“It is,” the desk man said.

“And the office printed it.”

“It did,” Wenna said from the doorway. “In its own hand. Once a quarter.”

The woman read the foot a third time and went out into Cordwain with her mouth tight, and a man took her place and read it once and laughed, short, and then stopped laughing when nobody laughed with him.

By half past five about forty people had stopped, and about eleven had read to the foot, and about four of the eleven had said something out loud, and none of the four agreed with any other.

## The Hour After Four

Adeyemi came down to the desk at about ten to five with his coat still on and read the foot himself, standing in the passage with his hands behind his back, and the desk man watched him the way men watch a Registrar who has come to see his own sentence out in weather.

“It holds,” Adeyemi said to Wenna. “The hand holds. About two thousand people a day cannot walk past it without reading it, and about four in every thousand who read a sheet get to the bottom, and now the bottom is the part they will remember.”

A clerk from a division asked whether the name under the first sentence could be struck if the man asked, and Adeyemi said no board and no office has an instrument to take a name back out once it is in, and the clerk wrote that down and went away.

Lin Hao stood in the passage with a deadline behind him for the first time in weeks. Four in the afternoon had been the hour all quarter, and now it was past, and the sheet was out, and nothing could be called back. Relief and nausea sat side by side in him.

He thought of the ward off Cordwain, and of a door shut and a woman who does not tell, and drew on that reserve the way a man draws on stored grain.

Lin Hao walked back to the branch through darkening streets with Wenna beside him, and neither spoke until they reached the third court.

“You did the thing,” Wenna said at the doorway, and then said she was not going to say the rest of it, and went home.

He went up to the second floor with the bucket and worked until half past five in the morning, and the two sentences went round Cordwain without him, and that was the point, and it was also the cost.

*The thirteenth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two days. Less one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, the ninth, is four. Thirteen less nine is four by the month count.*

*Less the third of November, one thousand seven hundred and twelve, is ten. Less the tenth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, is thirty-three. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two is forty-seven.*

# The Same Four Lines In Nine Hands

One day from the thirtieth of April to this morning. The thirtieth of April was one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine days off the first of February of the first year, and this morning is one thousand five hundred and thirty, and the first of May is the fourth month of that year and is the day after the thirtieth of April.

And it is ninety-one days from the thirtieth of January, which is one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine, and one thousand five hundred and thirty less one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine is ninety-one: one day to the first of February, thirty days of February, thirty of March, thirty of April, and no days of May at all, because this is the first, and one and thirty and thirty and thirty is ninety-one.

## Thirty Feet From A Board

The ninth division is four streets off Cordwain and its corridor is about forty feet long and has a counter in it, and about thirty people a day walk past the board at the end of it.

He went in at about eleven in the morning because he had come down Cordwain and there was nothing else in the district he had any business being in, and he had told nobody he was going and there was nobody to tell.

The sheet was up. It was an ordinary sheet of the printer's, about the size of a form, and it was at the height of a name on a plate, and it was pinned at one corner with a drawing pin.

Four lines in a clean even hand that was not the yard's hand and was not anybody's, and nothing at the top of it, and nothing under it, and no mark of this office anywhere on it.

*It changed what a person held.*

*It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name.*

*Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough that a person reading this in four years would know which person.*

*And it is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

He read it standing up at about thirty feet, which is the distance at which a man reads a sheet in a corridor, and it took about eleven seconds, and about nine people went past him while he did it and none of them stopped.

Nine boards in nine divisions, and about two hundred and seventy people a day walk past the nine of them, and the four lines are the same four lines in all nine. He worked out in about four seconds why each of the nine is right.

The first is four streets from where he was born and about nine hundred people walk past that board. The second is in a district where about forty of the four hundred and nineteen accounts came from, and those forty can now read the rule that decided they had not proved anything, on the way to a desk. The third has a returns clerk who asked for one and did not know what it was for.

The ninth is four streets off Cordwain and he is standing in front of it.

None of the nine has his name on it, and not one of the nine is about him, and he had not asked any of the nine for anything, and he was going to be asked about all nine of them for the next four years.

## What Somebody In The Corridor Said

A woman came in behind him and put a bundle of returns down on the counter and looked at the board over his shoulder for about two seconds.

"That is the one off the yard," she said.

"It is."

"The one with the name cut off it."

"That is a different sheet. That one has a name on it and about eight hundred copies of it have gone out in this district. That one is the four lines and nothing else."

"That one is the same four lines."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "And it has about nine hundred more of them about, in a yard, on a shutter, in chalk, at the top of a hill, and in three shops and a tea room and the back of a tram shelter, and in a room off a court in a form with four boxes on it, and on a plate off Cordwain that a man has had since the twenty-eighth of January."

The woman put her hand on her bundle and looked at the board again.

"Which one is the right one."

"All of them," he said. "They are the same four lines and every copy of them in this city is being used by somebody and not one of them is being used to find out whether it was me."

## The Question That Is Not About The Yard

"There is a man who comes to that counter twice a week," she said. "He is not from this division and he is not from any of them. He has a book and he asks people four questions and he writes the answers down, and he has asked me one and I said yes to all four of them and he wrote it down and he asked whether he could give it to somebody."

"What did you tell him."

"I told him to take it to a building off a third court with no sign on the door." She looked at him. "Are you the office at that address."

"I am."

"Then you should know that about nine people have been sent to it in the last nine days and I have watched every one of them walk in and not one of them has come out with their book."

"What happens to the book."

"Nothing happens to it. I do not know what happens to it," she said. "I have asked. I asked the man with the book and he said the same thing you would say, and he said it better than you are going to."

## The Room Off A Third Court

He was in at about four in the afternoon and the drawer was in front of him and he opened it.

A page with four columns and about a hundred and thirty-nine names in one hand, ruled off a straight edge, done in one night by a woman in Lamp Street on purpose so that it would cost her something.

Every name in it could describe what they had done and when and who had been there. Not one of them is on a page anywhere in this city and not one of them has been entered anywhere at all, in four years, by an office with a column and a book and a clerk.

Thirty-one days ago he read eleven accounts in about four minutes and a figure at the top of a public sheet moved for the first time in four quarters, and there was no column anywhere that said why.

This was the other half, written in the other half's hand, on the other half's time, by somebody who worked for nobody.

## What He Decided

He took the notebook out of his coat and shut the door of the room, which is a thing a man can do in a room with two chairs in it.

He wrote four lines in his own hand on the office's foolscap.

*Found at the foot of a board at the yard end on the thirtieth of January, out of a list made for nothing by a woman who keeps a list, and the list was made on the back of a printed sheet.*

*I had not asked her a single question. I had never seen her. There was no division in this city that went out for her and there is not one now.*

*This office holds no money and cannot pay her and has never been able to and will not be able to.*

*It is entered against me.*

And under it, in the column, in a hand that is not the office's hand:

*Berta Skell. Carried by Lin Hao, rank one.*

## The Reading

Wenna Raut came in at about ten to five and read it from the top, straight down, at one speed.

She read it twice. She squared it. She put it down and read it a third time because she had read it wrong the first time and knew that she had, which she has never done in four years.

"That is the same four lines," she said.

"Yes."

"You wrote four lines against yourself on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year and they went out on a public page on the thirtieth of December, and this is the same four lines and the third one of them is a sentence you have already said in a building with a window in it." She put her pen down.

"I am going to ask you the one question I am allowed, you are going to answer it, and I am going to enter it exactly as given. I will not be able to do one single thing else with it and neither will you."

"Ask it."

"Are these the words you wrote."

"Yes."

She wrote it. It took her about nine seconds and she did not look up while she did it.

## What It Costs

"She was not asked," said Wenna Raut.

"She was not asked."

"That is the whole of the cost and there is nothing else in this room that is the cost."

She capped the pen.

"The fourth of your four conditions says the thing it describes is not on a page anywhere in this city. You have just put her on a page in this city and you have entered her against a person who decided where she went."

"And the four conditions do not apply to her, and they do not apply to her because she did not know about them and has never been asked, and that is the reason the rule does not take her, and you have entered her anyway."

"I know what the rule does not take."

"Then say it out loud in this room. There is going to be one person reading that line at a public desk in about two months and it is not her and it is not me, and the only person who will ever be able to explain it to anybody is you." Wenna Raut put her hand flat on the margin and not on the ink.

"And what you said in a yard at half past eleven on the thirtieth of January is that you were going to do nothing about the people those four lines leave out."

"I said it twice."

"I know you said it twice," she said. "And I am a clerk and I enter what I am given and I am not permitted to have a view, and I have had one for about two minutes and it is going in the book and not out of my face."

He sat down.

He had done it in about four seconds and he had known the whole of it before he picked the pen up, because it was the reason he had picked the pen up.

There is no instrument anywhere in Ostara that can put a person back inside a definition. He said so in a yard to about nine hundred people on the sixteenth of January and again to a woman who kept a count on the twenty-eighth of February, and he has known it every day since the eleventh of July of the fourth year.

And on the first of May he took a name off a page a woman made for nothing and put it in a book in his own hand, against himself, without asking her one question.

That is the thing he said at half past eleven on the thirtieth of January. That is what doing it about it looks like from the inside.

## What It Closes

The second column was ruled on the ninth of March of the third year and was empty on seven quarter-ends and has had one line in it since the fifteenth of October of the fourth year, and the line was him.

It is going out on the sheet of the thirtieth of June, which is the eighteenth, at four, and about two hundred people a week read one, and four or five in a thousand of them get as far as the bottom.

Berta Skell of a street he cannot name would be on that page. She did not write it and she cannot read it. A woman she has never met wrote it in her own hand, in a house in Lamp Street, in one night, and Berta Skell does not know that it exists, and she never gave him anything, and she is going to find out from a stranger standing at a desk in Cordwain.

He cannot strike it. A clerk may only enter what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry, and he has entered it himself, and there is no form in this city for striking a line a person entered against themselves in their own hand.

He has closed the option of being a man who kept a book of deeds, and he said so out loud in a yard a year and three months ago, and about nine hundred people in that yard told him he was wrong, and he has spent four months being right about it in his own head.

Ruta Mielke came in at about twenty past five and stood in the doorway and did not come any further in.

"You looked at it," she said.

"Yes."

"Which name."

"The first one on it."

"Then she is the first name on the board, because that book was written from the top down and so was the board." She did not come in. "I carried that page in a coat for about five hours yesterday and you have had it in a drawer for about a day now, and I want to say one thing to you from a doorway and then I am going to go home."

"Go on."

"You did the thing," said Ruta Mielke. "I am not going to say the rest of it, and you will not ask me, and I have been in that yard for nine years and I am not going to stand in a doorway in an office and give a man who has just done a thing like that the satisfaction of the rest of it."

*First of May of year five, a room off a third court, about a quarter past five in the afternoon. One day after the thirtieth of April, and ninety-one days after the thirtieth of January, and one and thirty and thirty and thirty is ninety-one.*

*Nine divisions. Nine boards. Nine corridors. About two hundred and seventy people a day walk past the four lines and every one of the nine is right and there is no tenth.*

*And I put a name on a page in my own hand in the column that has had one line in it since the fifteenth of October, and I had not asked her anything, and I have never seen her, and I cannot strike it, and the four conditions do not take her and I entered her anyway.*

*And on the thirtieth of June the eighteenth sheet goes out at four with eight hundred and fifty-two or whatever it is by then, and with my name against hers on the same page under the same heading, and about two hundred people a week will read it and about four in every thousand will get to the bottom.*

*And I said in a yard at half past eleven on the thirtieth of January that I was going to do nothing about the people those four lines leave out.*

*And I have said it twice.*

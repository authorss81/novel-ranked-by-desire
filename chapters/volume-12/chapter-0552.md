# The Sixteenth Sheet

He was at the foot of the ranks wall at about ten to four on the afternoon of the thirtieth of December, and the office that paints it was taking the quarter's wall down to the stone in the ordinary course of business.

Three days from the twenty-seventh of December to this afternoon.

The two words were on the stone above the last tile, at the height of a name on a plate, in the office's own hand, and they were on the stone for the same reason they have been on it every month for four years, which is that the paint goes over the top of them and does not take them, and the boy with the paste brush is sixteen and has never once been asked what the two words are for.

The paint was four weeks old and had gone over them again and they were there.

## The Form And The Two Boxes

A form goes up with the wall every month and a form goes up with the wall every quarter, and it is a sheet of office paper with two ruled boxes at the foot of it. One is for the figure of the thing at the address and one is for the standing of it.

Both have been empty since the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, when a clerk was handed a sheet and a form and no instruction, and typed two words into one of them because the box was there and the words were there, and a boy painted what was in the box.

He stood and looked at both of them for about a minute and a half and he had walked past them about sixty times.

There is no form anywhere in this city for asking what a box is for.

## What Went Out At Four

At four o'clock the sheet went out with an address at the head of every line.

*Register of Unrecorded Merit. Sixteenth quarter. Entered: eight hundred and forty-one.*

It is the same figure as the thirtieth of September and the same figure as the thirtieth of June and the same figure as the eleventh of July when a satchel came in off a division. A quarter is ninety days, and three months of thirty days each is ninety, and that figure has now stood still through two whole quarters and both of them are ninety and thirty and thirty and thirty is ninety.

It is a figure about deeds. About two hundred people a week in this city read it as a figure about deeds and they are wrong about that.

There is nothing on the page that will put them right, because the page does not say what a deed is in this office. It says the office's word for it, and that word has meant something else since a woman stood at a table in a kitchen off Ferrier Street and read four lines out on the nineteenth of July of the second year.

Then the two columns.

The first carried about four hundred and nineteen lines, one of which had his name at the head of it and about four hundred and eighteen of which were other people's words with an hour against every one and no figure on any of them anywhere in this city.

And the second column, which had been ruled since the ninth of March of the third year and had been empty on seven quarter-ends, carried one line, and the line was his, and his name was at the bottom of it in his own hand, which is not the office's hand and is not the hand of a man who chalks figures on a surface people walk past.

And under the whole of it:

*Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing.*

He had read that sentence on eleven sheets. It is not his. It is the sentence the form takes and it is in a clerk's hand and it was typed at a desk off a fourth court and it is going to be read this afternoon by about two hundred people in Cordwain who will think it is the bravest thing on the page.

It is the only sentence on the page he did not write, and it is the only one that says what the page is.

## Nine Thousand People

There were about nine thousand people under that wall before the light went, because it is an ordinary afternoon at the end of an ordinary month and the trams go under it.

That figure is not a measurement. It is what a wall does to a street when a street is going somewhere, and the top of that wall is a row of figures that change on a fixed morning in every quarter, and about two hundred people a day stop at the bottom of it and read.

About two hundred of the nine thousand that afternoon stopped, and could not finish the last line.

A man in a delivery coat said the Board had admitted something. A woman beside him said the Board had admitted that it could not count. Both of them were talking in the flat voice of people who have already agreed with each other, and neither of them was wrong, and there is no instrument in this city that could tell a person standing at the foot of that wall which of the two was right.

He stood at the foot of the stone and worked out, the way he has worked it out about sixty times in four years, how many of them were going to stop at the last line this afternoon instead of the day after the sheet went out. He did not get an answer. There has never been an answer to that and he has never found one, and the whole of what he owns is a man with a bucket and a plate with no figure cut into it.

## Forty Feet

He stood at the public desk in Cordwain for about eleven minutes at about a quarter to four and watched two strangers read the bottom half of his own page.

The first was a man who wanted the figure. He got it in about four seconds and went away satisfied and did not read one word underneath it.

The second was a woman who read the four lines, and then went back up and read the two columns, and then went down and read the office's sentence twice, and then stood there for a while, and then said to the man beside her, in the ordinary voice people use in a street, *that is the address of the thing, that is not a count of anything.*

"That is correct," he said, from about nine feet, and she did not hear him and he did not say it again.

Nobody in this city can be stopped from reading a page at a public desk and about two hundred people a week do it and about four of them in every thousand stop at the bottom.

He went up Cordwain at about a quarter to five and turned in at the yard end and stopped about forty feet short of it.

There are about eleven thousand people in that yard on an ordinary evening and they stand on the ground and on the steps and spill into the street at either end, and there were about four thousand of them on the evening a woman sat at a kitchen table and promised them a column.

At the far end of the yard, against the wall that backs onto the Vestry, a board had been put up.

It was not new. It was an old board, the kind that comes off a shutter, and it had been whitewashed at some point in a decade nobody in the yard could name, and somebody had chalked on it in a hand that came up fast and did not stop to correct itself.

Four lines.

He read them at forty feet.

He had drafted them in forty days from the ninth of March of the second year and read them out at a kitchen off Ferrier Street on the nineteenth of July, and Alma Quayle had read them at a counter off a fourth court and put them in a box.

They had gone out on a public page at four on the sheet of the thirtieth of June of the second year, and about two hundred people a week in this city had read them since, and not one of them had ever been at the top of a hill.

And they were up there now in four-inch chalk on a shutter, and there was no notice at the top of them. No address at the head. No office. No figure and no quarter and no hand but the yard's own.

Somebody had copied them and somebody had left off the part that says where they came from.

He stood at forty feet and looked at it for about two minutes and did not go over.

The woman who keeps the stall at the yard end has known him since the eighteenth of October of the first year. She was packing it away and she saw him looking at the board.

"That is yours," she said.

"Yes."

"Are you going to do anything about it."

He thought about it for about four seconds, which was four seconds longer than he usually gave a question that could not be answered in a sentence.

"No," said Lin Hao. "And if you have anybody up there who wants me to, you can tell them I will not come up this hill and say one word about it, because I said in a room with two chairs in it that I would not take another man's two words off a slate for standing on the wrong surface, and a man who has said that does not get to arrive at a yard and tell a yard what to do with a board."

The woman at the stall put a box down and picked it up again.

"Then it is theirs," she said.

"That is what I have just told you."

"Then why are you standing there."

"Because it is going to be a rule by the seventeenth of January," said Lin Hao, "and I am going to be able to do one single thing about it, and I would rather have said that out loud to somebody than have said it to a shutter."

He went down the hill. He had about a fortnight in front of him and it did not take a fortnight.

*Thirtieth of December of the fourth year, the foot of the ranks wall at ten to four, and the yard end at about a quarter to five. Seventy-five days off the fifteenth of October, which is fifteen days of October after the fifteenth and thirty of November and thirty of December, and fifteen and thirty and thirty is seventy-five.*

*Eight hundred and forty-one for the third quarter running and two hundred people a week read it and none of them is wrong about what it does and all of them are wrong about what it is.*

*Two boxes at the foot of the form and both of them blank since the eighth of October of the first year and I have walked past them about sixty times.*

*And my four lines are on a shutter at the top of that hill with nothing at the top of them saying whose they are, and a woman at a stall asked me if I was going to do anything and I said no out loud on the street.*

# The Nineteenth Sheet

He was in the room off a third court from about ten in the morning and he left the bucket in the corridor, because a man carrying a bucket into a room with a book on the table is a man who is about to be told to leave again.

It is the nineteenth sheet and the ordinal is not in dispute: the first sheet this office put out was the one of the thirtieth of March of its first year, and there has been one every quarter since, and a quarter is three months of thirty days, and a year is three hundred and sixty.

Yusuf Adeyemi was standing with his coat still on and an envelope in his hand, because a Registrar who has been in a building since the first of February of the first year does not take his coat off before four.

“Nothing has come in since June,” he said. “Eleven sheets on the thirty-first of March and nothing at all since. I have looked at the tray four times this morning for a reason I could not put to you and I have now said it out loud.”

“That is not the same as nobody having done anything,” said Wenna Raut, from the counter, without turning round. “It is the same as nobody having sat down and written it out, and the two of those are not the same and there is no column anywhere that says which one a person is supposed to be reading.”

He said the figure anyway, because she reads the figure first and always has.

“Eight hundred and fifty-two,” he said. “For the third quarter running.”

“And it did not move in June either,” she said. “The eleventh of March moved it by eleven and the thirty-first of March did not move it at all, and there was a whole quarter after that where nobody wrote to this address, and this is the first time in four years that a figure has stood still three times running and there is nothing whatever on the page that can tell you why.”

The second column had two lines in it and has had two since the first of May. One of them is his. One of them is a woman he entered without asking, in his own hand, and it has been on a public page since the thirtieth of June, and she cannot read a page.

## The Last Line On The Page

He had read this sheet about nine times before it went out, and he had read the address at the top and the second column and the middle, and he had never once got to the last line.

The last line is the only sentence on the whole of the page that says what the page is, and it is typed at a desk off a fourth court because it is the sentence the form takes, and it has been at the foot of every sheet this office has put out since the first of February of the first year.

*Where this Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it does not say that there was nothing.*

It took him about forty seconds and the second reading took longer than the first, because the second reading is the one where he stops being a man reading a page and starts being an office that owns it.

Then he went down two flights into the third court and out into Cordwain and up the wall at about ten to five, where about nine thousand people went under it because it is an ordinary afternoon at the end of an ordinary month and the trams go under it, and Emil Brack was at the wall end with the slate under his arm.

## The Cross-Read

Brack had a slate under his arm and about nine people in the line and he looked at the man who had come up the wall at ten to five and read a sentence out of a page to a lane at half past four in the afternoon, and he waited for it.

“Read it out,” he said, and it was not a request, and he would not have been able to stop him if it had been.

He read it out, in the street, in his own voice, at about ten to five in the afternoon, and the lane was about half full and nobody came and stood at nine feet and nobody stopped doing what they were doing.

“That is not what I write in chalk,” Brack said. “That is exactly what I write in chalk. That is the whole of what I write in chalk.”

“For three years and about nine figures a morning I have been standing under a wall writing down a number I made up and saying out loud that I made it up, and your office has been printing that sentence four times a year at the bottom of a page for four years in a hand any person in this city can read.”

“They do not read it.”

“No,” said Brack. “They do not read it. That is not a complaint of mine, it is the finding, and I am right about it and so is the man at the desk off the fourth court who typed it, and neither of us can do one single thing about it, which is the reason I am a man with a slate and not a man with a page.”

He turned the slate round and showed him the back of it, which was clean.

“I have written about nine hundred of those figures in three years and I have never once written one of them on a page, because a page gets filed and a slate gets wiped.”

“If one of my numbers ever gets into a building with an address on it then somebody in four years will want to know whether it was one I decided off nothing or one I copied off a wall, and I will not be able to tell them, because it will have been in a hand that is not mine.”

He wiped the slate and put it under his arm again.

“That is the whole of the difference between your office and mine and it is worth exactly nothing to the nine thousand people going over the top of us.”

## What He Could Not Do With It

He stood at about nine feet off the wall with the bucket at his side and did not do the sum he had come down here to do, because he had done it in March and in June and it comes out the same every time.

About two hundred people a week in Cordwain read one of these sheets, and about four in every thousand of them get as far as the bottom, and the bottom is where the second column is and the bottom is where that sentence is. It has been at the foot of the page since the first sheet this office ever put out and there is no instrument in this city that can move a line up.

He wanted the two words where the city already reads every morning. He had been carrying that for four years and he had refused the only two boxes that could have put them there.

And the one sentence his office has ever put in a hand a city can read is not the two words. It is a sentence about having nothing, and it is true, and it is the sentence of a man with a slate, and it is at the bottom of every page.

He stood there with that for about four minutes and worked backwards down it, which is a thing he does and which nobody has ever taught him.

The figure at the top of the page is not a count of deeds and nobody in this city who is right about anything has ever said it is, and it has now stood still three quarter-ends running, and the word at the top of it means two things and the page has never once said which one.

The second column takes a name when somebody chooses to write one there, and it has two lines in it and both of them are his.

And under the whole of it the office says, in the plainest sentence this city has ever printed, that a blank in a book is not a claim that there was nothing.

That is a rule. It is four times a year, at the bottom, in a hand any person can read, and it has been there for four years, and it is the same rule a man with a chalk enforces every morning at a quarter to seven by writing a number he has made up and saying out loud that he has made it up.

“I am not going to be able to use that,” he said.

“You are not going to be able to use it,” said Brack, “because it is at the bottom, and about four in a thousand get to the bottom, and you cannot make anybody read a bottom.”

He went back in to his shift at eleven and did three floors and a stairwell and a bin room.

The sheet went out at four with a clerk's hand on it and the office's own sentence at the bottom, and at half past five in the morning he stood in the dark at the foot of the ranks wall for about ninety seconds and read the two words under the last tile, and the form beside it had both boxes empty in it and had had for about fourteen hundred days.

*The thirtieth of September, one thousand six hundred and seventy-nine days. Less one thousand six hundred and seventy, the twenty-first, is nine. Less one thousand five hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is ninety, and thirty and thirty and thirty is ninety. Less one thousand four hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December, is two hundred and seventy, and that is nine months of thirty days.*

*The nineteenth sheet has gone out at four with eight hundred and fifty-two at the top, unmoved, third quarter running, and two lines in the second column, and under the whole of it the one sentence on the page that says what the page is, which is the same sentence the man with the chalk says out loud every morning and which about four people in every thousand who read the page ever get to.*

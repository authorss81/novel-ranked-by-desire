# Whose Name Goes In A Box

Ten days from the thirtieth of June to this morning. The thirtieth of June was one thousand five hundred and eighty-nine days off the first of February of the first year, and this morning is one thousand five hundred and ninety-nine, and one thousand five hundred and ninety-nine less one thousand five hundred and eighty-nine is ten.

The tenth of July is ten days into the seventh month of that year, and the seventh month has thirty days and no thirty-first, and the twentieth of July is ten days off, because twenty less ten is ten.

The repainting comes round every month, and the form for July went up the morning after the quarter ended, with the eighteenth sheet under it on the public desk and the paint still tacky round the last tile.

## The Standing Box

The division that prints the form sent a second sheet to the room off a third court, asking for the standing to go in the right box. It was the same hand as the first sheet, on the same paper, with the quarter at the top and one line under it.

Yusuf Adeyemi read it twice and put it on the table beside the book. The book had twenty quarter-ends on its sheets now, counting the eighteenth, and the second column had two lines in it, and about two hundred people a week read one.

“They want a standing,” Adeyemi said. “The left box was answered in June and the answer was no. The right box has never been answered. A standing is a thing a city can read without stopping, and the two words at the foot of this wall are a standing no city has ever been able to read.”

Lin Hao stood with his hands in his coat pockets, because there was nothing in the room for a man to hold. He felt fear of a different kind than the dread of December, and it was fear that a box that holds a standing would be filled with a name that cannot carry it.

“The standing they want is the standing of the thing at the address,” he said. “The thing at the address is a Register with four hundred and nineteen accounts in it, with an hour against each and no figure on any of them, and a second column with two lines in it.”

Wenna Raut squared the division's sheet and said the one thing a clerk in that room is permitted to say about a box.

“There is no form for putting a name in a box,” she said. “A clerk may only enter what she is given. If a name goes in that box, it goes in because a person wrote it there, and no instrument in this building can take it back out.”

He wanted time, and there was no time to have, because the painters put the form up in the morning and take it down when the paint is dry, and the July form would come down before the twentieth.

## The Public Desk

He went to the public desk in Cordwain at about midday, because a question about a standing is a question about what a city reads, and the only way to answer it is to watch a city read.

The eighteenth sheet was on the desk under the quarter's heading, with eight hundred and fifty-two at the top and about four hundred and nineteen lines under it, one with his name at the head and about four hundred and eighteen other people's words underneath, and two lines in the second column. The office's own sentence was at the foot of it and he did not write it.

He stood at about nine feet for about eleven minutes and watched about nine people come to the desk. Four of them wanted a figure and got one in about four seconds and read not one word underneath. Two of them read the four lines at the top of a division board across the street and went away.

One of them, a woman with a basket, read the whole sheet top to bottom twice, the way Wenna Raut reads, and stopped at the second column and read the two lines there three times.

She looked up at him standing there in his coat with the white plate in it, and she did not know him.

“Two of them,” she said. “There was one of them on the last one.”

“There were seven quarter-ends with nothing in it,” Lin Hao said. “Then one line since the fifteenth of October. Now two.”

“What is the second one,” she said.

“It is a woman I never asked and never saw,” he said. “She did not write the page and she cannot read it. She is going to find out from a stranger at a desk, the way you are finding out.”

The woman put her basket down and read the two lines a fourth time. About four in every thousand of the people who read one get as far as the bottom, and she was one of the four.

“If her name is on your page,” she said, “then somebody ought to tell her before a stranger does.”

“There is no instrument for telling her,” Lin Hao said. “There is no form, no column, no fund and no hearing for it. A clerk may only enter what she is given, and what she was given was entered exactly as given.”

The woman picked up her basket and went out into Cordwain, and he stood at the desk for another nine seconds with his hands in his pockets. He felt the fear again, and under it a second feeling he had not expected, which was grief that the sheet he had gotten out without correcting anything was now a sheet that corrected people.

## What He Carried

Adeyemi looked at the two lines in the second column for a long time without touching the book.

“Whose name would you put there, if you were the division,” he said. It was not a question he wanted answered in the room. It was a question he wanted carried out of it.

Lin Hao carried it out into Cordwain in daylight, with the eighteenth sheet on every desk behind him and the July form blank ahead of him, and he understood that the movement that began with a figure now wanted a standing, and that the standing would cost more than the figure.

*The tenth of July, one thousand five hundred and ninety-nine days. Ten days from the thirtieth of June. The division wants a standing in the right box, and there is no form for putting a name in a box, and he has carried the question out of the room without answering it.*

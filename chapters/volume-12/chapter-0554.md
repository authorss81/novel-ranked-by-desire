# Her One Question In Eleven Years

One person reads it, and it is him, and he came down the two flights at about ten past four in the afternoon of the second of January with nothing in his coat.

One day from the first of January to this afternoon, and seventy-seven days from the morning he put a name in a column in a room off a third court: fifteen days of October after the fifteenth, and thirty of November, and thirty of December, and two of January, and fifteen and thirty and thirty and two is seventy-seven.

The chain came off the shelf and the book came off the shelf and the strap came off, and she put all three on the table and uncorked the ink and did not turn the page round to face him.

"You have until I have finished my cigarette," said Hanneke Ruis, "and after that you are going to say one thing and I am going to say one thing and then this room is going to close."

## What Is On It

He read it standing up, over her shoulder, the way anybody standing in that room reads anything on that table.

The address at the head of the block: premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight. She has written that line in the same hand for thirty years and it is the oldest ink on the sheet and it has gone grey at the edges.

The figure, which is eight.

And under it the six words.

*Carried forward. Unclosed. No holder.*

And under them her own name, in her own division, in her own hand, and she has never once in thirty years left that off.

And under her name there was a fifth line.

It was not in her hand. It was in a hand he would have known on a wall, on a slate, in a coat, in a cold room at a quarter to five, on a bin-room tally board at four in the morning, and there was no part of him that had to stop and decide.

*Premises thirty-four, six, one-one-eight, charged with a claim against a named person. The fifteenth of October. Lin Hao, rank one.*

A premises keeps what the city may read about it, and on the morning of the first of January that came in with the quarter's returns in a bundle off a desk off a fourth court, and a keeper cannot improve what she is given about a premises and cannot be asked to hurry about it, and so it went into the block underneath six words that had been written thirty years earlier by a woman standing on the same side of the same table.

Four inches of a page. One sentence under another, in one hand, saying it belonged to nobody, and a name four inches below it saying a man had taken it and owed it.

He read it twice.

## The Line She Cannot Take Out

She knew what he was looking at. She had known for four weeks and she had said nothing to anybody, and he had come down two flights on the first of January and stood in a cold room for two hours and she had watched him not ask.

"I am not going to explain it," she said. "If I explain it, it is me that put it there, and it is not me that put it there."

"It went out on a public page on the thirtieth of December."

"It did. With an address at the head of every line and about two hundred people a week in this city entitled to stand at a desk and read it." She put her hand flat on the margin, not on the ink. "I cannot take it out of a page that the whole of Ostara may read. I have no instrument for taking anything out of it and neither have you and neither has the man who is Registrar of something four floors up."

"And the six words."

"Are mine."

She squared the book about nine degrees, which she has done perhaps twice in four years of his coming down here.

"And if I took those off I would be striking a line I wrote myself out of a composition I have certified every quarter for thirty years, and I would be the woman who struck it, and then the composition would be nothing but a man's claim on premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight, and there would be no reason in this city for any of it to stay open."

She put the nib down on its string.

"So there are two lines on one page and they do not agree with each other, and that is what a premises thirty-four, six, one-one-eight is made of as of this afternoon, and I am the only person in Ostara who certifies what it is made of, and I have certified it as a thing that disagrees with itself."

"And that is what is holding it open."

"That is what is holding it open," said Hanneke Ruis. "Not the six words. The six words were what held it open while there was nothing underneath them to disagree with."

## Her One Question

She put the book down and turned round and looked at him, and it was the first time in four years that she had not been looking at a page while she spoke to him.

"I have had one question in this room for about eleven years."

She said it without moving her hands.

"I asked it of a man who has been dead twenty-two years and I have not asked a second one in a room in this city since, and I have told nobody that I still had it, including you. I have been in this room with you four times in two years and I have not asked it of you because I was not going to spend it on a man with a bucket."

"I did not know that was what was in it."

"Nobody knew. That is what having one is." She did not move her hands. "Here it is, and it cannot be asked twice, and I am asking it once."

"For thirty years I have written six words four times a year under a figure with my own name on them, and I have always believed — not hoped, believed, every quarter, in ink — that I was keeping an account open. And it has not been an account. I did not know that until a man whose name is on my page told me what an account is for in September of the third year and then told me again in a kitchen four years ago."

"So the question is the one I have had. Was there ever an account, or was there only the absence of a name. And if it was only the absence of a name, then a name which is the name of a debtor is not going to hold it open the way no name held it open, and the six words are a thing I have been saying out loud four times a year for thirty years about a thing that was never there."

She stopped, and she waited for it, and she had plainly been waiting eleven years.

He did not answer.

He stood in the cold with her and he understood the shape of the question exactly, and the answer to it is not a sentence anybody can say, because the answer depends on what the account turned out to be made of and he does not know that and has not known it since the first of October of the fourth year.

"I cannot answer that," he said, "and I am not going to pretend I can, and I am not going to give you a version of it that would make you feel better, and I have wanted to give you one for about eleven minutes."

"That is the answer I expected and it is the first time in eleven years that anybody has said it to me instead of saying something else."

"And it is spent. I want it understood that it is spent and that I spent it on you. You will not be able to go on being a name on a page after this afternoon, and neither am I going to be able to go on being a keeper who wrote six words and did not know what they were for."

## Afterward

She took the chain off the table and hung it on its hook and put the strap across the book and squared it.

That is when she said it, standing, with her hand on the strap and no page open any more and nothing in the room to be certified.

"You have come down these two flights four times in two years and you have never once looked at me, and I have counted all four, and I would like it on the record that I counted them and not that I was waiting."

She turned round.

"I have not been in a room with anybody in about nine years and the last one was a different sort of room and a worse one. I am not going to lie to you about what I am about to do, because I have never once lied to a person who came down here to read a page."

"I want this. I have wanted it since about the ninth of July and I worked that out on the ninth of July and did not do anything about it for four months, and that is the whole of my interest in your page and it has nothing to do with premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight."

He did not say a word, because there is one thing kept in that room and he was in it.

"Correct," said Hanneke Ruis.

She came off the stool and put her hand flat against the front of his coat the way a woman tests a door she has every intention of opening, and she was not cold after about nine seconds and neither was the room.

"I have been on my feet in rooms like this since before you were out of your year," she said. "So I know what I am doing and you are not going to be told anything about it twice."

She got his coat open and then his shirt, and her hands went down the flat of him and came back up, unhurried, the way a hand goes down a column of a page it has read many times and does not hurry itself. She looked up at him.

"Ask me the thing you have wanted to ask since about the ninth of July," she said. "Go on. It is the only question this room will answer today."

He asked it.

"Say that again."

"You have never once looked at me."

"Four times in two years and you have not looked at me once, and I have thought about that more than is reasonable," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I would like it understood that the reason is not modesty. It is that a man who looks at a keeper in her own room is a man who is about to put something on a page, and nothing has ever gone on one of my pages that I did not put there."

She got her own dress up over her hips and did not finish it and waited for him to come to her rather than telling him to, and when he did she took his weight on the edge of the table and the lamp on its stand moved about two inches and neither of them looked at it.

She was better at this than anybody he had ever been with, and she was not fast about it and she was not slow about it either, and there was nothing performed in the whole of it, because there was nobody to perform it for, and because she had said out loud that she wanted it and had meant it, and a woman who has said that out loud does not have to act as though anybody has been persuaded.

"You are used to people being careful with you," she said. "I can tell. It is in the shoulders and it is in the way you wait to be asked. I am thirty years in rooms that go cold and I have not been careful with anybody since about the year your Ferrier line was cut, and I am not going to start now with the only man who has come down two flights in two years."

She told him what she wanted next in one flat sentence and he did it, with his mouth against her throat where he could feel her pulse going and his hands where she had put them, and she told him what she wanted after that in one sentence and he did that too, and there was no part of it in which either of them asked the other whether they were sure.

She put a hand flat on the back of his head and held him there when she wanted him to stay, and let go of him when she wanted him to move, and neither of them used a word for it. Twice she got him up off the edge of the table and back into her with her own hips. Once she held him still under her palm and took her time getting her breath, and he held still, and he had not been still for anybody in four years.

"Wait," she said. "I have been in this room thirty years and I am going to do this properly and I am not going to be quick about the first part of it."

She put two fingers on his chest and turned him about ninety degrees and sat down on the table and drew him in front of her, took him into her mouth with her hand closed round the base of him, and worked on him with her mouth and her hands and no hurry at any point in it.

The thing that made it different from every other time in four years was that she kept stopping. Not to make a point. To look at him, in a room with no window and one lamp, about four times, for as long as it takes to read a figure.

"You have a cough," she said.

"I have had it since the first of October of the fourth year."

"It is four years." She said it without any sympathy in it at all. "It is not a cold. I am a keeper and I have been certifying the condition of about nine hundred premises for thirty years and I know what four years of that sounds like on a man in a cold room." She put her hand flat against his ribs. "And you have never once sat down in that room."

"I stood."

"I know you stood. I have watched you stand four times." She got her hand behind his neck and pulled his head down until their foreheads were touching. "You may sit on the table. That is the only new thing I am going to say about furniture."

She held the back of his neck with one hand and took him into her in her own time, all the way in, and told him what she wanted next in short flat sentences in the order she wanted it, with her mouth against his collarbone. She did not ask him once whether he wanted it and he did not tell her once that he did not have to, and the table went about half an inch across the floor on its old feet and neither of them heard it.

"I have thought about this room since about the ninth of July," she said. "Not about you. About the room. There is nowhere in this city a person can go where what is said cannot be used against them, and there are about eleven thousand people who go up a hill every week and are told there is one, and it is not here."

"And a keeper in a room with no window is not anybody anybody goes to. So I have been four months with a thing I had worked out and no reason to do anything with it, and you came down the stair, and I did not ask you a question for eleven years and I am not going to start now."

She said it into his collarbone and not to the room.

"That is the long version," she said. "You do not have to have asked for it."

She got her mouth on his throat and on the line of his collarbone and worked back up again and took him in her mouth and used her hands at the same time, unhurried, the way she was doing everything else, and she said something about his hands that made him laugh, which nobody has made him do since the first year, and which she was pleased with in a way she did not try to hide.

"There," she said, when he had finished laughing. "That is the face. I have been reading that book in my own handwriting for thirty years and the one thing it never says on the page is that anybody in this room can make that face."

He asked her why she had never asked anybody the question.

"Because they would all have given me an answer," said Hanneke Ruis, "and the whole of what six words are for is that nobody has answered it."

"You are warm," she said. "That is the whole of what I have got. You are warm and you are in a cold room and I have been in a cold room for thirty years."

"You can say what you want to say," she said, a while later, when he had started to say something. "There is nobody in this room. Say it."

He said it.

"Right," she said. "That is not written down anywhere."

Then she got her dress down and put her hand flat against his chest and left it there, and his heart was under it and hers was not, and he said he was sorry about that and she said he was not to.

"You do not know what that is," she said. "I have had it for thirty years and it has never once been useful and it is not going to improve because a man is in the room. That is a thing you will find out in about four years and it will not be a shock."

They took the lamp off the stand at some point and put it on the floor because it was in the way of an elbow, and the room got a little darker and neither of them cared.

She sat up on the edge of the table afterwards with her feet hanging and her hands on her knees and put her head back and laughed about four seconds, which she had not expected to do either.

"Thirty years," she said. "Thirty years of one lamp and one chair and a chained book that a person may read on one day and not the day before. And I have never once in this room had anybody in it who was not going to leave at a quarter to five, and I want it noticed that I noticed tonight and that it has not been a habit."

She got off the table and picked the lamp up off the floor and put it back on the stand and wound the wick down about a finger's width, which is what she does.

Then she said the thing, standing at the shelf with the strap in her hand, and she said it out loud and she said it to the room.

"You will go up those stairs now," said Hanneke Ruis. "And you will not come down them again until a quarter is past in a fortnight, and I will write the six words again exactly as I have written them four times a year for thirty years, in my hand, under my name, and I will not strike the line under it, and I will not be thanked for that either."

"And there is a page in this city with two lines on it that do not agree with each other, and that is what the premises is made of, and a thing two people have written down on one page and cannot agree about has never once in this city been shut."

She took the book off the shelf.

"The account is open," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is open because I wrote the six words first and then a man put a name under them, and not the other way round, and that is the only reason there is anything in this room to hold anything open with. It is in the order of the ink. Do not take it out of that order in your head, whatever anybody tells you on a hill."

*Second of January of year five. One day after the first and seventy-seven days after the fifteenth of October. The counting room, two floors under a sales floor.*

*She read the page first and asked afterwards, which is the only order that leaves it open, and she said so out loud and she was right.*

*And she spent her one question on a man she has met four times in two years and never once looked at, and she told him she wanted it before she had it and not afterwards.*

*And I have no answer for her and I did not give her a worse one, and there are two lines on that page and neither of them is true and both of them are hers, or mine, and the thing they are both about is still open, and it is still open because a keeper wrote down what she believed before a man told her what it was for.*

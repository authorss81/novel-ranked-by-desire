# Her Brother And A Copy Of It

Eight days from the thirtieth of March to this afternoon, and this afternoon is one thousand five hundred and seven days off the first of February of the first year.

One thousand four hundred and forty was the first of February of year five, the first of March was one thousand four hundred and seventy, the first of April is the day after the thirtieth of March and is one thousand five hundred, and this is the eighth, and one thousand five hundred and seven less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is eight.

The seventeenth sheet had been out eight days, and about two hundred people a week read a standing sheet somewhere in Cordwain, and about four in every thousand of them stop at the bottom.

## At The Foot Of The Hill

He went up Cordwain at about four in the afternoon and turned in at the yard end and stopped where the ground begins to go up, which is where he stopped on the tenth of January and on the thirtieth, and he had said out loud on a street in front of a woman at a stall that he would not come up that hill and say one word about a board.

He was not going to say one word about a board.

About four o'clock a man came down off that hill on the far side of the arcade and stopped at the bottom of it about nine feet off him and turned round and looked back up the slope, the way a man does when he has just finished something.

He had a folded sheet under his arm and about nine inches of paint on the back of his right hand and a book in the other pocket with the corners gone soft.

"You will be the man from the office," he said.

"I am."

"Then I have got about nine minutes and I have got two things to say and one of them is a question and I am going to ask it whatever you say, because that is the whole of what this is."

## What He Does For A Living

"I am at the coal wharf off Cordwain," said Piotr Vask. "I load. That is the whole of it — I put it on and I take it off and nobody in this city has ever asked me a question about my life and I have never once been asked one by anybody."

"That is not a thing anybody wants."

"It is the exact thing anybody wants," said Vask. "Eight hours a day, four hundred men coming past me, and I have to say a thing to every one of them. *Where is it going.* That is the job. I have been putting one question to a stranger after another for about eleven years and not one of them has ever cost me anything."

He took the folded sheet out from under his arm and opened it.

"Somebody gave me one of these on the twenty-second of March at about six in the morning at a gate on Cordwain, and I have read it about four hundred times, and I have got to the end of it and I do not think the man who wrote it knows what it is."

## What He Has Been Doing

"I have been at the foot of that yard every evening since the twenty-second of March," he said. "Not up the hill. At the bottom of it, at the foot of the steps, where about four hundred people come down at seven and about nine hundred on the days they come down in daylight."

"That is most days."

"That is most days and I have never worked out which days they are, because I do not count the days and I have decided that I am not going to start."

"You have been standing at the bottom of those steps asking people your four questions."

"Yes."

"Not the yard. Not a board. The people."

"The people, one at a time, in the ordinary way, in a voice, out loud, standing where they can hear me and answer me or not." He put the sheet down on the low wall.

"About two hundred and forty. I have asked two hundred and forty people and I have written down every answer in a book and I have got about a hundred and eleven yeses and about a hundred and twenty-nine noes and the noes are the better half and I want to explain the noes first because nobody asks about the noes."

## The Noes

"Ninety-one of them said no to the first one because they had not been given anything since about the year their mother died," said Piotr Vask. "About forty said no to the second one because they cannot name the day. About thirty of the rest said no to the third because they cannot tell you a face and they do not think anybody is going to believe one."

"And they are all right."

"They are all right and I am writing it down in that order, because that is the order they came out in, and if I put them in a different order then I have improved them and they are not mine to improve." He shut the book.

"I did not know I was doing that. I have been doing that for about a fortnight and I have only found out that I am doing it about an hour ago and I have come down here to be told I am wrong, and I have not got to the fourth question and the fourth question is the one that matters."

## The Fourth Question

"You have not asked the fourth one."

"I have not asked the fourth one to a single person in two hundred and forty attempts and I would like to know why before I ask it two hundred and forty times."

He said it as though it were a trade question.

"Because it is the one I wrote the other three for," he said. "I did not write any of them. Somebody in this city wrote all four and put them on a plate and a printer printed eight hundred of them and a woman on Cordwain handed me one at a gate at six in the morning. And I have worked out what the first three are. They are what I have to hold before I am allowed to ask. And the fourth one is the ask."

"And the fourth one is that the thing it describes is not on a page anywhere in this city."

"That is what I am told it says and I do not know if I have it right because I am a man who loads coal."

He looked at the book in his pocket.

"It is about the eighteenth or nineteenth of the year before last that about a hundred and eleven of the two hundred and forty people said yes, and every one of them could say the day and the place and the person, and there is not one page in this city about any of them, because there is not one page in this city about most things anybody does."

## What He Has Done With The Hundred And Eleven

He did not open the book.

"I have not taken them anywhere," he said. "I have got them in a book in a room in Lamp Street that a woman pays the rent of and that I am not going to tell you about, and I have not taken them to the Register off a third court, and I have not taken them to a magistrate, and I have not taken them to your branch, and I have thought about taking them to your branch for about a fortnight."

"Why have you not."

"Because a clerk in an office may only enter what she is given," said Piotr Vask.

"I know that. Everybody in this district knows that. And I am not going to walk a hundred and eleven people's own words into a room where one person is allowed to ask questions, because the day that is in a book is the day your office is administering them, and you said in a yard at half past eleven on the thirtieth that you were going to do nothing, and I have read about that on a shutter and I believed it."

## The Question He Was Going To Ask

"Ask it," said Lin Hao.

"You wrote them."

"I wrote them. Forty days in a kitchen off Ferrier Street in the second year, and I read them out at that kitchen on the nineteenth of July, and they went out on a public page on the thirtieth of June of that year, and there is about two hundred people a week in this city who have read them since."

Piotr Vask stood there for a moment with the folded sheet still in his hand.

"Then here it is, and I am asking it out loud on a street in front of nobody, and you can answer it or you can not answer it, and either way I have asked it and it is out of my mouth and it is not in your book."

He said it like a man reading out a number at a coal face.

"Where is it on a page."

And there was not one instrument in this city that could answer that in a street, in a voice, in the ordinary way, on an ordinary afternoon, and no form in Ostara for it, and no clerk who could be handed it, and no schedule and no fund and no hearing and no seal, and there is a line in a book off a third court with his name at the bottom of it and everything he asks this month is going to be entered somewhere.

Lin Hao stood on a pavement in the last of the afternoon light and worked out the true answer and then worked out whether the true answer was a thing he was permitted to say in a street.

"It is on about eight hundred pages in this city," he said. "The conditions are. A plate off Cordwain and a printer who could not say no to anybody."

"That is not what I asked you."

"No," he said. "It is not. And I am not able to answer the other one in a street, and there are three people in this city who would understand why, and not one of the three is standing on this pavement."

## Her Brother

"My sister reads everything twice," said Piotr Vask, when he had put the book away. "It is not a figure of speech and I am not joking. She has read this four times and she has been asking me for about a fortnight to bring her one and I have not brought her one, because she does not need one and if I bring her one she will put it in front of me and say what is wrong with it and she is right."

"What is wrong with it."

"That it is the right size for a counter," said Vask. "That is what is wrong with it, and I have known it since the fourth of April, and so have about nine hundred people who worked it out on their own in a yard on the sixteenth of January, and my sister worked it out before me off a printed sheet and she has never set foot in that yard in her life."

He picked up the folded sheet and looked at it again as though he were seeing it for the first time.

"She is going to put something down," he said.

"I am telling you that because you are the only man in this city who would want to know and because it is going to be on about a hundred and forty people's names and there is not one thing anybody in your office can do with a hundred and forty names and I would rather say it out loud on a pavement than have you find out about it afterwards and think I had come to warn you or had come to ask you for something."

"What is she going to put down."

"That is hers and I have been told about it and not asked," said Piotr Vask, "and I have stood on this pavement for about nine minutes and asked a man from an office one question and he has answered half of it honestly and told me why the other half cannot be answered, and I am going to go up to Lamp Street and I am not going to come down this hill again until I have read all four of them to a person who wants to know."

He went up Cordwain at the top of the street and did not stop, and he had about nine inches of paint on the back of his hand and about a hundred and eleven yeses in a book in his pocket.

*Eighth of April of year five, the foot of the yard, about ten to five in the afternoon. Eight days since the seventeenth sheet and about eight hundred copies of four lines in this city.*

*A man who loads coal at a wharf off Cordwain has been at the foot of those steps since the twenty-second of March and has put all four questions to about two hundred and forty people one at a time, and he has written down every answer, and he has got about a hundred and eleven yeses and about a hundred and twenty-nine noes, and the noes are the better half.*

*He has not brought them here because a clerk may only enter what she is given, and he does not want them in a book here, and he read what I said in a yard on a shutter and believed it.*

*And he asked me one question in the street and I answered half of it and I could not answer the other half, and the half I could not answer is the only thing I have ever wanted a standing for.*

*And his sister reads everything twice and she is going to put something down, and there are about a hundred and forty names on it, and he told me that on a pavement because he thought I would want to know.*

*And I have nothing to say to any of it and I am not going to see any of it again.*

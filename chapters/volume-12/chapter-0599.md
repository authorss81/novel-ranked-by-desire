# Thirty Days Of December

He worked the branch nights through the first twenty days of December of the fifth year, eleven at night until half past five, four nights and three off, with the rota page on its nail and four names on it and no band and no figure, and every morning after shift he walked past the standing sheet where it lay out in Cordwain.

Seventeen days had gone since the third. One thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and forty-two is seventeen, and twenty less three is seventeen by the month count, and in the seventeen days December had done what December always does, which was shorten the light and lengthen the lines.

He wanted the month to hold. That was his want, superstitious and plain, and he admitted it to himself on the stairs at about two in the morning the way a man admits a cough.

The December form stood at the foot of the ranks wall with both boxes blank, and the wall above it carried the office's two words in paint at the height of a name on a plate. Below the two words the white tile with his name on it in chalk stood with nothing at all under it.

About four inches of stone sat under the tile, cleaned every morning by a woman with a short-handled broom and a canvas bag, last, because it is under the wall and nobody spits there.

He stood about two feet off those four inches on the twentieth, because they belong to her and not to him, and looked at the two empty boxes for a long time.

Nobody had filled either. The left box holds a figure and the right holds a standing, and both have been blank since the eighth of October of the first year, and a division that asked for the figure in June was answered no, and a division that asked for the standing in July was answered not for her, and no hand in Ostara had touched either since.

That refusal held. He felt it hold the way a door holds, and he was glad of it with a gladness that had grief in it.

Isolde Marchetti came through the corridor at half past two with a bundle of returns under her arm and stopped at the board, which had lost a second corner to the damp since October, so that the three lines were folded over one another at the top and the blade-cut edge of the fourth had gone brown.

“They are still reading the foot,” she said. “About two hundred people a week read one somewhere in Cordwain and about four in every thousand get to the bottom, and now the bottom is the only part anybody talks about.”

“What do they say,” he said.

“That the office finally wrote something a person can disagree with,” she said. “And that disagreement is better than paint nobody can read.”

She went on down the corridor and he went up to the third floor with the bucket, and his chest hurt the way it has hurt since the spring of the third year, and Tessa Okonkwo's taking sat in him still, quiet, a reserve he had not spent.

## The Cold Stair

On the eighteenth he carried coal up an outside stair for a woman in Lamp Street who keeps a house of thirty-one rooms, because her man was down with a cough and the stair ices in December, and the work took him two hours and left his hands raw.

She keeps no roll and works for nobody and reads everything twice, and she told him on the landing that about ninety people who cannot read chalk had come to her doorway in April and said their names out loud, and that she had written them down and handed them on, and that she would do it again.

“You wrote them and you cannot read them back to them,” he said.

“I wrote them so they would be somewhere,” she said. “A list is a thing you do to people who cannot pay, and the moment it leaves my hand it stops being mine. I know that and I did it anyway, because a doorway is warmer than a shutter.”

He carried the last sack up and stood on the landing with his breath showing, and felt the physical risk of the iced stair under him, and felt glad of work that asked only his body. The office, the sheet, the two sentences, all of it fell away for an hour, and what was left was a stair and sacks and a woman who refuses the office twice and is right both times.

On the twentieth of December a man at the public desk told him the sheet with the two sentences had been out thirty-seven days, because thirteen less thirty with thirty after it is thirteen and thirty in November with twenty after it is fifty, and fifty less thirteen is thirty-seven, and nobody had written on it, because in nine years of desks in Ostara nobody ever has.

He went home in daylight and slept, and dreamed of eleven books with ringed figures, and woke with his hands tight and his want unchanged: to get the twentieth sheet out at four on the thirtieth with everything on it exactly as it is, and without correcting anything.

*The twentieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine days. Less one thousand seven hundred and forty-two, the third, is seventeen. Twenty less three is seventeen by the month count.*

*Less the twenty-third of November, one thousand seven hundred and thirty-two, is twenty-seven. Less the tenth of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, is seventy. The twentieth sheet goes out on the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine less one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine is ten.*

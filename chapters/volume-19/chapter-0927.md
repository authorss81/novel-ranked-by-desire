# Refused In Writing Again

Four thousand and one hundred and twenty-three was the fourteenth of last month and the twenty-first of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and thirty, and four thousand and one hundred and thirty less four thousand and one hundred and twenty-three is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

## Three Days After The Wall

The wall went up on the eighteenth and by the twenty-first the stair had noticed.

It was not a flood. It was about four people who asked about the same thing in three days, and it was a question that had never once been put in nine years, because there was nothing on a door to put it about until there was a paragraph about the door on a wall four hundred yards off.

The first of them was at about eleven on the nineteenth and the second at about half past ten on the twentieth, and the third came up on the twenty-first with the piece of paper already folded twice.

The first one was not asking for a refusal. He had come up to say that the paragraph on that wall was wrong, because it said that this office does not answer in writing, and he had in fact written to it, once, in the second month of this year, and had got a slip back.

That is the thing this office had not thought about, and it took the clerk about four seconds to see it.

A paragraph on a wall is read as a statement about everybody, and it had been up three days, and a man had gone back to the moment he wrote a letter, and had gone through the whole of it again, and had come up four flights to tell a clerk that a thing she had done was not true of him.

That is what a true sentence does in a hall. It does not have to be wrong. It only has to be true of most people, and then it finds the one it is not true of and goes and stands in front of him.

## What A Man Has To Do With A Refusal He Did Not Get

He was about fifty and he had come off a night shift, and he put a slip on that table and put his thumb on it and said that he had come on the twelfth at a quarter to eight because that is what the paper he had been given said, and there was not one person on the landing, and he had gone back to work, and the woman whose hours they were had been told that by him.

"And she does not believe I went," he said. "She thinks I did not go, or she thinks I did not care, and I have told her I went twice and she has asked me for something I can show her."

"What would you show her."

"Anything with your office on it." He took his thumb off the slip. "A piece of paper saying you are there between a quarter to ten and half past five and that I came at a quarter to eight on your own instructions. That is all. That is the whole of what I am asking for."

## She Answered Before He Did

Wenna Raut put her pen down.

She had been in that room since a quarter to ten and she had heard about four people and a wall and a figure and a caption, and she had heard Lin Hao refuse a division building on the fourteenth in about four seconds, and she answered a man who had been in the room for ninety seconds.

"I am going to answer this one," she said, "and not him, and he will tell you afterwards that I did it and you will think less of the office for it, and I want that to happen in that order."

"Say it."

"A refusal said in a doorway is heard by the person it is said to and by about two others, and it is gone by the end of the week." She did not look at Lin Hao.

"A refusal on paper is shown to people who were not in the room. Every one of them counts as a second refusal, and the second refusal is a true thing that happened. It is not true about this office's work being slow, it is true about a man being sent to a door at the wrong hour, and the paper is the reason the woman believes you did not go."

He stood there.

"The paper does not slow it down," said Wenna Raut. "It travels at the speed of an annoyance, and that is faster than a person goes up four flights, and there is no version of a piece of paper that arrives once."

## What Paper Does To A Refusal

"And if you gave me one," he said. "Just one."

"Then we have given one, and we will be asked again the next week by a person who has heard that we give them." She squared the pen against the table.

"About nine people a fortnight come up these stairs, and about nine of the nine have now been told by a wall four hundred yards off that this office does not answer in writing. If we hand over one sheet in this month, then in three months somebody in that hall is going to be handed a sheet about a man in a night shift. Halls keep things, and the woman who keeps them will be right, and she will tell him it came from us."

He put both hands on the table.

"Will you say the same thing next time."

"Next time, and the time after that, and every time you come, until you stop coming or until I stop being the one who says it, and I am telling you now that it has stopped being his sentence and it has become mine, because he said it on the fourteenth to a woman in a boardman's coat and it sounded better in his mouth than it was going to sound in mine."

## The Entry, And Who Decided It

At about two she said it out loud to that room, door wide, both carriers on that stair.

"Go out and tell the man," she said. "That I am going to say this where it is written down, and that it is going in the book, and that it is going in because this office said it and not because anybody wrote it about us."

"That is the rule you set in May and it applies to everything."

"It applies to everything and I am using it on myself." She wrote the date and the hour and her own words. "Read it back to me."

He read it back and she put the pen down.

"That is the first thing in that book since the second of December of the ninth year that is not a question and not an answer," she said, "and it is in there because a woman in a passage answered a man who was owed something, and he was owed it, and she was not wrong, and it is going to cost me about nine doors a month and it is going to cost him a woman who is owed something and cannot prove it either."

## The Man Went Down Four Flights With Nothing

He told the man in full, standing up, in the ordinary voice, the door wide and about four people on that stair, and he did not say it kindly and he did not say it short.

The office is there between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour. The card says so and the card is on the door four flights up this stair.

On that morning at a quarter to eight the door was shut because the door is shut at a quarter to eight, and the man was sent there by a piece of paper this office did not write, and this office will not write a second one to contradict it.

Then he added the thing she had told him to add, which was the part that was actually for her.

"Tell her you came at a quarter to eight on the twelfth and that you knocked and that nobody was there, because that is what happened, and tell her that you can say it again in front of her tomorrow, and that a piece of paper in your hand is not going to make it truer and is going to make it travel."

The man took about four seconds over that.

"My sister is the one who is not believing me," he said. "It is not the woman whose hours they are. It is my sister, and she went to that door herself at eight o'clock on the ninth before I ever had the paper, and she says the place is shut, and she says it like a person reporting a thing she has seen."

Then he went down four flights with nothing in his hand, which is what everybody goes down those four flights with.

Lin Hao stood at the top of that stair after he had gone and worked out that the refusal had cost him the only proof he had asked for, and that the man had gone away with less than he came up with, and that in about four months that man is going to tell somebody about a night shift and a stair and a woman at a wall.

He said that out loud in that room at about half past two with the door open, because he had learned in June that a price that is not heard is not a price.

And then he worked out the rest of it, which was the part that had been standing in the room since the fourteenth of this month and waiting for him to get to it.

From today the words are hers.

Not hers to choose. Hers to say.

She is a clerk in an office that has spent nine years keeping the door shut and the foot of the page clean, and from this month she is the voice at the top of four narrow flights at about eleven in the morning and again at about four, five days in seven, to about nine people a fortnight.

She cannot go into a room and shut a door, and she will not be able to be kind about it, because being kind about it is the soft sentence, and the soft sentence is a different set of words, and a different set of words gets copied.

"The rota," he said.

"I am on it four days and off three," said Wenna Raut, "and you are on four and seven at a branch office off a third court until half past five in the morning, and the two of us are the only two people who will ever say any of it."

One refusal in a book, entered in that office's own hand because that office said it out loud, and a division building four hundred yards off that has been told twice now in writing that this office does not answer in writing, and a woman who went to a door at eight o'clock on the ninth of the month and came back able to report it.

Four thousand and one hundred and thirty days. Less four thousand and one hundred and twenty-three, the fourteenth of last month, is seven, and a clerk answered a man before the man who owns the room did, and the office entered the reason for a refusal for the first time in nine years, and it cost her the door and him the proof.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty-one, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-one of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and ten days, which is fifty-seven months and no days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

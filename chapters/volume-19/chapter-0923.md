# The Returns Desk

Four thousand and ninety-six was the seventeenth of last month and the twenty-fourth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and three, and four thousand and one hundred and three less four thousand and ninety-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## The Half-Landing Between Two Floors

He came up the stairs of that building at about eleven and stopped on the half-landing between the ground floor and the first, where there is a stone step and a rail and nowhere to put anything down, and there was a piece of paper on the step.

It had his street on it and the door and the hours, in a good clean hand, and the hours were wrong by an hour at the start, and it had been folded twice and opened out again.

He stood on that landing and looked at it for about nine seconds and did not touch it, and then went on up to the counters hall.

He did not touch it because a thing left on a landing between two floors belongs to neither floor until somebody writes down which one it came from, and he had worked that out on the step before he had finished working out that he wanted to pick it up.

That is the whole rule and there is nobody in that building who has ever written it down.

## One Question Of A Thing

The second counter is hers and she has been at it eleven years and she takes one question of a thing at it and only one.

He had his ready and it had been ready since the seventeenth, and he said it out loud at the rail in about nine seconds, and she listened to all of it and then said the first four words back to him.

"That one I cannot."

"That is one question of a thing."

"It is one question of a thing and it is the one thing in this building I am not entitled to answer," said Wren Kallo, "so you have spent your question, and I am going to spend mine on something you did not ask for, and you are going to have to let me, because that is the arrangement at this counter and it is eleven years old."

She had a pen in her hand and she was not writing anything with it.

"Ask me something you can get."

## What The Copies Have No Origin For

He asked her what she wanted instead and she said it straight down the rail with about nine people at it.

"A thing left on a landing between two floors belongs to neither floor until somebody writes down which one it came from," she said. "I did not write it down. It has never been written down here, because there is no line on any sheet we use for which floor a slip was left on."

"A slip is not a return. A return is a thing that comes up through that hatch with a number on it, and a slip is paper that a person left behind on a step because they thought somebody would want it."

"So the paper on that step has no floor."

"It has no floor and no hour and no person who left it," said Wren Kallo, "and in about four months a man in this division is going to stand at the end of that row of cages and ask me which desk a thing came off, and I am going to have to tell him that it came off a landing."

She put the pen in her apron.

"And that is the reason your wrong hours cannot be corrected by anybody in this city, and I want you to hear the shape of it, because you came down here expecting to be told somebody in this building was careless with your card. Every desk in it would have told you it does not keep your card."

## Nine In Six Weeks

"Four people have come to my counter in six weeks holding a paper with your street on it and the hours wrong," she said, "and about nine have, and the difference is that the four came to the front of the hall where I can see them and the other five went past me and came back through the returns."

"Nine in six weeks."

"Nine in six weeks, and every one of the nine came to hand a paper back to the office it names, rather than to say the hours were wrong. That is what a person does with a bad slip, and a bad slip has one address on it, and your card is the address, and here is the counter where the cards in this city go to be given back."

She said it without any pleasure in it at all.

"Four of the nine went up four flights to you."

"Four of the nine."

"Four of the nine went up four flights and came down again inside a fortnight with a different hand on them, or with a fourth line somebody had added at a counter, and every one of the four came back through my hatch, and I could see what had happened to it before I had it out of the cage."

## What She Stopped Doing

"I have stopped sending them up."

She said it to the rail and then said it again to him, because she wanted it in two places.

"Since the eleventh of this month I have stopped putting a slip into a cage for your floor, and I have thrown nine of them in the wrong bin, and the wrong bin in this hall goes out on the cart on alternate mornings and I have watched it go out."

"Nine."

"Six. Six this month. And I have not told my supervisor and I am not going to, and if this comes up in any way I will say I put them in the wrong bin, which is what I will have done, and there is nothing in this building that will ever show a judge otherwise, because a slip is not a return and does not get a cage of its own."

Lin Hao stood at that rail and understood that a woman who had spent eleven years not answering a single question out of order had decided to break a rule about a man's door for him, on her own, with a cost, and had not told anybody, and would not be thanked for it because thanking her would put him in it too.

## Two Feet Between Two Rows

The hatch behind the second counter goes up about four feet and comes down on a bar. Behind it is the floor, which is bare boards and a hundred and forty wire cages on castors standing in rows about two feet apart, because returns have to be sorted by the floor they came off.

She looked up the hall at the clock, which is eleven forty, and then at him.

"Forty minutes," she said. "They start the tallies on this floor at half past twelve and the tallies are the quarter figures and I have to be standing at the board with the other eleven when they go up, and you have been at that rail since eleven and you are not going to get out of that hatch twice in a day."

She dropped the bar and the noise of a hall with about nine people in it went out like a lamp.

She took her apron off over her head and hung it on the end of a cage and came round the end of the row nearest the hatch.

"You have been in a space three and a half feet high under a counter before."

"I have."

"Then you have had that and you are going to have this and you are not going to manage to talk through either of them, and I have been thinking about that for about a fortnight, so take your coat off now."

He took his coat off and folded it and put it on the boards and there is nowhere else in that floor for a coat.

## The Hatch Goes Down

She came at him and did not go slowly, and she had been at that counter eleven years and had not let a man touch her in the middle of a working day since about the year before last, and she said so before she did it, which is a thing about her.

She got his belt open where it was and had him back against the row in about four seconds with the cages taking his weight behind him, and the whole row went about two inches on the castors and one wheel said something under the floor that both of them heard and neither of them stopped for.

"You will be able to hear me under that hatch," she said. "Every word. There are nine people in that hall and four hundred a week and the floor is boards and I am going to be as loud as I want."

"I know what the room does."

"You know what the room does," said Wren Kallo, "and you are rank one and you cannot pay for one hour of it and you have got a chest that has been spent since the fourth year, and I have watched you come down this hall four times in two months and get short of breath on the steps, and I am not asking you about any of it."

Then she got him inside her in one push with her back to the boards and her hands up on the cage frames either side of him, and the rows moved on the castors under both of them, and the tightness of it in two feet of clearance was so exact that the sound came out of him before he had decided to make it.

"Held that for nine seconds in a space you had no room in," she said. "You are going to do it again and this time I am going to move."

She did not wait for him to be ready. She rode up off the boards onto him and took him in short strokes with one hand going up and down the outside of her own thigh where the light from the high windows came down, and she talked the entire time.

Eleven years, and she said it, eleven years of a counter that takes one question of a thing and only one, and a woman at the front of the queue on the fourth of this month who had asked twice whether this building held anything about her brother, and what answering her would have cost, and what it would have cost the woman standing behind her.

She was slick between the legs before she had touched him and she had been since about the second he came round the end of the counter, and she let him feel how wet she was by taking his hand and putting it there, and then she took the hand away again and made him watch her work.

She got two fingers into her own cunt where she was riding him and worked them and told him in the ordinary voice exactly what she wanted done about it, without softening it and without explaining any of it, and then she stopped saying anything and made a sound instead, and it went up through the boards and out into the hall.

Then she put her hand back on him and made him come, and he did, hard, with the cage frames in his fists and the wheel still saying something under the floor, and she kept him there afterwards with her forehead against his collarbone for longer than either of them was counting.

## What She Wanted Out Of Him

She got a cup out of a tap against the far wall and gave it to him and sat down on the boards with her back against a cage and let her knees go.

"I am going to ask you for a thing and you are going to refuse me and I want it refused fast."

"Say it."

"Refuse in the same words every time," said Wren Kallo. "Not the same shape. The same words, in the same order, to every person who asks, for as long as you keep that door."

"That is not the same as saying no."

"It is the same as saying no and it is the whole difference between one refusal and nine," she said. "A refusal that changes its words gets copied as two refusals, because a person who heard the first one cannot repeat it and will describe it, and the description is a different set of words with your door at the end of it, and by the third quarter of a year that building has your street at the end of eleven sentences and every one of them is you."

He thought about it for longer than she wanted him to.

"I cannot promise you that."

"You cannot promise me that because you have not said the words twice yet and you do not know what they are going to be."

"That is not why."

"It is exactly why," said Wren Kallo, "and you can come back and tell me when you have said them twice, and I will not ask you for it before then."

## The Cage She Did Not Write Down

She got up and put the cage back where it had been, on a row she did not write down, in a hall where nothing gets written down unless it has a number on it.

"You came down here to find out which desk made your card wrong," she said. "Not one person in this building made it wrong and no desk in it can find out who did, and that is the same fact twice, and you have been carrying the first half of it since March."

"The tallies."

"The tallies go up on that wall at the end of every quarter with the numbers off this floor and the floor above it and the two above that," she said, "and about four hundred people a week walk past that wall going in and out of this hall, and there is nothing at the top of it except figures, and no line anywhere in this building for what a figure counts."

He got his coat off the boards.

"You are going to say something out loud in the next six days," said Wren Kallo, "and somebody in this division is going to need a caption for it, and I am telling you now so that you are not surprised and so that you know I have thought about it for longer than this morning."

Four thousand and one hundred and three days. Less four thousand and ninety-six, the seventeenth of last month, is seven, and about nine wrong slips came in at a returns desk in six weeks because a person holding a bad slip takes it to the counter it names, and a clerk stopped sending them up four flights, and the copy of a thing left on a landing has no floor and therefore can never be corrected by anybody.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is eighty-four, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and eighty-three days, which is fifty-six months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

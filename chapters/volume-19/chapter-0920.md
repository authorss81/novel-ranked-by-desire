# He Lets The Slate Stand

Four thousand and seventy-five was the twenty-sixth of last month and the third of this one is day four thousand and eighty-two, and four thousand and eighty-two less four thousand and seventy-five is seven, and thirty less twenty-six with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Nine People In A Lane

He came down the hill at about eleven on the third and there were nine people in that lane, which was more than there usually were, and he stood at the end of the nine stools with his hands in his coat and waited until the delivery at the seventh stool was finished.

Brack had the rag out before he had said anything.

"You have been three weeks," he said.

"I have been three weeks."

"You told me in this lane on the second of last month that you would come on the first morning of this month and ask me to rub that figure out."

"I did."

## What He Said

"Emil," said Lin Hao. "I am not going to ask you to rub it out. Not this morning, not in a year, and not on the day it would be kind, which is the day you have been waiting for me to come and ask, and it is the day I have been waiting for me to come and ask."

Brack put the rag in his apron.

"Say it slower," said Brack.

"I am going to say it slower."

"Because I have watched about nine hundred people a week go past that board since the first year of this lane, and you are about to stand at the end of my nine stools and put a name on it in front of nine of them, and once that happens I am the man with a slate that says whose slate it is."

"That is what I am about to do."

"Then do it facing me and not facing the mouth of the lane, because I have spent nine years being looked at by four hundred people a week and I would like this once to be a conversation."

"There were nine people in this lane," said Lin Hao, "and one of them was standing at the third stool and one of them works in a division building, and I said it out loud because I am not going to say it in here quietly and have it changed by the end of the week."

"Say what out loud."

"That the figure on your slate is mine. That it is on there because I put a card on a door, and it is about what that did to this lane, and if anybody in this city stands in front of that board and asks you what it means, you say that it belongs to the man in the white plate and that he has stood in this lane and told you so."

He said it in the ordinary voice, at the end of nine stools, with nine people in it.

Two of the nine did not look up. One of them wrote something on the back of his own hand, which is what a person does with a thing they expect to need, and it is the fifth time that particular thing has been done in that lane since the turn of the year, and it was the first time anybody in that lane had heard the figure claimed out loud by the man it was about.

Brack did not interrupt him and did not look at the board once, and that took more out of Emil Brack than the figure ever took out of that lane.

## Why He Was Not Going To Ask

"It would have cost you nothing," said Brack.

"It would have cost you about nine weeks of a lane that has carried a thing about me and made me stand in it."

"Which is what I am offering."

"You offered it and I said yes in about four seconds on the second of last month, and then I spent three weeks deciding whether I had meant it, and I had, and I was wrong the first time and I knew I was wrong the whole time." He put his hands back in his coat.

"That is not a man asking for a kindness. That is a man who wanted to be told that the figure did not matter, and you were the wrong person to ask because you would have done it."

Brack took the chalk off the ledge and put it in his pocket.

## What A Figure Rubbed Out Is In A Lane

"You want to know what that cost," he said.

"Yes."

"A figure rubbed out in a lane is a figure the lane knows was rubbed out." Brack said it the way he says the six lines, standing up, in the ordinary voice, and about four people in that lane heard it.

"Nine districts. Nine stools. Everybody who comes down that lane can see whether a thing is on that board or off it. Every erased figure in this city is a figure this city knows was there, and a lane like mine can count."

"So it stays."

"It stays, and now nine people in this lane know whose it is, and that is the price of the two sentences you have just said out loud at the end of my lane."

He took the rag and wiped the end of the slate and put nothing on it, and that is what the rag is for.

"You have just taken the only thing this lane keeps, which is that nine districts did not know what a slate in it was for, and you have put it in about nine heads in about eleven seconds."

"In four days one of them will be in another lane. In a month the fourth one will be a division building and somebody there will know that there is a number about that office in public, and they will want it for a wall, and they will come up four flights and ask a clerk."

## What He Bought

"What did I buy."

"You bought that not one person in this city can call it a lie," said Brack. "It has stood on a public board since the thirtieth of December in my own chalk with my own four days along the bottom edge, and in four months a man with a slate is the only kind of man in this city that has never been caught saying a thing that was not true. That is not a small thing and it is not mine, and I would have said no."

"Would you have."

"I would have done it for you inside a quarter," said Brack, "and I would have said no now, and I am not able to tell you which of those two men I am."

Then he said the thing that was left.

"And you have swapped them. You have made a thing public in order to protect a thing private and you cannot be sure you have not swapped them, and I am not going to be the man who tells you that you have, because you are the one who will have to live next to that board in four years."

## Not The Day She Asked For

At about half past two he was at the end of that table with the door open and both carriers in that passage.

"In the last month of the winter the clerk asked me for one day a year," he said, "when I had been wrong about something and had not noticed, with the door open and her in the room. I told her then that I would try and that it was worth about a quarter of what she asked."

"I remember what you told me."

"This was not that day," he said, "and I am not going to walk in and take it off you."

She put the pen down.

"You were wrong about something for twenty-four days," she said, "and you knew you were wrong for all twenty-four, and that is worse than being wrong and not noticing, and I am not going to pretend to you that this is the thing I asked for in the last month of the winter."

"I know what it is."

"It is a man telling a lane in public that a figure on a board is his, on a morning when he had decided that three weeks before he came down that hill." She squared the pen against that table.

"I asked for a day you were wrong and did not notice. I got a day you were wrong and had been noticing for three weeks and let a man in a white plate watch you do it. It is not worse. It is just not what I asked for."

## What Is Left Over

The third figure was in Emil Brack's head and had been since the ninth of the month before last, and it had no unit he could put after it, and it was not on the slate, and it was not going on the slate this year.

It would be wrong by about a quarter when it went up, and wrong by more than a quarter every quarter after that, and no person would be able to check it against anything, and the whole of that was his to have taken away and his to carry and had not been touched.

He said so out loud in that room, because it was owed and because he was in the habit now of saying the price of a thing at the end of the day it was decided.

"And there is one more thing owed at the end of that, and it is mine and not his," he said. "A man who will not rub a figure out is allowed one kind of relief and not another."

"I do not get to walk down a hill and tell a street that this office cost anybody anything. What I get is that a man with a board will say that figure is his, out loud, about four hundred times a week, for as long as that board is up in that lane."

At half past five he took that book down the four flights and the four hundred yards himself, with a twist of wire round the tongue of the buckle, and it was in the flat file by ten past and it was not in that room at any hour of that night.

And at the top of that hill he stood in the dark for about nine seconds and worked out what he had done, which was that he had said out loud, in a public lane, in front of nine people, that the only figure in this city that stood up about him belonged to him, and that the price of that had been the quiet of nine other people's mouths, which he had gone down that hill on purpose to give away.

The sheet comes off that rail again on the thirtieth of this month.

Four thousand and eighty-two days. Less four thousand and seventy-five, the twenty-sixth of last month, is seven, and a figure in small chalk stood in a public lane and was not rubbed out, and about nine people in a lane now know whose it is, and that is what it cost.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is sixty-three, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and sixty-two days, which is fifty-five months and twelve days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

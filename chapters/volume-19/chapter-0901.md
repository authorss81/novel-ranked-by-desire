# The Card Stands Its Winter

Three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine was the thirtieth of December and the twentieth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, and three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine is twenty, and the card has stood nineteen days of January.

## Before The Carriers

He went up the four flights early, in the dark, half an hour before that book was due, because he wanted to see that door with the landing empty.

The stair is stone to the second floor and brick above it, and the brick had taken a white skin of frost along the third-floor wall about a hand's width high, and the half landing between the second and third floors has no window in it and has been the warm part of that climb for nine years.

This morning it was not the warm part. This morning the warmth was at the top of it, off the lamps in the yard, and the middle of that stair was colder than the yard had been.

## Three Nails

The card was on the wood where he had put it, straight, with the three nails where he had driven them, and none of them had worked loose.

The paint had gone pale in a ring round each nail head about the width of a coin, and that was the frost taking the paint off the door from behind, one small circle at a time, and it had started at the nails and not at the corners, which told him something about how a door lets the weather in.

He did not touch it. He stood to one side of it and looked at it the way a stranger standing on that landing would look at it: three lines on a piece of stiff grey board, the writing of a man who has not slept enough, and behind it a door with a card about washing on Thursdays that has hung there so long the nail holes have closed over.

He wanted it to say what it had said on the day he nailed it. He understood there, with his hands in his coat, that it was never going to say that again.

## The Nineteen Days Said Once

The card went up on the last working day of last year and this is the twentieth of this one, and that is nineteen days of January and the evening before them, and in the whole of that time not one person has written down where the card is or that it is there at all.

He said that once, out loud, to the empty landing, and then he left it alone, because a thing said once in that building is a fact and a thing said twice is a man enjoying his own decision.

Then a lamp came up the stair two landings below him, and he heard Wenna Raut's voice behind it and the woman with the back's, and the two of them arguing in the ordinary way about a box of nails, and he stepped back off the door so that neither of them would find him standing over it.

## It Has Been Read

Wenna Raut came up last and stopped at the door and read the three lines the way she reads a row that has been read before, quickly, and then once more and slower, which is the whole of what she is.

"It has been read," she said.

"It has been read."

"Not by me. By somebody standing where you are standing." She put her hand up on the rail and left it there. "I have come up this stair twice this week and both times there was a person at that door who did not knock, and I did not ask either of them anything, and I am telling you because you will want to be told."

He did not ask her how many. He wanted to, and the wanting was itself a kind of making, because a count of the people who stand at that door is the one thing that would finish that card faster than any winter could.

"Your hour is not until ten," she said, and went in and set the lamp on that table and opened nothing, because there is nothing in that room at nine in the morning worth opening for.

## The Second Line

The book came up at a quarter to ten with the strap round it twice, because the strap is the wrong length and always has been, and the leather had gone stiff with the frost and would not lie flat until it was warm.

The woman with the back got her forearm under the bottom edge and said nothing about it, which is how she says the most.

About eleven people came up that morning before midday, and about four of the eleven were about a paper they had brought up in their own hands, and not one of them asked about the card on the door, which Lin Hao noticed and then made himself stop noticing, because silence on it is the only proof it has that works.

At about eleven he said it out loud with the door open and both carriers in that passage and Wenna Raut at that table, because anything said in here to one person at a time is somebody else's news by the end of a week.

"That card has been up twenty days," he said. "Three lines and not one name. And this morning the second line has begun to go soft at the corners, and the frost has taken the paint off that door round the nail heads and started at the nails."

He put a finger an inch off the wood and did not touch it.

"Somewhere in the next four winters that second line will be the first thing a stranger cannot read, and after that there will be people on that stair looking at a card and unable to tell which of three doors in that court it means."

"And you are telling us because," said Wenna Raut.

"Because it is going to have to be done again, and it will be done again in the same three lines with not one name on it, and I am saying that now so that neither of you has to stand at that door in a year and ask me whether it is still to stand."

The woman with the back said nothing at all, which is what she does when a thing has been said that she would have said herself.

## The Third Line

The first line was the street and it was as crisp as a nail head. The second was the door at the head of four flights off a third court, and that was the one going soft, and it was going soft because his wrist had shaken on it when he wrote it and he had steadied it with the other hand, and the pencil had gone down harder there than anywhere else on the card.

The third line was the hours, and the hours were true.

He read them out under his breath in that cold room, the whole of them, the start and the stop and the five days in seven and the part that said at no other hour, and they were true, and they would go on being true as long as that book went up four flights at a quarter to ten and came down at half past five, and they were the only line of the three that did not depend on a man's handwriting surviving a winter.

He had expected the street to go first, because a street is the longest of the three. It had not gone at all, and the thing that was going was the shortest line, and there is no sense in that, and he wrote nothing down about it.

## The Door Open All Morning

He left the door standing open from ten until half past five. That is not a practice and it is not going to become one. He chose it because the cold has been getting into the paper along the bottom edge of that table for nine years, and because a card on a door that is never opened is a card that gets read once and never again.

It went cold enough in that room by eleven that they had to move the lamp back towards the middle of the table. He noticed and did not move it, because the clerk cannot leave that room while that book is in it, and the clerk was not going to be the one who decided it was cold.

At about two a woman stood in that doorway for four minutes without coming in, which is a thing that happens perhaps twice a month, and she had a paper folded twice in her fist, and Wenna Raut put the pen down and waited her out rather than moving her on.

She came in at last and put the paper on that table and said she had walked up four flights twice since the card went up because she did not believe the third line, and that believing it now cost her a morning's work she would not get back.

Neither of them wrote it into the room as a thing about the card. The clerk entered the date and the hour and the words she used and left the space under them empty, and Lin Hao read the true hours out loud once, in the voice that office reads everything in, with the door open behind him, and the woman wrote them on the back of her own hand with her own pencil while he was still saying them.

That is allowed. That has always been allowed. What is not allowed is this office writing them down a second time anywhere but on that one card, and neither of the two women put a hand up for it, and he looked at them afterwards to see whether they had wanted to and they had not.

## At The Top Of The Stair

The carriers came for the strap at half past five and went down first with their own load, and he got the book round himself alone, with the buckle where it goes when he has to turn it himself.

He stopped on the top step with his hand on the rail, because the cold had got into the leather at the buckle and the tongue of it had begun to crack where it always cracks first, and because he wanted one second of standing still before the four flights.

Two people take that book up four flights in about nine minutes. He has it down four flights and about four hundred yards by himself in the dark, five days in seven, since the twenty-fifth of October, and the honest number for it in fair weather was fourteen, and in frost it is going to be worse than fourteen, and there is not a soul above him or below him at this hour who would do it instead.

The rail was cold under his palm and the yard lamps made the frost on the cobbles look like a floor that had been laid a moment ago and had not been walked on yet.

Then he went down one step and started, because a man standing at the top of four flights thinking about his hands is a man who comes up them again tomorrow with them worse.

Three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine days. Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is twenty, and the card has stood every one of them, and the second line has begun to go soft at the corners.

*The forty-fourth sheet came onto that rail at three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine and has been face up for twenty days, and at four on the thirtieth of March it comes down and another goes up in its place carrying no new line in the office hand.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine days, which is fifty months and twenty-nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

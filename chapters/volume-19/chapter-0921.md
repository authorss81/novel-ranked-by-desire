# The Lane Prices The Door

Four thousand and eighty-two was the third of last month and the tenth of this one is day four thousand and eighty-nine, and four thousand and eighty-nine less four thousand and eighty-two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## The Fold In His Hand

He had it under his arm at half past four on the tenth and it was the one about a foundry at the bottom of a street two districts off, where the man worked until six because the furnace in that building is banked at six and lit again at four in the morning.

The answer was in a fold with the flap gummed shut and the date and the hour on the outside and nothing else on the outside, and it had come up that morning on the strap with about thirty others and had been at the end of the table since a quarter to ten, and there was no way of telling that man that it was for him before six without standing in the street in front of his own door.

He stood in the street.

The light in that street at half past four in the sixth month comes off the tops of the roofs in one long sheet and does not reach the doorsteps, and the street is about nine feet wide and there is nothing at the far end of it to look at, and a man standing in it for ninety minutes is a man standing in it being looked at by every window on both sides.

## Ninety Minutes In A Street

He did the arithmetic three times and it came out the same each time, which is the sign of an arithmetic that is not arithmetic.

An answer goes by hand. It does not go on a sill and it does not go under a mat and it does not go in a letter box, because the moment it is out of a hand it is a thing in a street, and this city has eight ways of finding a door and he counted them himself in May and every one of them starts with a piece of paper in somebody's possession.

The card on the door says a quarter to ten to half past five, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

The man in the foundry finishes at six.

Ninety minutes is the whole distance between what this office promised and what that man needed, and it is not a gap anybody can be talked out of, because a man who keeps a furnace until six is standing at his own door after six and not before, and the card cannot meet him there.

At about five he went and knocked, because knocking costs nothing and because a person who has been told a door exists ought to knock on it.

Nobody answered, which he had put money on, and which he wrote down in his own head and not on anything, because a man knocking an empty door at five in the evening is not a fact about this office.

## One Hand Takes It

He stood back against the wall opposite at about ten past six with the fold in his right hand and his arm down his side so that it was not showing, because a man may carry paper in a street without breaking anything, and because a document held where nine windows can read the outside of it is a document that will be described out loud at a counter inside a week.

The man came round the corner at about ten past six with his sleeves still up and the back of his hands grey, and he did not know the street at that hour, and he walked to within four feet of Lin Hao and said the name of the foundry.

He took the fold in one hand without asking what it was, which is what a man does at the end of a shift when somebody is standing in his street holding something out to him, and he did not open it, and he said thank you, and he went in.

One hand. Ninety minutes. He wrote the order down in the back of his own head on the way up four flights because there was nothing to write it on, and the order was one, and one is an order, and it is the only order this office has ever been trusted with that means a person and not a date.

The man it was for was not asked what he was called. That was the whole of the trade and it took ninety minutes and it produced one entry that contains no person at all.

## Up Four Flights With Nothing In His Hands

He went up the stair with his hands empty, which he has not done at half past six in nine years, and Wenna Raut was at the end of the table with the lamp on and the pen squared and one question in the second column of that day with the space under it empty.

"How long was he standing there."

"An hour and a half."

"Then write it down in your own head and not on this table," said Wenna Raut, "because a row that says a man was in a street for ninety minutes is a row about a man in a street, and that book has not got a line for a street."

"It has got a line for the hour the answer went out."

"It has got a line for the hour the answer was given and it does not have a line for the hour it was in somebody's hand, and the truth is that you could write ninety minutes in that space and it would look like the most careful row anybody has ever entered, and it would be the first row in that book that is about how I felt about a delay."

He stood there with his hands still empty.

"That is the fairest thing anybody has said in that room in about four years," he said, "and I am not going to argue with it, and I am going to do the thing I have come down here to say with the door open, because the two of us worked it out between us and it is not fair that the carriers find out from the yard."

## What The Lane Says

He went down the hill at about half past six, which is early for that lane and late for a man who works until six.

There were four people in it and there are usually about nine, and the ninth stool had nothing on it and the third stool had a man on it who had been there since about half past four and was standing, not sitting.

That was the first thing Brack said about it, before Lin Hao got to the end of the lane.

"Standing," said Brack. "Not sitting. Twenty minutes I watched him think about sitting and decide against it, on the grounds that a man who is going to want to go the second it stops is not a man who should be sitting."

"Did he say what he was waiting for."

"He asked me whether that office was open after six," said Brack, "and I said no, and he said thank you, and he went away, and that is the whole of what happened and it is the first time in nine years a man has asked me a question in that lane whose answer made him unhappy, and I did not have to do anything about it."

He set the rag on the ledge and looked at the end of his own nine.

"You did not come down here to ask me what the door is worth."

"No."

"You have said that twice now, in the same lane, about the same door, and both times you were right not to ask."

## What The Card Cost

"Then I am going to price the card," said Brack, "and not the door, because the door is only the street and the street has been there nine years and it was not short of anything before you came to it."

He turned round and looked down the nine of them.

"A man in a lane prices a thing by what it puts in front of him. Before the thirtieth of December a stool in that lane was for stopping on and the pot is for the same and a person who waited at that rail waited because a delivery was late, which happens, and everybody in this lane knows what a late delivery looks like and does not mind it."

He put his hands in his apron and did not point at anything.

"Now there is a man in this lane who stands at my third stool from half past four and waits for a carrier that is not coming back, and he does not sit down, and he has been there once and he will be back."

"That is nine stools' worth of one man."

"It is one stool's worth of one man," said Brack, "and it is the only thing in that lane this year that is about you and not about me, and it costs me nothing, and I want you to understand that I have been trying since the ninth of last month to find a way of charging you for it and I cannot."

## What He Said In The Lane

"Emil," said Lin Hao. "I am not going to take the hours off that card."

Brack took the rag out of the apron.

"You have come down a hill at half past six in the evening to tell a delivery man about your own housekeeping."

"I came down because a man stood in a street for ninety minutes tonight because of a card I nailed up, and if I take the hours off it then there is no card and no street and no ninety minutes, and the answer in that fold gets to that foundry at seven o'clock at night, on a knock, like it did for nine years before I did anything."

"And you have worked out that the old arrangement was better for him."

"I have worked out that the old arrangement was better for him and worse for about nine people a fortnight who have nowhere to go on a working morning," said Lin Hao. "I built the thing that suits nobody this evening and I am going to say it out loud in a lane because that is where things I have built get said, and then I am going up four flights and I am not doing anything about it."

Brack looked at him for about nine seconds.

"A card with two sets of hours on it is two cards," he said. "And a man in nine districts with two cards in his head is a man who goes to the wrong door twice and then stops telling anybody. That is what I have got to add to the price and I have added it."

"That is worth more than the ninety minutes."

"It is worth exactly what it is worth, which is about nine weeks of a lane that has not had to think about it since the first year," said Brack, "and you have just made me think about it in front of four people."

## What Was Left Over

The book went down the four flights at half past five in the ordinary way, wired round the buckle twice, and he did not go to the flat file tonight and was not going to, because the flat file keeps a bound book and not tonight's.

At the bottom of that yard he stood for about nine seconds with his hands in his coat and worked out the number of hours of the week that the door's hours are wrong for the people who use that door, and the number was not a figure he was going to say to anybody.

It was somewhere between four and nine a week, and every one of them was a person who works, and every one of them had made their own arrangements about how to find a thing, and one of those arrangements had cost him an hour and three quarters on an ordinary working morning in the sixth month.

Then he went up four flights again, because the book goes down at half past five and the stairs are right there, and stood on the landing outside that door for a while before he went in.

This office has no way of reaching a person who keeps hours until six, and is not going to get one, and the card that promised otherwise is the thing he nailed up, and the promise was true about the building and was not true about the city.

Four thousand and eighty-nine days. Less four thousand and eighty-two, the third of last month, is seven, and an answer was taken in one hand after ninety minutes, and a man stood at a third stool and did not sit down.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is seventy, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and sixty-nine days, which is fifty-five months and nineteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# About Nine Who Knock Wrong

Three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight was the ninth of this one and the sixteenth of this one is day four thousand and five, and four thousand and five less three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

## She Asked Him To Stay

The rain had come in on the night and stopped about eleven, and that room with the door open smelled of wet brick for the first time since the turn of the year, and Wenna Raut had the windowsill wiped twice and had said nothing about it.

She had been at that table since a quarter to ten without moving from it, because she cannot leave that room while that book is in it, and at about half past eleven she put the pen in the tray and said the thing she had been keeping since the second of this month.

"Sit down," she said. "You are standing up like a man waiting for a train and you have not gone down that stair with a book in your hands since October."

## Nine A Month And Every One Of Them Knocks Wrong Once

"About nine people a month come up those four flights," said Wenna Raut. "It has been about nine every month since the spring before last and it has not gone up by one since the card went up, which I have expected and am glad of and have not told you I was glad of."

"You were right to be glad of it in December."

"I was right to be glad of it in December and I told you the other half that day, which is that I am not any happier." She turned the pen round. "The card has not given me a night. It has not given me one minute of a night, and it is not going to, and I have been standing in this room from a quarter to ten until half past five five days a week since the seventh of April and that has not moved a day."

"That is what you said in December."

"That is what I said in December and I have now had a month to be proved right about it, and I have been proved right, and here is the whole of what it was worth." She did not raise her voice.

"Before the card, about four of the nine arrived at a quarter to ten and waited, and the other five arrived at eleven or at half past four and were told, kindly, that we were shut at half past five and that the hours were inside on the door or in the court. About four of the nine did not find the door on the first morning and came back."

"Now every one of the nine comes up the stair and the half landing and about a quarter of ten, and not one of them knows the hours until they are in the room."

"And that is not better."

"It is not better and it is not worse, and it is a completely different job, and it has a name in it that I want to say out loud because I have been keeping it since the second." She squared the pen against the edge of the table, which she does when she is about to price something. "Before the card I was keeping the door. Now I am keeping the morning."

## What The Nine Do Wrong

"You said every one of them knocks wrong once."

"Every one of the nine. It is the only rule those nine have and it has held all winter and I do not know why." Wenna Raut read it off without looking at anything.

"One of the nine was at that door at two in the morning on the ninth of this month and knocked twice and went back down. About three in nine go to the third court and get as far as the yard gate and stop, because a card gives a court and not a stair."

"About two in nine come up four flights and then stand on the landing for four minutes before they knock, because they have come four flights to find out whether they are going to be stupid, and they are not, and they knock anyway."

"And none of the nine does it twice."

"None of the nine does it twice, and that is the part that frightens me, and not the two in the morning." She looked at him. "A person who knocks wrong once comes back. A person who knocks wrong twice has decided we are a place that does this to people, and they do not come back, they go to somebody who will tell them the address out loud in a passage."

## The Price Of It

"What does it cost you," said Lin Hao.

"Patience," said Wenna Raut. "Not hours. Hours I have had since the seventh of April and hours were never the thing." She counted it on her fingers the way she counts rows she is not entering.

"A card gives me mornings where the people who are coming know when they are coming, and a room that knows when it is full is smaller than one that never does. I told you that in December and I have not changed my mind about a word of it."

"But it also means that there is not an inch of slack in this morning at all now. Before, if an answer about a person ran long, I had about nine minutes of empty at the end of it. Now if an answer about a person runs long it runs into the next woman, and I have to choose in front of her, in this room, with her looking at me, which of the two gets the time."

"And you have chosen."

"I have chosen about four times since the card went up, and I chose the stranger every time, because the stranger can go somewhere else and the person on that page cannot, and that is the only reason and it is not a warm one." She put her hand down. "That is the price and I have paid it four times this winter out of my own morning and I have not asked for anything back."

## What She Wants Before The Quarter

"Then tell me what you want before the quarter turns," said Lin Hao.

She took her time over it, which she never does.

"Two things," she said. "The first is that you do not shut. Two weeks, three weeks, a fortnight closed in the worst weather with a bit of paper on that door saying the weather shut us, and every stranger in this city would read it and learn in one morning that the hours are a suggestion. I am telling you that as a woman who cannot leave this room and would have to stand in it alone through it."

"And the second."

"The second is the one I have been carrying since you asked me a thing in November that I did not answer." She looked straight at him.

"You asked me for something once that was not about a row. You said you wanted somebody in this room to tell you the truth about yourself once a year without being asked for it, and to do it whether or not you had been right that day, and I did not answer you, and I have thought about being able to answer for about two months."

"Then answer it."

"I am answering it now by telling you what I want and you are not going to be able to give me, and I would like you to understand that I know that."

She did not blink.

"I want you to stand in this room once a year, with the door open, and say out loud what that office costs and who pays it, and I want to be in the room when you say it, and I want it said on a day when you have been wrong about something and have not noticed."

"That is about a day a year."

"That is about a day a year and I am not promising you will manage it and I am not going to lower it."

## What He Promises Her

"Then I will not promise you that," said Lin Hao. "I will promise you that I will try, which is worth about a quarter of what you asked for, and I would rather hand you a quarter of it out loud than all of it and be the man who did not keep a thing he said in a room with a clerk at a table."

She nodded, and it was not a small nod.

"That is the correct answer and it took you about four seconds and you did not dress it up, and I have been waiting since the seventh of November for you to say something to me that I did not have to work out the cost of."

She put the pen back in the tray.

"A year from now I will ask you for it again and you will owe me nothing, and I will be here, because a clerk who has not been asked for anything for nine years can be got very tired by being asked for nothing at all."

## The Question She Did Not Get An Answer To

At about half past two a man came up four flights and stood in that doorway with nothing in his hands at all and asked whether a person could be asked about without saying who they were, and she entered the date and the hour and his words and left the space under them empty, and he went down again inside four minutes having been told, correctly and kindly, that he could.

Then Wenna Raut shut the book for the middle of the day and did not stand up.

"Are you going to shut it," she said.

He did not answer her, and she waited about nine seconds for it, and then she said "Right," and put the strap out for the evening, and neither of them said one word about the next six weeks because both of them knew what the frost had already taken off that buckle and what it was going to take off his hands.

Four thousand and five days. Less three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight, the ninth of this one, is seven, and about nine people a month come up those four flights and every one of the nine knocks wrong once, and a clerk priced that winter out loud and asked for one day a year and was given a quarter of it.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is seventy-six, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-six of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and eighty-five days, which is fifty-two months and twenty-five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

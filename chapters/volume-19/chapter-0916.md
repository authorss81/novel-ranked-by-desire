# The Figure Without A Holder

Four thousand and forty-seven was the twenty-eighth of this one and the fifth of the next is day four thousand and fifty-four, and four thousand and fifty-four less four thousand and forty-seven is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Three Things On A Piece Of Paper

She was on that landing at about eleven on the fifth with a piece of paper in her hand that had been opened out and folded again more than once, and she put it on that table face up, which is not what anybody does.

There were three things on it and they were in three different hands.

A figure, at the top, in a man's writing: four hundred a year, taken, out of the people who come in.

A standing, under it, in a woman's writing: they have stood in that room nine years and they have never once turned a page over, which is not a thing anybody can check.

And a place, at the bottom, in a third hand, which was a street and a court and a stair and a door at the head of four flights, written out longhand on the back of a trade bill.

"They are all about you," she said. "And I want to know which of the three of them is true."

## Where The Figure Came From

"I was in a lane off Cordwain on the second of this month," she said.

"There were two men at the rail and a woman standing at the end of it with her shopping on her arm. The man said that office takes four hundred a year out of whoever walks in, and the woman said that cannot be right, because they have never turned a page in front of anybody in nine years. Then a delivery man said the place, out loud, the street and the stair and the third door."

She put her finger on the bottom of it.

"That is a piece of paper with your door written on it and it has been in four hands and I have got the fifth, and there is a second one because the woman at the end of the rail had her own."

"What am I meant to do with it."

"Tell me which of those three is the true one," she said. "I will carry that back down the hill and leave the other two where they are."

She turned the bill round on that table so that all three of them were the right way up for him.

"I have carried it up four flights twice. That is two mornings of work and I am not a woman with a morning to give away, and I have already worked out what I am going to do with it if you tell me it is all three true, which is that I am going to put it up on my own door and let about nine people in my street read it and see whether any of them can do better than a piece of paper."

"Why would you do that."

"Because if I have it on my door then it is a thing people can argue with me about, and right now it is a thing a person cannot argue with me about, and that is the part I do not like."

## The Instrument He Gave Up

The figure was not answerable. Nothing in this city lets a man at a rail be found afterwards by anybody, including us, and the only thing that ever worked was a person standing in that passage on an ordinary afternoon.

And that last thing is the part he had to say slowly, because it was four months old and it was the bill he was being handed.

There was one instrument that office ever had for finding out what is said about its address in a public street, and it was a man standing in that street on an ordinary afternoon, and the instrument was a man of about forty who is paid nothing and who says the six lines to whoever stands at the rail.

He had taken that instrument away in the last month of that winter. He had stood at the counter under the window, asked for nothing, forbidden nothing, and been told he was wrong by a man who is paid nothing, and he had gone back up four flights and decided that the whole city ought not to end up owing that office for a thing it never did.

That was the eighteenth of the last month of that winter. It was four months old and it was on a piece of paper in a woman's hand, three feet from his own.

"The standing is not answerable either," he said. "It is true and it is not about you and it is not answerable, because we have never had to show anybody a page, and there is a great many people in this city who have decided what that means and about nine of them are right."

"And the place."

"The place is true and it is the reason you are standing in this room."

"It is on your bill because somebody in a lane said it out loud in front of a woman standing at a rail. That is where a door comes from now. Before the card went up this room was the only place that street existed, and since the card went up it has been said in about four hundred places a week by people who have never set eyes on it."

"That is true, and I have not got an answer for it."

"And we did not make that one and we cannot call it back, and the only thing this office is able to do about any of it is be exact every single time it is asked a question."

Wenna Raut put her pen down.

## The Clerk Names It First

"That is the second time those three have come up those stairs," she said.

Lin Hao did not turn round.

"It is," she said. "And the first time they came off the back of his own hand, in the sixth month, and he read them out and entered nothing, and this time they have come in somebody else's writing off a trade bill."

"And in between, in the last month of that winter, he gave up the only thing that office had for finding out what is said about that address in a passage, on purpose, in front of that counter, so that the whole city would not end up owing that office for a thing it never did."

"I did not do it for that reason alone."

"You did it for that reason and for two others and I have lived in the room that has to pay for it," said Wenna Raut. "I would like it noticed that I am naming the bill first and he is about to make a speech."

"Then name it and let him speak."

## What The Office Said With The Door Open

He turned round and put the door wide against the wall, which it was not by nine o'clock on any working morning of the year.

"Listen to me, and then go down that hill and do what you like with it," he said. "This office keeps no roll of who comes through that door. It has never kept one, it does not keep one now, and it is not going to start."

"That is not a principle. It is the fact that a clerk at that table can enter a date and an hour and the words a person used, and there is a piece of paper in this room with nothing ruled on it for a name."

"That is true," said Wenna Raut, "and I am going to write it down, and I am writing it down because this time it is a thing this office said, and not a thing somebody wrote about it."

"May I write the date against it."

"You may write the date and the hour and the words I used, and the words I used are that this office keeps no roll of who comes through that door, has never kept one, and is not going to. And you may write that I said it with the door wide and both carriers standing within about nine feet of it, because that is true, and because a rule made quietly in a room has a way of being a different rule by the end of the week."

She wrote for about two minutes and neither of them said one word while she did it, and about nine people went past that doorway in those two minutes.

She wrote it, and the date against it and the hour against that, and then she stopped, and then she added a second line underneath.

"And this is yours and not mine," she said, "and you will have to account for it. From this morning it is on a page that says we said it, which means that in about four years a stranger can come up those four flights and ask why a room with no roll in it nailed its address to a door."

## What Took Her Down That Stair

"Then how is anybody meant to find out whether it is true," the woman said.

She had not moved off the step and she had the paper in her hand again with her thumb on it.

"Not by asking us," said Lin Hao. "That is the answer and it is the correct one and it will not improve. You come, and you stand in that room, and somebody reads out of a book in the voice this office reads everything in, and it costs you a morning's work, and you can do it about four times in a year before it stops being worth your morning."

"And there are about four hundred people a week go past that kerb."

"Yes, and it does not help either of us."

"And every one of them has read those three things by the summer."

"And they will not read them wrong," she said. "That is what is so well made about them. Whoever wrote that figure did not make it up out of nothing in a lane. They heard the six lines, or one of the six, or a man who had heard the six, and they put a true thing in a false hat, and the hat is the part that is travelling."

"Which is why the figure is the one to worry about."

"Which is why the figure is the only one of the three I can put a finger on," she said. "The place is true. The standing is true and it is flattering. The figure is a lie built out of a true thing, and it is being carried by people who believe every word of it, and in a year there is going to be a family in this city that believes that office takes four hundred a year out of them."

"Yes," he said, "and in about four months about nine of them will believe all three, and two of the three will be about a place that exists, and the figure will be the one that travels, because a figure is the easiest thing a person can carry away without being proved wrong."

## Nine Ways To Find A Place

She stood in that doorway for about nine seconds with the trade bill in her hand.

"You have told me that you cannot find out who is saying this, and that you will not write down who comes to see you, and that the only true way to check anything is to climb four flights on a working morning," she said. "And you have not told me the one thing I came up here for, which is whether there is another way to get at you, and there is, and I have seen it."

"Then tell me what it is."

"I will not, because you have had a morning of somebody else's writing already and you have had about nine of those in a year." She put the paper back in her pocket. "You will find out about it from somebody else, in about a fortnight, and by then there will be a second piece of paper, and the second one will be the one that gets copied."

Then she went down four flights with her own three things in her pocket and did not look back at the door.

Four thousand and fifty-four days. Less four thousand and forty-seven, the twenty-eighth of this one, is seven, and a figure, a standing and a place came up four flights in three different hands and were refused entry for the second time, and the clerk wrote down the refusal because this time the office said it out loud.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is thirty-five, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and thirty-four days, which is fifty-four months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

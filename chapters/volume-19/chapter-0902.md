# Seventeen Minutes Down Four Flights

Three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine was the twentieth of this one and the twenty-seventh of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, and three thousand nine hundred and fifty-six less three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine is seven, and twenty-seven less twenty is seven by the month count.

## What Their Shoulders Said At A Quarter To Ten

The book came up at a quarter to ten with the strap round it twice, because the strap is the wrong length and always has been, and the two of them took it up four flights the way they have taken it up about two hundred times.

They took twelve minutes.

In fair weather they take nine, and Lin Hao has watched them do it often enough to know the difference without a clock, and the difference on the twenty-seventh of this month was in both of their shoulders before it was in anything else.

The woman with the back had her forearm under the bottom edge all the way to the third floor and her hands came out of it the colour of the paste at the foot of that desk. The other one took the strap at the turn instead of on the flat, which means the strap was riding high, which means the leather was stiff.

Neither of them said one word about it, and she would not have said one word about it if the book had gone down the stair.

He asked her at the top, with the book on that table and the strap still round it, how long the leather had been going like that, and she told him: since the turn of the year, and only in the mornings, and the afternoons were worse because the stair is warmer at four than it is at nine in that weather.

"Then I will come up it and meet you at the second floor."

"No," she said. "You will not. A book goes up two and comes down one and the two are women who have both got to be somewhere else at ten, and if you put a third pair of hands on that stair in the morning then the proportion is a different arrangement, and a different arrangement is a thing a person can be told about and repeated wrong in a lane."

He said nothing, and she went down four flights ahead of the day's first asking, and he understood that he had been about to buy something for himself out of two women's mornings, which is the arrangement he has been living inside since the twenty-fifth of October and had not once thought to look at from that side.

## Half Past Five And No Light

The lamp in the room had been out for the better part of a quarter of an hour by the time he got the strap round that book, and the stair is four flights of nothing at that hour, and on an ordinary night he can put his shoulder into the wall at the turn and know where he is.

He had done it ninety-two times. He was not going to pretend it was ninety-two tonight.

The frost had got into the brick between the second and third floors and the air on that half landing stood still and cold and had no window in it to be cold through, which is a thing a man only understands with his face.

## The Strap

The leather had gone stiff with the night and the buckle would not lie flat against the book's back, so the strap stood proud of it on the left and the weight of nine years of other people's words pulled a finger's width off centre, which is about a quarter of an inch of the whole way down four flights.

He got his hand up under the bottom edge, which he had learned to do in about the third week, and put it on the hip at the turn, the way she had taught him once that autumn on an empty strap with nothing in it at all, and went round the newel with the shoulder and not with the hands.

His left arm went on the second floor and stayed gone until the third, the way it does.

## The Tongue Of The Buckle

The tongue went on the second flight, and it went without a sound, which is the part he thought about afterwards.

Leather does that. It does not tear all at once and shout about it. It lets go along a grain that has been going since the day it was cut, and that strap is the wrong length and goes round that book twice, and it has been on this stair about eleven weeks of mornings and eleven weeks of evenings, and the tongue of the buckle has been the weak place since the first of them.

He got his arm back under the weight in about nine seconds. It was not a drop. It was not a fall. It was the whole of nine years arriving on his hip a quarter of an inch further left than it had been travelling for eleven weeks, and his body remembering it a moment before his arms did.

He stood in the dark on that landing and was angry, properly angry, at a piece of leather and at the weather and at a man in an office who had put an address on a door in December, and the anger was for about four seconds and then it went out of him the way it always goes out of him, because there was nothing in the room at the top of that stair for the anger to be about.

He put the book down on the step of the half landing for about ten seconds, because the woman with the back had told him in a yard in the autumn that a book that is put down once is a book a person knows how to carry, and a book that has never been put down is a thing a person was not allowed to put down.

Then he picked it up and went on down.

## Seventeen Minutes

The file drawer four floors down and about four hundred yards off stuck in the cold the way it always sticks in the cold, and took the heel of his hand, and he said one word through his teeth that the stairwell heard and no ear in this city will hear again.

He shut it and stood in that filing corridor with his arms hanging and waited for his fingers to open, and they took about four minutes, and the whole of that carry came to seventeen.

Before he shut it he had looked at the back of that book, because the tongue had gone and the weight had shifted, and there was a pale place on the back board about two inches long where the strap had ridden every morning for eleven weeks, and the leather had marked it, and the mark was the shape of the strap and not the shape of the buckle.

He put his thumb beside it and not on it. Nine years of other people's words, carried about two hundred times a week up four flights and four hundred times down them, and the first thing this winter has put into that book is a mark the book made on itself.

He is not a man who writes numbers down. He has not in nine years written down a number that was not already on some paper put there by somebody else, and seventeen is a number that only means anything in his own legs.

At the yard gate he could open them. At the yard gate they opened.

## What He Said To His Own Stairs

He got his coat open at his own door in about four seconds, which is the figure the woman with the back told him he would get down to in nine months, and he has reached it eleven weeks early, and it did not feel like an advance. It felt like the winter arriving.

He stood in his own hall in the dark with the coat hanging open and both hands out in front of him and said out loud, to the stairs, that the office was not going to close for the weather.

That was the whole of his thinking. He had spent eleven weeks walking up that hill working out how to shut that door for two weeks and put the weather on it, and it had taken four flights and a split strap to arrive at the opposite answer, and he was not going to examine how he had got there.

## The Bread Under The Stair

At the bottom of his own stairs, going in from the yard, the back door of the bakery shut, and the woman who sells the dark crust came out into the yard with her arm in a sack and stopped when she saw him.

"Four flights," she said. "In this. Every evening. Since the last working day of the year."

"Every evening."

"You have a woman on the branch rota and she has told the floor under her about it, and the floor under her has told the floor under her, and I sell bread to about nine districts."

She shifted the sack. "I am not saying it to make you stop. I am saying it because a woman who sells bread hears things about a man carrying a book down four flights in the dark, and it does not take a year for that to get round. That is all of it."

Then she looked at his left hand, which he had been holding behind his coat without noticing that he was doing it.

"Whatever you decide in the spring," she said, "decide it before the thaw and not after. After the thaw a thing goes soft and looks like a decision and it is only a wet week."

He thanked her, which he had not expected to do, and went up, and lay in the dark for a long time with his hands outside the blanket.

Three thousand nine hundred and fifty-six days. Less three thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, the twentieth of this one, is seven, and the buckle's tongue went on the second flight of a four-flight carry in frost, and the whole of that carry came to seventeen minutes, and a woman selling bread told him to decide in the spring.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is twenty-seven, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and thirty-six days, which is fifty-one months and six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Soft At The Corners

Four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five was the twenty-sixth of this one and the third of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two, and four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two less four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## A Quarter To One On The Third

He went out onto that landing at a quarter to one on the third with nothing in his hands, which was the first time in about a year he had gone out to that door for the purpose of looking at it.

The stair at that hour has about four people going up it and about nine coming down it, and the landing is lit by one bulb and by whatever the last window gives you. He had written it in that light with a bad hammer and a woman holding the door with her hip because her hands were full, and the clerk had stood at the top of that stair and told them both that a card with a name on it was a roll with a door on it.

It is stiff grey card about the length of a forearm. Three lines. The street at the top, the door under it, the hours under that. Three nails, one in each corner, put in by a man with a bad hammer on the thirtieth of December of the year before last, and every one of the three nails has been straight ever since.

The first line was still crisp.

That was the strange one and it took him about nine seconds to understand why. The second line is the line his wrist shook on, four flights up, on a landing, in a coat, with the clerk holding the door, and he had written the street first and steadied on it and then wrote the two long ones. The street is the only one of the three that went down straight.

## He Cannot Read It Two Feet Away

He stood about two feet off it and read it.

He could not read it.

He got the street off it, and then the second line came out of the card going soft at the left corner in a way that made the fourth word and the fifth word into one word, and he stopped and came at it again, and it did it again.

He was the man who has said those three lines out loud to about four people a night since the third of November at the bottom of that stair, in the dark, from memory, and he had never once got a word of it wrong.

"Twenty-three times," said Wenna Raut, from the doorway behind him.

"I do not know how many times."

"I have not counted either and I am not going to," she said. "And in October you gave away the counting of that stair for the rest of the year, so neither of us is going to be told which of these two is true."

"You have worked out what I was doing before I said it."

"I have watched you for nine years," said Wenna Raut. "You stopped counting the hour at the top of the page in March and you never asked me for it again, and I have been waiting since March for you to find out what you replaced it with."

"And what did I replace it with."

"A mouth," she said. "You replaced a number with your mouth and a dark stair and about ninety times a month, and that is what has been keeping those hours straight for eight weeks, and it is going to keep them straight for as long as you are standing down there, and I want it said out loud once to somebody who has not asked me for it."

## And He Can Read It In The Dark

They went and turned the lamp off in that room, which they do at about four when the light goes, and neither of them said anything about doing it, and he went back out and stood in the dark about four inches off that card with his hand on it.

He read it.

He read it straight through at about the speed he said it at the bottom of the stair, and he got every word, and when he took his hand off it in the dark and said it again without touching it he got the third line about half wrong.

"That is what a month of four nights a week has done to you," said Wenna Raut from the doorway.

"That is what a month of four nights a week has done to me," he agreed. "I have said those three lines in the dark about ninety times and I have not looked at them in daylight since the thirtieth of December, and the ones I have learned are the ones that are in my mouth and not the ones that are on that card."

"Which means that card could say anything and you would still say those."

"Yes."

## Three Nails Out

He got a screwdriver out of the tin behind the door at about ten past two and put a cloth over the new nail holes first, because the last of the frost in that stair had brought some grit up with it on about four people's boots that week.

The first nail came out with no trouble at all. The second one took about nine minutes and the head turned twice before it cleared the wood, and when it did there was a pale ring round it about a quarter of an inch wider than the head.

The card came off.

He held it up into what was left of the daylight coming down that stairwell and looked at it properly, the way he had refused to look at it for about a fortnight.

Both top corners of the second line were soft. Not torn and not missing. Soft, the way paper goes when it has been wet and dried about nine times, and the ink has gone out of the card there and left the fibres standing up about the depth of a coat button.

The street along the top was the only line with nothing wrong with it anywhere.

## The Same Card On The Same Three Nails

He put it back on the same three nails about ten minutes later, in the same three lines, in the same place, with nothing written on it and nothing added to it and no name on it, and the three nails went back into three holes that were about a quarter of an inch wider than they were in November.

It stood about a sixteenth of an inch further off the door than it had stood a year ago, which he could see if he looked at it from the side and which not one person in that city would ever notice.

He did not re-nail it in different holes. That would have started a set of marks on that door and there would then have been four holes and three nails and about four years of a stranger counting which of them was the first.

## What The Carrier With The Bad Back Said About The Holes

The carrier with the bad back came up with the strap at about twenty to five and stopped in the doorway, which she does about once a fortnight and it is always about the same thing.

"The holes have gone."

"That is what I said."

"That is not the card," she said, "that is the wood. You can read that door in the dark with your hand on it until you are eighty and the wood will go first." She put her hand up to the nail head and did not touch it. "There are two of them that are about the size of a nail and about nine people a month are going to put their fingers in them and find out what is behind that door."

"That is what happens to a door with three nails on it."

"That is what happens to a door with about four hundred people a month putting hands on it," said the woman with the bad back, "and I am telling you about the wood because you are a man who looks at cards."

## Said Out Loud And Not Written Down

He said it with the door open and the light off the window and about nine people on that stair going down behind him, and he said it to the room and not to the passage, which is a distinction that room has been making at him since June.

"It is coming down in the spring," he said. "Or whenever it comes down. That is the whole of what I know about it and neither of us is going to write it anywhere, and if it comes off that door on a wet morning in the middle of February then the door is a door from that morning and I will not have anybody tell me afterward that I had a date for it."

"I was not going to write it, and I am not going to now."

"I know you were not going to write it," he said. "You stopped me writing a thing about this office down on that second of December of the ninth year and you were right and you have been right about it every day since, and this is a smaller thing than that and I am still not going to have it on a page."

Then he went back in and left the door where it was, because that door stands open between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour, and there was nothing in that landing that had changed the arrangement.

Four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two days. Less four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five, the twenty-sixth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven, and a card that had stood on a door for a year came off three nails and went back on the same three nails with nothing written on it and the holes about a quarter of an inch wider than they were in November.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is sixty-three, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and forty-two days, which is sixty-one months and twelve days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

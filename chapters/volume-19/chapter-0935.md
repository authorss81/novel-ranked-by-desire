# The Row She Leaves Alone

Four thousand and one hundred and seventy-nine was the tenth of this one and the seventeenth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and eighty-six, and four thousand and one hundred and eighty-six less four thousand and one hundred and seventy-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## The Two Women Who Bring It Up

They bring it up at a quarter to ten, five days in seven, the strap between them because the strap is the wrong length and goes round it twice, and the woman with the bad back takes the bottom edge under both forearms and says nothing about it.

She said something on the seventeenth.

"Four flights," she said. "I have been counting the noise of it since March because I cannot carry anything and there is nothing to do but count. It is quieter and it is heavier and both of those are wrong for a stair that has four people on it at a quarter to ten."

"Quieter how."

"Not quieter people. Quieter about nine people. That is what it is." She got her arms free and rolled her right shoulder over. "In March I could tell what floor a person had got to by the door. Now I could not tell you what any of them wanted before they got to the top of it."

## The Order Of Hands, And Not The People

He asked her for the order, because the order is the only part of any morning that is true about anybody and it costs nothing to hold.

"There were nine," she said. "Two of them came to the top and stopped and then went away without saying anything, which I have counted twice because in March that never happened. Four of them waited to be spoken to. One of them went down again about four seconds after getting to the landing. One of them got to the top, said nine words, and went away."

"And who they were."

"That is the part I do not have and the part you are not getting," said the carrier. "You have never asked me for that in nine years and if you start now I will remember it."

## What Changed About The Shape Of The Asking

He had it by eleven.

A man came up at about eleven ten and stood at the end of the table and said he had come on the seventeenth, at about eleven, and wanted it written down.

There was no question in it.

"There is no question in that," said Lin Hao.

"No."

"What do you want written down."

"That I came up these four flights on the seventeenth, at about eleven, and was in this room." The man had a delivery coat on and he was not going anywhere.

"I have been to three places this month about a thing that will not go in writing anywhere. At two of them a person wrote it down. At this one I am told that this office does not answer in writing, which is on a wall four hundred yards off and which I read about four days ago."

"So you want a piece of paper."

"I want a piece of paper with the seventeenth and eleven on it," said the man, "and nothing else on it at all, and no description of what it is for. If there is a word about what it is for then it is a thing about me, and I have had three of those this year."

"You want a witness."

"I want a witness that does not want to know anything."

"Then that is what you are asking for and it is the first time in nine years anybody has asked me for it." He looked at the clerk and did not say anything else about it, because the clerk had put her hand on the table and not on the pen.

## The Reason He Has Not Asked Her Since March

"You have been quiet since March," said the carrier with the bad back, at about a quarter past three, when they were alone on the landing with the strap rolled up and the book in the flat file room four floors down.

"I have asked her twice."

"You have asked her twice and both times you asked her before you had a reason to want the answer." She went to the banister. "The first time you wanted to know if the card had made the door worse. The second time you wanted to know if the wall had."

"Is that different."

"It is the same question wearing a different coat, and the answer to it is the same, and the number is not." She looked down four flights. "Do not ask her again. You will not get a number out of her and if you do get one you will not be able to put it anywhere, and a number a man is carrying about a stair is a number that gets said at a counter."

"Three counters, in three months."

"Three counters in three months and one in a lane, and then it is not a number any more, it is an address with a quantity attached to it." She went down. "I am telling you because you did not ask and somebody should."

## About Nine Times A Week

"It is not the first one this week," said Wenna Raut.

He turned round.

"It is the fourth, and it is about nine times a week since the second week of the month, and the question is different every time and the shape of it is identical. I have said no nine times and I am going to keep saying no, and I would like you to hear me give the reason before you decide whether it is a row."

"Go on."

"Because if I write down that a person came on the seventeenth and wanted me to enter that they had come, then the next one is a person who came on the nineteenth and wanted the same. In about four months I am putting nine lines a week into a book that are about people."

"That looks like a great many things that are not a roll."

"It is a roll the first time anybody asks me who came," said Wenna Raut, "and the first time anybody asks me who came it will be somebody in this city who is entitled to ask, and he will not be cruel about it, and he will be right."

## A Row About A Class Of Person

He thought about it for longer than she wanted him to.

"It is about a class," he said. "Not about the people. About the class of people who come up a stair and want to be entered."

"It is about nine people a week, and it is about them as a class, and you cannot have the first without the second." She looked at the row she had not written. "The second is the thing that gets written down."

"I am the clerk in this office and I have only ever put in what I am given. You have spent nine years asking me to put in things. This month I am refusing you on exactly the same grounds."

## What He Stops Asking For

He stopped asking her for a count that afternoon.

Not because she had refused it. She had not been asked yet. He had asked her twice since March and she had answered twice, and both times she had told him afterwards that a count she keeps in her head and gives away is the first four lines of something.

"I am not going to ask you for the number any more," he said, at about half past four, out loud, with the door open and about nine people going past on that stair.

She looked up.

"Say why, out loud, because in about four years you are going to forget that you said it and it is going to be a rule in that room with nothing behind it."

"Because the day this office has a number of people who came up that stair, that number belongs to somebody," he said. "Not to me and not to you. To whoever it gets sent to, and it will be sent to, and the first number is never a name and the second number is not a name either and by the third one somebody in this city has to know which of our two heads it came out of."

"That is the reason. It is also the reason you have been carrying it since March."

"It is."

"I will take it," he said. "How did you know to ask me that at about half past four on the seventeenth."

"I did not ask you, I said it out loud," said Wenna Raut, "and you heard it, and that is the whole difference between what I do and what you do, and it has taken you nine years and about four hundred people a week to notice it."

## The Stair, Quieter And Heavier

The book went down at half past five in the ordinary way.

The carrier with the bad back stopped on the third landing with two fingers under the bottom edge and said one more thing on the way past him, because she says things on staircases and nowhere else.

"It is about four people lighter a morning than it was in March, and every one of the four has left without saying anything, and not one of the four has left without saying something to me on the landing first."

"That is not about the office."

"That is not about the office," she agreed, and went down.

About a quarter to five a man came up four flights with no paper at all in his hands and asked to be entered, and was told no by a clerk who did not look up, and said that he had asked at two other places this month, and went down again.

Four thousand and one hundred and eighty-six days. Less four thousand and one hundred and seventy-nine, the tenth of this one, is seven, and about nine people a fortnight came up those four flights in that month asking to be written down as having come, and not one of them was written down, and a stair got about four people lighter and heavier and neither of those numbers exists anywhere.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is seventy-seven, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and sixty-six days, which is fifty-eight months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

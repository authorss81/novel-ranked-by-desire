# The Seventh Of October

Four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine was the thirtieth of last month and the seventh of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and six, and four thousand and two hundred and six less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine is seven, and thirty less thirty is no days, and no days with seven taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## The Form That Goes Up On The Seventh

It goes up on the seventh, one a month, and it is a form with two ruled squares on it and about nine lines of printing and no name anywhere on it, and somebody comes up that hill about once a month and does that and nothing else.

The paste along the top batten was tacky under the back of a knuckle at about four in the afternoon, which is what cold paste does for about four days and not what it does afterwards, and the form had begun to curl at its top corner because the wind gets under it there.

The two ruled squares were empty. Under the lower batten, her sheet was held against the wood with a date at the top of it and a ruled pencil line across the foot of it, and it had not moved.

## A Man Who Has Been At That Corner Twice

He had come down the hill at about four and was still twenty feet short of the board when a man about forty came up beside it and said, without any preamble at all, that he had been trying to make that out since the middle of the afternoon.

"I cannot make it out at this end. I have been to the top end and it is worse. There is about nine lines and two of them are under my thumb and one of them looks like a time."

"It is a time."

"That is not the thing I do not know. I know it is a time. I want to know what the rest of it is, and I have been standing here about twenty minutes working out whether I am allowed to ask a stranger."

"You are."

"Will you read it."

## Four Seconds, And The Reason Said Out Loud

"No."

It took about four seconds and he did not soften it and he gave the reason straight after it, out loud, on that hill, because a reason said once in a passage is a reason four people have and he had learned that in July on a landing four hundred yards off this hill.

"If I read that out, then a man in a white plate has read that out at a kerb, and about four hundred people a week go past this kerb, and every one of them who hears it goes and looks at the top of the card on the door and thinks about it on their way to work."

"It is on a door already," said the man. "That is what it says on the poster. It is on a door and it is in a book at a counter and you can see it in about nine places in this district."

"Then it does not need a tenth thing that a man in a white plate said, and I am not going to be the tenth thing."

## Then A Stranger Reads It Aloud

The man turned round and looked for about four seconds and then he said, loudly, to nobody in particular and to everybody within about nine feet: "He says he will not read it. It is perfectly plain, hold on."

And he read it.

He read the whole of it out loud, the nine lines and the two squares and the fact that the squares are for a person to write a question in and a person to write their own name in, and he read it slowly, and he got two words wrong and a woman about four feet off corrected him on both.

He read it four times in about twenty minutes to about nine people, and about four of them asked him something, and he answered in the voice of a man who had been getting things wrong all afternoon and had decided to stop.

Lin Hao stood about twelve feet off for the whole of it and did not say one word.

## About Twenty Minutes And No Name

He could have stopped it at about the fourth minute. Any one of about nine people standing within earshot would have listened to a man in a white plate say that a card on a door had the address on it and the hours on it, and the reading would have stopped.

He said nothing, and the reading went on, and at the end of it a young man of about twenty came up off the road and asked the man with the paper whether he had to put his name in the square.

"You do not have to do anything. It says a person can."

"That is all I wanted to know."

He wrote nothing down. He was standing on a hill with about nine people on it who now had a street, a door and two ends of an hour, and every one of them went away without writing anything down, and that is the only thing he wanted out of the twenty minutes.

## What He Could Have Said At The Seventh Reading

The seventh time, the man with the paper got the whole of it right without being corrected, and he said so, and about four people clapped, which was not meant for the man with the paper.

A woman of about thirty came up the hill while they were clapping and asked what was being read, and the man read it to her, and she said thank you, and went down the hill towards the district it is in.

He was about twelve feet off the whole of that and he had a clear view of the top of the hill from where he was standing.

If he had gone up there and said one sentence, in the ordinary voice, that sentence would have been the last version of that form anybody heard, and every person within about nine feet would have heard a man in a white plate give the street, the door and the hours, out loud, at a kerb, in front of a woman walking away from him towards it.

Instead there were now two versions of that form in one street, one of them in chalk on a public board and one of them in the mouth of a man with a bad eye, and both of them said the same three things, and neither of them had anything to do with him.

He stood there for the rest of it. That was the whole of what he did about it and it took about nine minutes and it was the correct amount.

## Back Up Four Flights

The form was still up on the seventh and the paste had gone tacky to hard on the top third of the batten while he was standing there.

He went up that hill at about half past four and came in with grit on his boots and the light behind him and about four people going down the stair.

"What did you do at the kerb," said Wenna Raut.

"I said no. In the ordinary voice, with about nine feet of air either side of me and about nine people in it." He put his hands in his coat. "She has it now. She has had it since about half past four and she got it off a form."

"You said no out loud instead," said Wenna Raut, after he had put the rest of it into the room.

"That is correct. You have about nine people a week hearing a refusal now who were not in a room. Four of them will tell somebody. That is the price, and it is the right price, because a refusal said out loud travels as a refusal and an address said out loud travels as an address, and there are only going to be two more of the first kind in this city after today."

"I could have said it first at that kerb," he said. "The only reason I did not is that I did not want to be the man who says that address out loud on a hill. That is a reason about me and not about the office."

"Then it is a good reason for you and a bad one for the office, and the office will survive it, and I would like it recorded that you said that sentence about yourself in a room with the door open."

## What She Has Been Carrying Since The Eighth

"The stairs are quieter again," she said. "About four people lighter than March, and the four who are gone were four who did not want anything. That is what the man who brings it up told me this morning, and I have not told you, and I have been not telling you since the eighth of this month."

"Why since the eighth."

"Because on the eighth of this month a woman from a division building stood at that rail and told me that a return had been raised for the quarter and it wanted one figure out of that office for it. She said she had been told to ask in person and that she would be back on the twenty-first of this month at about half past ten."

"She is not entitled to it and she knows she is not entitled to it, and neither of us knows what it looks like when she does ask." She put the pen down and did not pick it up again. "You have not asked me for a number since the seventeenth, and I want to know whether that is a decision or a week."

"Is there a difference."

"There is about four days of difference and I would like to spend them knowing which one it is."

Four thousand and two hundred and six days. Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of last month, is seven, and a man with a bad eye read nine lines off a form at a kerb four times to about nine people on a hill, and the man who wrote the card stood twelve feet off and said nothing, and a clerk four flights up has been carrying something since the eighth.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seven, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and eighty-six days, which is fifty-nine months and sixteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

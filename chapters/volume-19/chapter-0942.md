# The Dark Comes Early

Four thousand and two hundred and twenty-seven was the twenty-eighth of this one and the fifth of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four, and four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four less four thousand and two hundred and twenty-seven is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Five Mornings And The Same Frost

The frost came in on the second of this month and it did not go off, and by the fifth there was a white edge on every tread of those four flights and about nine people a morning came up them more slowly than they had come up them in the spring.

The card on that door says a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

It also says, at the top, a street, and under that a door at the head of four flights off a third court, and there is no third thing on it but the hours, and not one name anywhere on it, and it has been there since the thirtieth of December.

Every one of those three things can be read in the street by anybody standing in front of that door, between a quarter to ten and about ten past four, and not one of them can be read in the street after ten past four, because by then the light has gone off the wall that door is in and off the hand that knocks on it.

## The Back Board Is Opening

On the second morning the carrier with the bad back put the book down on that table and did not let go of the strap straight away, which is not something she does.

"Listen to it," she said.

He listened. There was a sound in the board itself, a small dry tick about every four seconds, somewhere down near the bottom edge, and it was not the tick of a thing coming to rest and it was not the building.

The book has a pale mark on its back board in the shape of the strap, and it made that mark itself, over about four years, out of cold and a shoulder.

It had been a mark for months. What had changed in that month was that the mark had opened, and the two halves of the board now met along about four inches of it with a line of daylight between them, which he did not look at side on until the sixth morning and which the carrier with the bad back had known about since the second.

"Give it to a binder," he said.

That was on the second morning and he had not said it out loud to anybody else before the eighth.

## Why He Did Not Give It To A Binder

"There is one binder in this city who does bureau books and he is in a room off the market and he is out between the fourth and the ninth of every month, and his price is nine days, and you take it away on a cart in daylight."

"That is not the reason."

"No," he said. "It is that a cart in daylight going four hundred yards from a rail to a binder's bench, in the middle of a working morning, is a bound register of this office going past about nine streets with a strap round it and no cover on it, and every person who sees it comes and looks, and about four of them go and stand where it went and wait for it coming back, and then we have that."

"So you will carry it yourself."

"It is already mine to carry at half past five."

"It is nine more minutes of walking on a bad board and then it is however many more months of this."

That was the argument and he lost it in about four seconds, which he had expected.

"We will do what we are doing," he said. "And when it opens further than a finger, then it goes, and on the day it goes I will stand in the street with it and I will not put a coat round it."

## The Four O'Clock Light

The thing that put the rest of the month in front of him was not the frost. It was the light going off that street at about ten past four.

The frost made the stair slow. The light made the door unreadable.

He had been standing in that doorway at about twenty to five on the twenty-eighth of October and had noticed it, and had said nothing about it out loud, and had told himself it was a thing about the month and not a thing about the door, and it was a thing about the door.

By the fifth there were about four people a day standing outside that door between ten past four and half past five, and every one of them was standing outside it because a card had sent them and the card was on the inside of a door in the dark.

## What He Did About It At Half Past Four

The first morning he said nothing and four of them knocked and the door was open and they found it and two of them said thank you and one of them stood at the bottom of the four flights for a while before going.

The second morning he said nothing again.

On the third morning he stood on the second step, inside the door, with the card's edge against the frame where the light could not get at it and his own back to the passage, and he said it out loud to whoever was standing at the bottom of that stair.

He did not read it off the card. He had thought about that for about nine seconds and had decided it before he came down to the second step.

A man reading three lines off a card at half past four in the dark in front of four people is doing an official thing at a door. This office has no official thing at a door, and if it started one there would be four people a night standing there watching the man who does it.

He said it from memory.

He said the street. He said the door at the head of four flights off a third court. He said the hours, which are a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

About four people heard it on the third morning and about nine heard it on the fourth, and not one of them put a pen to anything, and on the fifth morning a woman who had heard it on the fourth was standing at the bottom of the stair waiting when he opened the door at twenty to five.

## What It Costs And Who Pays It

He said it with the door open in that room, standing, on the fifth morning, while the book was on the table and Wenna Raut was taking her coat on.

"I have stood in that doorway four times a night for three nights and said that address out loud to whoever is standing there, and I am going to keep doing it, and I want it written down that I know what it is."

"You cannot write this one down."

"I know that you cannot write this one down," he said, "which is the reason I want it said out loud instead, and that is the whole of my reasoning and it is a bad one."

She put her coat on over her arm.

"It is not a bad one. It is the same reasoning as every other one of them and you have got very good at it." She stopped in the doorway herself. "Say the rest of it. You have said the part that sounds like a decision and the cost has not been said."

"It is this. In August I let a man in a passage off Cordwain read that address out loud for nothing, and I stood four feet off and let him do it."

"You told me why."

"I told myself why. Forbidding him would have made him a man told by this office not to speak, and that was true, and it was not the reason." He put his thumb on the edge of the table. "The reason was that I handed the job to a man who is paid nothing and made it his, so that it would not be mine."

"And now it is yours."

"Now it is mine, four times a night, on my own doorstep, at half past four, in the dark, where a man who does it every night is a man anybody in this city can find by standing at the end of that street and waiting."

## The Fifth Of The Month

It went well enough that week. That is the honest version of it and it is not a small thing.

About four people came up that stair in the dark every evening in the last hour and a quarter and every one of them had the right address and most of them had the right hour, because the hour is the part that gets copied wrong and he was saying it out loud about four times a day.

One man thanked him. Two of them did not stop on the second landing and went down again, which they would have done anyway.

And at about half past five on the fifth of that month, with the book under his arm and the board ticking at about every four seconds, he stopped at the bottom of the yard with the flat file forty yards off and stood there in the dark for about nine seconds, because the last hour of that card's hours was over and he had said four sets of three lines out loud into a stairwell and had not been asked to by anybody.

There was a road out of that gate and about nine streets on it and one of them went up a walled road behind a tram line to a hall with a counter in it where a woman had been reading a name and a plate into a stone for twenty years and had never once been asked about herself.

He had a question for her. He had had it since the third of September and had not found a day.

Four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four days. Less four thousand and two hundred and twenty-seven, the twenty-eighth of this one, is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven, and about nine people a night now find a door in the dark because a man says three lines out loud from his own second step.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is thirty-five, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and fourteen days, which is sixty months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

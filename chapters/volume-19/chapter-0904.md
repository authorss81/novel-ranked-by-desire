# The First Stranger By Card

The eleventh of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and seventy, and three thousand nine hundred and seventy less three thousand nine hundred and sixty-three is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.

## The Counter Under The Window

The passage off Cordwain is four feet wide and about nine hundred people a day go through it on a working day without looking up, and the counter at the end of it has a window over it, and the window has been the same window since the first year.

The man behind it is paid nothing. He reads the foot of the quarterly sheet four times a year to whoever stands at that rail, and since the thirtieth of December he has also read the address on a door four times a day to whoever asks, and he does not do the two things in the same voice, and Lin Hao has noticed that and has not asked about it.

He came down at about four in the afternoon because a woman on the second floor of his own building had come up four flights that morning with a piece of stiff grey card in her hand, and had asked about the stair, and had been told the stair.

## What He Said To Me On The Fourth

"I have been trying for a week to work out how to tell you this without making it sound like an accusation," said the man. "So I am going to tell you it straight and you can do what you like with it."

"Go on."

"On the fourth a man stood at that rail with a card in his hand and asked me which stair. Not which door. Which stair. And I told him the stair, because that is what he asked for, and it is the truth, and I have been paid nothing for nine years and told the truth for nine years and I was not going to start making a distinction now that he happened to be holding a piece of board."

"And he had the card."

"He had the card in his hand and he did not look at it once while I was talking to him."

The man leaned on the rail.

"That is the part I have been turning over. Your card says a street, a door at the head of four flights off a third court, and the hours."

"It does not say anything about how you get from a court to a door. And a man who has never carried anything in his life wants the stair, and a man who has carried things wants the stair more than he wants the room."

## The Sentence That Grew

"Read me the address," said Lin Hao.

The man read it to him, out loud, in that passage, in the ordinary voice, and it was right.

"That is not the card," he said. "That is you, adding the stair and the third door off the landing."

"That is the part I have been turning over since the fourth."

He turned the quarterly sheet square with a knuckle.

"It came out of me the first time. I did not decide to say it."

"I have said it nine times since, and it is the same nine words every time, and about four of those nine have not had a card in their hands at all and came asking because somebody told them that a man stood at this counter and told a stranger where a door was, and that is the most flattering thing that has ever happened to me and I get no wage out of it."

"Who told them."

"Strangers. Strangers tell strangers. That is what a passage is." He shrugged. "And by the end of this month there will be people at this rail who say the hours before I open my mouth, and I will agree with them, because they will have them right."

## A Woman With A Card

A woman came along the passage while the man was still talking and put the card on the counter face down, the way people put down a thing they have already read too many times, and asked whether the door was open on the last working day of the week.

The man did not take the card and did not turn it over.

"It is," he said. "Mornings, from a quarter to ten to half past five, five days in seven, and at no other hour, and the card says so and the card is right. Head of the stairs, third door off the landing."

She said thank you and went, and that was the whole of it, and it had taken him about four seconds, and there had been about eleven people in that passage and every one of them had heard a door given away.

Lin Hao stood against the jamb and let it happen and said nothing at all, and the man behind the counter glanced at him and did not ask him whether he was going to object, which was the most professional thing anybody had done for him in a fortnight.

## The Hour He Does Not Have

"Then stop saying them," said Lin Hao.

The man looked at him for a while.

"No," he said. "You may say that to me and I will not do it, because I have never yet been told by anybody who owns a thing not to say a true thing in that passage, and I am not going to be told it by a man in a white plate who put a card on a door in December and came down here to be told he was right about it."

He put both hands on the rail.

"Besides. If you tell me to stop, then I am a man who has been told not to speak by that office, and in about four months there will not be one person in that passage who does not know that the office exists, and every one of the strangers I turn away will go away knowing it, and they will not be lost, they will be worse than lost, they will know where it is and not be able to get at it."

Lin Hao had not thought of that, and he stood at that counter and understood that he had come down here about four in the afternoon to be told no by a man who is paid nothing, and that the no was right.

## He Wrote Nothing Down

He had a pencil in his coat and he left it where it was.

That is the whole of what he did about it. He asked the man one more question and it was whether any of the nine had come twice, and the man said no, and none of them twice, and all of them strangers, and he did not ask for one more than that because one more is a list and a list is the thing this office has refused every time anybody has ever offered it one.

"Read it once more," he said, "and I will go and hear how it sounds from outside."

The man read it again, and Lin Hao listened to the whole of it, and understood the thing he had actually come down to find out.

A nail can be driven into a door. What cannot be done is anything at all about the two hundred people who have now read that card, because the moment a man in a passage says a true address out loud he becomes a copy of it, and a copy in a head cannot be nailed up and cannot be taken down and does not know it is wrong.

That is the price, understood a second time, a week after buying it. Being found is something other people do for you.

## Up The Hill

He went up the hill after that, slowly, with his hands in his coat, and on the third step from the bottom a delivery man going the other way said good evening to him and did not stop, and Lin Hao stood on a public step and found that he could say his own street, his own door and his own hours, complete, in about four seconds, without hesitating.

He had carried them on the back of his hand for months without knowing he was carrying them, and about six weeks ago he had written them out on a piece of stiff grey board and nailed that board to a door, and in the space between those two facts a thing that belongs to no particular person has become a thing that a stranger says to him in the street for nothing.

He got to the top of that hill and stood in the dark outside a building with no name over the door and no name on the yard gate and one card about washing on Thursdays that has been there so long the nail holes have closed over.

Halfway up he had wanted to turn back and say one thing to that man, and the thing was that a card with three lines on it was never supposed to grow a fourth line, and that the fourth line was the stair and the third door off the landing.

He had understood, halfway up, that it had been said nine times and was going to be said nine hundred more, and that in about two years the fourth line would be the first line anybody gave a stranger because it was the one that made a door a place instead of an address.

He did not turn back. There was no version of turning back that was not a man at a rail being told by a man in a white plate that his own passage is now his employer's, and he had come down here about four in the afternoon to be told the truth by somebody who gets no wage for it, and had been.

Three thousand nine hundred and seventy days. Less three thousand nine hundred and sixty-three, the fourth of this one, is seven, and a man at a rail has been saying the stair and the third door off the landing nine times since the card went up, in nine words, the same every time, and he will not stop.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-one, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-one of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and fifty days, which is fifty-one months and twenty days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Forty-Fifth Sheet

Four thousand and twelve was the twenty-third of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day four thousand and nineteen, and four thousand and nineteen less four thousand and twelve is seven, and that seven is the seam out of the last of that winter and stands in its own column, and thirtieth less twenty-third is seven by the month count.

## What A Quarter-End Brings In

A quarter-end brings people who have been sitting on something since the last one. About nine of them came up those four flights before midday on the strength of the day and about four of the nine were asking about a person, which is the proportion that room has been running at since the spring before last.

Two of the nine had a piece of paper in their hand. One of those was the door and the street and the hours, in his own writing, folded twice and opened out again. The other was the door and the stair and the third door off the landing, and that fourth line exists in about nine heads a fortnight and is not on anything.

Lin Hao said nothing about the second one when the man put it on that table, and Wenna Raut entered the date and the hour and the words he used and left the space under them empty, and the man went down four flights with his paper still in his hand.

A quarter-end brings one other thing up that stair, and it is the only one of the nine that gets entered. One of the women this morning was the one who came in the ninth month of last year to ask whether the second column could take another line. She asked it because the quarter was turning and she had worked out, standing in a kitchen somewhere, that a thing which is impossible in a month can be possible in three.

Wenna Raut told her it could not be done in three, and the woman said that she had thought as much and had come up anyway, because it is harder to be told no in a month than in three, and went back down four flights.

That was the shortest visit of the nine and it was the one that cost her the most.

## The Sheet Under His Arm

The quarterly sheet was on that table with the head at the top of it and six lines in the office hand above a striking. The last of the six went in on the second of December of the ninth year, and that is the whole of what has ever been written at the foot of that page.

He had it rolled under his arm at about eleven and went down with it, because the old one comes off that rail at four and a sheet cannot be nailed up folded.

That was the reason, and it was true, and it was not the reason. He worked out the difference at the third-floor window, where there is no glass in the frame, and did not argue with himself about it on a stair.

## The Six, Read First

The passage off Cordwain is four feet wide and about four hundred people a week go past the end of that counter without looking up. The man behind it is paid nothing, and he had the old sheet square on the rail with its face up and the head at the top of it where the head has always been at the top of it.

"Read me the six," said Lin Hao.

"I was going to at eleven."

"I know you were. Read me the six first, and then I am going to say the top of it out loud in that passage, and I would rather you had done the six before we had the argument about which of us got to the number."

The man read the six. It took him about eleven seconds and it is the thing he does four times a year, and he did it standing up, in the ordinary voice, with about four people going past and none of them stopping.

## The Head, In A Street

Then Lin Hao put the roll of the new sheet on the end of that counter, turned round, and said it out loud, in the voice that office reads everything in, to the passage and not to anybody in it.

"Eight hundred and fifty-two."

About four seconds went past and the passage did not move. A man at the far end put a hand on a crate he was not lifting.

"It is a count of sheets," Lin Hao said. "It has not moved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. Behind it there are twenty-nine sheets and twenty-eight quarters, and they differ by one, because the twenty-ninth has not gone up yet. Four thousand and nineteen less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand five hundred and twenty, and that is twenty-eight times ninety."

He said all of it with a rolled quarter-end sheet under his arm, in a passage four feet wide, at the height of a man's voice and not at the height of a room.

That was worked out in the yard before he went down the hill and had not been said to anybody. There was a version of it that would have been said into a room with the door open, and that version would have had nine people in a building instead of nine people in a street.

Then he said the part that was going to travel, and he said it to the passage on purpose, because a thing said in a passage is a thing the passage has.

"It is not money. It is not people. It is not a count of anybody who has come through that door and it is not a count of what has been done for anybody. It is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them."

## What A Number Sounds Like In A Street

"You have said a number in that passage," said the man behind the counter, "and four people have gone past since you started."

"Yes."

"And in about nine days there will be a man in this city who can tell you what that number is for, and he will tell people, and he will not be a liar."

"Yes, and I know it, and I cannot stop him."

"He will say it is a figure somebody owes."

"He will say something nearer that than what I said," said Lin Hao, "and he will say it in about nine days and I will not be standing next to him when he does."

The man turned the old sheet a quarter turn with a knuckle and left it square again.

"I have read the top of that page four times a year since the first year of it," he said, "and I have never once said it out loud, because a man in a white plate has never once stood in front of me with it in their hand and asked me to. I want you to understand that you have taken the one line I have never read in that passage and you have read it in it."

## Nine People In It

There were nine people standing in that passage by the time he had finished, which was more than he had expected. Two of them were waiting on a delivery and one of them was a woman with a bag of string and one of them was a child going through to the yard gate with a signature to be met.

One of them was a woman who had come in to buy a quarter of something and had stopped about six feet short of him when he started, and she stayed about six feet short of him for the whole of it.

She did not come nearer and she did not go away. When he had finished she said it to the man behind the counter and not to him, that her brother had had a number on a piece of paper in the spring and had never once been told what the number was.

The man behind the counter did not look in Lin Hao's direction while she said it.

He did not say it a second time.

He had said it once, out loud, in the ordinary voice, with nine people in it, and a thing said once in a passage can be put right by anybody who heard it. A thing said twice becomes a thing somebody owns.

He picked the roll up off the counter, put it back under his arm, and went back up the hill at the pace of a man who has done a thing and has not yet found out what it was.

## At Four

At four the forty-fourth sheet came down off that rail. It was face up and going brown at the edges, and the counterman held it while Lin Hao put the forty-fifth onto the same two nails, straight, and stepped back and looked at it.

Six lines in the office hand went up above the striking. Five instructions and one sentence, and the sentence is the second of December of the ninth year, and nothing new went in under them, which is what happens at four on the last working day of a quarter in a room that has refused six times to add a seventh.

The two nails were the same two nails that the sheet went in on nine years ago.

That is worth about four seconds of a man's attention on a quarter-end and about the whole of that room's history after it, because a rail that takes a fresh nail every ninety days becomes a rail that cannot be trued, and a rail that cannot be trued turns a sheet about a degree further out of true every year of it.

He took the forty-fourth under his arm. It weighed less than the roll did and it was warmer, because it had been up on a rail for ninety days of this quarter and had taken the light off that wall for all of them.

## What Is Left Over

At half past five he carried that book down four flights and about four hundred yards in the dark, with the wire twisted twice about the tongue of the buckle, and the quarter-end sheet went into the flat file under the strap with it, where a bound Register has been kept for nine years and where a page that has been in a street that morning was never going to be.

Then he stood at the bottom of that yard and worked out the whole of what he had done, standing still, in the cold, longer than a man usually stands at the bottom of a yard.

He had carried a number down a hill and said it out loud to about four hundred people a week go past a kerb, and the number was a count of paper.

He had said it out loud in a street because he had wanted to. That is the honest shape of it and it took him about eleven seconds at the bottom of a yard in the cold to arrive at it, and what he had wanted was not the telling at all.

What he had wanted was to be a man whose figure other people were guessing at, instead of a man with a figure he has to keep himself from guessing at.

And from this morning there was a figure in this city that belonged to a man in a white plate, and no office anywhere could take it back off him, because the only kind of office that can take a figure back off a thing is an office that keeps figures, and the thing in front of him kept six lines at the foot of a page and a drawer it could not open.

Four thousand and nineteen days. Less four thousand and twelve, the twenty-third of this one, is seven, and the forty-fourth came down off that rail at four this afternoon and the forty-fifth went up carrying no new line, and a number that had stood at the head of a page since the fifth year was said out loud in a public passage for the first time.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is ninety, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and it came down at four this afternoon and the forty-fifth went onto the same two nails with nothing new at the foot of it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and ninety-nine days, which is fifty-three months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

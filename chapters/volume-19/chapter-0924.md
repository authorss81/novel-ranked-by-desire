# The Forty-Sixth Sheet

Four thousand and one hundred and three was the twenty-fourth of last month and the thirtieth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and nine, and four thousand and one hundred and nine less four thousand and one hundred and three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## Four Floors Down And About Four Hundred Yards Off

The flat file is on a filing floor in another building, four floors down and about four hundred yards off, and the room has no window in it and has had none since the building was put up, and it is lit by four lamps on a bar across the middle.

The bound Register goes in there at the end of a quarter and it stays until the next one, and it has stayed there nine years, and it is the only place in this city where every piece of paper with this office's foot on it is kept in one room.

There were four people in it. Three of them were working on the tallies for a division four hundred yards off, which are the quarter's figures, which go up on a wall in the counters hall of that division at the end of every quarter with nothing at the top of them but numbers.

## The Sheet In Order

He had the forty-sixth under his arm when he came in and he put it in order himself against the edge of the shelf, which took him about a minute and a half because the sheets go in behind one another and the ones behind have to be lifted.

The bound Register was on the shelf in front of it and it is not a book anybody opens in that room, because it goes up at a quarter to ten on two carriers with a strap between them and it comes down at half past five and it is in here for the night of a quarter-end, which is the one night of the quarter it is in a building four hundred yards off from the door it belongs to.

He ran his hand along the spine of it and did not open it, and the woman at the second desk said, "That is the ninth quarter-end and you have not opened it once," and he said that was the arrangement, and she said, "It is a strange arrangement for a book nobody reads," and he said that was also the arrangement.

The forty-fifth went back into its envelope. It had been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain since the thirtieth of March, and it had ninety days on it, and at four this afternoon it comes down off that rail and the forty-sixth goes up on the same two nails with nothing new at the foot of it.

He had said the head of the last one out loud in that passage with a rolled sheet under his arm and about nine people standing in it, and he had wanted to, and he has thought about that wanting every day since the thirtieth of March.

## The Six, Read First

He read the six out loud in that room before he said anything else, which is not what the room was doing.

The six lines are in the office's hand above a striking and the last of them went in on the second of December of the ninth year, and the six are the whole of what has ever been written at the foot of the standing page, and he reads them when he reads the head because the head without the six is a figure with nothing under it.

He read them in the voice that office reads everything in, in a room with no window, to three people who were compiling figures and one young woman who stopped what she was doing and listened to all of it.

The man at the end of the second desk said, "Are those the same six as last quarter."

"They are the same six as the first quarter-end I ever stood in this city for."

"Then why do you read them out every time."

"Because a page with six lines at the foot of it and four hundred and nineteen lines above them and no figure on any of them is a page that nine years of this office have never been able to explain in one breath," he said.

"I have found that if you say it out loud in a room before you say a number, the number lands in a person instead of in a document. There is a young woman here who has never heard it said and she is going to be putting your figures on a wall in a week."

The young woman said, "I have."

"Then that is the reason," he said.

## The Head, In A Room With No Window

"Eight hundred and fifty-two," he said. "It has not moved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. Behind it there are thirty sheets and twenty-nine quarters and they differ by one, because the thirtieth has not gone up yet. Four thousand and one hundred and nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand six hundred and ten, and that is twenty-nine times ninety."

He said it standing up in the ordinary voice with the sheet still in his hand.

It is not money. It is not people. It is not a count of anybody who has come through a door four flights up off a third court, and it is not a count of what has been done for anybody, and it is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them.

The young woman said, "I have to write something under that."

## What A Filing Clerk Has To Write Under A Figure

She had the index sheet on the desk in front of her and the column heads on it in somebody else's hand, and the column she was on wanted four lines under every figure and the quarter's figures had gone in without any, because the figures in them were figures about things inside that building and everybody in that building already knew what they counted.

"Not you," she said. "That is not what I mean. Your figure is not a figure about anything in here, and if it goes onto a wall in a hall where about four hundred people a week walk past it, then somebody is going to read it and decide what it counts, and whatever they decide will be the truth about it inside a month."

"What does it count."

"That is what I am asking you."

"I know what it counts and you are not going to like it."

## A Count Of Sheets, Not Of People

He said it the way he says the six.

"A count of sheets," he said. "Not of people. It counts the pieces of paper that carry the foot of this office, and the difference between the two numbers under it is the sheet going up this afternoon, and the number above it has not moved in seven years, and if anybody asks you what it counts you can say it counts sheets."

She wrote it down in a small hand and read it back twice.

"Is that all of it."

"That is all of it and it took me nine years and a drawer."

She put it in the column and then looked at it for a moment with the pen not moving.

"I have been doing this for about four months," she said, "and I have never had to write what a figure counts before, and I have written about four hundred of them off other people's sheets without knowing what any of them were."

"You did not need to know. They were in here."

"They were in here," she said, "and yours is going out."

## The Forty-Fourth Comes Down At Four

He was back on the rail in the passage off Cordwain at about half past three with the rolled forty-sixth under his arm, and the counterman had the old one square on the rail with its face up and the head at the top of it where the head has always been at the top of it.

"It came down at four," the counterman said, before he was asked. "Forty-fourth down and forty-sixth up, same two nails, and there is nothing new at the foot of it and there has not been since the first one I saw."

"That is four quarter-ends."

"That is four quarter-ends and I have read the foot of that page out loud to whoever stands there four times a year since the first one, and I have never once had to read the top of it twice."

About four hundred people a week go past the end of that counter without looking up, and none of them looked up at about four o'clock on the thirtieth of this month, and the forty-sixth went up with nothing new at the foot of it.

## What Went Into A Record

He went up four flights empty-handed and stood at the end of that table with the door wide and both carriers on that stair.

"Two things went out of this office today," he said, "and one of them was me saying a number out loud in a room with no window, and the other one is four words in a young woman's hand in an index that goes on a wall about four hundred yards off where about four hundred people a week walk past it."

"That is the second thing you have put in another building this month," said Wenna Raut. "Write down what it cost."

"I cannot cost it."

"Then say what you do not know."

"I do not know whether a sentence written to stop a misreading stops one," he said. "I know what it says and I know it is true and I know that in four months somebody is going to read it and be certain of something it does not mean, and I will not be able to do a thing about it, because the second this office corrects a sentence in a division index that correction is a second copy of the hours."

The book went down at half past five and was in the flat file by ten past, and the lamp on the bar in that room with no window was still on, and a column of quarter figures was going onto a wall in a division four hundred yards off with four words under one of them that he had said out loud in the voice that office reads everything in.

Four thousand and one hundred and nine days. Less four thousand and one hundred and three, the twenty-fourth of last month, is six, and the head stood in a room with no window for the second time since the thirtieth of March, and thirty sheets and twenty-nine quarters went into an index with a caption under one of them, and the forty-fourth came off that rail at four and the forty-sixth went up on the same two nails.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is ninety, and the forty-fifth sheet was face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them, and it came down at four this afternoon and the forty-sixth went up on the same two nails carrying nothing new.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and eighty-nine days, which is fifty-six months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

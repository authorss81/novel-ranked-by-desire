# The Hours Read Twice

Four thousand and eighty-nine was the tenth of this one and the seventeenth of this one is day four thousand and ninety-six, and four thousand and ninety-six less four thousand and eighty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## A Woman And A Girl On The Stair

She came up those four flights at about ten in the morning with a girl of about nine behind her, and the woman was doing the thing with her hands that a woman does who has been deaf since she was born and has learned to say things fast, and she brought her up that stair one step at a time because she reads lips and a person who goes up fast is a person she cannot follow.

She had a slip in her own hand and it was not the true one. She put it on the table and turned it round so that the writing faced Lin Hao, and he read it before she had taken her hand off it, which is a thing about a person and not about the paper.

Three lines in a clean hand, the street, the door, and hours an hour wrong at the start, and at the bottom a word she did not need and could not pronounce.

## The Second Reading

He read the true hours twice.

The first reading was in the ordinary voice and it failed, and he worked out why it failed while he was still in the middle of it, and he stopped in the middle of it and started again.

A woman reading lips does not read the mouth of a person standing still at a table under a lamp at eleven in the morning with three people behind her. She reads it if his face is square on to her and nothing passes in front of it and the lamp is behind him.

So he came round the side of the table and stood with the light behind him and read the whole of it again, and it took about the same as the first one had.

"A quarter to ten," he said. "Not a quarter to eight. Half past five, and five days in seven, and at no other hour. I am going to say it a third time in the same words and you can check me against the second one."

He said it a third time and she wrote it on her own hand while he was saying it, and her hand had the same idea in it.

It is a very small thing to have read three times into a room, and this office has done a great many of them, and there was not one other of them in nine years in which the person the reading was for had to watch a mouth to get it at all.

## What The Two Readings Were For

The girl read her mother's hand.

That was all. That was the whole use of an hour and a quarter of that morning: the girl got the hours off her mother's hand and would have gone to that door at a quarter to ten on the following morning without anybody else in this city telling her anything at all.

A thing said out loud in this room goes to the people who are standing in the room, and that is the entire reach of this office, and nine years of its rules are built on the fact that the reach is short.

The woman said something with her hands and he watched her make it twice because the second time is for the one who is not quick.

She was asking why the paper in her hand said a quarter to eight.

He told her the truth in the voice that office reads everything in: that it was not his, that he had no idea who wrote it, that he had turned down about nine people this spring who had come up with paper of their own, and that the only true hours in this city are three lines on a door four flights up a stair off a third court.

She looked at the paper in her hand for a while and then she put it in her pocket.

## The Paper She Asked For

Then she asked him for a paper, and he understood that she had not come for the hours.

She had come because the girl goes to that door alone, and the girl is not old enough to read what is written on one, and a woman who cannot hear and has a child who cannot read a door is a person holding everything she knows about that building in her own head, and if she dies the girl does not know when to go.

He said no.

He said it the way he says the six lines, standing up, in the ordinary voice, with the door open behind him and about three people in that passage, and he gave the reason, which was that the second he put a piece of paper in her hand that paper becomes one of the copies that come back up four flights with somebody else's writing on it, and he had nine of those on the second of March and four of the nine had come from this street.

She watched his mouth the whole way through and did not look away once, and when he had finished she did the thing deaf people do at the end of a long sentence, which is to look at the hands.

He did not have any hands to give her, so he lifted them off the table edge and turned them over, which meant nothing to either of them, and she made a shape at it that was faster than the last one.

He knew exactly what it was to refuse a person who cannot hear the sentence that is being used to refuse them, and he had watched it happen at other counters in his life, and he had never before watched it happen to his own voice.

## What Was Said With The Door Open

At about two he said it out loud to that room, door wide, both carriers on the stair, because a thing that arrived in the morning does not get said in the afternoon quietly.

"If anybody cannot hear me," he said, "they send word up that stair, and this office reads it to them out loud, in the ordinary voice, in this room, and it goes in the book the way anything else does."

Wenna Raut wrote it down and then read it back to him, which she does about one time in nine.

"Read that back," she said, and he did, and she wrote the hour against it.

"And say the price with it," she said.

"The price is that it is not on the card."

"Then it is not on the card and it is in the book upstairs, and a person who cannot hear still has to send a person up that stair to ask for it, and that person is a second thing in this city that knows where this door is, and we did not make the second thing and we are the reason it exists."

"I know."

"Write that down in the same hour and the same words," said Wenna Raut, "because in about four months you will be tempted to put a line on that card about reading and hearing, and it will be the seventh line and you will not be able to take it off."

He wrote it in the same hour and in the words she had used.

## What The Girl Wrote On Her Own Hand

They went down the stair at about half past two, and at the second landing the woman turned and made the girl come back up three steps, and the girl gave her mother the hand that had the hours on it, and the woman closed her own fingers round her daughter's fingers one over another and held the whole shape, and that is how it went back down four flights.

At the door of the yard the girl wrote the hours across her own knuckles with a pencil she had in a pocket, slowly, because she could not do it quickly, and he stood on the third-floor landing where there is no glass in the frame and listened to a pencil going on a child's knuckles through a floor.

She got it right.

Then the woman looked up the stair at him and made one shape with her hand, which he did not know, and then she made it again.

He made one back, which was wrong, and she made it a third time slower with something at the end of it that had not been in the first two.

He stood on that landing afterwards with a thing he could not read sitting in him where a thing he could read usually went, because she had said it three times to make sure and he had got none of it.

## What The Slip Said At The Bottom Of It

Under the wrong hours on that slip was the name of a building four hundred yards off and the words RETURNS DESK, in a hand that had not been the hand that wrote the hours, and this woman had folded that piece of paper twice and opened it out again, which is what everybody in this city does to a paper that has been put away and then wanted again.

A counter that confirmed those hours would be a second copy of a door, so a counter in that building had not confirmed them, and the slip had gone to that counter anyway because a person holding a bad slip takes it to the institution the slip names.

And a girl of about nine is going to knock at a quarter to eight on a working morning, in a lane, in front of nine people, on the strength of a building four hundred yards off that has already sent somebody up these stairs twice.

Four thousand and ninety-six days. Less four thousand and eighty-nine, the tenth of this one, is seven, and an office that can only speak out loud offered to speak out loud to a person who could not hear it, and a child wrote the hours across her own knuckles on a landing with no glass in the frame, and a woman said something three times to a man who got none of it.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is seventy-seven, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and seventy-six days, which is fifty-five months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

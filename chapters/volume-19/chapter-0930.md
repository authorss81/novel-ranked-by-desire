# The Second Refusal Stands

Four thousand and one hundred and forty-four was the fifth of this one and the twelfth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one, and four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one less four thousand and one hundred and forty-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## The Request That Was Already Reasonable

It was reasonable by the second of August and had not been made by anybody.

Two refusals stand in that book. A paragraph about seventy words long stands on a wall four hundred yards off. A figure with four words under it stands above the paragraph. About nine people a fortnight come up those four flights and arrive once at the wrong end of the hours, and about nine of the nine have now been told by a wall that this office does not answer in writing.

So: a man comes on the twelfth of this month and asks for a piece of paper.

He is not rude about it. He has a slip and he wants it initialled, which is the whole of what he wants, and he says the reason out loud before he is asked, which very few people do in nine years.

"My sister says I did not go," he said. "I went. I want something with a stamp on it that says I went."

## What The Only Answer This Office Has Is Good For

Lin Hao did not answer him for a while.

Answers go in writing. Both question and answer are entered in the words used. A spoken answer reaches the person it is said to and about two people standing near them, and a written answer goes to a person who was not in the room and can be read at a counter four hundred yards off by a clerk with a bar on a desk, and that is not a defect in it.

That is what the whole of this office is built out of and it is the only thing in nine years that has ever worked.

"It is the best thing we have," he said. "That is what I am about to say out loud and I want you to hear the shape of it first, because you have come four flights for a stamp."

"Say the shape."

"If I put a stamp on that, then there is one about this office in this city that a person can carry to somebody else, and I have spent nine years refusing to make a second place where this door can be found. The stamp is a second place. The paper is a second place. Every one of them is a piece of paper with this office's name on it going into a house where it will be read tonight by somebody I will never meet."

## The Decision, Said With The Door Open

He said it at about half past ten with the door open and both carriers in that passage and about four people waiting on the stair.

"The second written refusal stands," he said, "and is not going to be argued this morning or any other morning, and this office is not going to give a third one on paper. Not this month, not after a quarter, and not on the day it would be kind, which is the day you come up here with a reason."

Wenna Raut put her pen down and squared it against the table.

"That closes it," she said.

"That closes it," he said. "From today, anybody who comes up this stair asking for a refusal in writing gets a person, in a doorway, in the ordinary voice, on the day they come, five days in seven, between a quarter to ten and half past five. And at no other hour, because that is what the card says and I am not adding to the card."

## The Price, Named Before It Is Spent

"Then say the price out loud," she said, "before it is spent, because you do not get to spend it quietly and you have not been able to do that since March."

He said it to the passage.

"The price is that this office can no longer say no to somebody who is not standing here." He turned round and looked at the four people on the stair. "A paper gets carried. A person does not. So from today every refusal this office gives is given by one of two people, on four flights, five days in seven, to about nine people a fortnight, and there is no third time of day and there is no version of it that goes in an envelope."

"And there is no third refusal in a book, so nobody anywhere can point at a precedent and ask for the fourth."

"There is not going to be a third refusal in a book, because there are two in it and two is where we stop."

He said the sentence about the two people being him and her, and then did not say it again.

## What It Buys

"I want it on the record what this is for," he said, "because in about four years somebody will come up here and say that this office stopped giving refusals in writing because it had gone soft, and that is not the reason and I am not going to be alive to argue with them properly."

"Write it."

"It is so that nothing this office says no to can be carried away and multiplied by us." He put his hands in his coat. "A refusal we put on paper is nine refusals by the end of a month and about nine people a month hearing of it for half a year, and that number is in that book in the clerk's hand and it was written with the door open. If we stop, the number stops. Not because the asking stops. Because we stop helping it along."

He went and told the man on the stair in full, standing up, in the ordinary voice, without softening it and without shortening it.

The man listened to all of it and then said, "You could have just written it."

"I know."

"You have had a sheet of paper and a week and you have decided not to."

"Two sheets and a fortnight."

"Then you are the only office in this city I have ever had to come up four flights for," said the man, "and I have come up them twice, and I am going to be back in a week because I have got nothing else to do about it and because my sister will not believe me and I would like to be able to say that I went again."

He went down with nothing. He came up again a week after, with nothing, and a week after that, with nothing, and he was still coming in the middle of the next quarter with nothing in his hand, and this office refused him in person on every one of those mornings, and about how many of them there were was never written down.

## Down The Hill, Where A Man Keeps Nine Stools

Brack had the rag out when he got there and the slate had been wiped that week and re-chalked, and the sooner figure was standing in his own chalk where he had put it on the last working day of the year.

"You have been four times since June," said Brack. "You have not come about that figure once."

"It is not going to be about that figure. You offered to rub it out in the last month of the winter and I have never asked you and I am not going to."

"I have noticed," said Brack. "A figure that gets looked at is a figure somebody maintains and a figure that goes unlooked-at is a fact, and you have worked that out in that room and I have watched you work it out across four visits without saying one word about it in a lane, and I would like you to know that I have noticed and I have not mentioned it and that is me being decent at you a second time, which I told you I would not be."

"You said that in May."

"I said it in May and I have been wrong once since," said Brack.

There were about nine people in that lane and one of them was standing at the third stool, not sitting, waiting for a delivery that was late in the ordinary way.

## What Is Left Over

The third figure is still in Emil Brack's head. It has no unit he can write after it, it is not on the slate, it is not going on the slate this year, and by the twelfth of this month it is out by more than half a quarter, and it is his.

His own blank row is a thousand and sixty-nine days old and has a space in the middle of it and nothing in the space.

Two refusals stand in a book four flights up, a woman two streets off is dating strangers' slips in her own hand, a clerk in a division building is throwing six of them a month in a wrong bin without telling anybody, a paragraph about seventy words long is on a wall four hundred yards off under a figure with four words under it, and about nine people a fortnight arrive once at the wrong end of the hours and will go on doing it.

He went up four flights with the book under his arm and the wire round the buckle twisted twice in his own pocket on the way, and at the top of that stair he stood in the dark for about nine seconds and worked out what he had just done.

He had closed the only instrument this office has that turns a no into something a stranger can carry out of the room, and he had done it out loud on a stair in front of four people and a clerk, and from today the only way he has of saying no to a person he is not standing in front of is to be standing in front of them, five days in seven, on four flights, for ever.

The demand travels further than the refusal. It always did.

It travels about nine people a fortnight, and out to about nine more for every one of them, and there is a wall four hundred yards off that will keep saying in seventy words that this office does not answer in writing, and now the wall is right.

Four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one days. Less four thousand and one hundred and forty-four, the fifth of this one, is seven, and the second written refusal stands and a third is not promised, and the only way this office will ever say no to somebody again is by being in front of them.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is forty-two, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and thirty-one days, which is fifty-seven months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

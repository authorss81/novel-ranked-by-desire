# The Hours Copied Wrong

Three thousand nine hundred and eighty-four was the twenty-fifth of this one and the second of the next is day three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one, and three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one less three thousand nine hundred and eighty-four is seven, and two days out of a month that ends on the thirtieth is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## A Slip That Came Back Up

It was on that table at about a quarter to ten on the morning of the second, with the date on the outside and the hour on the outside and nothing else on the outside, and Wenna Raut put it in front of him and took her hand off it.

"That came out of a returns desk in a division building and it came back up to us," she said. "It was given in at a counter by a woman who cannot read, which the counterman wrote on it in the clerk's hand, and a clerk at that desk turned it round and wrote that it could not be returned to the office it was addressed to because it was not an answer to anything that office had said."

"She wrote it was not an answer."

"She wrote four lines and the third one says that a desk takes one question of a thing and this was not one of hers." Wenna Raut turned it so the writing faced him. "Her name is Wren Kallo and she has been at that desk about eleven years and there is a bar on it that you would like to hear about another time."

## What She Would Not Enter

"What am I to do with it."

"Nothing," said Wenna Raut. "I cannot enter it, because what came up here is not a question. A row is a date and an hour and then the words a person used, in the words they used, with a space under it for an answer. There is nothing in this."

"A slip with four lines on it from a counter is a thing somebody wrote about us and it is not a thing anybody said to us, and if I put it in that book then that book has a row about itself for the first time in nine years, and from that day the book is a thing that keeps track of the book."

He put it down and left it where it lay, and about half an hour later a woman came up four flights and asked for it.

## The Woman Who Came Up About A Paper

She came up at about eleven with a shawl over her arm in the cold and a piece of paper in her hand that was not folded, because it had been folded twice already and opened out again, and she put it on that table and put her thumb on it and did not let go of it.

"My neighbour went up your four flights on the twenty-eighth and knocked," she said, "at a quarter to eight in the morning, because that is what that paper says, and there was nobody on the landing, and she went again the next morning at a quarter to eight and found the same, and she says you have put hours on a door and that the hours are wrong, and she says it to everybody in the lane, and now they all think you are a place that lies."

"Give it to me."

She gave it to him and took her hand back and stood with her arms crossed, which is a woman waiting to be told she is right and being fully prepared not to be.

The paper was a copy. The three lines were there in a good clean hand, the street and the door, and the hours at the bottom were wrong by one hour at one end. It said a quarter to eight instead of a quarter to ten.

## She Was Right About Half Of It

"This is not our copy," he said. "Our copy is on a door, and every copy there has ever been is that door."

"Then she has been sent somewhere else with your name on it."

"Somebody wrote those hours out for her, in that hand, in about four lines, with no name on it, from something that was not our door, and wrote them an hour wrong at the start, and she believed it, which is what happens to a person who is told a thing in writing by somebody she did not think of checking."

"Where does she get it?"

"I do not know. That is the honest answer and I have got nothing better." He put the paper down and turned it so she could see it. "And I can tell you where every other copy in this city came from, which is from a person who was told it by a person, or from a man at a counter who reads it out loud four times a day, and every one of those is a person being helpful, and not one of them has ever seen a door."

"She did not ask anybody. She said somebody gave it to her."

"Somebody gave it to her."

## What He Reads Out And What He Refuses

He read the true hours out loud once, in the voice that office reads everything in, with the door open behind him and about three people in that passage, and he read the whole of them, the start and the stop and the five days in seven and the part that says at no other hour.

She wrote them across her own knuckles with her own pencil while he was saying them, without being asked, which is the one thing he has never once had to explain to anybody.

"Now write them for me," she said. "On a piece of paper. Because that is what I came for, and I have told my neighbour you will not give me a paper, and she has told two more, and if I go down that hill with nothing in my hand I am going to be talking about this for a month."

"No."

"Then say it out loud here and let me ask somebody to hear you."

"I have said it out loud here. That is the whole of what I am able to do and I have done it." He put the copy back in front of her and turned it so her own thumb was where his had been. "If I write the hours on a piece of paper and put it in your hand, that paper is the second copy in this city and it is in a lane where a woman has just proved she will believe a paper."

## The Human Sentence

"Then what am I to tell her," the woman said. "That you are right and my neighbour is wrong and it is not your fault, and that I have a piece of paper proving both of those things?"

"You are to tell her that we are there between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, that the twenty-eighth of the month before this one was an ordinary morning and that door was open at a quarter to ten like any other, that a quarter to eight in the morning is two hours before we open, and that the paper in your hand is not our paper and never was."

That was the truth and it did not help her and he watched it fail to help her while she was standing there.

"You are telling me there is one true piece of paper about your own hours in the whole of this city and it is nailed to a door you have never once come up to."

"Yes."

"Then a person in my lane has been let down by about nine people and none of them work for you."

"Yes," said Lin Hao. "That is exactly what has happened, and I put it there, and I would rather have one true card on a door than nine wrong ones in nine hands, and you can take that to her or leave it, and I am not going to argue it."

## What He Wrote Down And What He Did Not

She went down four flights with the copy in her hand and her own handwriting across her own knuckles, and neither of them said anything on the stair.

He wrote nothing down about her. Wenna Raut entered the date and the hour and the words she used and left the space under them empty, because a question about a copy that the office did not make is not a question the office can answer, and she was right and they both knew it.

Then, at about four, Lin Hao said out loud what he had decided, with the door open and the two carriers in that passage, because a decision made in here quietly is a decision somebody else has already altered by the end of the week.

"This office does not correct a copy. Not this one, not the next one, not the one a woman puts out with her morning bread in three months. The only true set of hours in this city is three lines on a door, and the second we put a corrected one in a hand it becomes the copy that gets copied, and in about four months we will be the reason two pieces of paper in a lane say different things and both of them came from us."

"That is the whole of your answer."

"It is the whole of my answer and I am saying it in that room so that it stops being a thing I thought of at about half past four and becomes a thing the three of us agreed."

"Then I will be the one who says it," said Wenna Raut. "Next week, and the week after, and in every week of this year, to any woman who comes up that stair with a piece of paper in her hand. That is what your answer costs, and you have put it on me."

"I know I have."

Three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one days. Less three thousand nine hundred and eighty-four, the twenty-fifth of the month before this one, is seven, and a copy of three lines written out in a good clean hand in a lane gave the hours an hour wrong at one end, and this office will not correct a copy, and the refusal has moved from him to the clerk.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is sixty-two, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-two of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and seventy-one days, which is fifty-two months and eleven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# In Her Head Only

Four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight was the nineteenth of this one and the twenty-sixth of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five, and four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five less four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## The Road Up On The Twenty-Sixth

He went up the walled road at about one on the twenty-sixth and it was the third time in a fortnight and the gate was open again and he did not like that about it.

She was behind the counter and the queue was at the wall.

"You have come back twice in a fortnight," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and you have not brought anything either time, so this is the third time I am telling you that a man who comes three times has usually got nothing and has decided that the not having is mine."

"I have a question and it is about the book."

"Then I am going to tell you before you ask it," she said, "that the book is not going to open in front of you today, and that is not the refusal you are expecting, and I want about nine minutes before the queue."

## He Asked The Question He Came Up With

"Is my brother's page still in there."

"It is," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

She put both hands on the counter about two feet apart and did not turn round, and he understood the hands before she said anything, because she has been a woman at a counter for twenty years and hands on a counter is what she does instead of shutting a door.

"You told me three years ago that you would not give me the page and that you would not tell me why twice."

"I told you that I would not give you the page."

"You gave me a reason. You gave me three reasons and they were about a door that gets written down and about me coming back up that road a second time, and they were good reasons and I have thought about the second one for about three years."

"I did give you the second one for myself," she said, "and not for you. That is how that counter works and it is the only thing I have ever been good at."

## She Was Not Going To Refuse Him The Way She Refused Him Before

"I am not going to refuse you the way I refused you then," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

He had come up a stone road for about nine years' worth of a yes and he had built a great deal on the way to it, and the sentence took about four seconds to arrive anywhere.

"Then say what you are going to do."

"I am going to give you a worse answer than the page and a better one than no, in that order, and you are going to hate the first one."

"Go on."

"The page has not moved and it is not going to move and you are not getting it, and that has not moved since the evening you stood on that floor and I gave you three facts instead."

She took her hands off the counter.

"And if I gave it to you today then in about four years there is a page in a man's house with a name on it that came off a public post at the Terrace, and the next person who comes up this road wanting to be legible will be told by whoever they ask that there is a way of doing it."

"By you."

"By whoever they ask," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I am the one they will ask and you are the one they will ask and neither of those is the same as this, and the difference between this and that is about four years, and it is the only thing I have to give away in twenty years, and I am not giving it away for a man who has never once asked me for anything."

## About Four Times In Twenty Years

"Give me the worse one, then."

"The worse one is that there is no page anybody can produce," she said. "Not one I would not give you. There is no second copy anywhere in this city, there has never been one, and there is not going to be one."

"That is bad news about a piece of paper."

"It is bad news about me," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "Every name in that book is in my head and in no other place, and I have been wrong about about four of them in twenty years, and I have never had a way of finding out, because a thing you remember cannot be checked against anything."

She turned round and looked at him for the first time that afternoon.

"You have about nine years of a book with four hundred and something rows in it and you have never once counted them, and in October you decided you will never count who comes up your stair either."

"You have a woman two streets off dating strangers' slips in her own hand because an index is the thing you take a slip to. And there is not one copy of a single thing in that office of yours."

"The card on the door."

"One copy," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "in one place, that cannot be produced, and about nine copies in this city that are all wrong. That is my book and your door and it is the same shape, and I worked it out at about nine years old and you have had about a year, and neither of us has ever been able to check a word of it against anything."

## What He Asked Her Instead

He had come up that road with one question and he had it in his mouth and he put it back down where it was, in about four seconds, and she watched him do it and did not offer to help him with it.

She dealt with four people at the wall and made one of them read a line off it himself, and he stood about nine feet off and listened to a man in a coat read a line off a wall he could not see.

"You did not ask me to look."

"No, and you gave me the reason for it in about four seconds, and the reason was that I have been wrong."

"You were going to," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "You were going to stand there and ask me to go through about nine hundred names a week out of my own head and check one of them against what you know, and it would have been the worst afternoon of my working life and it would have had no result."

"Ask me why I did not."

"No," she said. "I will tell you why you did not and then we will both be finished. You did not ask because a person who has been wrong four times in twenty years without finding out is a person you do not ask to look. You would have been asking a woman to prove she cannot be trusted and you have never done that to anybody in your life."

He did not answer that.

She went back to the counter, and one of the four wanted the whole of the second line and she made him say it back to her before she would give him the first.

## Back Up Four Flights And Not One Word Entered

He told the whole of it up four flights at about half past two, standing, with the book shut on the table and about four people on that stair going down behind him.

Wenna Raut wrote nothing down.

"You did not enter any of it."

"I entered the date and the hour because I always do," she said, "and the words I used are a man came up four flights and asked about a page and was not given it, in those words, and that is the whole of what has gone in, and it is the same sentence I would enter for a man who came up and asked me the time of day."

"That is the right thing to write."

"It is the thin thing to write," said Wenna Raut. "You have given me about nine hundred words that are true and I have put four of them in a book that wants a date and an hour, because that is what a book with a date and an hour at the head of every line wants, and because I am not putting the rest of it in on my own authority about a building four hundred yards off that I have never been inside."

"Would you enter it if I had been the one who made the practice?"

"No," said Wenna Raut. "I would enter that the man made the practice, because that is the part that is mine."

## What He Did About It

At about ten past three he went out onto the fourth-floor landing and stood about four inches off the card on that door, in daylight, with the light behind him and his own shadow across the middle of it.

He read the street off it. He read the door off it. He got as far as the third line and then had to go and stand on the third step to finish it, because the whole of the card went grey at once under him and the second line was not where it had been when he put it there.

A memory that has never been checked can be wrong. He had been told that at about half past one that afternoon by a woman who has been wrong four times in twenty years and knows it.

And the thing on that door had been on that door since the thirtieth of December, in weather, and he had never once gone and looked at it.

Four thousand and two hundred and fifty-five days. Less four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight, the nineteenth of this one, is seven, and a man was told at about one on the day before that the only record of twenty years of that hall is a woman's memory and that she has been wrong about four of them and has never had a way of finding out, and then he went up four flights and looked at his own.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fifty-six, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-six of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and thirty-five days, which is sixty-one months and five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

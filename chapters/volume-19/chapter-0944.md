# Not On The Roll

Four thousand and two hundred and forty-one was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight, and four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and two hundred and forty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## A Week Later And The Same Hall

He went up the walled road again on the nineteenth at about half past one, on a working morning, in daylight, which he had not done on his own in about three years.

She was behind the counter. The queue was at the wall and not at the counter, which is what happens at that desk after about half past one, because the light is off the notices and there is a queue at them instead.

"You have come back," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and you have not brought anything, so either you have come to ask or you have come to give something away, and it is the first of those."

"I have come to ask you a thing I got wrong last week and you corrected."

"Then sit on that stool and ask it, because I have about nine people at my wall and about nine behind them."

## She Will Not Let It Be A Hardship

"You told me last week that I was going to tell you what you had been through, and say it well, and not say it was a hardship."

"I said do not tell me it was a hardship."

"And you have come up a walled road to do it to me anyway," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "so I am going to save you the walk and say the thing that is under it."

"Which is."

"That it was not one." She turned a page over. "About four hundred people a year come up that road wanting a number they can put on a table, and about nine hundred a week come up it at all, and I have never scored, and I have never been measured, and there has never been an instrument of any kind in me. I have had twenty years of that and I want you to sit there and notice that I am not a woman who has suffered."

"What have you had."

"A trade," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "The same bench in the same hall with the same lamp on the same counter for twenty years and about nine hundred names a week, and I have ended every one of them standing on this side of it instead of out in the street where they were standing when they came in."

"That is not a hardship."

"That is a better life than most of the people on that wall have," she said, "and I will not have it made into a story about a man who could not write."

## The Question He Came Back With

"Then let me ask the question properly," he said. "I have got it wrong since the thirtieth of December and you corrected me last week and I have not been able to leave it."

"Ask."

"A door with three lines on it finds anybody," said Lin Hao. "That is what I believed. And you have told me it finds anybody who has got something anybody can describe afterwards, and that is not the same claim, and I have been standing in a doorway in the dark for a month saying the wrong one to about four people a night."

"You have."

"And you are the person it was built for and it cannot reach you."

"You said that one last week in about nine words," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and you have come back up a hill to say it again slower, which means you have been doing something with it for a week. What."

## Why A Door Cannot Find Her

"I have been trying to make it reach," he said.

He got up off the stool, because he could not do the next part of this sitting down, and he walked the length of that hall to the wall of tiles and stood in front of it and came back.

"Describe how somebody finds me."

"How somebody finds you."

"In the city. Anywhere. From anywhere. Go on."

He described it and it took about a minute. Eight of the ways carry a paper and the paper has got an address or a division or a counter on it, and the ninth is a man in a passage under a window saying nine words, and every one of the nine arrives at a person who can be pointed at.

"Now do it for me," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

He stood in the middle of a stone floor in a hall with about nine people in it and could not do it in one sentence, and it took him four tries, and on the fourth one he heard himself say *ask somebody who knows*.

"There," she said. "There is your answer and you did not need me for it. You cannot get to me off a card because every road that card is on ends at somebody who is looking for a plate or a floor or a name, and there is nothing on me for any of them to look for."

## What Would Fix It

"There is a way," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

"I know."

"A person," she said. "Somebody who knows where I am and who can come up that road and knock on this counter. That is the whole mechanism and there is nothing else in it and you have known about it since August and you have said the words *we will not send anybody to a house* out loud in a passage in front of about nine people."

"I have."

"So that is the answer to your question and it is not complicated and it took me nine minutes."

"You are telling me the one thing that would make a door with three lines on it reach you, and telling me it is the one thing I have refused for nine years, in a hall, in daylight, on a working morning."

"I am telling you at about half past one on a working morning in a hall with about nine people in it because that is when there is anybody to hear," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "And I am not asking you to send anybody. If you want to know what a door with hours on it is worth to me then that is the whole of the arithmetic and it comes out the same as the last thing you worked out."

He did not answer that.

"You have not said no," she said.

"I am not going to say no in this hall and then say the other thing in that room four hundred yards off," he said, "because a thing said twice in two places in one day is a thing that has been prepared, and I have not prepared anything, and I am going back up four flights to ask the person who would have to carry it."

## The Same Question Four Flights Up

He asked it at about half past four on the nineteenth, standing up, with the book shut, and Wenna Raut answered before he had finished it.

"You cannot put the Terrace on a card," she said.

"I had not got that far."

"You were getting to it in about four seconds." She put her pen down. "A card is a thing a person finds once. That is the whole of what a card is for and it is why there is nothing else on the one on your door."

"The Terrace is a thing four hundred people a week go to and stand in front of."

"It is, and you cannot put a card on the front of a thing people walk up a stone road to reach. That is a worse version of both, and it would bring four hundred more up that road in a month wanting a number, and there is one of me and I am not there."

"That is the right answer."

"It is the answer that keeps a room of two and a table," said Wenna Raut. "There is a third one and you have had it in your mouth for about four seconds, and I am going to say it so that it is said and not left."

"Say it."

"The only way a door with three lines on it reaches a person who cannot be described is if somebody stands beside that door who can be," she said.

"You have spent nine years refusing to be a describable thing in public."

"That has a name and the name is not modesty," said Wenna Raut. "It is yours and it is the no-roll rule, and the cost is standing on this floor four times a week for the rest of your life without once being in a position to hand a stranger a name."

## What It Costs

He got the book down at half past five and took it four flights and about four hundred yards and it was not upstairs overnight.

The cost was in two parts and he worked out both of them on the way down that yard.

The first was the one he had known since the twelfth of November: there is a woman on a hill above this city who has never been scored, and a card with three lines on it will not find her, and every road that card is on ends at a person looking for something to describe.

The second was the one he had not known, and it arrived in the last thirty yards: he had spent a year making an address, and every single thing he had refused to do this year was on purpose, and the two halves of that sentence had never been in the same paragraph before.

When they were, the second one looked like the first one.

A drawer in that room has been shut for sixty months and twenty-eight days under an instruction with a man's name under it and he does not know what is in it and has not known for years, and there is no hour of his working day in which he could find out.

That was the whole of the twenty years she had spent at that counter and the whole of the one he had spent at that door, and they were the same shape, and she had known it in about nine minutes in one afternoon and he had taken a week to get to it.

Four thousand and two hundred and forty-eight days. Less four thousand and two hundred and forty-one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and a door with three lines on it went up a stone road on the nineteenth of that month and was told by two women that it could not reach the one person it was built for, and neither of them was wrong.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is forty-nine, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and twenty-eight days, which is sixty months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

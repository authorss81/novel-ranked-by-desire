# Perla Prices The Copy

Four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven was the twenty-eighth of last month and the fifth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and forty-four, and four thousand and one hundred and forty-four less four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven is seven, and thirty less twenty-eight is two, and two with five taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Four Flights And Two Streets

He went down to the laundry in the middle of the morning because a wall four hundred yards off had been up for about two and a half weeks and there was a thing he needed to ask a woman who had already told him in the ninth month of last year to send none of his strangers to her.

The back room off a street two streets wide smells of soap and hot iron and has a third table in it with nothing on it, and nine sheets of her own paper under the edge of that table, and he did not look at them and did not ask.

He came in with his hat in his hand and she looked at him for about four seconds and then put a chair out from the wall with her foot.

"Sit down," said Perla Quist. "You have not come to ask me anything and you have come because of the wall, and I am going to tell you about it before you get to the chair."

## Nine Slips Since The Wall

"Nine," she said. "Since the wall went up. Nine strangers in about two and a half weeks, and every one of them came in here and asked me whether a paper they were holding was right."

"How did they get here."

"They asked. That is the whole of it." She put her hands on the back of the chair.

"A woman keeps an index, and an index is the thing you take a slip to, and I have kept one under a third table for nine years without anybody in this city ever once bringing me a piece of paper. Now I am the counter people bring bad paper to, because there is a board four hundred yards off with a paragraph on it that says an office four flights up does not answer in writing."

"They are bringing them to you because you are the nearest thing to an answer in this district."

"They are bringing them to me because I am a woman who keeps an index," said Perla Quist, "and that is not the same thing and it is going to keep being the same thing for about four hundred years."

The other seven were nothing to do with hours, and she had him sit down and go through all nine of them on the back of a sheet of her own paper, in the order they had come in, which is the only order she keeps.

Two had been about hours. Three had been about a house at the end of a street and a person who wanted to know whether that office held anything about the last person in it, which it does not, and she told them that in her own words, without a date on anything.

Two had been about a sheet that had been sent to a division and come back from a division with a fourth line on it. And two were the same piece of paper twice, folded twice and opened out again, brought in by two different women from the same lane.

"That last one is the part I cannot price," she said. "Two women, one paper, and neither of them knew the other one had come. That is not a person bringing me a slip. That is a slip telling people there is a place to bring slips, and it told them before either of them was in this room, and it is not this room's doing."

## What She Writes On The Back

"Two of the nine held hours," she said, "and the other seven held something else about your office and were not about the hours at all. The two with the hours are the ones I am going to tell you about."

"Go on."

"I wrote the date on the back of each one in my own hand and gave it back to them," said Perla Quist.

The room got very quiet in the way that rooms with a table in them do.

"You wrote the date."

"I wrote the date and nothing else. Not the hours, which I cannot check and will not confirm, and not the street, and not a word about the office. The date means that this piece of paper passed through a laundry in the fifth month of this year and that the woman holding it did not go mad and it does not decide whether the hours on it were right, and it is true, and it is the only true thing available."

"You are correcting copies."

"I am dating them," said Perla Quist. "And a date on the back of a slip is a copy of that slip with my hand on it, and I have been the woman who does that four flights from a door for two and a half weeks without asking anybody, and it happened on my own."

## The Step She Took For Him

"You priced this once," he said. "In the ninth month of last year. You said one step to holding who comes through it, and that you would not take the step, and I said you were right, and I have thought about it about once a fortnight since."

"I did price it once."

"I am not asking you to un-price it. I am telling you that you took it anyway and that you took it for the same reason you took it, which is that a man holding a slip he cannot read is going to go away not knowing, and you are not a person who lets a man go away not knowing."

"No," said Perla Quist. "I am not, and I have not been since I was a girl, and it is the single most expensive habit I own, and it is the reason I have nine sheets under a table and never once gave them to anybody."

She let go of the chair.

"I cannot give it back. That is what I wanted you to hear. The step is mine now and it was never mine to take and I took it and I am not going to stop, and if you have come up those two streets to make me stop, you can go back down them now and I will not think less of you for going."

## The Question He Did Not Ask

He did not ask about the nine sheets.

That was the hardest nine seconds of that afternoon and it was not hard because of the rule. It was hard because he had come up those two streets with a question about what an index could do that was not what an index does, and he wanted to ask her whether those nine would answer a question the book upstairs cannot, and he wanted it in a way that would have been visible on her face.

He looked at the edge of the third table instead, and the edge of that table is nine years of a decision she has never explained to anybody, and he had looked at it once before and had decided then that asking would have been a kind of taking, and he did not take anything in that room then and he did not take anything in it now.

He asked her whether she had told anybody that she was doing it.

"Not the people bringing me the slips," she said. "I have told them nothing. And I have not told the woman who runs the front floor and I will not, because the front floor has four women on it who would immediately begin to keep a list, and I have spent nine years keeping an index and I am not spending one year keeping a list of slips."

## What He Gave Her Instead

"I am not going to tell you to stop," he said, "because you are right that it does not decide anything and it is the only true thing available, and because the second this office has an opinion about what you do in this room with a piece of paper that has my street on it, that room stops being yours and starts being somewhere I have been."

"That is the answer I wanted."

"I am going to say the other thing out loud with my own door open, on the fourth floor, in about nine days, and you will hear about it inside a week because half this street knows everything I do." He put his hat down on his knee.

"I am going to say that this office keeps no roll of who comes through it and never will, and that any person holding a slip they cannot have answered may come here and be told by you, in your own words, what you know about it, which is a date and nothing else. I am not going to put your room in my mouth and I am not going to name you to anybody."

"That is not a thing I asked for."

"It is not. And I am aware that the second I say that out loud, your back room is the place people get sent to by a man who has just said publicly that it keeps no roll of who comes." He stood up. "So do not do it, and do not stop writing the date, and do not tell me what you are going to do about either of those."

## What Is Left Over

He went back up that hill with nothing, which is the second time in about four months that he has gone up it with nothing.

There are two refusals in a book four flights above that landing now, and a third is a reasonable request, and a reasonable request is the hardest kind to refuse because refusing it looks like the office enjoying itself.

And a woman two streets and four flights away was going to go on dating a stranger's slip in her own hand for as long as people brought them to her, and would never once tell him what she wrote on the last one, and had put nine of them through her hands without once asking him for anything, which is the first time in about four years that anybody in this city has done a thing for that office and asked for nothing at all.

Four thousand and one hundred and forty-four days. Less four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven, the twenty-eighth of last month, is seven, and a woman who refused to hold a card is dating other people's bad slips in her own hand, and the man who put the door on it knows about it and did not ask her to stop and will not say her name again.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is thirty-five, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-four days, which is fifty-seven months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# Seven Days Out Of March

The thirtieth of this one was day four thousand and nineteen and the seventh of the next is day four thousand and twenty-six, and four thousand and twenty-six less four thousand and nineteen is seven, and seven days out of a month that began on the first is seven by the month count.

## The Form On The Seventh

A form goes up on the seventh of a month at the board by the kerb at the foot of that hill, one a month, and the one that went up on the seventh of this one was the hundred-and-sixteenth of them.

It came down and it went up again on the same morning, which is what a form does, and nothing else happened on that kerb, and Lin Hao was two hundred yards up the hill at about eight and knew all of it from the window.

It was the same size and the same weight as the one it had replaced, and both of them were sheets about the hill and not about the door. That is all the seventh of a month is: a thing that happens on a day, in a place, whether or not anybody walks up a hill to see it.

He had not gone down. There was nothing at that corner that could be got at from a window two hundred yards away, and he had been careful about that corner since the ninth of the last month of the winter, and this morning he was going to stay careful about it.

## Two Questions

He wanted, on the morning of the seventh, to know one thing, and it took him until about eleven to get it, and getting it was the whole trouble.

He went down to the counter under the window and asked the man one question, and it was how many people had stood at that rail in the week.

"About eleven," said the man. "Nine on an ordinary week. Eleven this week, and two of them were not asking me anything, they were standing out of the rain, and I counted them anyway because that is what a count in that passage is."

"And in the week before that."

"Nine."

He stood there for a moment and then went back up the hill, four flights, and asked Wenna Raut a different question in that room, and it was how many people had come up those four flights in the week.

"Nine," she said. "Nine last week as well. It has been nine every week since before the card and it does not go up or down for anything anybody can see."

## The Same Man Twice

"There was a man at your rail on the third," Lin Hao said, "with a piece of paper in his hand, asking for the stair."

"There was a man at my rail on the third."

"He came up here on the fourth at about a quarter to ten with a paper and asked which of three doors in that court, and he had already been told the third door off the landing by somebody, and I gave him the third door off the landing."

"That would be the same man," said Wenna Raut, "or it would not. There are nine of them every week and they are not the same nine, and I do not ask them what they have got in their hands, because a clerk who asks a stranger what he is carrying is a clerk who has started a list of what strangers carry."

That was the answer and it was correct and it was the end of it.

He asked her one more thing before he stopped, and it was whether she had heard the stair asked for, and she said she had heard it about four times this week, and that the stair had become part of the ordinary morning of that room in a way the door never was in the last year, and that she could not remember when that had happened.

"Then somebody has added a line to that card in about nine districts."

"Somebody has added a line to that card in nine districts, and they have all added the same one, and not one of them knows that the other eight are carrying it."

## What He Was Not Allowed To Do

He had a pencil. He did not take it out, and he stood at the end of that table for the better part of a minute working out what he was not allowed to do, and it was not a rule anybody had put in front of him.

It was that a sentence with two nouns in it is a sentence somebody can carry down a hill.

Eleven at a rail and nine up a stair, if they went on one page, would be one number by the end of the week. By the end of the month two people would be able to say that nine strangers a fortnight come to a door, and the office would have published it, and it would be wrong, because those were not the same nine and it was not nine of anything.

A count and a row are never the same thing, and an address and a crowd are never the same thing, and the whole difference between them fits inside the width of a line.

He said the sentence out loud once, with the door open, to find out what it sounded like.

"About eleven people a week find that door at a counter off Cordwain, and about nine people a week walk up four flights to it."

It sounded exactly like a sentence. It sounded like a line in a report with a heading over it, and it would have gone on to that table in four seconds if the standing sheet had anywhere for it, and the standing sheet has four columns that have all been in use for nine years and one of them is the second column, and the second column is four lines deep and is not going to be five lines deep this year.

He did not write it down and he did not say it out loud, and that was the first day this year he had wanted to say a thing and had found nothing to say it with.

## What He Asked Her Instead

"What did you ask me for," said Wenna Raut.

"You did not ask me for anything."

"You have been standing at that table with a pencil in your pocket and a face on you, and it is the second time this month, and the first time you went away and did not tell me why. Say what you want and I will tell you the answer I have."

He wanted her to tell him in which order the nine had come up, not who they were, and he had four seconds to work out why he wanted it before he said it out loud.

"The order," he said. "Not who. In which order the nine came up the stair this week."

She put the pen in the tray, which is what she does when a thing cannot go in a book.

"That is the only part of it that is true about anybody," she said. "Nine arrived and they came in an order and the order is on me and not on a page, and in about four months somebody in this building is going to retire and I am going to have to hand somebody else the order of a morning I can remember perfectly and cannot prove."

"Can you do that."

"I can do it for about eleven more years," said Wenna Raut, "and then I will not."

## What He Came Down There For

He went back down at about half past two, not to ask anything else, and stood at that counter for a while with nothing in his hands.

"You could give me the two numbers," he said. "The nine and the eleven, on a piece of paper, and I would not enter them and I would not keep the paper."

The man did not even look up.

"That is a list with two lines on it," he said, "and you have spent nine years not asking me for one, and the reason you have not asked me for one is that I would hand you two lines and you would hand me a version of it a month later with my name at the bottom of it."

"That is not true."

"It is not true," the man agreed, "and that is exactly why I am not doing it."

## What A Division Sends Instead

Going back up the hill he worked out the thing he had actually been down there for, and it was this.

If eleven people a fortnight find that door at a counter and nine people a fortnight walk up four flights, then somebody has to carry the answer back to the person who asked. The Bureau sends that back by a courier, and a courier goes to a counter and shows a docket and a courier can be stopped at a counter in four minutes by a clerk who does not like the look of a docket.

There is one kind of thing in this city that cannot be intercepted, and it is not a courier.

He had watched one go wrong in the autumn and had never written one word of it down, because it was not his business.

A courier went out of a division building on the ninth of the month before last with a fold in a satchel and a docket in a pocket. The fold came back eleven days later with the corner turned down and the runner unable to say at which counter it had been opened. The answer inside it was still correct and the person it was written for had spent eleven days going to a door and being told that nothing had come.

It is a woman with a bag, who will take a fold of paper to a door because the door is the door, and who writes down in a book of her own whose hand took it and in what order, and who has not read it and does not intend to.

He had known her since the ninth year. He had asked her nothing for nine years.

There was one more reason for the whole afternoon, and it was why he had gone down the hill at all instead of standing at that table with a pencil in his pocket.

A courier can be sent to any door a card has described. A courier cannot be sent to a person, because a person does not live at an address a card gives, and the entire difficulty in this city is that the answers go to doors while the questions come out of rooms.

Runa Sato was the only thing he had ever found that carries from the room to the door on somebody's own two feet, and in nine years he had not once asked her for anything, and the reason he had not was not modesty.

The reason he had not was that the first thing he would have asked her for, in the ninth year, would have been a list.

He had the order of nine off a clerk this morning who will not write it down. A woman four hundred yards off has been keeping that order in a book of her own since the ninth year, on her own two feet, and at that moment she was under a window two hundred yards below him, eating, and this office had never once asked her for a thing.

Four thousand and twenty-six days. Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of this one, is seven, and eleven people stood at a rail in a week and nine people came up four flights in a week, and the two numbers are about two different crowds, and he was two hundred yards above the only person in this city who could have carried an answer to either of them.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is seven, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and six days, which is fifty-three months and sixteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

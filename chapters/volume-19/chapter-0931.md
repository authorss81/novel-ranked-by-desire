# The Room That Cannot Wait

Four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and fifty-eight, and four thousand and one hundred and fifty-eight less four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Twenty Past Four

The last asking of that day came up those four flights at about twenty past four, and it was about a person.

The book was open to the third of that week and the light off that window had gone off the far side of the table by then.

The clerk had a lamp on her left arm and he had the last of the daylight behind him, and they worked under it like a room at the back of a shop. There had been about nine people up that stair since a quarter to ten, and two of them were on the landing at half past three waiting to be spoken to.

She came up in a coat she had not taken off in the passage and stood at the end of the table with her hands where she could see them.

"I want to ask about my sister," she said. "I am not in any hurry, because I know what the answer is going to be, and I have come anyway, because somebody has to be the one who keeps asking."

## The Row From The Eleventh

She had been on that stair on the eleventh of this month, and he knew it, because Wenna Raut had left that row open on the right-hand side of the table where it had been for eight days with the date at the head of it and an hour against the date and nothing under the hour.

There was one thing he could do for her and the room knew it before he did.

"Your sister cannot come."

"She cannot come," said the woman. "She is in a room she shares with a woman she does not speak to, and I have told her twice about this room and twice she said a thing about the hours and once she said she would come on the eighteenth and on the eighteenth she was not there when I went back at four o'clock in the afternoon."

"And you came up on the eleventh anyway."

"I came up on the eleventh and you told me what you have just told me and you were right, and I went down four flights and I did not know what I had come for."

## The Offer He Made And Was Not Allowed To Finish

He put his hand on the back of the empty chair beside the table.

"I will go down and ask her myself tonight."

He got about four words into what was going to be an explanation and Wenna Raut put her pen down.

"Sit down," she said.

"There is no rule about an officer going to a house."

"There is no rule about an officer going to a house and there is a rule about a person being asked twice by this office, and the second time an office asks you a thing you have decided the answer twice, and the second time you say no you do not come a third time, and she is not coming a third time." She looked at the woman. "That is not about a rule. That is about your sister, and you know it better than I do."

The woman said it herself, about nine seconds later, and she said it better.

"Do not send anybody to that house," she said. "She has had two people in her life this month deciding what she would find useful, and she shut the door on one of them."

"And if it was you instead of me."

"Then it would be me, and she would be right about that as well." She said it with her hands still in front of her and not moving. "I have been up this stair three times in a month for the same thing. If a fourth person comes, she stops hearing any of you. That is not a thing you can put right afterwards."

## A Space Under A Question

So the row stood.

He wrote nothing under the hour in the row from the eighteenth and he did not write anything under the hour in the row she had brought up that afternoon either, because the practice is that both go down in the words used and the words used this afternoon were a question about a person and an answer about that person being in the room, and she was in a room two streets off with a woman she did not speak to.

"It is going to stay like that," he said. "Not today. Ever. It does not have a rule that lets it out later."

"Then what is the row for."

"The row is for you to be able to come back and say that you asked, on this day, at this hour, in these words, and that this office gave you the answer it gives everybody in your position."

"That is a row about the office."

"It is a row about the office and it is the only thing I have got for you, and it is not nothing, and I am not going to tell you it is something else."

## Said In The Passage With People Going Past

Then he did a thing he had thought about for about nine seconds and then did anyway.

He carried that row out to the passage and said it where the lamp from that room falls on the wall, with about nine people going past on four narrow flights between a quarter past four and half past five, which is the most people any one stretch of that stair holds at any hour.

"This office can bring the answer to a person," he said. "If a person cannot come up four flights, then a person from this room can go to them, in the ordinary way, with a date and an hour on it, and read it to them in their own room, once, and be gone."

Four of them stopped. Two did not.

Then Wenna Raut said it from the table, loud enough to carry, and it was aimed at him and not at the passage.

"I am not entering that."

"Write the date and the hour and what I said."

"No," she said. "And here is the shape of it, so that you have heard it out loud before you have done it twice. A promise about a person that is written down is a thing that can be produced. Produced in her front room, by somebody who is owed something, read out in front of her by a man in a white plate, with a date on it."

## The Price Of Saying It In A Corridor

It stayed in that passage for about nine seconds and the clerk let it.

He could have taken it back. That is the thing about a thing said out loud in a corridor: it is gone before anybody has finished agreeing with it, and the only way to get it back is to stand there and say the other thing, which is two things now instead of one.

"That is fair," he said.

"It is not fair, it is correct, and you will get it wrong again in about four months and I will be here." She had her pen in her hand and she was not using it.

"You want to reach people who cannot come to you, and the only ways to do that are to keep a record of who they are, or to send a person, and both of those are a second thing in this city that knows where they are. We did not make it. We are the reason it exists."

"I know."

"You have known since the seventeenth of the month of the woman who cannot hear," said Wenna Raut, "and you have not said it out loud until now, and I would like you to understand that the saying of it is the part I mind. The offer costs nothing. The offer in a corridor costs about nine people a week knowing it."

## What She Would Have To Write If He Asked Twice

"I am going to ask you one question and it is the last one this afternoon," he said. "If I offered it in there, at that table, with the door shut, what would you have written."

"The date, and the hour, and that an officer of this office offered to carry an answer to a house in person, to a person who could not come up four flights." She did not hesitate about it. "And it would be true, and it would be the best thing anybody has ever written in that book, and I would have had to file it under the question of a person."

"Which means it would be read out in that room once, in the ordinary voice, with the person it is about in the room."

"Yes. There would not be a person in the room," said Wenna Raut. "That is the entire reason for the offer, and it is the reason the offer cannot go in."

## What Is Left Of The Day

At about five the carriers came up with the strap and the book went down four flights and about four hundred yards in the ordinary way, wired round the buckle twice, and he carried it as far as the yard and not to the flat file, because the flat file keeps a bound book and not tonight's.

At about ten past five a man came up those four flights with a slip in his hand about a form in a division building and he got an answer inside about six minutes and he went down with a fold with the flap shut and the date and the hour on the outside and nothing else on the outside.

He had come at the right hour about a paper.

She had come at the wrong hour about a person and she went down four flights with nothing, and she said thank you on the second landing and he said there was nothing to thank him for, and she said that too was correct and that she would be back in the spring.

Nobody up those four flights can say whether she told her sister about the offer or not. He put it in the passage on purpose, so that the reason would travel instead of the hours, and what it travelled to was a passage of a building, and a woman going down a stair with her coat on.

Four thousand and one hundred and fifty-eight days. Less four thousand and one hundred and fifty-one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and a row about a person stood on that table with nothing under the hour in it and would stand there until that building came down, and a man in a white plate said out loud on four narrow flights that he would come to a house, and about nine people heard it.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is forty-nine, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and thirty-eight days, which is fifty-seven months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Rail Tells Strangers

Three thousand nine hundred and seventy was the eleventh of this one and the eighteenth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven, and three thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven less three thousand nine hundred and seventy is seven, and eighteen less eleven is seven by the month count.

## Before The Book Came Up

He came down into that yard before the book was due, which he does perhaps twice a month, and stood at the top of the steps looking into the passage off Cordwain from about nine feet away, where a person can see the whole of it and be in none of it.

The morning had that brightness in it that a cold month gets about two hours after the light comes, where the stones underfoot are the same colour as the sky and every window in that passage is a different grey.

He was there to find out one thing, and he had it in about four minutes, and it was not the thing he had come for.

## The Woman Who Says It And Walks Out

A woman came along that passage at about a quarter to ten with a bundle on her back and a stranger beside her, and stopped in the middle of it, and turned to the stranger, and said the address out loud, all of it, in the ordinary voice, the street and the door at the head of four flights and the hours, in about nine words.

Then she walked out, and did not stop.

The stranger got a pencil out, wrote it across his own knuckles, and went up the street the other way. She did not wait for anything out of it, and about four people who were going past looked at each other.

Lin Hao stood on the steps nine feet away and understood that he had just watched a woman do in the middle of a public passage in broad daylight the exact thing that a man at a counter does for a living, except that she had not been asked and got nothing out of it and had not needed to be right, because she was only saying it to one man who needed it.

He had heard about this once before, in a passage, in the autumn, from the man at the rail, and he had taken it then for one woman's one afternoon.

## The Number He Came For

The man at the counter had it ready, because the man at the counter has everything ready and has had for nine years.

"About nine a fortnight," he said. "Before the card it was about four and most of them were lost rather than asking. Now they are asking and not lost, and that is the whole difference and I want you to hear it from a man who is paid nothing before you hear it from anybody else."

"None of them twice."

"None of them twice. Nine, and nine strangers, and not one of them has ever come back and asked me what I said to the last one. They do not know I am a fixed point. They think I am part of the finding."

"Are they angrier than they were."

The man thought about that properly, which not one other person in that city would have done for him.

"No," he said. "They are better informed and they are not angry. In the autumn about half of them arrived angry because they had been lost for a day and had made it somebody's fault. Now the ones who come are mildly put out about a stair and that is the whole of the damage. You have made me a better service and a worse one, because a man who is not angry comes back, and a man who comes back has a second question."

"And when the second question is about a person."

"Then you will have to stand in that room and be right, and there is not one thing on my counter that will help you with it."

He squared the quarterly sheet.

"I have been saying the address nine times a fortnight to people who are not angry, and it does not cost me anything, and one day one of them is going to say, you gave me the door in about four seconds, so tell me about my mother, and I am going to have to say, that is a different counter."

## Four True Sentences

"I told a man at this rail four true sentences about the card in December," said Lin Hao, "and you told me that four true sentences do not end the asking, they move it up a hill."

"That is what I told you."

"It is what happened." He put his hands in his coat. "I spent eleven weeks waiting for it to get worse and it got better, and it got better in the only way it can, which is that strangers are no longer lost, and I have to decide whether that is the thing I wanted."

"It is the thing you wanted," said the man, "and it is not the thing you get, and you are allowed to want both."

## He Asks For Nothing

He had one thing he could have asked for and he did not ask for it.

He could have asked the man to stop saying the stair, or to say it in a different way, or to say it only to people holding a card, and every version of that would have made the passage work for that office instead of happening next to it, and he understood at about the second minute of standing there that he would rather be a thing a whole city knows about than a thing two men have agreed to be quiet about.

"So I am not going to ask you for anything," he said. "Not today and not at the end of the month. The telling stands."

"You understand what you have just given up."

"I have given up the only instrument this office has ever had for finding out what is said about that door in a passage, and it was worth about a pencil's worth of nothing to me on the thirtieth of December, and I told you then what the card would do and you told me then what you have told me today, and we were both right, and here we are."

The man looked at him for a moment and then went back to his rail and put the quarterly sheet straight, and neither of them said anything else, because there is a time when a conversation in that passage has said everything it is going to say and continuing is only noise.

Lin Hao stood there for about nine seconds after the man had gone back to his window, working out whether what he had just done was modesty, and established that it was not.

He could have gone back up that hill and made it a practice. A practice can be said out loud with a door open to four people in a passage, and it can be entered afterwards by somebody with a pen, and in about four years it would be a rule that the office does not ask a man at a rail to stop telling a passage where a door is. It would have been a good rule.

It would also have meant that from this morning the only reason that man says those nine words is that that office has never asked him not to, and the whole city would be owed for a thing that was never asked of anybody.

## The Yard Afterwards

He went back up the hill and down again, because four flights and a yard is the whole of what he has between a quarter to ten and half past five, and came back into that yard about four minutes before that book was due.

## The Morning Afterwards

About eleven people came up those four flights before midday, and about four of them were about a paper they had brought up in their own hands, and not one of them asked for the door, which Lin Hao had been counting twice a day since the turn of the year and had stopped counting this morning somewhere between the yard and the second landing without noticing he had stopped.

That was the finding and he took it at the top of the stairs on his own, with his hand on the rail.

He had spent nineteen days keeping a count of a thing he was not going to be able to use.

A count of who came up a stair is a count of who found an address, and an address is not people.

The day a man who works a counter four floors up starts keeping it is the day that stair becomes a thing with a roll on it, and about four years after that somebody would be able to ask him whether the woman who came up in the second week of this month was the same one who came up in the fourth.

So he stopped. Not as a refusal and not as a practice, and he did not say it out loud to anybody, and Wenna Raut asked him at about eleven why he had stopped writing the hour at the top of the page when he had written it every day since the turn of the year, and he told her the true reason in about nine words and she put the pen down.

"That is the best thing that card has done," she said.

"It is the only thing it has done."

"It is the only thing in nine years that has been done by putting something down."

The carriers came up the stair with the strap and did not stop, because a yard in the middle of a working morning is not a place a person stops in, and went through into that room, and he followed them in and the morning began.

At half past five he carried it down four flights and about four hundred yards by himself, in the dark, with a buckle that has cracked at the tongue and is being kept together with a twist of wire out of his own pocket, and his left arm went on the second floor and came back on the third.

Then he went home up the hill, and on the third step from the bottom a delivery man going the other way said good evening to him, and Lin Hao said good evening back, and the man said it back, and neither of them mentioned a door, and that was the day.

Three thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven days. Less three thousand nine hundred and seventy, the eleventh of this one, is seven, and about nine strangers a fortnight come by a card, and not one of them twice, and a woman with a bundle on her back said the whole of it out loud in a passage and walked out without stopping.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is forty-eight, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-eight of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and fifty-seven days, which is fifty-one months and twenty-seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

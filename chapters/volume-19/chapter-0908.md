# Paste Four Days Old

Three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one was the second of this one and the ninth of this one is day three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight, and three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight less three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## The Corner At The Foot Of The Hill

The light goes off that kerb about eleven in the morning and this is the ninth of a month in which it comes back later every day and is still gone at four in the afternoon, because a wall with a hill on the other side of it does not care what the rest of the city is doing.

He went down at about four on the ninth with the copy of three lines in a good clean hand still folded in his coat, and he did not take it out.

He had brought it because he had wanted, for about nine seconds on the second of this month, to put it against that stone and see whether anybody had written about it. There were about sixty words in seven hands in four inches of dressed stone at the foot of that hill and not one of them was about a card.

## The Board By The Kerb

The board stands nine feet back from the kerb, and the two ruled squares on it are both empty, and the paste along its top batten is about four days old.

Somebody comes up this hill about once a month and does that and nothing else. It has been going on about nine years.

It was tacky under the back of a knuckle when he touched the edge of the batten, which is what cold paste does for about four days and not what it does afterwards, and he took his hand away again.

The boy with the brush was at the far side of that board with the pot at his feet and a cloth in his hand, doing nothing to it, waiting for whatever it is that he comes for, and Lin Hao did not ask him a single thing.

He wanted to.

That was the whole of what he wanted to do down there that afternoon.

The wanting was so clean and so simple that it nearly got the better of him. That boy pastes a board about once a month for nine years and has never been asked by anybody and would answer a question if he were asked one, and if a man were to ask him who cuts the words in the stone below, the boy would very likely say, and that would be the beginning of a thing this office has never once done at that corner.

So he did not ask, and he did not stand near him, and he did not look for him afterwards on the hill or at the top of the stair.

## About Four Inches Of Stone

The light was on the stone just enough to make out the shapes of the cuts and not one letter of what they said, and he knew about sixty words were in there in seven hands and he could have said the whole of them out of his own head in the dark, which is what he did last time he stood there, and he decided this time not to.

The seventh hand is low down at the right-hand end, cut small and hurried, about nine words, and it says that a woman came up four flights and was read to and went home, and then it stops in the middle with the cutting clean behind it.

He looked along the stone past where that cut stopped, the way a man looks along a wall for the next thing on it.

Nothing is cut there. The last inch and a half at that end is dressed flat and has been dressed flat for years, so anybody coming to add to it would have to take a chisel to a face with no history on it.

That is a different act from adding to a wall that about four hundred people a week already read past, and a person who has never done the second one cannot be assumed to want the first.

Two words are scratched small and hard in that stone, well apart from the seven, and they are not one of those seven and are never going to become one, and he has never once found out who put them there and is not going to start this afternoon.

## Her Sheet Under The Batten

Under the lower batten her sheet is still held against the wood where it has been since before this office had a name for itself. There is a date at the top of it and a ruled pencil line across the foot of it in her own hand.

It has never been folded, never turned over, never taken.

He did not touch it and he did not put the back of his knuckles against it, and he did not stand there working out whether a person who cannot read has ever known that this thing was hers.

Nine years of rain and of paste going on and coming away again, and not one messenger has ever gone up this hill to find the woman whose hand ruled that line, and nothing has ever been put to her by anybody, and nothing is going to be put to her by anybody he brings up here.

## What He Came Down There To Find Out

He wanted to know whether any of those about sixty words was about being sent to a door and finding nothing at it.

That was the actual reason. On the second of this month a woman in a lane said her neighbour had gone up four flights twice and knocked, and he had spent the whole of that morning being told by a man with a right answer that the fault lay with a person being helpful, and somewhere under all of it was the question he had not asked out loud, which was whether anybody has ever cut it into a stone that somebody has already done.

There are three answers to that question on this hill and he could not have had any of them.

In the dark he cannot read the words. With a lamp he could, and he had thought about it on the way down, and the reason he had not brought one is not a rule anybody wrote.

It is that a man crouching at that board at dusk with a lamp on it is a man that about four hundred people a week go past the kerb and remember, and in about four years a stranger would come and ask who he was, and the day that happens this corner stops being the one place in the city that has never asked him for anything.

So he came down there with a question and went up it without one, and that is the first time in about nine years he has done that at the foot of that hill.

## Standing There Longer Than The Work Requires

He stayed about nine minutes at that corner and the work he had come to do took him about forty seconds.

The paste was four days old. The two squares were empty. The stone had seven hands in it and nothing cut past the end of the last one. The boy had not said anything and had not looked up. Her sheet had not moved.

That corner gave him nothing at all, and he stood there in the cold until he understood that he had not come down that hill to find out anything.

For about nine years he had been coming down that hill because it is the one place in this city where nothing has ever been written down about him.

This afternoon he had come down it to find out who had copied three lines out of a good clean hand, and the answer was a delivery man in a lane off Cordwain and a woman selling bread and a man at a counter, and not one of them had ever been to this kerb and never would be.

There is a thing he wants from that corner and has never named, which is that the seventh hand stops in the middle. About nine words, cut small and hurried near the right-hand end, the cutting clean behind it, and about nine hundred people a week go past that kerb and not one of them knows that the sentence on that wall is broken off in the middle of a thought.

He has wanted somebody to finish it for about two months. He did not ask the boy. He did not go back on the second day with a light, and he has not gone back with one since, and there is no arrangement by which that sentence is ever going to be finished by anybody but the hand that started it.

## Back Up Four Flights

He went back up that hill at about four and a quarter with his hands in his coat and the copy of the hours still folded in the inside pocket where it had been all afternoon, unused, and he did not take it out in that room either.

Wenna Raut was at the table with that book open and about nine rows in it for the day, and she looked at his face and at the grit on his boots and said what she always says when he has come up off that hill.

"Nothing," he said.

"Nothing since the seventh of November?"

"Seven hands and nothing cut past the last of them. Both squares empty. Paste four days old. Her sheet unmoved. Nothing new and nothing to bring up and nothing to enter."

She put her pen down, which she does when a thing cannot go in a book.

Then she said: "You have gone down that corner twice this month and come back with nothing both times, and I want to say one thing about that before the quarter turns, and it is not about the corner."

"Say it."

"This morning nine people came up four flights and every one of the nine knocked wrong once," said Wenna Raut. "That is what I have been keeping since the second of this month, and I have not written it down, and I am going to price it before the thirtieth of March, out loud, with the door open, and you are going to stand there and hear it."

She opened the book again.

Three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight days. Less three thousand nine hundred and ninety-one, the second of this one, is seven, and the paste on the top batten of a board at the foot of that hill was four days old, and four inches of dressed stone at the foot of that hill hold seven hands and the newest of them stops in the middle with the cutting clean behind it, and nothing was asked of the boy with the brush.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is sixty-nine, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and seventy-eight days, which is fifty-two months and eighteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

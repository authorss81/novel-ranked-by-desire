# The Worn Card Read Back

Four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two was the third of this one and the tenth of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and sixty-nine, and four thousand and two hundred and sixty-nine less four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## A Man Who Could Not Make It Out

He came up the last of those four flights at about twenty past eleven on the tenth of this month with a piece of paper in his hand and he stopped about nine feet short of that door and said, out loud to the stair, that he had been standing at the bottom of it for a quarter of an hour.

"I cannot make out the end of it. I have been coming up this street since about ten and there is somebody on the board in the market about it and it is the same thing with different words on it."

"What board."

"The one by the foot of the hill," said the man. "The one with two squares on it."

"There are no two squares on that door."

"Then that is not what it is about," said the man, "and I would like to read the three lines, and I am asking you because I have been on those four flights twice this morning and I have got about forty minutes before I have to be back at a counter."

## He Read It And Stopped In The Middle

He had told himself in the corridor that he would refuse.

He read it.

He got the street off it and the door under the street, and then he came to the second line and he stopped dead, because about the fourth word along that line the card went soft under his eyes and the ink that had been in the fibres in November was not in them in December, and two words that had been separate for a year were standing about the depth of a coat button apart.

That is a thing that does not happen in the half second it takes to read a card. It took him about four seconds, and there were about nine people on that stair.

## And Then The Man Said It Back

The man with the paper looked at the card and then looked at him and said, "Go on."

He went on.

And then the man with the paper said the whole of it back to him.

Not the first line. The second one and the third, from the door, in the ordinary voice of a man at a counter who hands people things all day, and he got every word of it.

He said the door at the head of four flights off a third court. He said the hours, which are a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour. He got the second half of the second line right, where the corners were gone, and he got it right because he had been looking at the card for a quarter of an hour at the bottom of that stair in the last three days.

"Did I get it right," said the man.

"Not a word of it wrong."

"That is not the hours I had on the paper," he said. "That is half an hour different at one end and the same at the other end, and I have been at a counter since ten."

"Then the paper is not the card."

"The paper is not the card," said the man, "and you have just said the hours out loud on a stair, and about four people behind me have heard them, and I am going to say them to a man at my counter tonight and he is going to write them on the back of something." He put the paper in his coat. "You know that."

"Yes."

## What The Rules Cover

"I am going to say the reason out loud," said Lin Hao, "and the reason travels with it, and I am saying it anyway."

"There is a reason."

"There is always a reason." He had his thumb on the frame of the door. "This office keeps no roll, no index and no count of who comes up that stair. It has never corrected a copy of that card in any form."

"That is about nine words so far."

"It is, and here are the rest of them," he said. "It has two written refusals in a book that had nothing at the foot of its page for nine years, and about nine hundred people a week read a figure off a wall four hundred yards off and get it wrong, because a count of pieces of paper travels like anything else that is written down."

"And you read three lines out loud to a stranger on a stair at about twenty past eleven."

"Then you know it better than the paper does."

"That is not the same as any of those."

"It is not," he said. "Every rule in this office is about paper. A written answer, a copy, a transcript, a slip, a form, a wall with four words under a number on it."

"That is six things and I have watched you refuse all six."

"I have refused five of them at least twice and one of them for about four years," he said. "I have never in nine years refused a man a thing he said out loud with his own mouth in a passage. The second one goes by somebody's mouth and not by anybody's hand."

"And you know what that means."

"I know that there is no rule on this earth that stops a mouth."

## The Part He Has Never Said To A Stranger

"You should not be doing that," said the man. "You are a man in a white plate and you have just given four people on a stair the address of an office."

"I know exactly what I have done and I did it four seconds after deciding not to do it, and I want to tell you why, and this is the part I have never said to a stranger and I am saying it to you because you have asked me to read it and that means you are owed the whole of it."

"Go on."

"I could stop reading it to strangers," said Lin Hao. "I could do it in about four seconds and I could give the reason and go on with my morning, and about four people a week would stop hearing it, and every one of the four would then go and stand at the bottom of that stair on the wrong morning in the dark with a piece of paper."

"So it goes by mouth instead."

"It goes by mouth instead and I cannot stop the mouth, and I am not going to stand at the top of four flights in the ordinary voice pretending that refusing to write things down is the same as refusing to say things."

## What Is Left Of The Tenth

The man with the paper went down at about half past eleven and did not write anything down on that stair and did not look back, and he had a piece of paper in his coat that was wrong at one end and was going to be right at the other end tonight.

Two of the four people who had been on that stair behind him did not go down. They stood at the turn while he was gone and then one of them came up and asked what the hours were again, and he said them again, slowly, the second time, and she wrote about half of them on the back of her hand and got the half wrong and he told her which half.

Wenna Raut came in from the passage at about ten past twelve with the door still standing open.

"You read it out."

"I read it out to a man who had been standing at the bottom of that stair for a quarter of an hour and who could not make out the end of the second line."

"Not to a man who asked you to."

"Not to a man who asked me to," he said. "That is the part I have been wrong about since March, and I did it at about eleven twenty this morning, and I would do it again before lunch."

"You have written the whole of this down nowhere."

"There is nowhere to write it and that is the reason I said it out loud," he said. "Not one person in this room is going to be able to check what I just did, and you are going to have to take it on the four people who were on that stair, and that is a worse arrangement than a page and I have had it since the eighth of September."

"The man on the market board."

"He said there are two squares on that card."

"There are not two squares on that card."

"There are two squares on a board at the foot of a hill four hundred yards off. I stood four feet from that board about nine minutes in September and could not read a word of it."

"And you will not take a lamp to it."

"I will not take a lamp to it in my life," he said. "And if he comes up here again with a paper that says the hours and a pair of squares on it, I am going to read him the hours and say nothing at all about the squares."

Then he said the other thing, which he had been carrying down that stair in his head since the twenty-fourth of October and had not said to a person.

"Somebody is going to come up here with a piece of paper that says that card stood there a year and that this office is shut at the turn of the year. That is going to happen in the next nine days and I cannot stop it and I am not going to correct it when it does, because correcting a copy is how we got nine wrong ones in this city in the first place."

Four thousand and two hundred and sixty-nine days. Less four thousand and two hundred and sixty-two, the third of this one, is seven, and a man on a fourth-floor landing read three lines off a door and then said them back from memory and got all of them right, and there is not a piece of paper anywhere in this city that says he was there.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September, is seventy, and the forty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand eight hundred and forty-nine days, which are sixty-one months and nineteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# A Field Officer's Return

Four thousand and twenty-six was the seventh of this one and the fourteenth of this one is day four thousand and thirty-three, and four thousand and thirty-three less four thousand and twenty-six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## A Route And Not A Cart

He met her at the end of a district at about six in the evening, at the corner where a delivery ends, and she was on foot because she is always on foot.

There is a bag on her with a strap across it and nine folds of paper in it, each fold closed, each with a date and an hour on the outside and nothing else.

"Give me that," he said.

"No."

"It is four miles from here."

"It is about four miles and I have walked it with a bag since the first year, and I will not put it down for you because a bag in two hands has no column in any book in the world, and the column is mine."

She turned her head about nine degrees, which she does at a counter and at nowhere else, and went on.

## Six Of Them About People

"Three of the nine are about a paper," she said, before he had asked. "Six of them are about a person, and of those six, four had already found your door before I got to it."

He walked a street in silence for nine seconds.

"Say the rest of it."

"The card is on the door. A carrier arrives about a fortnight after a person has read a card, because a division does not know that anybody has read a card until a person comes to a counter and asks." She shifted the bag. "So for two weeks, in every case, they have you first and me second, and I am a woman arriving afterwards with the answer to a question they have already asked somebody else."

"That is not your division's fault."

"It is nobody's fault. That is what a door with hours on it does to a woman with a bag, and I have thought about it for nine days and I have come to the conclusion that all of it is fair and that I am the person in this arrangement who is behind."

Then she said the part she had been carrying.

"One of the four asked me at my own counter last week whether I was the woman who worked four flights up."

"What did you say."

"I said no," said Runa Sato, "and she said thank you, and she came to the door on the fourth instead of to my counter, and that was the correct decision on her part and it is nine days of my walk."

## The Return She Signed In The Third Month

They came off the main road into a back street with a laundry at one end, and it smelled of wet stone and of soap.

"There is a thing I am going to tell you on my own two feet," she said, "before I have eaten, and you did not ask me for it."

He said nothing, which was the correct thing and cost him the four seconds it took to keep it.

"In the third month of the year before last I signed a return that said a thing had gone back where it came from. It had not gone anywhere. It was in a folder on the second floor and I knew it was in the folder."

"And you signed it."

"The counter above mine was short by one in that month and the count had to come out, and the man at that counter was going to be asked where the one had gone." She said it in the flat voice, walking. "I signed it and I stayed in the service, and in four years the question has never once been put to me by a superior in that building, and you have not either, and you have known about it for three years."

He had. He had never decided what to do with it, and he understood at the third step of that street that a man who has kept a thing about a woman for three years without a plan is holding it so that he can say he is not holding it.

"It costs you something," he said.

"It costs me one thing only," said Runa Sato. "I cannot sit at a table with a person and be shown a figure I got wrong and know it was mine. I can hold any amount of other people's numbers, all day, for years. It is only mine I cannot sit next to."

## Knocked Twice

Her rooms are two, up a stair that smells of soap and cold plaster, with a door that shuts on an iron catch she keeps clicking with her thumb.

Before she had the lamp lit she stopped with her hand on the catch and knocked on her own door, and did not open it.

Lin Hao stood on the step in the cold and understood, and did not ask, and knocked.

She opened it.

"You did not ask me why," she said.

"You were not going to tell me on the step."

"I was going to tell you on the step if you asked, because I have found that if a man asks on the step then I answer on the step and it is over." She let the door stand open. "Here is the rule of my own front door and neither of us is going to call it one. A person knocked for is fetched. A person knocking is kept. And they are not the same person twice in one day."

"That costs something."

"It costs nine seconds and I pay them every night of my life," she said, "and nothing in this city makes them the same, and you understood that in four seconds standing on a cold step, which is more than my last four officers managed."

## Two Rooms Over A Yard

The rooms are two over a yard with boards underfoot and a table in the middle of the first one, a chair, a shelf with a jug on it, and a bed against the far wall. The lamp stands on the table at the height of a man's chest. The bag of nine folds is on that table with the strap across it, where she put it down the second they came in.

The yard door stands open on the chain, so that after nine somebody can come in from the lane without knocking and go out again, and four or five of them do it in a night.

The rain came in under it and went about four inches across the boards and stopped.

She shut it with her heel, and turned round, and put her thumb under his jaw and tipped his face up and looked at him.

"I have wanted this since the seventh of April," she said. "I said so in a corridor that day to a man who wanted a docket. I am saying it a second time in front of you so that you have got it twice in the same voice."

He told her about the chest that has been spent since the fourth year and about a cold room over a yard.

"Then stop twice," said Runa Sato, "and I am not going to be polite about the waiting, because I have a district at eight and I have not eaten."

## She Sets It Up Herself

She got a hand on his chest and pushed him backwards until the back of his legs found the edge of the table, and then she got herself up onto it on her heels, shifting the bag four inches out of her own way.

"Sit on it," she said. "Do not hold me. I am a stone and a half and you have got a chest that stops."

He took a seat on it and she went down in front of him where he was and used her own hand first, with two fingers, low, and set the pace herself, and said nothing at all for nine seconds.

Then she got her mouth on him.

She did not go straight down onto it. She took him along the length first and only then opened her mouth, and she set the depth with a hand at the back of his skull and would not let his hips move, and she watched his face the whole way with the patience of a woman who counts things for a living.

He came hard enough to put his heels against the boards and she held on and swallowed and did not move off him for four seconds afterwards.

"That is one," she said, wiping her mouth with the back of her wrist. "Now you are going to do something and I am going to tell you what."

## On The Table And Then Off It

He got his hands under her and took her weight off the table and she came up with him and got herself over him against the edge of it, and she was wet already and it made a noise against the boards a man in the yard could hear, and she did not lower her voice about it.

"You are going too fast," she said, "because you have been carrying that book down four flights since October and you think this is the end of the day."

He slowed down.

"Good," said Runa Sato.

She rode him at the edge of that table with her fingers curled over the rim behind him and her mouth against his jaw, and she talked the whole time in the voice she keeps at a counter, and what she talked about was the division.

She took him deeper and said something in her own language that he was not meant to understand, and then went back to the counter voice.

"That is the corridor I work in," she said. "That is what your card did to it. I am not saying it as a complaint, because a man who is paid nothing said it out loud in a street and the rest of us cannot make him un-say it."

He got his hand down between them and found her cunt already swollen and slick under two fingers and worked her there while she pushed down onto him, and her breath went short and her thigh began to shake against his wrist.

She came with her mouth shut and her fingers closed on the edge of the table behind him, and it went through her in four beats and she rode out six or seven more before she let go of anything at all.

## In The Wet Four Inches

Then she put him off the table and went down onto the boards on her knees in front of him, with the lamp standing on the table behind his shoulder throwing both of them nine feet up the wall.

It had gone off him and come back on him while she was talking, and she read it off his face the way she reads a docket, and she told him what she wanted done to her instead of asking.

He got an arm under her and took her up onto her back in the wet four inches by the yard door with her knees hooked over his, and she was slick inside and hot and took all of it in one push, and she said out loud that she had nine folds in a bag, six of them about people, and this was what she was carrying at six o'clock.

He put his hand back between her legs and closed it on her and she came a second time nine seconds later with her heels digging into the boards and her voice going, and then she got a hand on his chest and stopped him, hard, about two seconds early.

"No. I have eight districts left and I am not finishing on this floor," she said.

She took him out of her and finished him with her hand at her own pace, looking straight up at him, and she took nine seconds longer over it than the job needed, and wiped her hands down the front of her trousers before she said anything about it.

Then she shut the lamp off and took a seat on the step outside her own door in the dark while he got his breath.


## What She Will Not Carry

She got her book out of her coat when he was dressed, and opened it on her knee, and there were two columns on the page and neither of them was the division's.

"Since the card went up, I have been asked nine times in nine corridors to carry a list," she said. "The names of everybody who has asked at that door, so that a courier can be sent to them instead of a person walking."

"And."

"And I have said no nine times, in person, in a corridor, standing up." She shut the book on her thumb. "And I have not written one word of it down anywhere, because a refusal in writing is a document, and a document about that office is a row about itself, and I am not the woman who gives that building a row about itself."

"So in four months somebody will ask you in writing."

"Somebody will ask me in writing in about four months and I will have to answer in writing, and I have not got one word of a reason yet," said Runa Sato. "That is the whole of what I will not carry, and I have carried nine other things this month and none of them were heavy."

## Nine Degrees

She walked him down to the bottom of the stair, and the rain had stopped coming under the yard door by then and the lane outside had gone quiet.

"You will be wanting something delivered," she said.

"I will."

"Then it is by hand, it is not by a courier, and it is not by a man in a white plate who walks four miles to be honest about it." She turned her head the nine degrees. "And when it is by hand I write down whose hand took it and in what order, and that is all I write, and the order is the one piece of it that is true about anybody, and that is why I do it."

"I would want the order."

"You will want the names," said Runa Sato. "And you will not have them, and I am going to hand you the order before the week is out because I would rather hand you something I can defend than something you will ask me for again in a month."

Four thousand and thirty-three days. Less four thousand and twenty-six, the seventh of this one, is seven, and a woman with nine folds in a bag has said out loud in a street that strangers find that door before a carrier can be sent to it, and has refused nine times in nine corridors without once writing one of them down.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is fourteen, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fourteen of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and thirteen days, which is fifty-three months and twenty-three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

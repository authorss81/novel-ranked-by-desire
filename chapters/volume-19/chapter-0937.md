# Same Two Nails

Four thousand and one hundred and ninety-three was the twenty-fourth of this one and the thirtieth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine, and four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## The Morning Of A Quarter-End

The six lines at the foot of that standing sheet were read out first, in the ordinary voice, and that is the order they are read in and it is not changed because it is a quarter-end.

Then the head.

"Eight hundred and fifty-two," said Lin Hao. "It has not moved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, when the sheet it is printed on was one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine."

About nine people had come up that stair that morning and four of them were still on it at about eleven.

"Three of them came about papers," he said. "One of them came about a person and is standing on the second landing because the practice says that person has to be in this room, and he is not, and I have told him so, and he will be back at a quarter to ten on the third."

"Then say the head and stop looking at the landing."

"I am not looking at the landing," said Lin Hao. "I am aware of the landing."

"It is the same thing in you and it is not the same thing in the book."

"It is thirty-one sheets and thirty quarters and they differ by one, because the thirty-first has not gone up yet and will go up at four this afternoon on the same two nails as the forty-sixth."

"You have said that at a counter," said Wenna Raut, "and you have said it in a filing room with no window, and today you are saying it in a lane."

## The Woman With The Slip

He went down the hill at half past eleven, which is early for that lane, and there were four people in it and the ninth stool had a delivery on it.

She was standing at the rail with a piece of paper in her hand and she had been standing there long enough to have decided she was going to wait.

"It is off that wall," she said, before he had got to the end of it. "Four hundred yards off. There is a number at the top of it and four words underneath the number, and I have read it about six times in three weeks and I want to know what it is."

"It is what the four words say it is."

"The four words say it is a count of sheets and not of people, and there is a division slip in my hand with the same number on it at the top and a total at the bottom and about nine lines in between it, and I am not going to stand in a lane and be told that my nine lines are pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them."

She had it folded into eight. She had not lost it.

## Saying It Where A Person Can Check It

He told her the whole of it in that lane, standing up, in about four seconds, and then told her the rest of it, which took longer.

"Eight hundred and fifty-two, unmoved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. Thirty-one sheets and thirty quarters, differing by one, the thirty-first not yet gone up. Four thousand one hundred and ninety-nine less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is two thousand seven hundred, and two thousand seven hundred is thirty times ninety."

"And it is a count of pieces of paper with a striking at the foot of them. It is not money and it is not people and it is not a count of anybody who has come through that door."

"Then why does my slip have it at the top."

"Because somebody in that hall put the number from a wall onto a form, and the number went onto the form, and now a number that counts pieces of paper is the heading of a division return about nine lines long." He looked at the folded paper in her hand and did not ask for it. "That is what happened to it and I was the person who said the words it was copied from."

## Brack Writes It In His Own Hand

Emil Brack had the rag out and he had been listening from the third stool for about nine minutes.

"Say the number again," he said.

"There is no reason to say it twice."

"There is every reason to say it twice, and the reason is mine." He had the chalk in his hand and he was not using it yet. "A figure that gets said once in a lane is a thing nine people heard. A figure written in a lane is a fact anybody can stand here and check, and I have priced things in this lane for nine years and I know the difference between the two."

"Say it again, then."

He said it again. Eight hundred and fifty-two, and the two thousand seven hundred, and thirty times ninety, and what it counts.

Brack wrote it on the slate in his own small hand where the sooner figure already stood in his own small hand, lower down and to the left, in chalk that had been wiped and re-chalked about nine times since the thirtieth of December and had never been rubbed out.

## Two Figures On One Slate

"That number has never been in this lane," said Brack, "and it belongs to a sheet that has come off a rail four times a year since the first year of it, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do not know what that means, because I have priced a door twice this year and I am going to price this once."

"Price it."

"Before today that slate had one figure on it that anybody could check, and that figure is yours. It is the number of your neighbours who have been told where you are by somebody who was told first."

"It was out by more than half a quarter on the twelfth of this month."

"It was, and it has got no unit after it, and it does not go on this slate, and I have told you that twice this year and I am telling you a third time for free." Brack put the chalk down and stood back about two feet. "Now there are two figures on that board. One of them is about nine people in this lane. One of them is about pieces of paper in a building four hundred yards off."

"Everybody who comes down this hill from now on reads both of them off the same slate. Neither of them says anything about the other. That is a thing you have done, and not me."

The woman with the slip had come closer. She was looking at the slate and not at either of them.

"Those two are not about the same thing," she said.

"They are not."

"You could say so under it."

"No," said Lin Hao. "The day a second figure goes under the first one is the day that slate has two numbers on it that are about different things, and a person standing here in about four months will read them as a heading and a total, and there is nothing in nine years that stops that."

## What He Refused, And What It Cost Him To Say It In A Lane

"You have refused that to a woman in a lane who came about four hundred yards to ask a fair question," said Brack. "In my lane."

"I have refused it to myself, and you are the place where saying it travels, and that is what you have been for about nine years." He looked at the third stool. "Nine people read that slate a week. One number under another one is a total under a heading. I am not going to let a woman with a division slip find out in about four months that the number at the top of her return is the sum of the number underneath it."

The woman with the slip was quiet for about four seconds.

"That is not what I asked you."

"No," he said. "And you can put that in your own form, and I will not correct it, and in about a month the version on it will not be what I said here today, and you will not be able to check it. That is the arrangement and it is not going to change."

## The Woman With The Slip Goes Back Down The Hill

She folded the paper back into eight and put it away.

"You have told me three times in about two minutes that the office does not correct a copy, and you told the woman who wrote seventy words for that wall that on a landing in July, and both of those are on a thing I have read." She said it without any weight on it at all. "I am going to leave this slip exactly as it is. I want you to know I am leaving it because I was told to and not because I believed it."

"That is the correct reason to leave a paper alone."

"It is a poor reason to leave a paper alone and it is the only one anybody has given me." She went down the hill with the slip still folded into eight.

There is nobody else on this hill who knows what he did in that lane, and about nine people a week come down it, and one of them every week for the next four years is going to read two figures off one slate and be left working out why they are there.

## The Forty-Sixth Comes Down At Four

At about four that afternoon the forty-sixth came off that rail and the forty-seventh went up on the same two nails with nothing new at the foot of it.

He did it himself, at four, with the rail in the flat file room four hundred yards off, and there was a young clerk in that room with no window who had copied about four hundred figures off other people's sheets that year without knowing what any of them counted.

"Same two nails," she said.

"Same two nails," he said. "It has been the same two nails since the first year."

She wrote the date at the top of the sheet that went up and the hour against it and nothing else, because that is what the sheet that went up last quarter had on it, and because he had told her in June that a caption under a figure is a sentence the office cannot take back.

Four thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine days. Less four thousand and one hundred and ninety-three, the twenty-fourth of this one, is six, and the head of that standing sheet went onto a slate in a lane off Cordwain in another man's small chalk for the first time that any part of that office has ever been in a lane, beside a sooner figure that was not rubbed out, and the two of them are about different things and neither one of them says so.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is ninety, and the forty-sixth sheet was face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them and came down at four this afternoon, and the forty-seventh is up on the same two nails.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine days, which is fifty-nine months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Winter Mornings Kept

Four thousand and five was the sixteenth of this one and the twenty-third of this one is day four thousand and twelve, and four thousand and twelve less four thousand and five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## Two Weeks On A Bit Of Paper

The carrier who had the bad back put it to him at about eleven on the twenty-first, at the top of that stair with the strap over her shoulder, in front of the other one and in front of Wenna Raut, because she does things in front of everybody or not at all.

"Two weeks," she said. "Two weeks shut, and a piece of paper on that door saying the weather has shut us, and we go back in on the first good morning. Not a rumour. Paper. And the room can stand empty the whole time, and you do not have to come down that stair in the dark in the worst of it, and the weather gets the blame because the weather has earned it."

"And the hours."

"The hours get a fortnight's grace," she said, "and that is what I am asking for. Every stranger who knocks at that door in those two weeks learns one thing, and it is the one thing we have spent nine years and one card not teaching, which is that what is written on that card can be put aside."

## What Two Weeks Teaches

Lin Hao stood on that landing and let it be true for about nine seconds before he answered her, because she was owed that and because the answer was not the answer he had ready.

"A fortnight's grace," he said. "And what does a man who has come up four flights with a question do on the third morning of a fortnight's grace."

"He waits."

"He waits, and then he tries it again later in the day, and then he comes on a day he has not seen the paper, and then he stops, and he does not stop because we did him a kindness. He stops because the door taught him that the door is a mood."

He shifted so she could get past.

"We have spent nine years building a thing that is true when it is shut, and two weeks of paper would teach this whole city in about four days that our hours are the nearest thing we have to weather."

"You would rather carry it down four flights with a cracked buckle."

"I would rather carry it down four flights with a cracked buckle than find out in the autumn that half a year of strangers thinks that room opens when it feels like it."

## He Says It To Everybody Together

He made her wait for it until half past nine the next morning, with the door open and both carriers on that stair and Wenna Raut at that table and about four people going past in the passage.

He had worked out where to say it four days earlier, standing at the bottom of his own stair at about seven in the evening with a cracked buckle in his pocket, and the place he had chosen was that room and not this landing, because a thing said on a landing is a thing the four people on a landing carry down and a thing said at a table with the door open is a thing the whole building owns.

He said it in the ordinary voice, standing at the end of that table.

"From the worst weeks, that book comes up. It comes up at a quarter to ten, five days in seven, and it comes down at half past five, and I carry it down. Not two weeks and not three. Every working morning from here for as long as I stand at that table, in whatever the weather is doing."

Not one of them said a word for about four seconds, which is a long time in a room where people talk.

"The woman who asked me for it first has a back that goes, and she asked because she was frightened for me and not because she thought I could not do it."

He turned round to her on the stair.

"You did not ask me because it was hard on you, and you did not ask me because it was right for the building. I am going to say yes to you, and you are going to have to be one of the people who heard me, and that is the whole of what you get out of it."

"I am saying it now so that in a month you cannot come back to me and claim you were never told what you were agreeing to stand in."

She said, "Then I would rather be asked at seven in the evening," and he said it had to be at half past nine with the door open, and that was the end of that.

## What It Closes And What It Costs

"What it closes," he said, "is shutting that door for the winter. Not for two weeks. Not for a fortnight at the turn of the year. There is going to be a morning in the third week of a bad month when that stair is the worst thing in this city and I am going up it anyway with a book under my arm, and no piece of paper is going to go on that door at all."

"And the cost," said Wenna Raut, from the table, because she always asks the cost before anybody else does and she has done it since the spring.

"The cost is these hands." He turned them over and showed them to that room, palms up, the way a man shows a thing he is not going to argue about.

"Four flights and about four hundred yards in the dark, five days in seven, and not for this winter. For as long as I stand at that table."

"And on the bad mornings," said the carrier with the back, "when the buckle has gone again and the stair is the worst thing in this city."

"Especially on those. If I carry it down on a bad morning then it is a practice. If I carry it down on a good morning for a year and then cannot do a bad one, I have taught about nine hundred strangers that our hours depend on the weather, and that is the one thing the card was nailed up to prevent."

"That is everything you have got."

"That is everything I have got. A drawer in this room shut since the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, and it is not mine to open and never will be. And nothing has been in the inside left of this coat since the third of December two winters ago, and nothing is going into it while I stand here."

"A book up four flights by two women in the morning, and the buckle's tongue has gone on the second flight in the frost and is being kept together with a twist of wire out of my own pocket. A chest that was spent in the fourth year, and three floors of washrooms in a branch office four nights in seven."

"I am rank one at the start of this and I will be rank one at the end of it. Nothing is coming my way and I have stopped arranging my life around a thing that has not arrived for about six years."

She nodded, and looked at his hands, and did not say anything kind, which was the correct thing and cost her something.

"So we are both standing in it," said the carrier with the back.

"We are all standing in it, and the worst weeks are the next ones, and I have had the whole of the winter of deciding about it, and I decided it at about a quarter to ten this morning with the door open."

## The Morning Went On

Nothing in that room changed at half past ten, which Lin Hao had expected, and had also known he would not.

About nine people came up those four flights before midday and about four of them were about a paper they had brought up in their own hands, and one of the nine was a man who said he had been told at a counter off Cordwain that the door was open, and that the man at the counter had got the door right and had got the day wrong, and he had come up on the wrong morning and would come again.

"Then come again," Lin Hao told him. "That is what the third line is for."

At half past five he got the strap round that book himself, with the buckle where it goes when there is nobody to turn it and the wire twisted round the tongue, and the woman with the back came past going home and said one thing to him on the stair without stopping, which was that the wire will need twisting about twice a week and that on the bad nights she would rather do it than hear about it.

At the bottom of the yard he stood with his arms hanging in the cold and did not try to raise either of them, and gave them the four minutes they wanted, and then walked home up the hill at the pace of a man who has carried something a long way and is counting nothing at all.

## The Price Of Saying It Out Loud

The price of saying it out loud caught him on the third step from the bottom and it was his own fault.

A delivery man was coming the other way and he stopped, and said, to a man in a white plate on a public step, that the place four flights up off that court wanted knowing about and it was open mornings.

"I wrote it out for my mate," he said. "From what the fella at the counter told me."

"Then it is open mornings," said Lin Hao, and went on up the hill, and stood in his own hall in the dark for about four minutes before he understood what he had done.

He had made a practice out loud, in a room, in the ordinary voice, with a clerk at a table and two women on a stair and about four people in a passage. That is how anything gets decided in that room.

And there is a man in a passage off Cordwain who is paid nothing and reads the foot of a sheet out loud to whoever stands there, and by the end of this week he will have the worst weeks of that winter too, out loud, in that passage, with about nine people standing in it who did not have to be told by anybody in that room.

Seven days from now it comes down off that rail at four, and another goes up in its place, and on that morning there will be a number at the head of a page that has not moved since the fifth year, and he will say it to somebody, once, in the ordinary voice, because that is what a head is for.

He did not go back down and correct anything. He went to his table, and put his hands out in front of him in the dark, and found that they were still open.

Four thousand and twelve days. Less four thousand and five, the sixteenth of this one, is seven, and the book comes up through the worst weeks, five days in seven, from a quarter to ten to half past five, and what it costs is his own hands, in the dark, for as long as he stands at that table.

*Less three thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is eighty-three, and the forty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand five hundred and ninety-two days, which is fifty-three months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

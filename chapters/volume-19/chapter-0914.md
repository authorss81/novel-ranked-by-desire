# The Hand That Takes It

Four thousand and thirty-three was the fourteenth of this one and the twenty-first of this one is day four thousand and forty, and four thousand and forty less four thousand and thirty-three is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

## Why On Foot

The Bureau can stop a courier.

A courier goes to a counter and shows a docket, and a clerk in a division building can look at a docket for as long as it takes to decide whether he likes the look of it, and if he does not like the look of it the thing in the courier's bag does not go anywhere that week, and the person who asked finds out about nine days later from a woman at a counter.

One kind of movement in this city cannot be stopped in that way, and it is a man on foot who was going that way anyway. He carries no docket. He shows nothing to anybody. There is a fold of paper going into a hand on a door and no counter standing between the two of them, and a clerk who would have liked to look at the paperwork has nothing to look at.

Lin Hao went out at about half past two on the twenty-first with an answer in a fold of paper with the date and the hour on the outside and nothing else on the outside.

## Four Streets And A Yard

It was four miles. He had walked it once before, in the autumn, in daylight, and it had seemed like a walk.

At about ten minutes to five the light went off the tops of the yards the way it does in that month, and by twenty past it there were four usable minutes of a day left and then the lamps came on in the streets and not in the yards.

He went through the middle of it at about half past four, when the yards are the colour of the sky and every window in them is a different grey, and he went through it slowly because the alternative was standing still in a street in that month with a fold of somebody's answer in his coat.

There are four miles of that city between the room and that door and none of it is difficult. What is difficult is four miles of it at the wrong end of the afternoon, once, on a day when the office is closing at half past five and has been closing at half past five for nine years.

The wire had been twisted twice about the tongue of the buckle since the middle of the morning, because the strap is nine years heavy and the tongue has gone.

Somewhere in the fourth mile his back went, the way it goes, at the bottom of a step it had no business being at the bottom of. He got up in nine seconds without putting a hand anywhere, because a man who puts a hand on a step on an empty street in that month is a man who explains his back to whoever finds him.

## The First Door

The first door was a door he had never seen and he was four minutes past five when he got to it.

A woman came to it with a cloth in her hands and he gave her the fold and told her the date and the hour on the outside and nothing else, and she took it and read it standing in the doorway in nine seconds.

"It is about your mother's ceiling," she said, to somebody behind her.

"It is."

She turned round and said, "It says they have it on a page from the sixth of the last month and the ceiling is not in it, and they want to know whether she is still willing to go up," and a man behind her in that doorway said, no, and did not say anything else, and she turned back to Lin Hao and said, "That is what it says and you may tell her I read it out."

Then she handed the fold to the man.

He could not read it. He had said so at the door before Lin Hao had it out of his coat, without embarrassment and without asking for it to be read again, and he took the fold in both hands and looked at the date on the outside the way a man looks at a thing he has decided to trust.

## Four Hands

Then he had to get it forty yards and he did not want to walk forty yards, and he called a child out of a yard and gave it to her and told her the door, and she ran it, and he stood in his own doorway and watched her go.

That was three hands and he had been the fourth.

She had run it to the right door. That was all the risk in the two hundred yards between the man's door and the child's and it came out right, and the child came back along the yard at a run and went past him without stopping, because a child who has been sent to do a thing and has done it does not stop on the way back.

Three of those hands were strangers to each other, and the child knew the man she was running for, and had said good morning to him about nine days ago, and the woman in the first doorway had never seen any of the other three.

Lin Hao wrote all of that down in his own book, in his own hand, at the top of a page in a yard, and then he did the thing he had come out to do, which was that he did not write the rest of it down.

## What He Wrote Down

Four lines, and nothing else.

One. The woman in the first doorway, who read it out and gave it to the man.

Two. The man, who could not read it and sent a child forty yards with it.

Three. The child, who ran it.

Four. The woman at the far door, who took it off the child and did not send the child back with anything.

Then he tore the page out along the line the clerk rules with, which she does at the bottom of every book in that building, and put it in the inside of his coat on the right, and nothing has ever gone into the inside of that coat on the left.

He read it over twice at the bottom of that yard before he tore it.

Four lines and the shape of a thing, and not a single thing in the four lines that could be asked about in a year. Any person who came at him with that page in four years would learn that on the twenty-first of this month a man on foot carried an answer four miles to a street off the east end and that four people took it, and would learn nothing whatever about who they were.

That is a real record. It is not a small one either. It is the first record of anything that office has made in nine years that would survive being read by a stranger, and it survives by not saying anything.

## Three Wards Off Cordwain

He got back to that room at about twenty to nine, which was after half past five, which means he carried the Register down that night as usual and came up again and did the second half of the stair in the dark for nothing.

She read the four lines.

"This is short," said Wenna Raut.

"It is not wrong. It is short."

"It is the first entry in nine years that is short on purpose." She put it down and squared it. "Nine years and every line in that book is a whole thing, and this one has four lines in it with the people taken out, and you did that at the bottom of a stair in a pair of boots that have not been off since a quarter to ten."

"That is exactly what I did."

"And it is the first entry in nine years that anybody could get hold of."

"Yes," he said. "That is all I want from you to say about it."

"I know it is. I said it because somebody has to have said it in this room tonight, and you were not going to, because you have never once in nine years come up that stair and told me the cost of a thing at the same moment as you did it."

"I have."

"You have told me the cost. You have never once told me the cost before the fact, and this is the first time in nine years you have brought me a thing half done."

She shut the book and put the strap out for the evening.

"Then that goes in this room and nowhere else," she said, "and if you come down that stair at the end of the week with the four names, I will hand the page back to you without reading it, and we will both know what that book is for."

He looked up.

"You hand that page to the two women on that stair and one of them reads it aloud in a yard one day this year, and this room has a book with four fingers in it," said Wenna Raut. "You have done the correct thing and the correct thing is about nine words long, and I am the one who has to work out how to be the person who holds it."

"Would you rather I had written nothing."

"I would rather you had asked me at a quarter to ten," she said, "and you did not, and we will both go on with it."

## What Was Actually Frightening Him

He went down that stair at about nine and stood at the top of it in the dark with a book that was not to be carried anywhere until half past five tomorrow, and worked out what he had been frightened of for the last five hours.

It had not been the four miles, and it had not been the light going off the tops of the yards, and it had not been the step at the bottom of which his back went off. He had done all of those before.

It was the other page.

There is one, and there is a very short distance between one and four, and the distance is one sentence, and the sentence is the names.

Four names would go in beside four numbers and then it would be a record of a man, a woman, a child and a stranger in four streets in one afternoon, and then in about two years there would be a second afternoon, and then it would be a column, and a column is a thing a person can be asked about, and a person asked about twice begins to be a person somebody is keeping.

He had spent nine years refusing to build one thing, and he had built it this afternoon and taken four names out of it and let that stand as all of what happened.

At the bottom of the yard a delivery man was going out and said good evening to him on the step, in the ordinary way, and neither of them mentioned a door.

Four thousand and forty days. Less four thousand and thirty-three, the fourteenth of this one, is seven, and four hands took one answer across four miles in the wrong light, and three of the four were strangers to each other, and an order of four was written down and the people in it were not.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is twenty-one, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-one of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and twenty days, which is fifty-four months and no days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

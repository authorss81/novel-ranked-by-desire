# Nine Ways To Make A List

Four thousand and fifty-four was the fifth of this one and the twelfth of this one is day four thousand and sixty-one, and four thousand and sixty-one less four thousand and fifty-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## Nine Feet Of Table

On the morning of the twelfth he took the length of that table, from the drawer at the left end to the edge at the right, and stood at the left end of it with his hands behind his back, and worked out every way a person could be found in this city by somebody standing where strangers stand.

There were nine.

He had known four of them since the thirtieth of December, and four more had come out of that room in the three months since, and the ninth came last, and it had taken him until about eleven.

He said each of them out loud as he got to it, because he was not going to hold nine of them in his head past lunch.

## The Eight That Work

The first is the man at the counter under the window, who says the whole of it to anybody who stands there, and who is right, and who is paid nothing, and who will not stop.

The second is a woman with a bundle who stops in the middle of a passage and says it to one stranger and walks out.

The third is a neighbour telling a neighbour, with nothing on paper, and there is nothing on any paper anywhere in this city that can put it right.

The fourth is somebody who has seen that door and writes the three lines out afresh on the back of a form at a counter, and hands it over, and believes every word of it.

The fifth is a man who writes the hours across his own knuckles because that is what you do when you are going somewhere, and a delivery who cannot read them has to ask.

The sixth is a clerk in a division building who knows the address and writes it on a slip for a man to take away, and there is a bar on that desk and it is not the same thing twice.

He stopped on that one, because it is the one he had never thought about, and the reason he had never thought about it is that it is not an act of kindness at all.

It is an act of procedure. A man goes to a division building and asks a question, and a clerk looks the address up in a book that every counter in that division keeps, and writes it on a slip, and hands it over, and goes back to work.

That clerk breaks nine rules a day and is not breaking any of them, because the book is the book and the slip is the slip, and every address in it sits in a book that is never opened out loud in a waiting room.

In four months this office is going to be asked for those hours for a wall, and the asker will come up four flights, and the sixth of these nine is the one that will do it.

The seventh is a shop: somebody asks in a shop for the card that is about washing, and the woman behind the counter goes and looks at the door, and hands the information back over the same counter.

The eighth is a man who was going that way anyway, who writes it on a delivery docket because it costs him nothing to write four lines on the back of a docket.

The fourth, the seventh and the eighth are the three that took nine years of his life each, and he had spent an entire winter being wrong about the fourth.

He had assumed, for four months, that the way this city would find his door was a copy. Somebody would write it out, and the copy would spread, and one day about four hundred people a week would walk past a door that had never existed, built entirely out of paper.

What had actually happened is the reverse of that. Eight ways had come up, and every one of them was a person being useful to another person for nothing, and none of the eight had been in a hurry, and none of the eight had ever set eyes on the door.

## The One That Is Free

"That is eight," he said, "and every one of the eight works, and I want you to understand that all eight are somebody being helpful and none of them is doing me a favour."

Wenna Raut had her pen in her hand.

"And the ninth," she said, "is the door."

"The ninth is the door," said Lin Hao. "It is free, it is exact, it takes about four flights, and it has an address and a set of hours on it and it cannot be written to, because a door is not a counter and there is a person standing in front of it."

"It cannot be copied wrong."

"It is the only one of the nine that has to be walked to," he said. "And that is all this office has got, and eight other ways are going round it in the city while we are standing here."

## What Every One Of Them Ends In

They went through the eight again and made themselves say what each one finished with, because that was the part neither of them had said yet.

The counterman finished with a stranger carrying four lines in his head.

The woman with the bundle finished with a stranger's knuckles.

The neighbour finished with nothing at all.

The man at the counter with the form finished with a piece of paper and a signature.

The man with the hours on his own hands finished with somebody having to ask him.

The division clerk finished with a slip.

The woman in the shop finished with the card about washing in her own hand.

The delivery man finished with a docket.

Eight of them ended with three lines on something in somebody's possession, and the thing in the possession had no authority whatever and could not be corrected by anybody, and would be copied from.

"That is a list," said Wenna Raut. "That is a list of eight ways of finding this office and every line of it goes with a bit of paper."

"Yes."

"Then it exists somewhere, and the somebody it exists for is a woman who has to hand it on."

"It exists here," said Lin Hao. "Two of us, on the length of a table, and neither of us is going to have it in the same order by the autumn, and that is the arrangement and I would rather have that arrangement than the other one."

"You would rather be the only man in this city who cannot check his own list."

"Yes. And I would rather that than the other arrangement."

## Why He Wrote None Of It Down

"And you are not going to write it down."

"I am not going to write down one word of it."

"I am not going to either," said Wenna Raut, "and I want it on the record that you said that at about eleven in the morning of a day when you had already decided it at eight, and that you were pleased with yourself for nine seconds and then went and took the strap out of the drawer and wanted to write the first line anyway."

He did not deny it.

"The strap is there because the book is about to go down," he said.

"The strap has been in that drawer since March."

"It has," he said, "and that is the ninth thing, and it is not a way anybody gets found, and I am going to leave it there."

## The Man In The Passage

He said all of it out loud in that room at four in the afternoon with the door open, because a thing worked out in a room with the door shut on the twelfth of a month is a thing somebody else has changed by the end of the week.

He had got as far as the fifth when a man came up those four flights and stopped in that doorway and listened to the rest of it, and when he had finished, the man came about two steps into the room.

"Could it be found by writing to it," the man said. "Not coming. Writing."

"A written address would be a second copy and the second copy is the one that gets copied."

"Then it has to be walked to."

"It has to be walked to."

"That is nine ways a person can get hold of you," said the man, "and eight of them are on other people's hands, and if you ever want to tell anybody else in this city how to find you, you will have to start by fixing the eight."

"And I could fix them," said Lin Hao.

"You could put the true hours in a division building, and every copy in this city would be wrong inside a month, and every wrong copy would still be a copy, and every person holding one would say that the office gave it to them, which it did."

"About nine copies a month."

"Nine copies a month, and four hundred people a week going past a kerb with all of it in their heads."

He went back down four flights and did not give his name and was not asked, and neither of those two facts was entered, because neither of them is a question anybody said out loud.

## What He Took Away From It

The book went down the four flights at half past five, wired round the buckle twice, and at the bottom of that yard he stood still for nine seconds before he went down the hill.

Because the man at the counter was right. He had been right in a lane for nine years without anybody asking him for a single fact, and somewhere between here and Cordwain there was a man who kept nine stools in a lane and had been right about every fact since the first year of it.

If eight ways were walking round his door in this city, then they were walking round that lane first.

Four thousand and sixty-one days. Less four thousand and fifty-four, the fifth of this one, is seven, and eight ways of finding that office were counted out loud in that room and written down nowhere, and the ninth one is a door four flights up off a third court.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is forty-two, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and forty-one days, which is fifty-four months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

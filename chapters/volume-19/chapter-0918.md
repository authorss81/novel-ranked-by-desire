# The Telling Priced Right

Four thousand and sixty-one was the twelfth of this one and the nineteenth of this one is day four thousand and sixty-eight, and four thousand and sixty-eight less four thousand and sixty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Nine Stools And A Slate Four Feet By Three

He went down to that lane at a quarter to twelve on the nineteenth and stood at the end of it until Brack had finished giving out two rations and wiped the end of the board, which is what he does while he is getting to a thing he means.

The board had been wiped and re-chalked that week, which it is not every week. There is a row of days along the lower part of it in his own hand where a person standing there could set their own against it, and under that row, in the same hand and the same size of chalk, the sooner figure is standing where he put it on the last working day of the year.

"In the last month of the winter I came down here and asked what a card like that does to a street of mine," said Lin Hao, "and you priced the door at me, and you were right not to. I have come back to ask the same question about a different thing, and I am going to be wrong about the second one as well."

## What He Would Not Price

"You are asking me to price the door," said Brack.

"I am."

"No."

"No, and I am going to tell you why in about four sentences and then you can go up that hill." He set the chalk down on the ledge. "A price goes into a rent. That is not a figure of speech, that is a thing that has happened in this city about nine hundred times, and every time it happened it was a man in my position who put a figure next to a door and somebody else with more need than me read the two things together."

"I know that."

"You know it and you came and asked me again anyway, and I am going to treat it as a compliment rather than as an insult, because a man who knows a thing and asks for it once is rare."

## What A Stool Is For

"Then price the telling," said Lin Hao.

Brack looked at him.

"Nine a week," he said. "Nine in my line, in this quarter, the same as it was in the last quarter, unmoved. About nine in nine of them are strangers and about nine in nine of them are asking where something is, and they ask it well, and they go away."

"And that is the price."

"That is the quantity. The price is what a stool is for."

He turned round and looked at his own nine, going down the lane from the mouth to the wall.

A lane is a working arrangement between about nine hundred people and nine stools, and Emil Brack has kept every part of it for nine years without ever once being asked to.

That is the whole of what he is and it is a larger thing than most things. It is also the reason he can price anything at all: he has no wage, so nothing he says in that lane can be traced back to anybody paying him for it, and in a city where nine figures out of every ten reach a rent through a person, a man with no wage is a man whose numbers go where he puts them.

He is the last honest instrument in this city and he has never once been thanked for it by anybody who matters to him, and on the thirtieth of December of last year he took four years off a board and wrote four years less in his own hand in front of about nine people, and got nothing for it except a man in a white plate being frightened of him for a month.

"Before the card, a stool was for stopping on. A man comes up that lane because he lives in the ninth district or he has business in the fourth, and a stool is for a man who is staying."

"Now about nine times a week a stool is for a man who is passing through on his way to a third court off a hill. A man who is passing through sits down for the length of about nine words and then he is gone for good, and he is never in this lane again."

## The Price Said Out Loud

"So nine men a week turn a lane into a place people go through on the way to somewhere," said Lin Hao.

"That is the whole of it and you may write it on that slate if you have a pencil."

He put his hands in his coat.

"What else."

"One stool in nine now has a man on it who is waiting to be told where to go," said Brack. "By the end of the year I will have two stools that people sit at, because a man comes back for the pot and for the noise, and seven that people wait at."

"And a stool a man waits at is worth about what a stool is worth in this city, which is nothing. I have got nine of them and a slate and my own books and I am not going to be able to stop anybody working that out over a year."

"And the telling."

"And the telling is the only good thing that has happened to that lane in nine years," said Brack. "About nine strangers a week, and every one of them polite, and about four of them have said thank you to me, and I have not been thanked for anything since I was thirty."

He set the chalk down on the ledge.

"And I am going to say the thing I have not said, because you asked me a real question and that earns a man a straight answer to one. That telling is the reason I have nine stools instead of four."

"Four."

"Before the card this was a lane people walked through on their way to the ninth district. I could have kept three stools and done it on about half of what I spend on the pot," said Brack.

"A man comes down that lane now and stops, because about nine times a week there is a stranger at that rail who wants to know where a door is. A stranger who is asking is the most interesting thing that has happened in this lane since I was young."

"And when the stranger comes back a second time about a month later, and he has, and he has not come back a third time, that is a person in my lane who now knows a thing."

"That is a business."

"That is not a business," said Brack. "It is about nine more strangers a week coming through that mouth of the lane, and every one of them takes about nine seconds, and nine seconds times nine is about a minute and a quarter, and I have got that minute and a quarter for nothing and I am not going to be able to keep it."

## What Brack Would Not Let Him Do

Lin Hao stood in that lane and let it be what it was for about nine seconds.

"It is the right price," he said.

"It is a price and it is mine and I have said it out loud in a public lane at about eleven in the morning, and about four people heard it, and I am not going back to it in a week with a better number because I am the sort of man who has one."

"I was not going to argue it."

"You were going to argue that the door is worth more than the telling, and you were going to be wrong," said Brack. "You have been in that room four days a week since the seventh of April and I have been in this lane every day since the first year, and I am telling you what the telling does and the telling is what is actually arriving in this city."

He put the rag in the apron and nodded at the slate without turning round at it.

"And there is still a third thing in my head that is not on that board and is not going on it this year. It has been there since the ninth of the month before last. You asked me about it once, in the last month of the winter, and I said no, and you have not asked me about it since, and I have noticed the not asking."

"I have noticed it myself."

"You are owed it," said Brack, "and it is getting older, and I am not going to say another word about it in a public lane."

## Respect Plainly Given

"You have offered me nothing about that figure and I am going to keep it that way."

"That is a better sentence than the one you had ready," said Brack, "and it costs you the same."

"I stood on that step in the last month of the winter waiting for you to take it out on me, and you had already decided not to, and I have carried that about for four months, and there is nowhere else I would rather say it than in front of nine stools."

"You are not going to get it twice," said Brack. "Once is what a man gives, and I said that to you in the last month of the winter and I am saying it now because you have come down here twice this year and asked me about money."

"And I am not going to be decent at you a second time either, because I did that once and it cost me about a month of work and I am still carrying it."

"A man in a white plate who is kind to me in the same breath as asking me a thing is a man who has learned how to buy. You have not learned how to buy. You came down here in the winter frightened of me and in the spring asking me a real question, and those are the two arrangements I can work with."

"That is fair."

"And the office still does not close."

"Still."

"Five days in seven, and it came up four flights this morning with a strap round it twice because the strap is the wrong one, and I carry it down at half past five in the dark."

"With the wire on the buckle."

"With a twist of wire out of my own pocket, which the woman who brings it up rewires about twice a week and would rather do it than hear about it."

## A Question About The Slate

Then Brack picked the rag out of his apron and folded it once, and stood with it in his hand, and looked at the slate instead of at him.

"That figure has been on that board since the last working day of the year," he said. "Not a soul has come in here and asked me whether it is still right, in four months."

"And what would you have said."

"I would have said it is on the slate and it is my chalk and it is mine." He put the rag in his apron. "You are going to want to come down here and look at it before the quarter turns. I know you the way I know a man who checks a door twice when it is already locked."

"I have not decided that."

"You decided it about four steps down that hill and you have been carrying it since the fourth," said Brack. "Come and look at it when you like. And when you do, do not touch the board, because a man who touches a slate to read it leaves a smear on it, and the smear says he was here, and I have got nine stools of people who can read a smear."

Four thousand and sixty-eight days. Less four thousand and sixty-one, the twelfth of this one, is seven, and a man with nine stools priced the telling rather than the door, out loud in a lane, at about eleven, and said the number of stools a lane has left for people who are staying is two.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is forty-nine, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and forty-eight days, which is fifty-four months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

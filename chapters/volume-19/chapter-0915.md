# The Midnight Knocking

Four thousand and forty was the twenty-first of this one and the twenty-eighth of this one is day four thousand and forty-seven, and four thousand and forty-seven less four thousand and forty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## Sitting In The Dark On Purpose

On the afternoon of the twenty-seventh the woman with the back stopped him on the top step and said she had heard somebody on the third-floor landing at about one in the morning on the twenty-fifth, going up and going down again, and that whoever it was had not knocked, and that she had not gone up.

He had not believed her for a day and a half, and then at about one o'clock on the twenty-eighth he went up those four flights with no lamp and let himself into that room, pulled a chair over to the window, and stayed in the dark with the door shut and the outer door on the latch.

He had not come up to do anything. He had come up to sit in the dark and find out whether a person comes up those stairs at that hour and what they do when they do.

That is not a reason anybody would put in a book, and it is the reason he was there.

He had a lamp in his coat and left it in his coat, and he had known that before he came up those stairs and had checked the pocket with his thumb on the second landing, because the whole of a man sitting in a dark room for an hour is that he comes out of it holding a light.

There is also the matter of what a lamp does to a door. A lamp under a landing at one in the morning puts a man four flights up a building on the record of every person in it who was not asleep, and there is no version of that which ends with him still being a stranger to them.

## Two Knocks And Then No Movement

At two o'clock there were two knocks on the outside of that door.

They were not hard. They were the knocks of a man who does not want to wake a building, which is a knock with the weight taken out of it, and between them there was about two seconds.

Then nothing. Then a footstep that turned about nine inches and stopped.

Lin Hao got up without making the chair do anything and crossed the room in the dark by memory and stood with his hand on the bolt.

## On The Landing With The Door Shut

He did not open the door. He opened it onto the landing and stepped out and pulled the outside door to behind him, so that the room behind him was shut, and the landing was dark and the stair was dark above and below it.

"Sit down on the step," he said. "I am going to stand here and I am going to say this once and you are going to hear all of it before you answer anything."

He said it there, on the landing, in the ordinary voice, to one man in the dark at two in the morning, and the room door stayed shut behind him, which was the arrangement he had chosen in about two seconds and had not decided on.

He had worked out that sentence in about nine seconds with his hand on a bolt, and the nine seconds were the whole of the time it took to establish that there was not one version of it that was kinder and one version of it that was softer, and that softening it was the same thing as apologising for the card being true.

The man did not answer that. Somewhere below them a gate went along a yard and the sound went away down the hill, and that stair holds everybody at that hour in the same way.

"It is a quarter to ten. That is what that card says and the card is right, and you have done everything that card told you to do, and I am the one who put it there."

## Everything The Card Told Him To Do

The man was about four minutes getting it out, and he was not angry and he was not drunk, and he had come off a shift at one that morning and walked from there.

"I finish at one," he said. "I have finished at one for eleven years. I saw your card in a fortnight, in a bookbinder's, off a man who had it in his pocket and I read the hours and I thought, a quarter to ten, and I have had a morning at a quarter to ten every day of my life and I never once thought about the other end of it."

He put his hand up against the back of his own neck.

"And then I got on the tram and it was one o'clock in the morning and I said, well, that is a quarter to ten, and I am here at one, and if it says a quarter to ten then it opens, and I came up four flights in the dark like a fool with my boots in my hand so as not to wake the building."

"You came up the third court."

"I came up the third court. There is a card about washing on that door with the holes closed over, and a man at the bottom of it told me the third door off the landing in a street, and I have been to that door before. In March. At two in the morning. I knocked twice and went back down."

Lin Hao stood on that landing in the dark and did not say the first thing that came into his head, and the first thing that came into his head was an apology.

## What He Was Not Going To Soften

"What I am not going to do," he said, "is tell you the card should say something else, or that we are sorry, or that it is a kind of place. It is exactly what it says. You have read a true thing and you have acted on it and the trouble is that a card carries an hour and not a day, and an hour on a piece of paper is standing at both ends of the day in a man's head."

He heard the man breathing.

"So here is what I would like you to do, and it is all I have got. You have a shift that ends at one and you have finished. Come at a quarter to ten, in the morning, with the light on, and you will find that door open, and it will be open until half past five, five days in seven, and it will be open for you in particular because I will be standing at the end of that table."

"And if I cannot get a morning."

"Then you come on the morning you can and not at one in the morning, and you will not be turned away at a quarter to ten for anything."

## The Second Time Is The One That Counts

The man stayed on that step for a while and did not say anything, and then he said the thing that made Lin Hao put a hand out for the stair rail.

"I knocked in March," he said, "and that landing was empty, and I went back down, and I did not come back because I had decided you were a place that does that to people."

He got up.

"I have got a boy who does not sleep till four," said the man, "and I have walked past this court at about half one every night for eleven years, and since the turn of the year I have looked up at this stair about nine times and worked out which of those four doors it is, and tonight I looked up at it and came up."

He said it with no weight on it at all, and it was the sentence that Lin Hao was going to be carrying for about nine years.

"You looked up at it nine times."

"I looked up at it about nine times and I have never once counted the floors wrong."

"You have had two," Lin Hao said. "That is the second one. And I know what that costs, because the woman at that table has said it out loud to me and she was right about it, and a person who knocks wrong once comes back."

"Then you had better be there at a quarter to ten."

"I will be there at a quarter to ten."

## What Is Coming Instead

The man went down four flights in the dark holding his boots, and Lin Hao stood on the landing and then went back into that room, shut the door, and took the chair over to the window again.

He had been right. That was the part he could not get round. The card was true, the hours were true, the man had done everything a true card told him to do, and the correct answer at two in the morning was the same answer as at nine, and he had gone out on to a landing in the cold to say it himself because Wenna Raut was not there and he could not send anybody.

At about four he got up and did not write one word down.

He stayed with it until about a quarter to four and then got up and did not write one word down, and the reason is not a rule anybody made.

A row in that book takes the date and the hour and the words a person used, and a row about a man who comes at the wrong end of an hour has nowhere to go, because the words he used were about a door and the thing that happened was to a man, and those are two subjects and the book takes one of them at a time.

He had learned one thing in three hours and it was not about the hours. A door with a time on it cannot be stopped at the wrong end of that time by anything the office does, and every one of about nine people a fortnight arrives at the wrong end of it once, and about one of them arrives twice, and the second one does not come a third time.

And a man who has told nine people in a week will tell nine more inside a month, and what he will tell them will be whatever a woman in a lane or a man at a counter gave him for nothing, and there is a kind of person in this city who has a figure and a standing and a place and cannot put any of the three down anywhere, and she has been writing them on the backs of things for about a month.

That is not going to arrive this week. That is going to arrive within a fortnight, and it is going to arrive in somebody else's handwriting.

Four thousand and forty-seven days. Less four thousand and forty, the twenty-first of this one, is seven, and a man who had done everything that card told him to do was turned away correctly at his own door at two in the morning, by the man who wrote the card.

*Less four thousand and nineteen, the thirtieth of March, is twenty-eight, and the forty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-eight of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand six hundred and twenty-seven days, which is fifty-four months and seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Stopped Platform

Four thousand and one hundred and sixty-five was the twenty-sixth of last month and the third of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and seventy-two, and four thousand and one hundred and seventy-two less four thousand and one hundred and sixty-five is seven, and thirty less twenty-six is four, and four with three taken out of the next month is seven by the month count with the crossing named as itself.

## Two Of Four Names On One Rota

The rota page in that branch carries four names and two of them came off the same night shift for about four years and had never once walked it together.

He came off at half past five. She came off at six. The stop is at the end of a street about nine hundred yards from the branch gate and they had been going to it at the same hour for so long that neither of them had asked the other the way in about a year.

"You have not asked me the way in about a year," said Nadia Ferrous.

"You have not asked me."

"That is because neither of us needs to and one of us would have said something about it."

## Nine People Waiting For A Service That Stopped Calling

The last service goes at about ten past six and the first comes at about nine past six, and in between that platform belongs to whoever is standing on it.

It stopped calling in the fourth month of this year. A notice went up at the two stops either side of it and none went up at this one, which is the ordinary way a stop dies: not by a decision but by four lines of print a quarter of a mile away from the place.

There were about nine people on that platform at six o'clock in the morning, which is about nine times what there should be for a service that runs twice in the dark.

"That is the fourth month," she said. "There were four people on this platform the week it stopped and there have been nine every morning since, and the ones who are here in September are not the ones who were here in May."

## What She Has Been Doing With Three Lines

He had asked her about the market end twice since the card went up and got a lane, a rate and the weather twice.

"About nine times since the thirtieth of December I have read those hours out loud to somebody who could not read them," said Nadia Ferrous.

She said it in the voice she handed things over in, which is level and quick and does not ask for anything back.

"Eleven. I have said eleven and I have said it to people who could not read a clock on a wall, which is not the same thing, and about four of the eleven were standing at a counter with a piece of paper in their hand with the hours written out on it already and got them wrong at one end."

"What did you say about the wrong one."

"I said the hours are the hours on the door and the paper is somebody's handwriting."

"And did they believe you."

"They believed me because I am the one with the lift," she said, "and I hand people things all night and they take what I hand them, and that is about nine mornings of the week a person standing in front of me, and it is not because I am right. It is because I am the one who has a thing in her hands."

## Where A Safe Answer Lives

They were at the far end of that platform by then and the nine of them were spread along it, and four people came through from the far side about once every two minutes and went on.

She stopped walking.

"A thing said in a passage with people going past it belongs to everybody going past and to nobody standing still," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is why I say them here and not in that room. Everybody on this platform heard my voice about nine words ago and about nine of them could not afterwards tell you that I said it, and I could not be found out by anybody who was not standing here, and that is the only safe place left in this city."

"That is the whole of what I came down this hill to be told and I have been waiting four months for it."

"It is also the reason you cannot run your office out of one," she said. "That book of yours wants a date at the head of a line and an hour against the date. A passage has not got either."

"So I would have about nine good ones a day and none of them would be a row."

"You would have about nine good ones a day and none of them would be a row, and in about four months a woman in a lane would work out that the only way to get anything out of that door is to stand near it and talk."

## The Shelter At The Far End

It is a brick shelter about four feet by six with three sides and a bench in it and a light that has not worked since the winter before last, and it stands at the end of that platform where the ballast is, and it is not locked, because there is nothing in it to take.

It takes about nine seconds to get in and out of and they had about forty minutes.

"Forty minutes," said Nadia Ferrous, sitting down on the bench and not taking her coat off. "The first is at nine past six and if it is late I get one at about half past, so do not stand in that doorway looking at the sky."

"I am going to say something about what I am doing in that doorway and I am going to do it now."

"Say it."

## What Happens In The Shelter With The Door Open

She was on him before he had finished deciding about the bench, standing up off it with her hands going into the front of his coat at the shoulders and turning him round about nine inches so that his back was to the brick at the far end and hers was not.

"I have wanted this since the fifth of August," she said, "and I have been carrying it up four flights of that branch on about nine nights a month, and I have not said so out loud because the second I say it out loud I have to say why."

"Say why."

"Because in about four years somebody at that office is going to understand something," she said. "They are going to work out that the only person in this city who can get a person who cannot read to the right door is a woman who runs a lift at a market end. It is going to be found out, and it is going to turn out that your whole office has been standing on me."

"Since the thirty-seventh of the year before last."

"Since about four years before that, which is the night you carried a bundle of nothing up four flights." She had the front of his coat open and her fingers were hooked in it, not against him. "Do not touch me for a second. I am going to do this first."

Then she did, and she was quick about it, and she said so.

"You are going to stand there and count," she said. "I have been up since about six yesterday and I want this in the first four minutes and I do not care what your chest is doing, and you can tell me afterwards."

He got his hands up and she took them and put one of them where she wanted it and left it there. She was slick between the legs and had been since about the second she said the word passage.

She made him feel how wet she was with his own hand and then took the hand away again and made him watch her get at it herself, two fingers, the way she does about nine hundred times a year with a strap and a hook and no audience at all.

She got her own cock out of her trousers after that and did not hurry about it and did not ask him to do anything with it, and she told him exactly what she wanted done with it in the voice she used for a price at about nine hundred at eleven o'clock at night.

He did it the way she told him to and she counted the seconds out loud under her breath the entire time, which he had been told about on a stair two years before and had not believed.

"I have thought about this about four times in about four months and every time it was in a stair," she said, and then she stopped talking, and then she came, which she did inside about a minute with her mouth open and one hand closed in the front of his coat and her back against the cold brick.

There were about nine people at the other end of that platform and the wall of the shelter was brick and the window faced the track and not the platform, so what came out of that shelter and into the morning air was about four words and a sound of boots.

Afterwards she got him with her hand, and her cunt was still slick with what she had done to it, and he came hard with the wire of the buckle in his own fist because there is nowhere in a shelter four feet by six to put anything down. She held him there with her forehead against his jaw for longer than either of them counted.

Then she made him sit down and drink the last of the water from the bottle she had brought up the hill, and she put her boots on, and she looked at the clock on the wall of that shelter, which is a station clock and is about nine minutes slow and has been for years.

"Twelve minutes," she said. "If it is not on the track in twelve minutes I am walking, and you are walking with me because you are going that way anyway."

## The Cost Of A Practice That Is Not Written Down

They were on that bench with their coats open and their breath going and about nine people at the other end of a platform.

"Have you kept what I asked you for," she said.

"Not once."

"You have not kept it, and that is not a fault. You have not been asked." She put her hands on her knees. "That means it is not a practice. It is a thing you agreed to, and a thing you agreed to and never used is a promise, and a promise that has never been used is not a thing anybody is keeping."

"What does it cost," said Lin Hao. "Say it here. You are on a platform with about nine people on it and four of them come past about every two minutes, and I would like it said where it is said and not in that room."

"That is a filthy place to say a serious thing."

"It is the only place I have got at six in the morning and you are the one who taught me it."

Nadia Ferrous looked out at the ballast for about four seconds.

"It costs that neither of us can ever prove it happened," she said. "If I come up four flights in about four years and ask you whether I asked you something in about nine years, and you say yes, then it is two people saying a thing happened, and in about four years one of us is dead, and the other one is sitting in a room with a book that wants a date and an hour, and there is nothing on the table to put in front of anybody."

"That is the whole cost."

"That is the whole cost," she said, "and I am telling you that I do not mind paying it, and I am telling you now so that you cannot decide later that I did not know what it was."

## What She Gave Him Instead Of A Rule

"And you are not going to ask me to stop," she said.

"I was not going to ask you to stop."

"You were going to. You had it in your mouth about four minutes ago and you have been chewing it since the second I mentioned the eleven."

"I was," he said, "and I am not going to, and I am not going to thank you either, and both of those are the same decision."

"They are not the same decision. One of them costs you something." She looked at him. "The other one puts my back room in your mouth."

"And there is going to be a fifth."

"There are four people in this city keeping something for you that is not written down, and there is going to be a fifth." She put her hands on her knees. "You will never be able to check any of them. Get hit by a cart in a street tomorrow and every one of them stops inside a week, and nothing in the world knows there was anything to stop."

## The First Of The Morning

The first came in at about ten past six with about four people already on it, and about five of the nine people on that platform got on with about nine parcels between them.

"It was here at nine past," said Nadia Ferrous. "It is never here at nine past."

She went to work at about half past six and he went up four flights and got in at about a quarter to ten with his hands smelling of cold brick.

She had not written any of it down. He had not asked her to, and the reason he did not ask her to was that asking is a kind of keeping, and he had spent nine years learning what that costs.

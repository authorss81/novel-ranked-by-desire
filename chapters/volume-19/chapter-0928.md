# The Heavier Refusal

Four thousand and one hundred and thirty was the twenty-first of last month and the twenty-eighth of this one is day four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven, and four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven less four thousand and one hundred and thirty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## The Person Who Was Entitled To Ask

She came up four flights at about ten on the twenty-eighth of this month and she was not the woman in the boardman's coat, and she had a folder under her arm with something in it that had been folded and opened out and folded again.

She was not a board's clerk. She was the officer in that division who is entitled to ask one institution in this city for a statement of what it is, and she had used that entitlement four times in nineteen years and three of the four had been about other institutions and one had been about a laundry.

"You are not going to ask me for the hours," she said, at the top of the stair, before she was in the room.

"No."

"You refused those on the fourteenth and the paragraph on the wall is the reason I am not asking again. I want you to understand that we have stopped asking. That is not a favour. That is because you were right about the wall and we were right about the wall, and two right answers make a paragraph."

The folder under her arm had two sheets in it and both of them had been written on and neither of them had been posted.

"The first one I wrote in the second month of this year and it is a form request for your office's standing in the ordinary way, which is a thing any registered institution gets," she said.

"The second one I wrote on the eighteenth, after the wall, and it is not a form request. It is a letter, and it is addressed to an office that has no written address, and I have been holding it for ten days because I could not work out how to post a letter to a stair."

"You could have walked it up."

"I could have walked it up," said the woman, "and I did not, because that would have been me sending the letter to myself and calling it a request from a division, and I wanted it to be a real request, and the only way it can be real is if it comes up your stair in a person's hand."

## What She Named Before He Could

"Then what is it," he said.

She put the folder on that table.

"A statement of what this office is," said the woman. "What it answers and what it does not, what it keeps and what it does not, in writing, on the letterhead of nothing, with no name on it, for us to put beside the paragraph."

"And the reason you can ask me for it."

"Because you have posted a refusal on our wall," she said. "An institution that publishes what it will not do has told the city it has a position, and a position can be asked for in the form it is published in, and if you say no to this then the paragraph on that wall is the whole of what anybody in this division knows about you, and it was written by a clerk who took it down at a rate of nine words a minute."

"That is the cost."

"That is the cost and it is correct and I have named it before you could, because I have been sitting with it since the eighteenth and you have had eight days."

She did not open the folder.

## The Price Before The Answer

"The price of the second one," she said. "There is a first one in a book four hundred yards off that I know about because somebody read it out to a queue, and you put it there because this office said it out loud, and this office does not do that twice without a reason."

"We are not going to do it twice."

"You are about to," said the woman, "and here is the number, and I have worked it out on the stair."

"About nine people a month come to that door, and about nine of them are now told by a wall that you do not answer in writing."

"If the second statement goes into that book today, then about nine people a month for the next six months are going to hear of it from somebody. About half of those are going to be told it is a form of application, and by the first of next year there is a queue of people at that stair with paper in their hands and a paragraph about it in their heads."

"That is a lot of people."

"That is about nine people a month for half a year and it is the price of the thing and it is the same price whether you say it or not, because we are going to print whatever you tell us, and if you tell us nothing we print the paragraph."

## Three Sentences

He wrote them himself, which is the part he thought about for two days afterwards.

It answers a question about a person only with that person in the room, and not before that person can be there.

It does not confirm, correct or supply the hours on its address in any copy, and it has one address and it is on a door and it is not written down anywhere.

It keeps no roll of who comes through that door and never will.

That is the whole of it and it is three sentences and it is the only thing in nine years of that office that has ever been written down in a hand that is not the office's and has left the room.

"Read that back," said Wenna Raut.

## The Arithmetic With The Door Open

She entered the date and the hour and the three sentences in the words he had used, because both question and answer go in in the words used, and the three sentences are an answer to a question asked on the twenty-eighth of this month.

Then she said the arithmetic out loud in that room with the door open and both carriers in the passage, because a figure that is only ever worked out in somebody's head is a figure that gets changed in somebody's head.

"About nine people a month for six months," she said. "About nine of the nine of them are strangers and none of them will come twice. Fifty-four people over half a year, and about twenty-seven of the fifty-four will be shown this by somebody who was shown it, and about nine of the twenty-seven will come up this stair and want to know whether it applies to them."

"Say the part that matters."

"The part that matters is that this office chose that number when it decided the second one was owed," said Wenna Raut, "and the choosing is done and the number does not care, and I have written it down in the same hour so that whoever reads that book later can see that somebody knew the figure when they wrote it and wrote it anyway."

## Two Of Them In Nine Years

He stood at the end of that table and worked out the thing he had done and there was not much of it.

There is one of them in there from the seventeenth of this month and there is one of them going in now, and they are the only two things that office has ever entered in nine years that were not a question and not an answer to a question, and one of them is a sentence about paper and the other is a description of the office written by the man who runs it.

Nine years of not writing anything down, and then two in a fortnight, both of them because other people had made them for him, and neither of them his idea.

"They are both true," he said.

"They are both true and they are both permanent," said Wenna Raut, "and in about four years somebody in this building is going to be asked why an office that had nothing at the foot of its page for nine years has two paragraphs in its book, and the answer is going to be that it was asked for twice, and the answer is going to be worse than the two paragraphs."

He read the three sentences over once more on the page, on the morning after, with the room otherwise empty.

It does not confirm a copy. It does not correct a copy. It keeps no roll of who comes through the door.

Three sentences about a thing that is good at what it does, and every one of them is about something the office will not do. A stranger reading them in four months is going to come away with the impression of an office that does very little and is proud of it, which is not the truth and is not a lie either.

The truth is that it does one thing an hour, in a room four flights up, for people no other office will see, and has been doing that since the first year, and is the only office in this city that cannot be got at by writing to it.

He left the page where it was and did not add anything to it, which is the hardest available act in that room and took him about nine seconds.

## What Is Left Over

The book went up at a quarter to ten on the two carriers' shoulders and came down at half past five in the ordinary way, and he stood on that landing afterwards and worked out what the next request would look like, because it was already a reasonable request and it had been reasonable since the second of this month.

Two refusals in a book and a paragraph on a wall four hundred yards off and a figure with four words under it, and about nine people a fortnight arriving once at the wrong end of a morning, and a clerk at a third table four flights away keeping nine sheets of her own paper under a table that she has never opened and is not going to open, and about nine strangers a fortnight bringing her slips she has not asked for.

And somewhere in that building a clerk had thrown six of his slips in the wrong bin in a fortnight and watched the cart go out, and had told nobody at all, and would not be thanked for it.

Four thousand and one hundred and thirty-seven days. Less four thousand and one hundred and thirty, the twenty-first of last month, is seven, and a division building asked for what an office is in writing and was given three sentences for ever, and the clerk named the price of them out loud in a room with the door open before they were written.

*Less four thousand and one hundred and nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty-eight, and the forty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-eight of them, and it does not move until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand seven hundred and seventeen days, which is fifty-seven months and seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

# The Intake Desk

Four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four was the fifth of this one and the twelfth of this one is day four thousand and two hundred and forty-one, and four thousand and two hundred and forty-one less four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## A Hall With People In It At Half Past One

The intake hall on the Ninth Terrace takes the public at a post with a person behind it, and it has shut at four in the afternoon every working day for twenty years, and at half past one on the twelfth of this month it had about nine people in it and the queue going back from the counter into the middle of the floor.

He came up the walled road in daylight for the second time in three years. The gate was open and two carts were coming down it.

She was behind the counter taking a name and a plate and writing four things into a book in about four minutes and reading them back, and she looked up at him before he was there, which she has never done for anybody.

"You are the man with the top line off the wall," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "You came in the evening three years ago and you got nothing and I gave you three facts and you went down that hill and did whatever it is you do."

"I came at about two o'clock on a working morning to ask you a question."

"Then sit on that stool and ask it while I do this. There are four people behind you and one has been here since about twelve."

## Nine Hundred People A Week And About Four Minutes Each

She did the four minutes while she answered it, which was the first thing about her that was different and it stayed different for the whole of the afternoon.

"A name and a plate," she said, to the man at the front. "Out loud, and I write what you say and not what I think you said, and if you change it in the middle I start the line again. Next."

"Four minutes each," she said. "Nine hundred a week. Twenty years. That is the whole of what I am and I have never once been asked to account for it by anybody including the man who wrote it down."

"You have never been asked about yourself."

"Never by any of the nine hundred," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and eleven of them have come back to ask me whether I wrote it down, and I said yes to all eleven, and not one of them has ever come back to ask me about me."

## The Wall Behind The Counter And The Rule About A Lamp

Above and behind that counter there is a plaster wall about nine feet high with the day's notices on it in chalk: which desk is taking what, what closes when, where a person goes who cannot read.

In November the light leaves that wall at about twenty past three.

"There is a rule in this hall about that wall," she said, "and the rule is that when the light has gone off it, nothing on it is read until morning, and that goes for me and it goes for the women who clean this floor at six."

"Because of what a lamp does."

"Because of what a lamp does," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "A carried lamp puts the shadow of whoever is holding it on the words, and you cannot read your own shadow, and then you have stood in a public post at ten past four in the dark reading a wall by a light you brought, and everybody in the room watched you do it."

"And that looks like something else."

"It has looked like something else here twice in twenty years and both times it was a man doing it on purpose." She put the chalk down. "So nobody reads it after twenty past three. They walk up and stand at it. That is the whole trade, in the end. About nine hundred people a week walk up to a counter and stand at a thing and somebody reads it to them."

## The Only Person Who Has Never Been Scored

"That is where you are," she said, nodding at the wall of tiles behind her. "Bottom of the fourth line, white, and about nine people a week look at the bottom of that wall because they are used to looking at the top of it."

"Twenty years, and I have read about nine hundred names a week into it, and I have never been on it, and about four hundred people a year come up that road wanting a number out of me so that they can be on something." "Do not do the thing you are about to do."

"What thing am I about to do."

"You are about to tell me what a door with three lines on it is worth to a person who has never been measured," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I have watched you get ready to say it three times in about nine minutes. Say it. But do not tell me it is a hardship."

## What A Door With Hours On It Is Worth

"It is the only address in this city a person can find without producing anything," he said.

"That is correct, and you have got there in one sentence, which took me about nine years." She went back to the book. "Go on. There is a second half and you have not said it, and it is the half that costs you."

"It cannot reach you."

"No," she said. "Say why, because you have not worked it out and I have."

"Every person who goes looking for that door is looking for somebody they can describe afterwards," said Lin Hao. "They are looking for a plate, or a floor, or a name somebody gave them."

"And you have got about nine ways to find that office and eight of them carry paper."

"Eight carry paper and the paper says who you are on your way to it, and the ninth is a stranger saying it out loud in a passage, and all nine of them end up at a person who can be described."

"That is it," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "That is the whole of it and you have had it for about two months and you did not want it. So write it down for yourself. A door with three lines on it finds anybody who has got something anybody can describe, and it will never in nine hundred years find me, and I have known that since the thirtieth of March and I went on being at this counter for a reason."

"And you do not want it fixed."

"I want it said out loud by somebody who means it," she said, "once, in this hall, and I do not want it fixed, and those are not the same wish and I have had about twenty years to get them confused and they are not confused."

## On The Floor Where The Light Is

At about half past three the light came off the wall and down onto the counter, and about four people stopped at the counter at once, and she said, "Back of the line, the wall is not readable and I am not going to pretend," and they went and stood at it.

Then it came off the counter.

"It is going," she said. "About nine minutes and then this floor goes, and there is a lamp on that counter and I am not going to touch it, and that is the rule, and we are both still standing in it."

She got up off the stool and came round the end of the counter, which she has not done for anybody in the time the last person on that road could remember.

"I have wanted to do that since the fifth of August," she said, "and I have about nine minutes and have not got four hours for a speech."

She put a hand into the front of his coat and took hold of the lapel and did not pull him.

"Say nothing," she said. "I have listened to four hundred people a week describe what they are about to do for two hours. You are not going to describe it."

He did not describe it.

Her hands went at the back of his collar and at his belt, and she got his coat open the way she got a book open, without hurry and without ceremony.

He got a hand round the back of her neck and found that she was warm, and she pushed back into the hand rather than away from it, which told him most of what the rest of the afternoon was going to be.

There was a lamp on the counter behind them both and neither of them turned round.

She took hold of him herself and told him, in the voice she used for a direction at a public post, exactly what she wanted done and in what order and in what words. He had been handed things in that voice four hundred times a week and had never once been on the receiving end of it.

He got her back against the cold stone of the front of that counter and she let him, and she was slick between the legs before that and stayed that way, wet enough that he heard it, and she told him under her breath that she had been hard since about half past one, which is when he came in.

About nine people were in that hall and not one of them looked round. She told him they would not.

She told him what to do with her and said it again in case the first time had been a direction about something else. She got her own hand onto her own cunt and made him watch her do it, two fingers, the way she does about nine hundred times a day with a pen and a book and no audience whatever, and then she told him to finish it and counted under her breath in seconds, which he had heard about before and had never believed.

And then she came, with her fingers still in his collar and her mouth open and her back on the cold stone, inside about a minute, and the noise of it went up into the roof of that hall and came down again and about nine people stood at a wall that had gone dark and did not turn round.

He got his own cock out of his trousers after that and did not hurry about it and she did not take her hands off the counter.

"All right," she said. "Then do it, and be quick. The light has gone off the floor, I am not touching that lamp, and I have still got four people at my wall."

He did it the way she told him and she held the edge of the counter over her shoulder with one hand and told him at every stage what it was doing, and she came a second time inside about half as long as the first.

She enjoyed it. That was the part he had not been ready for and it was not a surprise: she enjoyed it the way she does everything at that counter, which is completely, and without any part of it being about him.

Afterwards she went down onto the floor with her back against the front of the counter in the part of the hall where the light had been, and it was dark there, and she did not reach for the lamp on the counter behind them both.

"That is the rule," she said. "You see it. It goes and I do not read it and I do not touch the lamp and I do not ask you to hold anything."

"It is a good rule."

"It is an expensive one, and I paid for it in about four hundred bad evenings in a winter nine years ago, and if you ever use that phrase about it I will read your plate back to you in the wrong order."

## Afterward

She got up first and got the counter straight and squared the book and put the chalk where the chalk goes, in about nine seconds, the way a person does a thing four hundred times a day. He took the stool where she had been sitting.

"Two things," she said, "and then the four people at my wall can come and be dealt with, and both of them are about you and not about me."

"Ask."

"You have said about four hundred words to me about a door and a light," she said, "and you have not once asked me a question that was not about how a door works."

She had her arms folded and she was not unfriendly.

"I have been at this counter for twenty years and never wanted anybody to ask me about me. A man stood at the other end of it about four years ago at ten past six at night and asked me what I was going to do with three facts. I have been waiting about eleven years for a second one."

"So I am asking."

"You are asking, and I will answer it in a minute, and it is not going to be about the door."

"Second thing."

"Read me the three lines."

## The Three Lines Said Out Loud In A Hall

"I have never seen them," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "I have had a woman at a market end read them out loud to about nine people a fortnight since about the thirtieth of December, and I have read them myself about four times, and every one of those four times was somebody else's mouth."

She put both hands on the edge of the counter, which is what a person does when they are about to work.

"Read them to me. Out loud, here, in daylight, in this hall. Not writing them down and not on a piece of anything. I have never in my life been told a thing by the person who was writing it down at the time."

There were about nine people in that hall and there had been for about an hour.

So he said them.

The street. The door at the head of four flights off a third court. The hours, which are a quarter to ten to half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

He said them slowly enough for a person to hear once.

And not one of the nine had a pen out, and about nine people heard a man in a white plate say his own address out loud in a public hall.

"That is the first time anybody has said those three lines to me," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

She did not say thank you for it and he did not look for it.

"You may ask me your question now," she said, "and then the four at my wall can come and be dealt with, and the light is off that wall and will be off it until morning."

Four thousand and two hundred and forty-one days. Less four thousand and two hundred and thirty-four, the fifth of this one, is seven, and about nine people stood in a public hall and heard a man say his own three lines out loud once, and a woman at a market end and a woman at a stop where the service had stopped calling had both been saying them longer.

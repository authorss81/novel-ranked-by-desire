# The Interchange at Four

The interchange at four o'clock was rain and brakes and two hundred people under the awning, and Lin Hao stood in it with his coat soaked through and his hands in his pockets and no plan except the arithmetic.

He needed four hundred grains. Four hundred was four thousand eight hundred of the eight thousand he needed, real distance on a plate, the difference between a hearing this week and a hearing in March. It had to come from a deed somebody else stood in front of and wrote down, in a place where two hundred people could see him doing it.

The deed did not present itself. He had to make it, and the specific clumsy intention of a man shoving a door was not available to him here, because a staged rescue in front of two hundred people is a staged rescue, and the moment he had decided to do it on purpose he had already made it a performance.

Then the number nineteen tram came round the curve too fast, with rain on the rails and the brake screaming, and a woman with a basket stepped off the kerb looking the wrong way because nobody at the interchange was looking at the road.

He moved. He did not decide to move the way he had decided to stand in the box. His body went, three strides across wet stone, and he caught her by the coat and hauled her back onto the kerb as the car went past with an inch to spare and the whole awning gasped at once.

Silence. Then noise. Hands on his shoulders, somebody shouting for the Exchange clerk, the driver half out of the cab with her face white.

He stood in the interchange and watched his name go up on a slip that two hundred people watched being printed. The clerk wrote it, stamped it, held it up. Four hundred grains, attested, public, permanent. The first time in his life a machine had credited him for something he did rather than something the branch aggregate decided about him.

The delight was specific and immediate. He was visible. He had been seen on purpose and scored for it.

Then the horror came, in the same minute, in the same city, at a distance he could not reach.

Three people felt the spend at once, cold in the hand or the chest or an old injury, one to four seconds, the price of four hundred grains taken out of sixteen lines.

Beatrix Halm felt it in the cab of the nineteen at four o'clock, with thirty people on board and her hand on the brake. She is a signal operator on the Ferrier line, she sleeps in two rooms over the depot store on Yard Row, and she said through her teeth to the conductor, "Hold the doors, I'm going to finish this shift if it kills me."

Nadia Ferrous felt it in the arcade car at the terminus, where she works the lift doors for nine hundred passengers a day. She lives above a chandler's on the Vestry side with her sister, and she laughed and shook her hand out and said, "There it is again, that's four, I'm going to start charging it rent."

Halvard Rees felt it at the bench in the Cordwain yards, where he splices wire for the tram feed with a shoulder four years off a gantry. He keeps a room at 11 Cutler's Row, second floor back, and he put his head down on his arms and said, "Not again. Whatever you're doing up there, lad, stop spending me."

[Ledger. Sixteen lines. Three turned.]

He did not go back. He stood in the interchange and watched the slip dry in the clerk's hand and knew exactly what he had done. Four thousand four hundred and twelve grains. He had moved four hundred and he had spent three cold minutes and one of them was keeping track.

The Exchange counter opened at five. He had never in his life been in a room with a rank five in it, and he had just paid three people a cold minute each for the right to stand nearer to one.

# The Interchange at Four

The interchange at four o'clock was rain and brakes and two hundred people under the awning, and Lin Hao stood in it with his coat soaked through and no plan except the arithmetic.

Rank five was eight thousand grains. He stood at four thousand and twelve. So he needed three thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight, and the only instrument in the city that moved that number was a deed somebody else stood in front of and wrote down, in a place where two hundred people could see him doing it.

Four hundred grains was a tenth of the distance. Four hundred grains was the difference between a hearing this week and a hearing in March, and he had been forty minutes standing in the rain working out that arithmetic and every one of the forty minutes had been spent on getting the angle of his face right.

That was the new part. He had spent four years being looked through, and now he had discovered that being looked at was a thing he knew how to do, and the discovery had made him sick with wanting it.

So he stood where two streams of people crossed and he practised. He looked at the board across the Square, which was being repainted, and made himself go on looking at it. He looked at a woman reading a notice. He looked at the man selling bootlaces and stayed on him for eleven full seconds, long past the point where a man who was not looking would have moved on to the next thing.

Nobody looked back. He could stand in the open at a four-way crossing for a whole evening and be looked at by the entire city without once being returned, which was both the most useful thing he had learned in four years and the most useless, and he filed it in the place where he kept the things he intended to use later.

The deed did not present itself. He had to make it, and the specific clumsy intention of a man shoving a door was not available to him here, because a staged rescue in front of two hundred people is a staged rescue, and the moment he had decided to do it on purpose he had already made it a performance.

He spent the first hour working out how to do it honestly and could not find a way that was not dishonest.

Then the number nineteen tram came round the curve too fast, with rain on the rails and the brake screaming, and a woman with a basket stepped off the kerb looking the wrong way, because at four o'clock on a wet day two hundred people were looking at the board in the Reading Square three streets away and not one of them was looking at the road.

He moved. He did not decide to move the way he had decided to stand in the box. His body went, three strides across wet stone, and he caught her by the coat and hauled her back onto the kerb as the car went past with an inch to spare, and the awning gasped all at once.

There is a half second in that which he would keep for the rest of his life, and he understood it afterwards and not during: that for about half a second he was not doing it for the Exchange at all. He was doing it because the car was going to hit a woman who had looked the wrong way and he was the only thing in that street closer to her than the wheels were. Everything after that was machinery.

Silence, which was worse than noise. Then noise: hands on his shoulders from four directions at once, somebody shouting for the Exchange clerk, somebody else shouting that the driver had cut it fine, and the driver half out of the cab with her face white and both hands still on the brake.

The woman with the basket sat down on the kerb where he had put her and said thank you about nine times, and he said it was nothing, and it was not nothing, and he felt sick again.

The driver came down off the step to ask whether she was all right and she told him, at length, that she was perfectly fine and that somebody had taken hold of her coat at the shoulder. Then the driver stood there and looked round the awning for who, and there were about forty faces in there belonging to people who had all decided at exactly the same moment that they had not seen anything.

Then the clerk came out with the box on her hip and the stamp in her hand, because that is what a clerk does, and he understood it at the time and much more clearly afterwards.

Two hundred people watched it. That was the part he had spent an hour arranging for without knowing he was arranging it. She wrote his name in a book. She read it back. She said it aloud so the crowd could hear it, because the rule requires that, and then she said the deed and then the figure, and then she stamped it, and held the slip up at arm's length while the rain ran off the corner of it.

"Four hundred grains. Witnessed deed, rescue at the road, one instance, four hundred grains. Name, plate, time, place, two hundred persons present." She looked round the awning. "Somebody's to attest. Plate, please."

Eleven people put a plate up. He did not know any of them. Somebody else put his own plate up, and the clerk read it, and then looked at him.

"That'll be you, sir."

"Yes."

"Then I'll witness it myself." She wrote his number twice. "You'll get it Thursday. It's permanent, mind. Once it's in, it's in. It doesn't come off for being sorry."

Four hundred grains, attested, public, permanent. The first time in his life a machine had credited him for something he did rather than something the branch aggregate decided about him.

The delight was specific and immediate and it came up through him like heat off a stove. He was visible. He had been seen on purpose, and scored for it, and the figure would go up on the Exchange and the Exchange would have to believe it, and for about nine seconds he was the man at the centre of an interchange instead of a man at the edge of one.

He looked for the woman with the basket and could not find her, which was the correct outcome and the first thing in four years that had worked exactly as intended. She had gone into the shelter and was drinking something out of a paper cup with two other women who did not know what had nearly happened to her, and neither of them would ever know, and the entire of what he had just done was going to be carried for the rest of his life by a man nobody had asked to carry any of it.

He wanted another minute in the middle of it and he had already lost the thread of where the middle had been, because the thread had been the woman and not the deed, and the deed was over.

Then the horror came, in the same minute, in the same city, at a distance he could not reach.

Three people felt the spend at once. Cold in the hand or the chest or an old injury, one to four seconds, the price of four hundred grains taken out of sixteen lines.

Beatrix Halm felt it in the cab of the nineteen at four o'clock with thirty people aboard and her hand still on the brake. She was a signal operator on the Ferrier line and she slept in two rooms over the depot store on Yard Row, and she said through her teeth to the conductor, "Hold the doors, I'm going to finish this shift if it kills me."

Nadia Ferrous felt it in the arcade car at the terminus, where she worked the lift doors for nine hundred passengers a day. She lived above a chandler's on the Vestry side with her sister, and she laughed and shook her hand out and said, "There it is again, that's four, I'm going to start charging it rent."

Halvard Rees felt it at the bench in the Cordwain yards, where he spliced wire for the tram feed with a shoulder four years off a gantry. He kept a room at 11 Cutler's Row, second floor back, and he put his head down on his arms and said, "Not again. Whatever you're doing up there, lad, stop spending me."

Sixteen lines. Three of them turned. The count had not moved at all, which was the point, and one of the three was a woman at the far end of a tram route with a book under her coat who now had four hundred grains' worth of evidence about him written in a hand she had been keeping for four years.

He did not go back. He stood in the interchange and watched the slip dry in the clerk's hand and knew exactly what he had done.

Four thousand four hundred and twelve grains. He had moved four hundred, and he had paid three people a cold minute each for the right to stand nearer to one, and he had not asked them, and they could not have refused.

He stayed in the awning for forty minutes after the stamp went back in the box, through the whole last hour of the rain, with the clerk shutting her book and two hundred people going round the edge of him. He had never in his life been at the centre of anything and he did not know how to leave a room where he had been at the centre of something.

The Exchange counter opened at five. He had never in his life been in a room with a rank five in it, and he had just paid three strangers a piece of himself to walk toward it.

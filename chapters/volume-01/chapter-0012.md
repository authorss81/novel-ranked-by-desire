# The Ferrier Line

He bought a ticket the next morning, the morning the draw-down opened on the nineteenth with its ninety-one names. That was the whole of it, and it took him forty minutes to do.

A rank-four plate opens the staff gate at seven. He had used it for four years, pressing the disc to the socket by the yards and walking through while the reader nodded him past without looking, and he had never once stood in the public queue with the morning crowd. That morning he joined it, with wet sleeves and a pocket full of coppers, and nobody looked at him at all.

He had expected that to be the worst thing. It turned out to be the best. Standing unwatched among eighty strangers with rain on the windows was like being back inside his own coat after a week of being fitted for one.

He rode the Ferrier line end to end, from the yards to the Slough, and then he rode it back. The arcade car was third in the rake, with the long bench seats and the door that stuck at the market stop, and the roster on the bulkhead said the same driver four days a week in a hand he was starting to know from a notebook.

The car filled at the market. Women with baskets, men with tool rolls, two children sent ahead to hold seats, an old man who could not manage the step and was lifted by three hands at once without anybody discussing it. Doors held. Parcels passed. A girl gave up her seat and was thanked and waved it off.

He got on with a good day and got off heavy. That was the sentence he wrote in his head at the terminus, standing on the wet platform with his hands in his pockets, and he knew it was true before he had finished thinking it.

The account fills by proximity. Not by touch, not by asking, not by anything he could refuse or permit. By being the person in the tram nobody could afterwards place, while unrecorded good happened all round him in doorways. A tram full of strangers helping each other to the door was the densest thing in the city, denser than the Branch, denser than the clinic queue, and he had been commuting through it for four years without knowing.

He could not bank it and he could not stop earning it and both of those were the same sentence. Understanding it as a job rather than a curse ruined the only advantage he had, which was not knowing, and he stood on the platform and felt a specific unfamiliar hunger go through him.

He wanted to be in the room. He wanted to be counted, not looked through, and he did not know what to do with wanting it, because wanting it meant standing where the instruments could see him and standing where they could see him meant every quiet grain stopped being quiet.

Ines found him at the yards that evening, waiting for the second run, with his ticket stub soft in his pocket.

"You're riding," she said.

"Twice. End to end."

"And?"

"The nine hundred and eighty isn't people." He looked at the cars coming in, lights on in the rain. "It's passengers. The shortfall Rhee keeps filing about, nine hundred and eighty grains a head. It's not a number of staff. It's a number of rides where somebody does something nobody writes down."

She was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Show me."

So they rode together, into the dark, with the car filling and emptying round them, and he watched her watch it, an auditor watching unrecorded good happen at every stop with no form anywhere to catch it, and her face did something he had never seen on it.

"You feel it?" she asked, low.

"I feel full. Sick-full. Like I've eaten standing up."

"Good. Remember that. That's the job." She put her hand on the rail and did not touch him. "You can't do this forever without being seen, Lin."

He knew. He had known since the low wall by the bread stall. The hunger stayed with him all the way back to the Slough, unfamiliar and plain, and he wanted the box he had never wanted, and he wanted a witness, and he wanted his name written down for something he did on purpose.

That night he spread Ola's copied pages on his table and went through the four mis-spellings with a ruler. Three were slips. A tired hand, a bad pencil, Bright for Halm the way tired people write the name they just heard.

One was a date. Written small, under a cold entry, in the margin where she put the car numbers. A date that matched the rotation for the arcade car to the yards.

On the days Beatrix Halm drove it to the yards, somebody in this city had been writing down which cars she drove.

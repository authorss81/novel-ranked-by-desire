# The Ferrier Line

He bought a ticket the next morning, the morning the draw-down opened on the nineteenth with its ninety-one names. That was the whole of it, and it took him forty minutes to do.

The rank-four plate went through the staff gate at the yards at seven, and he had gone through it four years of mornings, pressing the disc to the socket and walking past a reader that nodded him through without looking up. This time he went down the ramp to the public side and joined the queue, in a wet coat, with four coppers in his fist.

The queue took twenty minutes. He had not known that. He had never once in his working life stood behind a stranger and waited to be allowed on a tram, and the waiting turned out to be the entire event: a woman in a mill apron reading a notice board with her mouth, two porters on break, a school run, and not one person in the whole of it who turned around and saw him.

The ticket clerk was an old woman who sold tickets through a brass grille and did not look up to count the money either.

"Ferrier, to the end?"

"To the end," he said, and meant the Slough, and she wrote it on a card and tore it off and gave it to him.

The car was third in the rake that morning, the long one with the bench seats and the door that stuck at the market. He got on at the yards and went to the far end and stood, because sitting would have made it a commute, and the car filled behind him.

It filled the way water fills a bath.

At the Cordwain gate a man got on with a crate of boots and a boy got on behind him and held the strap until the crate was down. Nobody thanked the boy.

At the Vestry a woman came up the ramp three steps behind two men carrying a mattress, and they turned round and carried it up as well, and put it in the rack, and went out, and the whole thing took eleven seconds and made no mark anywhere at all.

At the market the door stuck and a girl put her shoulder to it and a stranger took the other side and they got it shut together and neither of them said anything about it, and a woman gave up her seat and was waved off before it was finished being offered.

He stood at the far end with his ticket going soft in his fist and understood what he had come for.

It came for him the way weather comes, off the whole car at once. Somewhere behind his shoulder a woman said "go on then" to somebody who had not moved, and a man laughed, and a child was lifted onto a knee, and every one of those was a thing nobody would ever be asked about by anybody, and the machine took all of it out of the room and put it into the man standing at the end of it with his hands open.

He watched a man at the Vestry get up and give his seat to a woman with a bag and then stand holding the rail for four stops with his weight on one foot. He watched a conductor stamp a ticket twice because the woman could not read it. He watched a girl carry a child's shoes the length of the car and put them on the child and not once be thanked for it.

Not one of those had a form, a rate, or a name attached to it, and all of them were in him by the time the car came into the yards.

The account fills by proximity. Not by touch, not by asking, not by anything he could refuse or permit. By being the person in the tram nobody could afterwards place, while unrecorded good happened all round him in doorways. A tram full of strangers helping each other to the door was the densest thing in the city, denser than the Branch, denser than the clinic queue, and he had been commuting through it for four years without knowing.

He got off at the Slough, went to the end of the platform, came back on the next car, and got off again at the market heavy.

Heavy was the right word and there was no better one. He got down the steps and stood in the shelter with his back to the advertisement and waited for the floor to stop moving, sick-full, like a man who has eaten standing up for two hours, with sweat coming cold off the back of his neck and his hands shaking around a ticket stub he could no longer read. An old man under the shelter watched him with the frank clinical interest of somebody who has seen that before.

"Mister," the old man said. "You'll want to sit a bit."

"I will," Lin Hao said.

He sat. It took eleven minutes and it cost him nothing he could measure, which was the part that frightened him.

He could not bank it and could not switch it off either, and both of those were the same sentence. Understanding it as a job rather than a curse ruined the only advantage he had, which was not knowing, and he stood up off the bench and felt a specific unfamiliar hunger go through him that he had no name for at all.

He wanted to be in the room. He wanted to be counted, not looked through, and he did not know what to do with wanting it, because wanting it meant standing where the instruments could see him, and standing where they could see him meant every quiet grain stopped being quiet.

Ines found him at the yards that evening, waiting for the second run, with his ticket stub soft in his pocket and his face shut like a door.

"You're riding," she said.

"Twice. End to end."

"You bought a ticket."

"I bought a ticket."

"And?"

"The nine hundred and eighty isn't people." He looked at the cars coming in, lights on in the rain. "It's passengers. The shortfall Rhee keeps filing about, nine hundred and eighty grains a head. It's not a number of staff. It's a number of rides where somebody does something nobody writes down."

She was quiet a moment. The rake came in with the doors sighing and the wet off the roof.

"What figure did you finish on last week?"

"Four thousand and twelve."

"And a rank-four man at the Exchange is three thousand." She said it without any pleasure at all. "So you are over a thousand over the rate of a job you do every working day of your life, and you are two hours away from a rank that changes what a Thursday means, and you did that by riding a tram."

"I did that by buying a ticket."

"You did that by standing where the doors were." She wrote nothing down, because there was nothing in her satchel for this. "Say the next part out loud, Lin. I want to hear whether you can."

"It doesn't stop when I want it to stop."

"That isn't the next part. That is the complaint." She waited. "The next part is the one where you say what you would like."

"I would like a place where it counts."

"Say the other one."

"I would like somebody to write my name down for something I did on purpose."

She stood with that for about four seconds, and the rake came in and emptied around them with the doors sighing and the wet coming off the roof.

"Right," Ines said. "Now say that to somebody who can give you a box and see what happens."

"Show me," he said.

So they rode together, into the dark, with the car filling and emptying round them. He sat down this time because his legs had gone, and she sat down next to him with the file flat on her knees, and he watched her watch it.

She did not look at the people. That was the first thing he noticed and it unnerved him. An auditor watching unrecorded good happen at every stop should have been watching the people. Ines watched the doors, and the way they were taken, and the gap held or not held at each one, and her lips moved once at Cordwain and again at the market and she wrote nothing down.

"Thirty-one people got on here," she said. "Nine of them did something for another of the thirty-one and I could put a name to every one. And the Exchange has a machine for the number of doors a man holds in a week. Has it?"

"He stopped writing. It was his turn to look."

"It is not a machine that doesn't exist," she said. "That's the part you'd like. There is a machine, it is very good, and it has no answer for a door, because a door is not merit. You have discovered that the entire city is full of things that are not merit, and you are living in one of them."

She looked over at him. "And there is a word for what that arcade is, and it isn't *unrecorded*. Unrecorded means we missed it. That arcade isn't being missed. It isn't being looked at at all."

The train came on and she put her hand on the rail and did not touch him. Behind her the car doors hissed shut and thirty people took out small pieces of their lives in a public place and put them down again in public, and none of it was anywhere.

"You can't do this forever without being seen," she said.

He knew. He had known since the bread stall. The hunger stayed with him all the way back to the Slough, unfamiliar and plain, and he wanted the box he had never wanted, and he wanted a witness, and he wanted his name written down for something he did on purpose.

That night he spread Ola's copied pages on his table and went through the four mis-spellings with a ruler.

He had made the copies himself, in a public clerk's hand, at a rate of one line to six seconds, over two evenings. Three were slips. A tired hand, a bad pencil, Bright for Halm the way tired people write the name they have just heard called.

One was a date. Written small, under a cold entry, in the margin where she put the car numbers. A date that matched the rotation for the arcade car to the yards.

On the days Beatrix Halm drove it to the yards, somebody in this city had been writing down which cars she drove.

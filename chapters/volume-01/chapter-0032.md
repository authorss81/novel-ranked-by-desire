# The Vant's Price

The Vant's counting house at the top of Bellhouse is two floors above a chandler's and has the smell of every other counting house in Ostara, which is wax, paper, and men who have not had to shout since the Veil.

Marguerite Vale had a table by the window, two chairs, a plate of nothing, and a document on the table with the corners turned down, and she let him read the room for about eight seconds before she sat down.

"You have looked at the ceiling," she said. "Everything up there is original. Nobody steals the ceiling. Take the chair with your back to the wall, I am not going to be able to hurt you and I would rather we were not both looking at the door."

He took the chair with his back to the wall because he could not help it.

"Your name is on a wall in the largest type in the city," Marguerite Vale said. "Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. And you declared in a corridor in front of ninety-one people and you have been to a counter twice and a woman at the Vant has waited twenty-one days to ask you a question, and I am going to assume you have worked out by now that you are not a private matter any more, because you have spent four years being one."

"Who told you what I said in a corridor."

"Everybody. It was in a corridor." She put her hands flat on the table. "Do not make me pretend I bought it. I did not. I bought a man on the Ferrier line who counts what happens on the trams, because I wanted to know where your figure comes from, and it turns out it comes from everywhere, and that is the least interesting and most useful thing anybody has told me in four years."

"Then you already know."

"I know that you are the standing end of an arc that runs between the yards and the free clinics four times a day, and I know that you stopped walking it on the 29th and your number kept going up anyway." She tipped her head. "I want to know whether it is an arc or a person. That is the only question and everything else this morning is furniture."

"Both," Lin Hao said. "It's both and I don't know which one it is."

"Honest. Good. Sit still then."

She turned the document round and pushed it across the table, and it was four sheets and the top one had a Vant seal on it and nothing else, and the seal was the kind that means a thing has been prepared by an office rather than written by a man.

"Here is what I can do," Marguerite Vale said. "I can take your figure off the Board's arithmetic entirely. Not your band — your band is a public instrument and I would not touch it and you should not let me. Your figure. The number."

"I can hold it for you in a line that the Board does not read and the Exchange does not reconcile and the Classification Wing does not hold an entry for, because there is nothing in it for them to hold. A sealed line. You would keep it. You would be the only person alive who could open it, and you would open it when you chose, and no court in this city could take it off you, because a court cannot seize a thing it has never been told exists."

"Whose record."

"Mine." She let that sit for a second, on purpose. "Not the guild's. Mine, personally, for as long as I hold the seat I hold now. That is the whole of the offer and I am telling you the defect in it before you find it, because you will find it in a week and I would rather be the one who says it."

"The defect is that a line held by a person can be compelled out of the person."

"That is exactly the defect." She looked pleased with him and then said why. "I can protect an account. I cannot protect you. You are a man who reads a plate at a public counter every week and gets a figure, and in four years somebody will come with a paper that says produce the line, and if I hold it I will have to produce it, and I will produce it, because the Vant does not go to prison for a client."

She put one finger on the corner of the document.

"So. A thing that cannot be seized by a court can be seized by a question. Everything I have ever done has that in it. You want to know how to tell whether I am telling you the truth about a price, and the answer is that I have just told you the true price."

Lin Hao read the four sheets. He had been reading documents since June and he could get through a bad one in about ninety seconds, and he got through this one in about forty and then went back to the second sheet and read it again.

It was a clean offer. That was the first thing he noticed and it took him a while to trust it.

There was a handling charge. One grain in four, taken off the top, from everything that came into the line, for as long as the line stood. There was a term of five years with an exit at either end and an exit at either end was not an exit, because you cannot leave a line and come back to it and he was too tired that morning to work out what he had just signed and why she had given him a door that was not a door.

And there was a second thing on the third sheet, under a heading about other people's balances.

"No," he said.

"It has not been offered yet."

"You have written it down. Other citizens' balances. Merit moved into this line because they cannot afford a hearing and cannot pay a permission board." He put the sheet down flat. "You are not offering me a hiding place. You are offering me a road, and I am to be the road, and the price of the road is that it never stops."

Marguerite Vale sat back in her chair and put both hands behind her head.

"Now you have it," she said. "Yes. Four years I have run this guild's cleanest book and I have watched every rank in this city spend down to a number with nothing behind it, and every one of them has been given the same two offers by the same two offices. Be employed. Be classified. I am offering the third thing and the third thing is the only one where not a single person in this arrangement gets hurt, and I want you to sit with that for a minute, because it is the first proposition you have been offered that costs me nothing to make and costs nobody in the room anything at all."

She was enjoying herself. That was the thing about her that he could not get a hand around: she was not being kind, and she was not trying, and a woman with grey hair on the other side of a chandler's in Bellhouse was telling a man who mopped the second floor of a branch office that she had built a machine for keeping other people's losses out of the reach of the Correction Office, and she was enjoying herself, and it was the first time in four years somebody had offered him something that did not want a piece of him.

"The favour," he said.

"The favour is smaller and I will not get it if I do not get this, so it is not for sale." She sat forward. "When they replace me they will do it on a Thursday and I will be gone by the following month. When I have this, it survives me, because a line that is written into the record of a man on a wall on the Board is a line the next broker in this chair cannot pretend was not there. I am asking you to let me be the woman who found it. That is what I am buying and I am not dressing it up."

"And if you hold it and your chair turns over anyway."

"Then you will have lost a year and I will have lost a seat and somebody in the Vant will have spent five years learning that an account which cannot be banded is worth more than anything the guild has ever moved, and they will take it." She shrugged. "I have thought about it. I have thought about it for a long time, which is why I have waited twenty-one days and let a woman with a clipboard tell my board about you before I came."

She got up and went to a shelf and took down four books in a canvas carrier and put them on the table with a care that had nothing to do with their covers, which were worn and had no titles on the spine.

"These are not mine," she said.

Lin Hao looked at them. Four books, a carrier, and a city man's fear.

"Solveig Rask keeps the real books," Marguerite Vale said. "Not the guild's ledger, which is a beautiful lie. Rask's are the actual movements, the ones that reconcile, and she has never shown them to me and she is not going to, and they were on this table when I came in and I am going to tell you why."

She opened the third one on a page with a paper marker in it and turned it round.

It was a column of plate numbers. Thirty-one of them, in the order they had been entered, in a clerk's hand, and a good half of them had a date and the rest had a question mark instead of a date, and Lin Hao's own number was at the bottom of it, entered on the 3rd of this month.

The number above his was nine years old.

"That is not a client list," he said.

"No. It is a list of accounts that settled without an instrument and Rask has had it for four years and I found it in her office in the spring and copied it out by hand in a night shift and kept it." Marguerite Vale put her finger on the column. "Thirty-one. Nine years. Yours is the thirty-first. And this is the only person in the room who can tell you what it is worth, which is why she would rather it was not in the building."

"And the arithmetic."

"Rask does arithmetic the way you clean washrooms," Marguerite Vale said, "which is to say she does it properly and for nothing but her own satisfaction. There is a page under the list. One page. She has run what a figure of yours is worth if it is never touched, at the rate it has moved since the 1st of June, and she has got it down to a single number and dated it and initialled it, and she left it in the book on purpose."

Lin Hao read the number. Then he read it again, because it was not the number he had expected, and then he worked out why it was not the number he had expected, which was that it required knowing the rate since the 1st of June, and the rate since the 1st of June was known to two people in Ostara, and one of them was him.

He had never met Rask. He had been thinking about her for eleven seconds and he was going to be thinking about her for a long time.

"That is not a fair number," he said. "That is a guess with a rate in it."

"That is a guess with a rate in it," said Marguerite Vale, "and the rate is hers, and she has not published it, and she has not shown it to her chair, and she put it in a book she does not lock and left it where I would find it in the spring." She closed the book. "Now. You have read the offer and you have read a page that is not mine, and you have about an hour before two o'clock and there are two chairs and one of them has a man in it who is deciding whether to spend somebody."

Lin Hao sat in it and thought about four things at once, which was the wrong number and was all he had.

He thought about a corridor on the 3rd and four names struck off a board because a man said a true thing out loud.

He thought about three grains.

He thought about Garrick Neve's counter and a family of questions the index decides by looking at what was on his tile, and the third family he would not read to him.

He thought about nineteen people and a floor in a branch with a mop in his hand and no way to spend anybody.

"Write it down," he said.

Marguerite Vale pulled the corners back off the four sheets and turned over the third one, which had a great deal of blank space on the back, and put a pen beside it.

"Write what down," she said. "You want the terms. I am not giving you the terms twice. Say no today and the terms are dead, and there is a broker in Bellhouse who will tell you they were never printed, and it will be true, because I will have taken them back down."

"Write down what I am going to ask you on Thursday," Lin Hao said, "and write down that you offered it to me on the 12th, and the time, and the room, and my name. That is all I want in writing."

Marguerite Vale put the pen down on the table between them and did not touch it again.

"That," she said, "is the first sensible thing anybody has done with an offer from this house in eleven years, and I would rather have it than the deal, and I am going to go back to my chair and tell Rask that a man in a washroom asked me for the date and the room instead of the money, and she is going to be extremely interested in you, and I am sorry about that."

She stood up and did not shake his hand.

"Thursday is the publication day," she said. "You will be at the counter at nine. Ask your question. If the answer is that I cannot help you, I will say so in front of the queue and you will not be able to pretend later that I said something else."

# Provisionally Six

The Exchange's public counter has a brass rail in front of it, a clerk on a raised seat behind it, and a queue that forms before nine and has gone by half past, and on the morning of the 11th there were nine people in that queue, which was five more than there had been the week before, which Lin Hao understood before he had got to the front of it to be his own doing.

He read his plate out loud. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains, interval unsettled. The clerk read it back and entered it twice, once in the column and once in a separate book, and the separate book was the one with forty years of standing readings in it and every entry in it belonging to an office.

The clerk was called Garrick Neve and he had a Delta badge and a way of looking at a figure before he looked at the man holding it.

"You asked for that yourself," he said.

"I did."

"Then it is yours and not mine, which matters more than it sounds." He shut the reading book and did not shut the index. "I have been at this counter nine years and in eleven of those years I have entered about four standing readings a week, and every one of them was somebody's office telling me to enter it. You are the first person who has come to this counter and asked for a standing reading and then asked what it does."

"What does it do."

"That depends entirely," Garrick Neve said, "on whether you want it to do anything."

He took the index round and turned it so Lin Hao could see the page, and it was a page of rules in the Exchange's own small type, and the third heading said STANDING READING — PROVISIONAL BAND, and under it in a sub-line: *a declared unsettled reading, read publicly on a fixed day before a counter clerk and entered in the index, may be banded provisionally against the band table by the figure it holds on the day of banding.*

"You cannot band me," Lin Hao said. "You said so. A reading with no interval has no band and that is the whole problem with it."

"A reading has no interval and therefore no band. Correct. A figure has a band. Yours does not have one because a band is calculated from an interval and you have not got an interval, but a band is also a range, and a range can be struck against a figure by hand, and by hand is what a provisional band is." He shut the book. "It prints on the Board. It prints the band and it prints the standing of the account and it prints the day the band was struck. And the figure stays where it is, because the figure is yours and the Board does not take it."

Lin Hao stood at the rail for a moment with the marble of the Exchange doing what it always did to a person who stood in it too long.

"So the Board would print a six on my tile," he said.

"No."

"Then a five."

"A five, because the figure you hold is a figure in the five band and I have no authority to band you above the number in your hand." Garrick Neve tipped his head. "Which is not what you were listening for and I am not going to pretend you were."

The permissions board was eleven feet along the wall behind the rail, priced in waiting time, and a woman with grey hair was at it with a slip she had been carrying since the winter, and it did not occur to Lin Hao to look at it and it did occur to him that he had stopped looking at anything.

"Tell me the price," he said.

"Two things and they are not the same shape." Garrick Neve put one finger on the counter book. "One. A provisional band is a public instrument. It cannot be read out by its holder without the counter putting a question, because an instrument with nothing behind it has to be interrogated or it is a piece of paper with a number on it."

"Once a week, at the counter, in the open, with whatever people are in the queue, the counter asks you one question and you answer it and the answer goes into the index against your name in my hand. It is not a question I would enjoy asking you. It is whatever the index decides it is, and the index decides it by looking at what is on your plate and what is on the tile, and it has been doing it since before I came here."

"And two."

"Two. A declared unsettled reading is re-read quarterly. It always has been. The only thing a provisional band changes is that the date gets printed. On the tile, in the Board's own type, where your name is." He looked at Lin Hao. "You do not get to choose the date. You get to stop being surprised by it."

The marble was very cold and the queue behind him had four people in it and one of them was listening openly.

"And if I say no."

"Then the figure on your tile stays on your tile, where it has been since the 3rd, and the quarterly re-read stays quarterly, which means it happens and you have not known when for four months and you go on not knowing." Garrick Neve picked up his pen and looked at the time. "It is the better bargain for you. I want that on the counter record. I have offered a man the instrument and he has heard the two prices and I have said which of them is cheaper."

"Read me the questions."

"That is not in the rule."

"You said the index decides by looking at what is on my plate and what is on the tile. Read me what is on the tile." Lin Hao had not been told a thing in four years by a person who wanted something, and he wanted this one badly enough that his hands were doing the thing they did at a plate office counter. "I have four figures and a number on a wall the size of my forearm. Work out what a clerk would ask a man about those and say it to me now, while there are four people in the queue and I can go and stand outside."

Garrick Neve put the pen down and considered the queue, which was a thing Lin Hao had not expected him to consider.

"Two families," he said. "Either the figure moves and where the movement comes from, or it does not move and what is holding it still. The index has never had an account that behaved and it does not have a rule for one." He opened the index again and turned the page. "There is a third family and I am not going to read it to you, because you would spend a month working out which one it was, and you would stop coming to this counter, and then you would have no standing reading at all and no floor, and the re-read would find you anyway."

"So I do not know."

"You will be standing at this rail and you will be told." Garrick Neve turned the page back. "That is the whole instrument. It is not a favour and it is not a privilege and it is the first thing anybody has offered you in this city that does not want a document out of you."

Lin Hao thought about Ottoline Barret holding a door for eleven years and a man in the second column who had counted eleven thousand words a day and a boy carrying stock up from a yard at six in the morning.

"No," he said. "Yes. I want it."

Garrick Neve wrote something in the counter book for about four minutes, and read it back, and made him read it back, and then went and got a stamp off the shelf behind the rail that Lin Hao had not known existed and stamped the index page with it, and the sound of it in the marble hall was the single most frightening thing that had happened to him since the 3rd.

It went on the Board on the following publication. Not the band, which took a week of the Board's own arithmetic — the band line printed under the four figures he already had and under the fifth, the number, and read: PROVISIONAL, STRUCK FROM FIGURE, FLOOR 8,141.

The floor was the figure he held on the day it was struck. He read that twice and then read the next line, which was small and in the same upright print as the rest of it and which he had to stand in the rain to read because there was a man in front of him reading the number.

TOLERANCE THREE GRAINS. BELOW THE FLOOR LESS TOLERANCE THE BAND LAPSES AT THE NEXT PRESS.

He stood in the Reading Square with his hood down for about a minute and a half and worked the arithmetic that anybody standing in that square could have worked, which was that a man whose account was 8,141 could lose three grains and keep a band, and four and lose the band, and that a man who cleans washrooms does not have four grains of anything he can put a hand on.

Marguerite Vale arrived in the Fourth Branch corridor at ten past one.

She was not a large woman and she was wearing a coat that had been made in Bellhouse and she did not come up the stairs, because the Fourth Branch's ground floor corridor is where people come in and out of forty times a day and standing in it is not a thing anybody does twice.

"Lin Hao," she said. "I have been waiting twenty-one days for you to make the box a habit, because a man who stands in a box is a man with a schedule, and a schedule is the only thing in this city you can be moved by."

"Who are you."

"Marguerite Vale. I am a broker and I keep the books for the Vant, which is a guild, which is a word that means a group of people who are trusted with a thing for money." She put a card on the ledge by the door with a printed address on it and a time on it and nothing else. "Bellhouse. Tomorrow until two. After that I go back to the Vant and I will not come to a branch corridor again this year."

"And if I don't come."

"Then I will have lost about four hundred a day for twenty-one days," Marguerite Vale said, "which I can afford, and you will have lost the only door in this city that does not open with a form. I am going to tell you the truth about one thing before I go, because I do not want you agreeing to the wrong offer. Everything else you will find out in the room."

She buttoned her coat.

"What thing."

"The number on your tile," she said. "That is not your merit. Every instrument in this city will tell you it is, and one of us will tell you what it actually is, and the price of being told is the conversation. I have waited twenty-one days for the conversation."

She went out through the door the branch sends four thousand people out of every morning and the door closed behind her at the correct speed, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor with a card in his hand and three grains of margin and eleven minutes until the second stair.

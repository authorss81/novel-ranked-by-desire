# Nine Hundred and Eighty

The Fourth Branch kept its figures on the ground floor, behind a public counter with a brass rail and a clerk who had been there eight years and had no curiosity left in him, and Lin Hao went down there the next morning with his plate in his pocket and stood in the queue like any man with a question.

He wanted to know what rank five actually bought. He wanted to know whether it could be had before the audit opened, with the draw-down list already typed and the nine rooms waiting. That was the whole want, plain and felt, and it embarrassed him to feel it in a queue.

Tobias Renn found him there. Tobias was rank two, four years in the filing room, twenty-four and bored and precise, and he explained the mechanism with pleasure, the way a man explains a lock he owns.

"A reading isn't what you remember," Tobias said. "It's what gets filed. Four thousand and twelve, that's you last week. Rank five is eight thousand a week. The last four thousand seven hundred of yours aren't in any ledger you can see, because quiet acts score nothing."

They walked to the plate office while Tobias talked, past the filing room with its racks and the smell of carbon, and Tobias showed him the witness box. It was a marked square on the floor by the counter, with a rail and a stamp pad and a form pinned above it, and the rule above it in plain print.

A deed is scored when a second person stands in the marked place and attests it. The attestation is free. Any holder of a plate may attest, and the attested figure goes to the Exchange the same week.

Lin Hao understood the rule perfectly. Emrys Vaz had a rota line until the twenty-second and no witness box in his life, and nobody had ever asked him to attest anything, and it had never once occurred to anyone that he might.

He wanted the box. That was the whole emotion and it was shameful and it was correct, and he stood looking at the square of floor with his hands at his sides while the counter worked round him.

"You've never used it," Tobias said. "Four years. Why?"

"Using it would mean standing in it."

Tobias laughed, genuine and unkind at once. "Yes. That's the price. Standing where they can see you."

The clerk behind the counter was not unkind. She showed him the book, the stamp, the place for the witness to sign, and when he asked what a man did with nobody to witness anything, she said a man might attest his own hands, and watched him get into the box alone.

He stood in the marked square with nobody watching and felt the rail under his palm and the floor under his shoes, and it was the first deliberate act of visibility in four years, and nobody saw it.

Then Ines brought him the copied pages that evening, and they laid Ola's eleven entries against his own count on his table, and the mis-spellings explained themselves.

Bright for Halm where Ola had heard the name called down the platform. Bright for Halm where the car number matched. Two entries where the cold had come in the cab end of the car, with thirty people aboard, and the operator's hand on the brake. Those two were new. They belonged to people he had never touched, and they moved the count while they sat there watching it move.

[Ledger. Sixteen lines. Three turned.]

He stared at it. Sixteen lines. Three turned. Two more mouths feeding him from a tram he had ridden twice, and one of the three keeping track, and he had done nothing but buy a ticket and stand in a queue.

He had a box and he needed a witness. There were two kinds of witness in this city, the kind who could stand in a square and the kind who could not leave a lift, and one of them was a woman who could not leave a lift.

# Nine Hundred and Eighty

The Fourth Branch kept its figures on the ground floor, behind a public counter with a brass rail, and Lin Hao went down there the next morning with his plate in his pocket and stood in the queue like any man with a question.

He wanted to know what rank five actually bought. He wanted to know whether it could be had before the re-reads, with the draw-down list already typed and the nine rooms waiting. That was the whole want, plain and felt, and it embarrassed him to feel it in a queue.

The queue at the ground-floor counter was the only place in the building where nobody knew his face, because on every other floor he was the man with the mop and the trolley and the keys, and here he was a plate number in a line.

Ahead of him a woman asked about a transfer and was told to come back on the nineteenth. Behind him a young man asked about a transfer and was told to come back on the nineteenth as well. Nobody said it unkindly. It was not personal. It was a schedule, and the schedule had been printed in the winter, and everybody in the line had known it when they joined.

Tobias Renn found him there.

Tobias was rank two, four years in the filing room, bored and precise, and he explained the mechanism with pleasure, the way a man explains a lock he owns.

"A reading isn't what you remember," Tobias said. "It's what gets filed. Four thousand and twelve, that's you last week. Rank five is eight thousand a week. Three thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight of yours aren't in any ledger you can see, because quiet acts score nothing."

"You've done that before."

"I've done it eleven times. People come down here with the same face you've got." Tobias pushed his sleeves up. "The face is: I want to know what I'm not being told. Nobody is not telling you anything. The machine is telling you the entire truth and the truth is that you have been doing four years of work in a corridor where there is nobody to see it."

They walked to the plate office while Tobias talked, past the filing room with its racks and the smell of carbon and the sound of forty people not talking. He showed Lin Hao the witness box. It was a marked square on the floor by the counter, with a rail on three sides and a stamp pad on a post and a printed notice screwed to the wall above it.

The notice, in plain print, said: a deed is scored when a second person stands in the marked place and attests it. The attestation is free. Any holder of a plate may attest. The attested figure goes to the Exchange the same week.

"A second person," Lin Hao said.

"A second person. Not a superior. Not anybody. A person with a plate." Tobias spread his hands. "Any man in this building could put you in a box tomorrow and you'd have four hundred grains on your plate by Thursday, and half of them would do it out of spite."

Before Lin Hao could answer it happened in front of them.

A porter came up the stairs from the sub-levels with a bad leg and a heavy bag of brass fittings, and a second porter stood up off the wall and said, "I'll carry it to the yard gate." And the first one said, "No, I've got it." And the second said, "You've got a leg like a hinge and it's raining, put it down." And the first one put it down.

So the second porter walked into the marked square, and the clerk on the post took his plate, and read it, and wrote a slip, and stamped it, and read the slip out loud in the flat voice of a woman who had done this forty thousand times.

"Co-operation in the movement of goods, yard stair, one instance, forty-one grains. Plate, name, time, place. Attested by plate two-nine-one-one. Is that you?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you?"

"Yes, ma'am."

Forty-one grains. The first porter took the bag again, and said thank you, and went down the stairs, and neither porter looked at Lin Hao and neither of them knew that he was standing two feet away with his hands at his sides.

Lin Hao understood the rule perfectly. Emrys Vaz had a rota line until the twenty-second and no witness box in his life, and no one in this building had ever thought to ask him to attest anything, and it had never once occurred to anybody that he might.

He wanted the box. That was the whole emotion and it was shameful and it was correct, and he stood looking at the square of floor with the porters' wet prints drying on it while the counter worked round him.

"You've never used it," Tobias said. "Four years. Why?"

"Using it would mean standing in it."

Tobias laughed, genuine and unkind at once. "Yes. That's the price. Standing where they can see you. It's the only price in the building. Everybody pays it. Half of us just do it so often we stop noticing."

The clerk behind the counter was not unkind. She had stood at that counter for twenty-nine years and she showed him the book, the stamp, the place for the witness to sign, and she did not once tell him it was a waste of anybody's time.

"And if there's nobody to see?" he said. "If I do a thing and there's nobody to stand there."

"Then it isn't a deed yet," she said. "It's just something you did." She put the stamp down flat. "A man may attest his own hands. It counts the same as anybody else. It is not worth as much, because it is worth exactly the same to the machine and the machine has no opinion about you."

"And if the person I need will not stand there?"

"Then you do not have the deed. That is all that happens." She began writing in the book again. "A man may decline to attest. He may not decline after he has attested. And nobody may decline on another man's behalf."

"So it comes down to asking."

"It comes down to asking," she said, "which is the only part of this machine that still requires a person, and it has been the only part for forty years, and every clerk in this building has quietly got very good at not asking anybody anything."

"That is not an accident."

"No," said the clerk. "It is on a rota somewhere. I have never seen the rota."

He stood in the marked square with the counter working and nobody in it and felt the rail under his palm and the floor under his shoes, and it was the first deliberate act of visibility in four years, and it was not visible to anybody.

"That'll be you in there, sir," the clerk said, not unkindly. "Mind the rail. It sticks."

Then Ines brought him the copied pages that evening, and they laid Ola's eleven entries against his own count on his table, and the mis-spellings explained themselves.

Bright for Halm where Ola had heard the name called down the platform. Bright for Halm where the car number matched. Two entries where the cold had come in the cab end of the car, with thirty people aboard, and the operator's hand on the brake. Those two were new. They belonged to people he had never touched, and they moved the count while they sat there watching it move.

[Ledger. Sixteen lines. Three turned.]

He stared at it.

Sixteen lines. Three turned. Two more mouths feeding him off a tram he had ridden twice, and one of the three keeping a record of its own, and he had done nothing but buy a ticket and stand in a queue.

Ines read the two new entries twice, in order, with the lamp pulled close to them, and then she put her finger on the second one and left it there.

"This one is at four o'clock. And it is at the yards end." She looked up. "Lin, the arcade car does not turn at the yards. It goes through and comes back empty. Which number turns at the yards?"

"Nineteen."

"And who has the nineteen at four o'clock on a wet evening?"

He did not answer, and she watched him not answer for the length of a whole breath, and then she wrote it down anyway — in her own book, with the time and the place and the fact that he had not said it, which was at least a fact.

"You are going to ask that woman to put her name on a line," she said.

"Yes."

"Before you do, hear what you are asking. She feels your supply and has never been told what it is, and she has kept four years of it in a book under a heading her own division gave her, because it was the only heading anybody offered her." Ines closed the book. "When you ask her for that book she is going to hand it over because she is glad that somebody finally wants it. That is the moment you become responsible for a stranger's four years. It does not go back."

"I know."

"You do not know it. Nobody knows it until they have done it." She stood and put her coat on. "Write to me when you have asked her. Tell me exactly what you asked for and exactly what you were given. If you get it in writing it is worth something. If you get it in the rain at eleven at night it is worth nothing, and it will be the first thing anybody in this building asks you about."

He had a box and he needed a witness. There were two kinds of witness in this city, the kind who could stand in a square and the kind who could not leave a lift, and one of them was a woman who could not leave a lift.

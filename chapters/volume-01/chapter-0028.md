# Public Record

The plate office took readings in ones and twos behind a half-height screen and called the results out loud so that the person waiting would know before they got to the door, which was a kindness in a city that mostly had very few.

At 11.19 the clerk at the screen said, "Thirtieth. Ottoline Barret. Unable to settle."

Nobody in the corridor said anything. Lin Hao had been standing in it for three hours and he had watched three people walk out of that door and go and stand against a wall and he understood now that the corridor was a machine too and that it had learned how to be quiet around the thing that happened at that screen.

"Thirty-eighth."

He had the plate in his hand and he had walked forty-one minutes to get here and his coat was soaked through at the shoulders and there were about seventy people still in the corridor and a clerk behind a screen and Runa Sato at the rail with her notepad shut.

"Lin Hao. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains."

Every head in the corridor turned, and he understood the exact mechanics of it afterward: eighty-one was in the band table at rank five and the man whose figure it was was a man who mopped the second floor, and the two facts did not fit each other and the city could not help looking.

"Interval," the clerk said.

She said it the way she had said it in June, flatly, as a column that is empty rather than a person who is unwell.

"Unsettled."

She wrote it on the board by the door in the tick column in red, and Lin Hao looked at his own name on a piece of paper in a building where four thousand people had watched him mop a landing, and it was over. Four years. There was not going to be a morning after this one where somebody in a corridor failed to recognise him.

He should have gone back down the line. That was the thing to do. Thirty-eighth done, sixty more people behind him, the machine wants a body in the corridor so that the next body comes in.

He walked out of the door instead, and past the clerk, and to the rail where the second-floor corridor was, and stopped in front of the board with the ninety-one names on it and the red ticks going up it.

Nobody stopped him, because he was a man holding a plate and forty people behind him had their own reading to get to.

"My name's Lin Hao," he said. "Thirty-eighth. I've got the tile on the Board, in case anybody didn't see it."

He was aware of doing this in the way a man is aware of stepping off a kerb.

"I want to say a thing and then I'll get out of the line and everybody can get their reading done."

Two women at the front of the queue had turned round. Vole was in the doorway of the plate office with a folder in her hand and had stopped exactly where she was. Rhee was at the top of the stair with his coat on his arm and had stopped exactly where he was too.

"Four names this morning didn't settle," Lin Hao said. "Ottoline Barret. Emrys Vaz. Tobias Renn. And one at eleven o'clock I don't have the name of yet. They go on a slip at four o'clock and a man from the Wing collects it at half past four, and that is how it works, and I am not going to stand here and tell you it does not, because it does."

Nobody said anything at all.

"There is one unsettled reading in this building," he said, "and it is mine. Not one of those four. Mine. My figure came back on the first of June with a figure on it and nothing under it for an interval, and I have been sitting on it since June and letting the machine look for it in ninety-one other people, and it found four this morning and it will keep finding them, and every one it finds is somebody who has spent their life being the person nobody looked at, and it is finding them because of me."

The corridor made a sound that was not a word. Somebody at the back said something to somebody else and it went along the line the way anything does.

"I'm not standing here to explain it," he said, "because I can't, not properly, and I've got a woman upstairs who could explain it and I'm not going to use her. What I'm standing here to do is take four names off a piece of paper."

He turned round to the board.

"Lin Hao, second floor, eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, unsettled. That's the reading. That's the one. It will be on this board every Thursday, in this column, in this hand, for as long as it takes, and anybody on this floor who wants to come and look at the plate can look at the plate, and nobody else gets to look at anybody else's. If anybody comes and asks you why you did not settle this morning, you say there was nothing to settle, because it is sitting on this board with a name on it and the name is mine."

He stopped there because he had said what he had come to say and the next part was the part that made it irreversible, and he made himself say it anyway.

"I'm also going to stand in the box at the Exchange," he said, "on Thursdays, at nine, at the public counter, and read my plate out loud, and stand there while somebody else reads it back. Every week. Until it settles or until I don't do it any more, and the day I stop, that's the day you can all say I lied about this."

Nobody said anything.

Emrys Vaz had not gone anywhere. He was standing against the wall by the filing-room door where Lin Hao had found him two hours earlier, with his hands at his sides in exactly the same position, and when Lin Hao came down the corridor he turned his head and followed him all the way past and did not say one word.

He had understood. That was the thing about it.

He had carried stock up from a yard at six in the morning for four years and nobody above him had ever asked him a question, and in the space of four minutes in a corridor he had worked out that the man who mops the second floor had felt him, and had felt him *at six in the morning*, and had come down forty-one minutes in the rain to say so in front of everybody.

He had understood all of it and he had no words for any of it and he would have to find them later, and Lin Hao had made sure he would have to, forever, in public, in a corridor full of people.

Ottoline Barret came past at ten past two with her coat over her arm, on her way to a hearing she had not been able to afford in April and could still not afford, and she stopped.

"You are the one who does the fourth-floor stair," she said, which was what they all said.

"Yes."

"I have held that door since before you worked here," she said. "Eleven years, and before you it was a different man held it." She did not thank him and she did not ask anything else. She went down the stairs. That was all she ever said to him, and she said it once, and she was right to.

He got his plate out and held it up so that people at the back could see the number, which was the single most ridiculous thing he had ever done in his life, and then he said, "That's me. Back of the line," and stepped aside, and about half the corridor did not move at all and about half of it moved a very small amount to the left.

Runa Sato had not written any of it down. She stood at the rail with the notepad closed in her hand and the strap over her thumb, watching him rather than writing, and when he came back down the corridor she put it in her coat pocket and said one thing to him in a voice pitched under the noise of ninety-one people.

"That is going in," she said. "Word for word. Signed and dated, filed on the 6th, read by a man I have never been in the room with. Whatever else you meant by it, that is what it has become."

"I know."

"You do not know, because you have never had anything said about you in a document before in your life, and you have not yet found out what it does to the next four years." She took her hand off the pocket. "I will not pretend that was a kindness."

That was the entirety of it.

He had prepared a speech for nine days and it had been one sentence, and there was no applause, because there is no applause for a man telling a corridor that he has been letting it take the blame for something since June, and what there was instead was about forty people looking at the red ticks going up the board and doing arithmetic of their own.

He went and stood behind the man at the front of the line.

It was eleven twenty-six and the man at the front had worked in the second column for twenty-two years. He turned round, looked at the board, and then back at him.

"You are the one that does the second floor."

"Yes."

"Eleven thousand words a day," the man said. "I have counted them."

Lin Hao did not have an answer for that and never found one, and it was not gratitude, and it was not an apology either, and it went into the pile with the rest of it and stayed there for the rest of his life.

Vole came out of the plate office with the folder under her arm and went up the stairs past him without speaking.

Rhee came down the stairs and stopped on the landing above and looked at him for about six seconds, and then went back up without speaking, and his door at the top of the corridor shut, and Lin Hao understood that had been the end of that conversation with an extraordinary clarity and no information at all.

Cassius Rhee had offered him a plate reissued in the branch name, four mornings a week for two hours in a room with a door that closed, on the record, credited to the Branch and not to him, and had said out loud in that room that the day he wanted out was the day somebody in an office asked a question, and had pointed at the door with a man in it.

There was no man in a door now. There was a man on a board in a corridor in front of ninety-one people.

"I heard you," Rhee said through the door, not opening it. "I'll hold the audit a fortnight. From today. That is the last fortnight I will put my name to in writing and after that I will do it the way it is normally done."

"Understood."

"No, you have not." The door opened about two inches. "Listen to me, because you have cost yourself the only thing you owned in this building and I want it noted that you did it in a corridor with no floor under it and not one piece of paper in front of you. You cannot go back. I want that on the record, because I am the man who wrote it down." He shut the door again. "A fortnight. Go and get your washroom done."

The readings went on until half past one. Lin Hao stood in the line for two hours and heard eleven more unsettle and then settle as people remembered things they had forgotten, and at twenty past two the counter clerk took the tick column off the board by the door and pinned up a new sheet.

His name was on it. Thirty-eighth down, in red, under a printed heading that had not been there at seven in the morning:

UNSETTLED AT READING — CARRIED AND DECLARED. SUBJECT PRESENT. QUARTERLY RE-READ.

And underneath it, written by hand, in the clerk's own neat upright letters, four more lines had been struck through. Ottoline Barret. Emrys Vaz. Tobias Renn. And a fifth name Lin Hao did not know, who had been the one at eleven o'clock and had turned out to have spent nine minutes in the corridor deciding whether to go home.

The slips printed at four.

There were none.

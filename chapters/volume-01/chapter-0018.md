# Signal

The days after the yards he laid Beatrix's four years on his table next to his own count and found they did not agree.

They disagreed first because they counted different things. She counted effects: cold minutes, cars, hours, clusters, the word headache written in the margin wherever the night had been too loud to hear yourself think. He held addresses. Faces, streets, hours, sixteen lines and three turned, people he had stood near on trams and in queues and at the foot of a counter where a clerk said come back after the audit.

It took him four hours to make the two agree enough to compare. He wrote his own lines onto a sheet in the same order she had written hers, and then he wrote hers onto a second sheet in his order, and then he laid the second sheet over the first on the table with the ruler across it and the lamp pulled close, and where the two agreed he put a pencil mark, and where they did not he put nothing at all and moved on.

When the two were laid on top of each other, on his table, with a ruler across them and the lamp pulled close, the map made a shape neither of them could say out loud at first.

The unrecorded good in the Slough and the Vestry was not spread across the city. It was in one arc between the yards and the free clinics, and it moved along it four times a day, morning and evening, with the shifts.

He understood it sitting on his floor with pages all round him and the long accumulating history of four years of pencil going under his hands. There was a geography to the account. It was not a private accident. It fed where people were kind in doorways, and doorways on that arc were the busiest in Ostara, and the whole of the Slough's kindness went along that one line between the yards and the clinic four times a day because that was where the doors were.

The geography meant the only man in Ostara fed by the whole of the Slough was a man who commuted the Ferrier line, which he did because his rent was eleven minutes' walk from the stop. His poverty was a power stat. His invisibility had an address.

He went out and tested it the same night, because a shape on a table is only a shape until somebody walks it.

He walked it. Ferrier Street to the yards on foot in the rain, and back to the Vestry clinic by the arc, and home by whichever of the two long ways his legs would give him, and every hundred yards he counted the doors and who was holding them and how many of those people had a plate at all.

Eleven doors in two hundred yards. Four of the eleven by strangers. Three of them by people he knew. One held by a man who was doing it for money.

He sat down on the clinic steps in the rain and did the multiplication out on his cuff, and the multiplication was the whole engine of his life in a line of sums. The account could be filled by proximity and could not be filled by asking, and the only man in Ostara standing where the doors were was a man who could not afford to live anywhere else.

Nineteen lines. Three of them turned. All nineteen located, and located to a shape.

He felt elation and then shame, together, because the hoard read as the correct choice for the first time and it was still wrong. He was deliberately, openly increasing what he took, and he could not defend it, and he could not stop, because the alternative was that the Slough stopped being kind in doorways, which was worse.

So he started taking the tram the long way on purpose, every day, in both directions, riding end to end to feed the lines, and Ines found out within four days because an auditor notices a pattern in a man's commuting and cannot help herself.

"You've inverted it," she said, standing in his rooms with her coat on and her hand flat on the map. "You're farming it."

"I'm keeping it moving."

"You're hoarding it bigger." She moved his hand off the paper with two fingers, the way she moved everybody's hands off documents. "Lin. Nineteen lines. Three turned. All located. You have a map that cannot be intercepted because the only people who could intercept it are asleep at four in the morning, and you are using it to get hungrier, and you have started doing the long ride the way other men start doing something else at that point in their lives."

"I know what it looks like."

"Do you. Because I've read this file before, not this one — I've read every complaint any attendant on this floor has ever filed, and there are four men in nine thousand who have noticed what they're sitting in, and three of them drink." She took her coat off and dropped it on his table, which she had never once done, which meant she was going to stay. "The fourth is a man called Emrys Vaz who is seventeen and carries stock up from the yard at six and has never once been asked a question by anybody above him."

"He's not on the list."

"He's on the rota. He's not on the list because the list is a list." She sat down on the floor by the stove. "Tell me the thing you haven't told me."

He looked at the map for a while.

"Beatrix Halm keeps a second column," he said. "She calls it turned. It is a cold she took and never gave back, and there are three of them in four years, and one of the three is a name I have seen on a rota in my own building, and she will not write the address down, because in four years not one person has asked her for one."

Ines went very quiet.

"Then she is the most dangerous person in this city and she does not know it," she said. "And you know it, and you have read her book, and you know where she is at eleven o'clock at night."

"I know."

"Do you? Because a woman driving the nineteen to the yards is a description that takes four seconds to give, and after that she is a file, and after the file she is somebody who has sat in a room like this one and answered for it."

"Somebody in this building is going to be asked where your figure comes from," Ines said. "Not in those words. In the form of a question, from a man with a seal, in a room like this one. And on the day it happens you will want to hand him a name, because a name is the only thing you have ever been able to hand anybody."

He knew she was right. What he wanted, concretely, with a date on it, was the ninety-one off the list before the day happened, and every long ride bought him distance from that and cost somebody a cold minute, and the arithmetic of it was the arithmetic of a man deciding which of his two appetites to feed.

His want was plain and felt and it did not excuse him.

Beatrix brought him the week's plate figures herself, coming off shift with grease on her cuffs and rain still in her hair, and she put the slip on top of his own papers without sitting down.

"Two hundred," she said. "One instance, held door, reading room, an ordinary afternoon. Not four hundred. I checked the attestation and I checked the deed and there's no second one."

"No," he said.

"Because it wasn't four hundred." She sat on the floor by the stove, which was the only way anybody ever sat in his rooms. "You bought a door being held in the rain for eleven minutes. The man holding it isn't a porter with a bag of fittings. He's a man who wanted to smoke and stood in the rain instead, and the Exchange priced it at what it was worth, which is two hundred, and it is right."

"I was short."

"You're always short." She looked up at him. "The ninety-one go down in five days, Lin. That's the date that matters now. Not your plate. Not my book. Ninety-one people go into a room and nine of them do not come out, and every one of those nine is somebody whose job you could describe better than they could."

"Who is it for you?"

"That's a stupid question."

"You've been up two nights."

"I've been up two nights," she agreed, "because a man came to my cab and a man came to my book and now I know what a triangle looks like from the inside. So here is what I am going to say and then I am going to go home." She got up off the floor. "The ninth one is me. Not next month. In September, when somebody in a warm room asks my division for the cold minutes by district, and they have got a year of my handwriting on a map."

Lin Hao tried to say something and she put her hand flat on his chest, not hard, the way she kept a file shut.

"Don't. You'd say something kind, and kind is exactly what I do not want at this hour, because I have spent four years being a condition." She got her coat off the table and put it on. "Give me something I can use instead. Keep the three where they are and don't spend anybody new until the ninety-one are read. That is all. That is the whole favour and I am not asking twice."

He walked her to the depot gate and watched her go in, with her book under her arm and her deaf side to the traffic, leaning in to hear the shunter, joking about it even then, and he stood in the rain and felt the map in his coat like a weight.

The account had a shape. The shape had an address. The address was him.

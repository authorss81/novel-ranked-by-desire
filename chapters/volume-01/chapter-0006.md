# Supply Room Three

Supply room three was on the third floor between the filing room and the stair, and it had been shut since six o'clock that morning, and by nine that night Lin Hao had established that four people had told him about it and not one of them had offered to open it.

He had a list in his head and it was the same list it had been since the Wednesday.

The washrooms on six floors were cleaned on a stock issue, and the stock came out of room three, and room three was locked and the key was in a drawer in the small office next door, and that drawer belonged to a man called Colm Deveraux, who was the supply supervisor for the third floor and the second and the fourth, and who had been in the Fourth Branch stores for twenty-two years and had a plate with a reader's thumbprint worn into the face of it.

At ten past ten Lin Hao went and knocked on the small office.

"Closed," said Colm Deveraux, without stopping what he was doing.

"I know it's closed. That's what I've come about."

"Then you've come about the correct thing and got the correct answer, and I'd have said the same to anybody." He had the kettle going and a newspaper folded to the page the tram alterations were on, and a lamp with a green shade, and the manner of a man with nowhere he would rather be. He had grey at the side of his head and the shoulders of a man who had once carried shelving and had not put it down.

"I need the stock out," Lin Hao said.

"You need the stock out on Monday."

"I need it out tomorrow. There's a boy on the yard rota at six who carries it up."

"Aye, and if the room's shut, there's nothing to carry, and the boy goes down to the sub-levels, and I'll be honest with you, Lin, that's a matter for the rota." Colm poured the water. "That's not me being hard. That's me being accurate. If you want the room open before the nineteenth then you want a counter-signature on the opening, and the counter-signature is the deputy's, and you are not going to get it and neither am I."

"Give me a form."

"There's no form for this. There's no form in this building that opens a store in a draw-down week, because the form would be the discrepancy." He said it as though he were being kind. "Come back on Monday and it'll be open Monday."

So that was the door, and he had put his hand flat on it, and he had been told the shape of it, and he stood there on the other side of the desk with nothing to do with his hands.

And he noticed Colm Deveraux wince.

It was nothing. He had lifted the kettle off the ring and put it down on a table that was lower than the ring, and somewhere in the middle of that his right shoulder came up and went round and he made a small sound through his teeth and immediately stopped making it and sat down, carefully, the way a man sits down when he has decided that the sitting is the whole of the movement.

Twenty-two years of shelving. Lin Hao knew what it looked like. He had cleaned up after a man with that shoulder twice a week for four years.

He thought: I could take that.

He thought it clearly, in words, standing there with his hands at his sides. Not *I could give him that.* Not *I could help him.* I could take that, the way you take a weight off a shelf, and if I did it he would have one minute in which his shoulder did not hurt, and then he would open the door, and the door was worth nine days and a boy of seventeen and ninety-one names.

It was the first time in four years that he had ever thought of the thing he was carrying as a tool.

He put his hand on the desk.

Colm Deveraux came round the side of the desk to show him out — not unkindly, the way you move a chair for a guest — and put his hand on Lin Hao's chest above the collar bone, and said, "Not tonight, Lin."

And Lin Hao pushed.

It was not a push on a man. It was a push of the kind a man makes against a door that has swollen in the frame, with the flat of the hand, all of it in the shoulder and none of it in the arm, and he did it *toward* Colm Deveraux with the specific clumsy intention of shoving a door, and he felt the moment it went.

What came out of him was not cold.

That was the thing he understood later, at three in the morning, over and over, and it was the thing that put him on a stair for nine days afterwards. What came out of him was *warm*, and it had weight and it had direction, and it went *into* the man, and it went down through his hand and his wrist and into his chest and settled somewhere under the shoulder with a sound in Colm Deveraux's breathing like a man stepping off a stair.

Colm Deveraux took his hand off Lin Hao's chest and stepped back.

He did not feel cold. He did not feel anything at all. He stood there in his stocking feet with his hand half up, and his face went through three things in about two seconds — surprise, then the particular blankness of a man checking a room for the source of a noise, and then something that Lin Hao recognised instantly because he had watched it arrive on a tram eight days before and had not been able to stop it: *a man who cannot account for a thing that has just happened to him.*

"Did you—" Colm Deveraux said. He stopped. He said, "That's — hold on."

He went out of the office and down the stairs at a speed Lin Hao would not have believed, and Lin Hao followed him because there was nothing else to do, and forty minutes later a man who had been perfectly content in a green-shaded lamp was standing at the Exchange counter on the ground floor with his plate in the socket and his mouth open.

"How much was it Tuesday?" he said.

"Two, one-six-eight," said the clerk.

"It's three, one-six-nine."

The Exchange counter did not care. The Exchange counter was a slot in a counter with a man behind it who processed forty plates an hour and could not have cared less about the difference between two numbers and three.

Colm Deveraux pressed it again, to be sure, and got the same figure, and the interval underneath the figure said PER WEEK — HOLDING, which meant that the rise had not come from anything he had done, because the rate behind it had not moved at all. In three days the total had gone up by a thousand one grains and the rate behind it was exactly what it had been on Tuesday, and the only way that could happen is if the thousand one grains had arrived in one lump from somewhere that had nothing to do with him at all.

"That's a thousand and one in three days," Colm Deveraux said, to nobody. "I did not — I have not *done* anything." He looked up, and he was looking past Lin Hao, at the whole of the ground floor. "I haven't done anything."

He turned round and found Lin Hao standing there.

For about two seconds neither of them said anything at all.

Then Colm Deveraux went past him, went up the back stair, went into the small office, unlocked the drawer, and put the key in Lin Hao's hand.

"You'll bring it straight back," he said. His voice had gone strange and thin. "You'll bring it straight back and you'll not say I've given you it and you'll not come up here again."

"I won't."

"Ten minutes ago," Colm Deveraux said, "you put a hand on my chest and I got a thousand grains I did not earn, and I have got no idea what you are." He went back into his office and shut the door and did not open it again that shift.

The Vestry kitchens sent supper to the branch on the nights the branch was working late, which during a draw-down was most of them, and the tray came up on the goods lift at eleven at night and was collected at the third-floor landing by whoever was on the landing.

Lin Hao had the landing.

The woman who put the trays out was small and quick in a Vestry kitchen apron with a woollen hat pulled down over one ear, and she was talking the entire time, in the flat good-humoured way of a person who has decided that the person in front of her is an audience.

"—and I said to her, Nadia, I said, if you're cold in the lift, be cold in the lift, but you'll not bring it up to the table — and she said, Ola, it's my *hands* —"

The other one came up behind her with the last tray. Young, laughing, wiping her hands on her skirt. Nadia.

"Hands aren't the table," Nadia Ferrous said. "That's the whole thing about hands, they're not socially—"

"I'm going to put you both down for the early shift again."

Lin Hao took the two trays off the landing rail and put his hands where they had to go, and the woman with the hat gave him the spare ones, and as she turned round to go she took hold of his forearm to steady herself on the step, briefly, in the way of somebody who has been on their feet since four o'clock.

It came out of him and it went into her hand and it was over before he had it out of his fingers.

She let go and shook the hand out and laughed at it.

"Oh, there it is," she said. "There it is, that's the third one this week." She shook it again, cheerfully, the way you shake off a wasp. "Never mind. It's the weather in here. It's nothing. Do you want a roll, I've got two rolls, you can't have two rolls, I'm going to eat both of them—" She laughed again and went down the stairs.

Lin Hao stood on the third-floor landing of the Fourth Branch holding four trays and a key.

He had broken the rule in a green-shaded office at ten past ten, on a man who had not wanted anything. And he had done it on purpose, for a door.

And then he had done it again, forty minutes later, for no reason at all except that a woman on a step had been tired and he could not stand it, and he had not chosen to, and it had gone out of him the way water goes out of a tilted cup.

Down at the goods lift, Nadia Ferrous said, "You all right? You look like a man who's lost a plate."

"I'm all right."

"You'll want to stop drinking the branch kettle," Nadia said, "there's a man on the second floor did his shoulder in on that and he'd have a word."

Emrys Vaz came up off the yard at six with the barrow and a blanket over his shoulders and no idea that there had been any question about it at all, and they moved the stock between a quarter past six and half past eight on the Friday morning, and Lin Hao carried down to the yard and back eleven times, and at four o'clock that afternoon the supply supervisor's office was shut with the door locked and nobody in it, and the key was back in the drawer, and the stock was on the floors.

He was grey at the temple and he had not slept and he was going to be grey at the temple for the rest of the week.

[Ledger. Fourteen lines. Two turned.]

Two.

The first one was a lift attendant on the far side of the city who had told him she had had three in a week. The second one was a woman in a hat who had made a joke about it.

He stood in the middle of the third-floor landing at half past eight in the morning with the notation behind his eyes and the whole of the building moving around him for the first time in seven days, and he understood two things.

The first was that he had no rule left. He had made a rule on the Thursday morning and he had broken it that same night for a door, and he had broken it again forty minutes later for nothing, and there was no third version of the rule, because the only rule that had ever worked was the one that said *do not touch anybody*, and he had known since Wednesday that he could not hold that for a week, and he had been right.

The second was worse.

He had not spent anything on Colm Deveraux. He had put the wrong thing in, and the man had a thousand and one grains he did not earn and could not explain and did not want, and had gone up a back stair with his face gone, and had locked an office door.

He had done that to a stranger to get a key.

Then he went down to clear the trays, because the trays came back on the same cart, and the landing rail was still damp where they had stood, and the cloth the woman with the hat had been using had fallen behind the rail and been caught on the bracket.

Under the cloth, weighed down with a saucer, there were four sheets of paper with pencil on them.

He picked them up.

The top sheet had a column down the left side with dates in it and a column down the right side that only ever said the same one word.

4/6 — COLD

11/6 — COLD

19/6 — COLD — hand, bad

28/6 — COLD

9/7 — COLD

3/7 — COLD

Four months of it. Four sheets, written small and neat and slightly bored, the handwriting of somebody who had run out of anything else to write about. There were places in a second column — *the lift, again*, *arcade*, *market floor*, *clinic run* — and there was nothing at the bottom of the fourth sheet but the word *again*, and the word *again* had been underlined twice by somebody who had been irritated with herself.

# A Woman With a File

The deputy's office had a door that closed. That was the first thing Lin Hao noticed, standing in it at nineteen with his new band still strange in his coat, because no room he cleaned had one.

There was a window, a good one, with the rain coming down it and the Reading Square going about its business eleven feet below and no curtain. There was a desk with two lamps, one of them on, and a coat on the back of it, and a shelf of files with the spines squared off. There was a chair opposite the desk, and Cassius Rhee was not behind it.

"I'd have you stand a moment, if you don't mind," Rhee said. "It's the only way I can tell whether you're real. They put a man in a chair in this building to see how he sits in it. You stand like an inspector."

He sat down anyway when the second chair turned out to have a broken caster and catching it on a wheelbelt. Rhee laughed at that, once, genuinely, and it made the room smaller.

Cassius Rhee was rank five and losing ground to men half his age with better files, and his district was nine hundred and eighty grains short per head per week, and he had been losing that ground for two years. He was not a monster and did not behave like one. There were two plates on his desk and neither of them was a category of person.

"I've known for eleven days," he said. "Eleven, exactly, and I'll give you the date because you can check it and I'd rather you did. I've read every plate you've submitted. I'm not going to file anything, because a filed supply is a supply somebody else owns, and this branch has been asset-stripped twice in my tenure already."

He laid the arithmetic out plainly, correctly, and without hurry, the way a man lays out a thing he has laid out in front of other people and been right about.

"A portable merit source inside a branch is a stabilisation. I don't care whose it is and I'm not going to ask, and I would think less of you if you told me. A stabilised district is nine thousand people who do not get moved. It is the largest single thing in this building. It is worth more than every requisition I have ever signed."

He wanted Lin Hao employed. Not punished. Employed, with a door that closed and a roster and a quiet arrangement, and nine thousand people upright because of it.

"And you would want to be able to see me," Lin Hao said. "A closed door can be opened from your side."

"On any morning I choose."

"And you would open it."

"I would open it," Rhee said, "and I would tell you first, and you would think of me as a man who keeps his word, which is worse than a man who doesn't."

"What would it be, day to day? If I said yes. What is it on an ordinary morning?"

"Your rota. You keep the rota. Your plate is reissued in the branch name and you carry the branch figure, which is a real figure and it would be yours to hold." Rhee turned one of the lamps off, for no reason except that he was thinking.

"You would not clean anything. You would not be looked through. In exchange, four times a week for two hours, you would sit in this room and do whatever it is you have learned to do, on the record, in front of me, and whatever you did in this room would be entered against the Fourth Branch's account and not against yours."

"And in eight months the Branch holds a supply and the supply has an owner."

"In eight months the Branch holds a supply and the supply has an address," Rhee said, "which is the only kind any of us ever get, and I would be the address, and the day you want out is the day somebody in an office asks me a question and I can point at a door with a man in it."

He said it without any leverage in it at all. That was the part Lin Hao could not get past. Every other person who had ever wanted something from him in four years had wanted it by being clever or being sorry, and this man had just told him the entire mechanism out loud, including the bit where the man stops being useful.

Lin Hao felt the vertigo of being recruited. He had gone four years without one person looking through him, and the worst thing that had ever happened to him was a man in a good coat being kind to him, and he sat in the chair with the broken caster and understood that kindness with a seal behind it was still a hand on his shoulder.

"You're offering to pay me," he said.

"I'm offering to stop you."

"I'm not yours to stop."

"You're a man in my building on a draw-down list with a reading I cannot settle and a supply I cannot account for." Rhee sat back. "You are extremely not mine to stop. That is the entire problem, and it is why I am sitting here at nineteen instead of sending a form."

He did not take the job and did not refuse it outright, because a man who says no to the only person in the building offering him anything gets nothing at all. He asked for one thing. The audit. Nine days for ninety-one people, deferred, re-typed, moved off the list.

Rhee did not ask why. That was the frightening part. He wrote a number on a pad, and looked at the calendar on the wall, and struck the old date through, and wrote a new one a fortnight out, and initialed it, and pushed the pad across the desk.

"Fourteen days," he said. "You asked for nine and I've given you fourteen, because nine would have you back in here on the ninth asking me for more. Do not waste it and do not come and thank me. You will want to thank me and it will cost me something to be thanked."

It cost Lin Hao everything he was going to use the audit for, because the audit was the only deadline the world had ever given him, and buying the Branch a fortnight bought him a longer wait with a band he had paid cold for.

He wanted the audit off ninety-one people. That was the true want, concrete and felt, more than rank six, more than the count of turned lines under a number he had never told anybody. He wanted Ditta Halse off a bench and Emrys Vaz off a deleted rota and Bernt Halse out of a room with no window, and he had just traded his only urgency for it.

He stood up. Rhee did not stand with him, which Lin Hao understood afterwards had been deliberate.

"Ninety-one people," Rhee said, to the papers. "Go and save them. I'd rather you were at six and still dangerous than not here at all."

He went out into the corridor with his hands shaking and the door closing behind him, and the corridor was the same corridor, four floors of wet stone and a smell of carbon and somebody's supper on a landing, and he stood against the wall by the stair head and got his breath back before anybody saw him.

It took him a while. He could not make the shaking stop by breathing, which was a thing he had been able to do since he was nineteen, and so he did it the other way and let it run and held his own wrist over it until it was finished, the way you hold a horse's head.

He had just bought ninety-one people a fortnight of not knowing. He had done it with one sentence, and that sentence had cost him the only deadline the world had ever handed him, and he would have said it again in the same room with the same broken caster under the chair.

Then he noticed that he was still standing outside a door that closed, on a floor he cleaned, in a building that had just offered to make him its property, and he went down the stairs.

Runa Sato was standing at the bottom of them, under the lamp.

She was broad-shouldered, with her hair cut short and a file under her arm and a coat that hung like a uniform, and she looked at him before she looked at Rhee's door. The file had a wax seal on it that Lin Hao had only ever seen on one other thing in his life, on his brother's cross-reference card.

"Lin Hao," she said. "Field. I have an order and I have a file, and both of them have your name on them."

She did not move, and she did not take the file out from under her arm, and she said, in a voice with no particular heat in it at all:

"Before you say anything. The order is not Rhee's, and it is not Tobias Renn's, and it was written before the twentieth. I can tell you that much without opening it, because I carried it in."

His hands went cold and then stopped shaking, which was worse.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" she said. "It isn't that somebody chose you. It's that whoever chose you wrote it down before you had done anything worth choosing you for. That means the choice was made about a shape, and you fit the shape, and there will be other people who fit it, and they are all in a folder somewhere with your name on it, and I have read four of them this month."

She looked up the stairs at the door that had closed, and then back at him.

"You were in that room about twenty minutes," she said. "What did he offer you?"

Someone with a seal had chosen him, and had done it eleven days before a junior clerk with a gift for arithmetic noticed anything at all.

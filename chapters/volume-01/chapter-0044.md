# An Empty Account

The 29th came up grey and still, with no wind off the yards and the paint on the Board drying too fast in the morning heat, and Lin Hao stood in the Exchange box at nine for the second Thursday running with his plate in his hand and sixty people outside the rail.

Garrick Neve read the plate number and the figure and the band line the way he always did, and then he read the weekly question, which was the same one the index had asked the week before.

“What instrument do you hold for the figure on that plate.”

“I hold the requisition order of the 19th and my own declaration of the 23rd,” Lin Hao said. “No other instrument.”

Neve wrote it down and read it back, and Lin Hao read it back after him, and the queue listened, and Neve stamped the sheet and gave him the counterfoil. The whole of it took four minutes. That was the cheaper price, and Lin Hao paid it standing up, with his forearm in the sling and his coat buttoned wrong.

Then he went with the rest of the queue to the Reading Office window, because the 29th was the printed date on his band line, and a provisional band was not a band at all unless it survived the quarterly re-read.

The clerk who had entered his reading on the 3rd was behind the window. She had his slip in front of her and the query sheet beside it, and she looked at him over the top of them with the expression of a woman who had been thinking about this for two days.

“Plate,” she said.

He put it through the window.

She put it on the machine and the machine read it and printed a slip, and she read the slip and wrote the figure down in the book, and then she put the band table beside the book and ran her finger down it.

“Eight thousand one hundred and forty,” she said. “One grain down on the published standing.”

Sixty people went quiet in a different way from the box. In the box quiet meant listening. Here it meant arithmetic.

“Floor eight thousand one hundred and forty-one,” she said, writing it down. “Tolerance three grains. Reading eight thousand one hundred and forty. One grain inside tolerance. Band holds.”

She stamped the slip and pushed it through the window.

He stood there with the slip in his hand and understood that he had survived by one grain. One grain down, two grains of margin left. The figure had drifted the way Runa said it would, wherever he stood, and the drift had cost him one grain in two days, and if it cost him three more before the Review he would go back to being a five without anybody doing anything to him.

“Is that the instrument,” he said.

“That is the re-read.” She squared the query sheet. “The query is separate and it is still open. A query wants an instrument and a re-read is not one. You hold a band through today and you hold no instrument behind it, and the Review will ask for one in the sixth week, and I cannot invent you one.”

He thanked her and put the slip in his pocket with the two counterfoils.

Outside, the Board had been repainted and his tile was still white. Provisional six. Struck on a requisition of property. Cordwain. 19th. Floor 8,141. The paint was fresh and the black six stood out against it, and he stood in the crowd and looked at it for a while without feeling anything at all.

Beatrix Halm found him there. She had come down from the yards in her uniform with her brake handle marks on her hands, and she stood beside him and looked at the tile without speaking.

“You held,” she said at last.

“By one.”

“I know. Tessa told me. Four yesterday and two this morning and every one of them between six and ten.” She turned to face him. “I came down to tell you one thing and then I am going back up. My three ticks stand. The ninth is me, in September. That favour is discharged and the ticks are not spent, and I am telling you that because you are counting and I want you to count correctly.”

“What am I counting.”

“People you can spend without killing.” She said it bluntly, because bluntness was the only thing she had ever offered him. “The answer is none. Forty-one lines, five turned, and the five are the only ones with names you know well enough to find in a crowd, and every one of the five has kept something. Another cold into any of them is a reportable episode. You know the threshold. Three in a season.”

“I know.”

“Then that is your account.” She put her hand on his good arm for a second. “Empty. Not poor. Empty in the one respect that matters, which is that there is nobody left in it you can reach.”

She went back to the yards.

He stood a while longer in front of the Board with the re-read slip in his pocket, watching the painters finish the frame. The painters worked from the top down, whites first, and his tile sat in the third row from the top where a man going from four to five would have sat without fuss. Two thousand people went past and none of them stopped, because a tile that held by one grain was not a story.

Ola Bright found him there with a tray over her arm and a pencil behind her ear.

“You held,” she said. “Nadia says you held by one and Tessa says the same and Beatrix will have told you third. I am telling you fourth so you cannot pretend it did not happen.”

“Fourth is good.”

She put the tray down and showed him the notebook, open to that morning’s page. Three entries, all in her hand, all in the same words in different order. The Exchange box at nine. The Reading Office window after. The Board repainted with his tile still white.

“You made a clerk,” he said.

“I am a clerk.” She closed the book. “Twenty-two entries since the 18th and every one of them says the same thing. Week beginning the 18th. I am writing it because you told me the third one is different, and the third one is what gets somebody a file, and somebody ought to write down when it happens.”

He walked back to the Fourth Branch the long way, forty-one minutes in heat that made the sling stick to his arm, and went up to the second floor and stood in front of the corridor board.

Five names. Same gap at the top. Emrys Vaz second one down with four hundred and forty-one against it.

Ines Duarte was standing at the end of the corridor with a folder under her arm. She had been waiting for him, and she did not pretend she had not.

“You held,” she said.

“By one grain.”

“I know. The clerk told me.” She came up beside him. “I carried the name up the corridor on the 23rd because a drawer is not a place to keep a person. It is in your file and in his. That part is done and I am not asking you for anything about it.”

“What are you asking.”

“Nothing.” She looked at the board. “My paper is discharged. The month ran out on the 20th and I said the rota name out loud on the 20th and you have not given me the word. Whether you give it is a decision and not a fact, and I am not going to make it for you by asking twice.”

He looked at her. She had her coat on though the day was warm, and she looked tired in the way she had looked since June, and he felt the tenderness come up in him the way it always did when she stated a thing plainly and left him room around it.

“I have not decided,” he said.

“Then do not decide today.” She put her hand flat on the board for a second, beside Emrys Vaz’s name but not on it. “The second of May is still on my table and you still have not said a word about it. I have the date and the figure and I have not asked. That is also a decision and I have made mine.”

She went down the stair.

Nadia was waiting at the bottom of the stair in her uniform with her hands on the brass rail of the corridor gate, though the gate was not hers. She had come off shift early and she looked tired and bright at once, the way she always looked when she had decided something.

“You held,” she said. “By one. I felt you being careful all morning from the arcade. It is the most frightening thing in the lift.”

“I am being careful.”

“Do not be careful at me.” She hit the rail lightly. “I have had three since June and the third one is the line where the Bureau stops believing coincidence. I worked that out from Ola’s diary like everybody else. I am telling you I know, and I am telling you not to spend me, because I am the one thing in this quarter that goes up.”

“I will not spend you.”

“Good.” She nodded sharply and went back up toward the Vestry, because a lift attendant with a route had somewhere to be even off shift.

He cleaned the basins slowly, though they were already clean, because the work steadied his hands. The night porter stopped to ask about the Review date and he told him the sixth week, and the porter nodded and said his sister had sat one. The soap was low and he cut a fresh bar with the knife from his room, the way he had done every week for four years. The counterfoils curled at the edges from the damp.

He went into the washroom and laid the re-read slip beside the soap with the two counterfoils, three slips of paper in a row, each one stamped, each one true, none of them an instrument.

For Beatrix Halm, who had kept a column of cold minutes for four years and had come down off a tram that morning to tell him the sum of it, the re-read meant one grain of drift on a plate that could only go down.

He wanted, concretely, to keep the next grain from drifting before Thursday came round again, and he had no means to do it except standing still, and standing still was what made the figure move.

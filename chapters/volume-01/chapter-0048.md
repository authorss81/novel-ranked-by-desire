# One Grain of Itself

The open-question slot was a square of floor in front of the panel with no rail round it, and Lin Hao stepped into it with his plate in his hands and sixty people behind him, and the chair put the question without looking down.

“What is the figure on that plate made of.”

It was the question he had been asked in different words every Thursday for a month, and it was the question he had answered truly every time without an instrument behind the answer. Where does the movement come from. What instrument do you hold. What does a sealed file ask. Every one of them had been a version of this one, and every answer had been true and unusable.

He stood in the square and looked at the nine chairs and then at the public seats, and he saw Ines in the back row with her folder closed on her knees, and Tessa beside her with her sleeves down, and Beatrix by the door with her hands marked from the brake, and Nadia in the aisle because she would not sit, and Runa in the doorway with her hands behind her back, and Marguerite in the third row with her house round her.

Nineteen people in four years and then twenty-two more in one day on the 15th, and then the weeks of Thursdays and queues and corridors, forty-one lines gone to ninety-one because he had stood where strangers held doors for each other and the faces had come back whether he wanted them or not. Five turned. Nadia, Ola, Emrys, Teodor, Tessa. Every one of them had said yes to something else first. Every one of them would be marked by another cold.

He had zero to spend. The best remaining lead was already turned. Spending on a turned person was how you got them read.

There was one line he had never spent. His own.

He put the plate down on the clerk’s desk and put his good hand flat on it, the way he had put his hands flat on Runa’s table on the 23rd, and he did the thing he had never done in the book, not by accident on a tram, not on purpose in a cold store, not across a room with the door open.

He spent the account on himself, once.

The cold went out of his chest and into his own hand under his own palm, and it was not relief the way it had always been relief in other people. It was a minute of himself held still, the way a body holds still when it is being read correctly, and in that minute he knew the answer to the question the way a man knows his own name.

“Nineteen people in four years and fifty more since,” he said. His voice sounded strange to him, level, without the shake. “Doors held, trays carried, washrooms cleaned, a woman pulled out of a tram’s path in front of two hundred witnesses. Four hundred of it came off a woman with a basket. Two hundred off a door held in the rain. Three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine out of the Branch aggregate behind the first witnessed one. The rest is strangers holding doors for each other four times a day with the shifts, wherever I stand. I did none of it. I stood in it. That is what the figure is made of.”

The room was silent.

The machine on the table printed a slip. The clerk read it and her face changed, and she read it again, and she put it in front of the chair without a word. The panel leaned in. One of the nine put a hand flat on the table to steady himself, though nothing had moved.

The chair read it out.

“Published standing eight thousand one hundred and thirty-seven, with a witnessed answer in the room scored at four hundred grains under the open-question rule, attested by nine chairs and sixty witnesses. Standing twenty thousand and above. Band six.”

Sixty people drew breath at once, and the usher had to tell the public seats to be still. That was the sound a room makes when arithmetic moves. A man in the second row stood up without knowing he had stood, and sat down again when the usher touched his arm.

The panel sat back in its nine chairs. One of the nine removed his spectacles and cleaned them, though they were clean. Marguerite Vale closed her eyes. Ines put both hands flat on her folder. Runa did not move. Nadia made a sound in the aisle that was not a word and put both hands over her mouth. Beatrix shut her eyes. Tessa looked down at her own forearms, sleeves down over them, and then back up.

The clerk wrote the finding down with a hand that was not steady, and stamped it, and pushed it across the desk.

[Ledger. Ninety-one lines. Eight turned. One line reads himself.]

He felt it go out across the room the way he had always felt it go out across a tram or a lift or a ward, only this time it was larger than anything he had ever done, because nothing he had ever done was this size. Three colds in three bodies at once, all of them people who had never kept anything before. Nell Ardery on the landing of number 14, who had given him water in a chipped mug. Garrick Neve at the counter, who had read him fairly four Thursdays running. The checker at the yard gate, who had nodded him through every morning for a month.

Three feeders turning at once. Eight turned where there had been five.

He had broken the only promise that ever bound the count, given in a cab at eleven at night before anything started, and the count showed it. Ninety-one lines where there had been forty-one, because the weeks of boxes and queues and corridors had brought fifty new faces. Eight turned where the five had been, because nothing he ever did was this size.

And a line in his own ledger that said himself, which had never been there before and should never be.

He stood in the square with his hand still on the plate and understood what he had paid. The option of being invisible was gone forever. A man who had spent the account on himself in front of nine chairs and sixty witnesses could never again be a man who cleaned washrooms without being looked at. The account had his name in it now, in his own hand, the way his declaration had his name in it.

He had promised Beatrix in a cab at the end of her shift, rain on the roof, to spend on people and not on himself, with her price agreed out loud before anything started. That promise was broken in the square and it could not be mended, because a line that said himself could not be taken out.

The chair struck the band.

“Rank six,” the chair said. “Held. Not provisional. Seated on a witnessed deed of this day.”

The corridor outside filled while the clerk wrote. Word went down the stair faster than any slip. Twenty-second of twenty-two, provisional, one grain, two grains, four grains down, held. The branch would hear it by noon. The Vestry would hear it by night.

The chair ordered the finding copied and the copies signed, and the clerk worked with both hands while the room watched, stamping each copy separately with great care. The machine cooled with a click between each copy. The register sheet was marked at the thirty-first line, his line, dated the 3rd, with the finding number beside it in the margin in red ink, dated that day.

He stepped out of the square on legs that barely held him, and That night the Vestry was full of talk. Ola read the finding aloud from the landing rail to twenty people, slowly, stumbling on the long words, and Nadia corrected her without malice. The arcade listened the way it listened to announcements. A man asked what a witnessed answer meant and Ola said it meant he told the truth in a room with nine chairs while sixty people listened.

Tessa examined the three new colds after hours in the cold store, one by one, with the lamp on its cord. Nell Ardery with her eleven weeks of figures, steady, angry at no one. Garrick Neve with nine years at the rail, precise about the minute it happened. The checker with his docket, confused, asking whether he should report it. She charted all three and put the charts on the rack, fifteenth and sixteenth and seventeenth boards, and sat a while with her sleeves up, looking at her own arms.

Runa caught his elbow once, steadying, professional, letting go the instant he was balanced. Ines was standing in the back row with her face in her hands. Tessa had her hand on Ines’s shoulder. Those were the people the machinery had passed through to reach him.

He came out into the corridor with the finding in his hand and the branch porter took it to copy before he could protest. Copies went down the stair in three hands. By noon the corridor board would carry the date of the Review at the top in place of the gap, and the five names would be gone from it by instrument and not by declaration.

Tessa caught his sleeve at the stairwell and held it a moment, hard, and let go. Beatrix nodded once from the doorway, brake marks on her hands, and went back to the yards. Ola wrote the finding down on the landing rail from his dictation, word for word in her own hand, and underlined the band line twice with a ruler.

For Nell Ardery, who had eleven weeks of figures in her pocket and had asked him to come back, his answer meant a cold minute on a landing and a line in a ledger she would never see.

He slept two hours on the bed with his coat on and woke with the finding on the table and the grey at his temple aching. The room above the chop-house smelled of rain and soap and damp coats drying. He washed in cold water at the basin and went down to work the night basins, because the branch never slept even when he did. He wanted, concretely, to get out of the square without falling over — and he did it, because Ines was standing in the back row with her face in her hands, and he had to walk past her to reach the door.

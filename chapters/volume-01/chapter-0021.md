# Ninety-One Names

Runa Sato's observation room was a store cupboard on the second floor with a table in it, and she had put the table there herself, which meant she had carried it up the back stair on her own.

"Two things," she said. "Sit if you want. And understand that whatever you say in here goes on paper with my name at the bottom, and there is no version of this conversation where it doesn't."

He sat. The chair was a stacking chair from a room upstairs and it had a strip of tape on the leg.

"Then ask."

"What did he offer you." She had her file open on the table with a pen in it and she had not written anything yet, and she said the question looking at the page rather than at him, which he understood afterwards was the most professional thing she did all morning. "Don't tidy it. I have read four files this month and every one of them had a tidy version in it, and the tidy version is always where the man disappeared."

"He wants me employed." Lin Hao put his hands flat on the table because he wanted them visible. "Plate reissued in the branch name, so the figure I carry is the Branch's figure and not mine. Four times a week for two hours in his own room, on the record, credited to the Fourth Branch. Everything I do in that room goes into the Branch's account and not mine."

"Two hours."

"Two hours. And he said out loud that the day I want out is the day somebody in an office asks him a question, and that on that day he can point at a door with a man in it."

Runa wrote for a while. The pen went through the paper on the third stroke and she turned it round and wrote on the back of the sheet, which told him she had done this before and run out of forms.

"That's a good offer," she said. "It's a good offer, Lin Hao. I want you to sit with how good it is, because in about eleven days you're going to be offered something worse and it will feel like relief."

"What can you do?"

"That's what I have to write down next." She turned the sheet round and read from it. "A Field order under this heading is an observation order. It lets me attend. It lets me be present at a reading, at an interview, and at a removal. It lets me follow you on any working day without a warrant. It does not let me open your plate, your rooms, or your file."

"And it does not let you take me."

"It does not let me take you." She looked up. "Not on this paper. A requisition is a different instrument, and it needs a second seal, and the second seal does not come from the branch that wants you."

"So you're watching."

"I'm writing." She put the pen down flat, which was the first time she had stopped moving since he came in. "That's the part nobody puts on the order. Everybody thinks the cost of watching is the watching. The cost of watching is that every word I put down is a thing I did. If you're a supply, I'm the instrument that took you. If you're a supply being run by a man in a good coat in a district that's nine hundred and eighty short, I'm the instrument that took him, and he has a closed door and four years of file and a floor of four thousand people behind him, and I have a cupboard."

She said it without any self-pity at all, which made it worse.

"How much are you carrying?"

"That's not in the order either."

"How much are you carrying, Runa."

She looked at the door. "A stone and a half," she said. "I've carried it eleven years and there's a difference between carrying and weighing, and nobody in my division has ever been able to tell me which one I'm doing. If it keeps going, it stops being something I carry and starts being something I'm made of, and I'd like another twenty years before I find out which."

She wrote that down too. He understood that she had written it down because she had said it out loud, and that this was going to be in a document somebody read, and that she had decided to be in it.

Then he did the thing he had been not doing for nineteen days.

"The thing you want to know is whether I am being milked," he said. "I'll tell you. It isn't intention. It's geography."

He put his finger on the table and it was a small thing to do on a small table and it took him a while to explain and she let him take a while.

The unrecorded good in the Slough and the Vestry is not spread across the city. It sits in one arc between the yards and the free clinics and it moves along the arc four times a day, morning and evening, with the shifts, because that is when the doors are busiest. His plate fills at the rate that arc does unrecorded kindness. He does not choose it. He cannot switch it off, and he had taken a whole day off and stood against a wall on the arcade all day and the figure went up four hundred grains anyway.

Runa wrote it down in flat economical strokes and did not ask a question for nearly four minutes, which was the longest she had been quiet.

"Say that last part again," she said.

"I can't stop it. There's no life in which I decide to earn nothing and ninety people stop being kind at doors."

"That's not the part." She had stopped writing. "Say the other part. Four hundred grains, standing still, against a wall."

"That's what it took."

"Nobody accrues like that."

"I know."

"So it isn't a supply," she said, "it's a *condition*." She wrote that down, and underlined it, and then looked at the underline the way people look at a thing they have just done to themselves. "You understand what you've done to me. I've had this order open for three weeks and it says suspect drawn source, requisition recommended on confirmation. And you have just handed me the one thing that lets me write a different sentence, and I am going to write it, and I am going to sign it, and it is going to sit in a folder with your name on it in a building I do not control."

"Will it hold?"

"It will hold for a week. That's what I can promise you and it's worth exactly a week, and I want you to hear me say the word instead of finding it out." She finished the sentence, and signed the bottom of the page, and did not tear it off. "Runa Sato, Field. Observation only. No requisition recommended at this time."

Then she sat back in the stacking chair with the tape on its leg and looked at him properly for the first time.

"You said it out loud in a room that writes it down," she said. "I want to make sure you know what that is, because people who have never done it tend to think it is a confidence. It isn't. It's the opposite. You have given a stranger the map, on purpose, because the alternative was being a thing on a list, and you did not ask me for anything first."

"I asked you for a week."

"You asked me for a week and I gave you a week and you paid for it in the currency nobody ever spends, which is the truth, given before anybody has earned it from you."

He felt the vertigo of being described. He had spent four years being looked through, and a woman had just written him down in a cupboard, and it was the first time in his life that being written down had felt like handing somebody a knife rather than being handed one.

She gave him back the file and told him what the observation actually was, in practical terms, because she was thorough. She would be at the readings. She would be on the floor with a notepad and no plate visible. She would not attend anything she was not invited to, she would not follow him home, and she would not touch him, and he was to note that last one as a professional courtesy and not a small one.

Then she said: "My report goes in on the 6th. That buys you a week and possibly the whole thing, and it will be read by a man in an office I have never been in, and if he agrees with me you will find out because nothing happens, and if he doesn't you will find out because something happens very fast."

He went down the back stair to the ground floor and Rhee was standing outside the plate office with his coat already on, at ten in the morning, which was four hours before Rhee came in.

"Heard you had a visitor," Rhee said.

"The Field."

"Yes." Rhee's face did not do anything. He looked at the gate and then back. "I want to say one thing and then I am going to go and do a district. You have taken a thing that was on my table and put it in a folder upstairs, and there is now a version of me in Ostara that says a deputy warden offered to buy a man who works in his washroom. I cannot un-say it and I cannot be angry about it, because if I had been in your boots I would have said the same three sentences to the Field before the end of the week."

"Then I am sorry."

"Don't be. Be useful." He pulled his coat straight. "My door is not shut. It is going to be shut, because a man who has made a public record of what I offered cannot then come and sit in my private room twice a week on the record — that would be a thing a man in an office asks about, and that was the entire condition I described to you." He almost smiled. "You have spent the offer. That is what you did with it. Keep the week."

He walked out. He did not slam anything, and Lin Hao stood in the concourse and understood that the last friendly door in his working life had just closed with a courtesy, and that he had done it himself, on purpose, for a week he could not spend on himself.

Then he went round to the board by the second-floor door.

There was a new sheet on it, printed rather than pinned, gone up before seven, and it had a heading that the old copy had not: DRAW-DOWN, FOURTH BRANCH — DEFERRED SCHEDULE. READINGS AT TIMES SHOWN. NINETY-ONE.

A time against every name. Not a date, a time, to the minute, in a column, in a building of four thousand people who had all walked past this board every working day for years without reading it and who would now have to stand at it and find out when they were going into a room.

Thirty-eighth down: LIN HAO. 11:20.

Under the column of names, at the foot of the sheet, in the small print the Bureau used for things it did not want looked at and could not avoid printing:

STAFF UNABLE TO SETTLE AT READING ARE REFERRED IN ACCORDANCE WITH WING PRACTICE. ATTENDANCE COMPULSORY. THOSE NOT ATTENDING ARE DEEMED UNSETTLED.

He read it twice. It said what it said, in the plainest legal language in the city, and it meant that a man who did not turn up for his own reading would be found unsettled without anybody having to look at him at all.

Thirty-eighth down. Eleven twenty.

Rhee's fortnight had put the reading five days out and had nine days of it still unspent behind it. He had spent his only deadline in the world on a date forty-eight hours further away, and a date further away was worth exactly one week of somebody not knowing, and then it was a date again.

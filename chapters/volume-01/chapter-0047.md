# The Review

The Winter Review convened on Thursday in the sixth week in a room off the Exchange on the second floor with nine chairs behind a long table and public seats for sixty, and Lin Hao stood in the Exchange box at nine that morning first, because a provisional band paid its cheaper price even on the day it might lose the dearer one.

Garrick Neve read the figure without preamble.

“Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight,” he said. “Three grains down on the published standing. Three grains inside tolerance. Band holds on the edge of it.”

He read the weekly question. Lin Hao answered it with the same two instruments and no other, and Neve read it back, and the queue listened, and the counterfoil was stamped. Four Thursdays, four counterfoils, four true answers, each one read aloud and read back with the queue listening. That was the box discharged through the Review, and Neve handed him the last one with a nod that meant he knew what day it was.

He checked the four counterfoils were dry and in order, dates facing out, stamps clear, the way Neve had handed them to him across the rail. Then Lin Hao went upstairs to the Review room with the four counterfoils in his coat pocket and the re-read slip with them.

Twenty-two names on the queue sheet, ordered by published standing. He was the twenty-second and the last, and the word provisional on his line was the only reason he was on the sheet. He knew that going in. Everybody in the corridor knew it. The sheet had been up since the 26th of last month with the small print at the bottom since the 24th: a provisional band is not carried into review, a candidate is seated on published standing at the date of the Review.

The room sat nine at a time, no more, no fewer. That was the rule and it was printed on the door. Nine seats behind the table for the panel, nine seats in front of it for the candidates called, sixty behind those for the public. The twenty-two were called in order, nine by nine, and the band was struck off the figure on the day, in the room, from the plate, with a clerk reading it out, plate by plate, finding by finding, stamp by stamp.

He sat in the corridor through the morning with the other thirteen of the second call and watched the first nine go in and be called back out again. A woman from the supply floor who had been a five for nine years came out white with a black five still on her tile. Two men from the Reading Office came out without looking at anybody. The corridor stayed silent. That was the design of it. A man went in with a figure and came out with a rank, and the rank was what the table said it was.

Ines came down the corridor with her folder under her arm and sat on his other side, though auditors did not sit with candidates. She did it anyway, because the paper between them was discharged and this was not about the rota name.

“The second of May is still on my table,” she said, low. “I have the date and the figure and I have not asked. Whatever happens in that room, that part does not change today.”

“I know.”

Tessa came next with Beatrix, both of them in working dress, both of them refused seats by the usher and standing anyway by the wall. Nadia came last, in uniform, and stood in the aisle because she would not sit, and the usher gave up telling her.

Runa Sato came down the corridor with her hands behind her back and sat on the bench beside him.

“You are in the room because of the query,” she said, low. “Hear that part first. Without the query you would not be in it at all, because a provisional band is not carried and your published standing is a five. The query put you in it because a query has to be answered in a room with the entry present. Mr Hartle pulled the register on the 24th and the clerk who entered your reading on the 3rd owns it, and she cannot invent you an instrument, so the panel has to look at you.”

“Then I am here to be removed.”

“You are here to be read.” She did not look at him.

He asked what the panel would do with a declaration and no instrument, and she said the panel would do what the table said. Three grains down inside tolerance held the band through the day, with nothing at all under the third grain. The query stood beside the band and not under it. One required arithmetic and the other required an answer, and the room would take them in that order.

The usher came down the corridor with a list and read nine names for the second call, his among them, last. The thirteen who were not called settled back on the benches. Ines closed her folder and put both hands on it. Tessa touched his shoulder once as he stood, brief, professional, the way she touched a patient sitting up. “There is a difference and the panel knows it. The first nine are deeds and hearings and findings. You are a declaration and a requisition and a re-read that held by one grain and then by two. The panel has never seen that shape before.”

The first nine took an hour. The corridor heard nothing through the door, which was the point of the door. When it opened the usher read nine names without expression, and the nine who had been waiting stood up with their plates in their hands.

The clerk called the second nine and he went in with eight others and sat in the third seat from the left, with his plate in his hands.

The panel read each plate in turn. The machine was on the table, the band table beside it, the register sheet beside that, and the clerk read every finding out as the stamp came down. The room was hot and the public seats stayed still, because a Review was not a spectacle. It was a table doing arithmetic out loud.

Marguerite Vale was in the public seats in the third row, in a dark dress, with two of her house beside her. She caught his eye once and nodded, nothing more. She had said she would be in the room and she was, with the terms down and unsigned and the house behind her anyway.

The clerk at the end of the table had entered his reading on the 3rd and owned the query on it, and she stood with her arms folded and her eyes on the docket, not on him. She had told him she could not invent him an instrument. She had told the panel the same, in writing, on the 24th.

The second nine took their seats in order, plates on knees, hands folded. One by one the machine read and the clerk wrote, and the chair spoke the finding for each. Four grains down lapsed a man who had held five for six years, and he went out with his plate in both hands. Two grains up held another who wept when the stamp came down. Each finding was stamped and counterfoiled, and each counterfoil went into a pocket the way his four had gone into his.

The usher kept the public seats silent with a look. The panel conferred in whispers between readings, passing the register sheet along the table. The register entry dated the 3rd went past three pairs of hands before it came back to the chair.

His turn came last of the nine.

“Lin Hao,” the clerk said. “Provisional six. Floor eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Reading eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. Three grains down. Inside tolerance by nothing.”

The panel looked at the query sheet.

“The index holds a query on this entry,” the chair said. “Entered by the Reading Office on the 24th. What instrument do you hold for the figure on that plate.”

“The requisition order of the 19th and my own declaration of the 23rd,” Lin Hao said. He stood up because standing was the only way to say it without going round it twice. “No other instrument.”

“A provisional band is not carried into review,” the chair said. “A candidate is seated on published standing at the date of the Review. Your published standing is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, which is a five.”

“Yes.”

The chair conferred with the panel in whispers. The register sheet went along the table again. The clerk stood with her arms folded, waiting, because the query was hers and the answer would be read against her entry. The public seats did not move. Marguerite sat with her house round her, hands folded, watching the table and not the candidates. The two women with her took notes without being asked. Ines had her folder open now on her knees, with a blank page uppermost and a pen in her hand, though she had no standing to write a finding.

The room went quiet. That was the whole of the arithmetic in two sentences, and everybody in it knew it.

“The query requires an answer,” the chair said. “The panel will hear one question put and answered under the open-question rule, if the candidate takes the slot. The slot is public, without notice, on the floor, now.”

Beatrix had her brake-marked hands folded on her knees, though she stood by the door. Nadia stood in the aisle with her arms at her sides, breathing hard, the way she stood on her own car between floors. Tessa had her sleeves down and her hands folded over them.

Runa Sato, in the corridor doorway, went very still.

Lin Hao stood on the floor in front of nine chairs and sixty people with his plate in his hands and four counterfoils in his pocket, and felt the fear come up clean, without the arithmetic round it.

The clerk at the end of the table had entered his reading on the 3rd and owned the query on it, and whatever he said next would either close her entry in good faith or take it off the sheet with her name still in the clerk’s column.

The usher closed the door for the finding. The corridor held its breath the way corridors do when a room is deciding. He wanted, concretely, to answer one true question before the band was struck off him — and he said he would take the slot.

# Held Account One

The file was brought up in a trolley with a guard on it, and the trolley was put in the Reading Office side room at ten in the morning, and the clerk laid the docket on the table beside it.

Four thousand one hundred and twelve folios. Four thousand one hundred and eleven sealed to him. One not.

Lin Hao stood in the side room with Ines in the corridor outside the door, because an auditor might stand in a corridor, and the clerk opened the docket and turned past the seal sheets one by one, each one stamped and numbered, each one closed to a rank-six petitioner who had not earned the rank behind it.

“Four thousand one hundred and eleven sealed,” the clerk said. “One open. The open folio is at the back by order of the Wing, filed three years ago, never called.”

The guard signed for the trolley at the door in the docket book and took up station outside it, the way guards do for files of this size, with his hands folded. The clerk checked the seals at the door and again at the table, docket against trolley, number against number. Four thousand one hundred and twelve folios, bound in parts, each part sealed with the Wing stamp. She laid the heavy sealed parts out carefully in order and put the docket on top.

She brought it out and laid it on the table. One sheet of paper, folded once, with a transfer receipt on the front in a registrar’s hand and a second line underneath it in a different hand. The ink had held well for three years without fading. The paper was thin with age and the fold had softened, the way paper softens when it has sat three years without being called.

He knew the first hand before he read a word of it. Three years gone and he knew it the way a man knows his own face. His brother had written the numbers onto the Board for two years, and the figures had been exact every Thursday, and the hand on the receipt was the same hand that had made them.

The receipt recorded two hundred and fourteen thousand grains moved on the eleventh of a month three years ago into an account the index did not contain. The plate number was given. The account number was not in any ledger the Reading Office held. The registrar’s signature was at the bottom, full and clear: Lin Wei.

Underneath it, in the upright print of the Classification Wing, a second line.

The file of the person who received it is Held Account One.

He read both lines twice and then a third time, standing up at the table with his hands flat on either side of the paper, because if he touched it he would crease it. The figures were exact in the way his brother’s figures were always exact on the Board every Thursday for two years. The plate number below the amount was written without hesitation. The signature at the bottom was full and clear, the same hand that had signed his school reports.

The clerk gave him time. She stood back from the table with her hands folded and looked quietly at the wall, because a petitioner reading an open folio was entitled to complete quiet. The tall uniformed guard shifted patiently outside the open door. The trolley stood with its seals unbroken except for the one part.

“There is no Held Account Zero,” the clerk said. She had been watching his face. “The index was asked that morning and the answer came back the same day. One is the first.”

He nodded. He could not speak.

The clerk folded the docket back and put the open folio on top of it, and gave him a copy slip with the folio number on it, stamped, and he put it in his pocket with the four counterfoils and the re-read slip and the finding.

The clerk made the copy herself at the side press, folio number and receipt front, and stamped it twice, once for the file and once for the petitioner. She logged the call in the docket book with the date and his band number, and the guard signed beneath her entry. That was the procedure for a file of this size, and every step of it was witnessed by the room.

She had entered his reading on the 3rd and owned the query on it through the 24th, and her entry had closed on a finding in front of nine chairs instead of coming off the sheet on the morning of the Review, and she would go on reading plates for the Office for another nineteen years and never once see the shape of that again.

Ines was waiting in the corridor with her folder closed under her arm. She had stood there the whole hour without sitting, though the corridor had benches. An auditor might stand in a corridor and no further, and she had kept to that exactly, folder closed the whole time.

“One,” he said.

“One what.”

“One folio open. A transfer receipt in his hand. Two hundred and fourteen thousand grains into an account the index does not contain. And under it a line in the Wing’s hand saying the file of the person who received it is Held Account One.”

She looked at him for a long moment, the way she looked at a record that changed the shape of the room. The long corridor was empty except for a porter with a trolley at the far end, waiting patiently to take the heavy file down again. The side room door stood open with the guard beside it, and the trolley inside with its seals checked and logged.

“Then he did it,” she said. “He said the number was wrong in public and by morning he was gone, and what he did was give it away where no ledger could follow it.” She put her hand on his arm. “And you are the address. Ninety-one lines and one of them is yourself. I do not know what a Bureau does with a line it has never had to read, Lin Hao. Nobody does. That is not a gap in the rules. That is the rules never having had to cover it.”

They stood a while in the corridor without speaking, because there was nothing in the corridor that could hold what the side room held. Ines asked whether the second line was in the Wing print and he said yes, upright, below the receipt. She asked whether the account number was on the index and he said the clerk had told him plainly it was not in any ledger the large office held. She nodded and put those two facts away the way she put all facts away, squared and dated.

He walked slowly back to the Fourth Branch by the long road, forty-one minutes, with the copy slip in his pocket beside the four counterfoils and the re-read slip and the finding. The pavements were dry and the busy crossing ran freely, strangers holding doors for each other with the shifts. The familiar figure moved wherever he stood and he let it move. Spending on nobody at all was the only posture left to him that did harm.

At the branch the corridor board stood empty except for the Review date at the top in the clerk’s hand. Tobias was in the annexe carrying boxes, thirty-one of forty, and nodded through the stairwell. Runa’s cupboard stood open and empty. The washrooms needed doing and he was glad of it.

He went up to the second floor and cleaned the last row of basins with the filed petition drying on the counter beside the soap. The work took him an hour and he did it properly, basin by basin, taps and rims and the lino beneath, because the basins were the one thing in the city that asked nothing of him. The petition copy dried beside the soap with the folio number showing, and he turned it face down halfway through, because a washroom counter was no place to leave a brother’s hand uncovered.

Runa met him on the stair with her hands behind her back, the way she always stood when a file closed. The Field file had his declaration at the front of it with the finding number beside it, she said. Observation had ended with the Review. She would file no further on the account unless the division ordered it. He thanked her and she accepted it with a nod, professional to the last.

Marguerite wrote by runner that afternoon. The house had heard the file opened by one folio, she said. The terms stayed down. She would be at Bellhouse when he wanted tea. He put the folded note with the envelope of three sheets and kept it safe in his coat pocket beside the copy slip.

The great painted Board was nine streets away with his name on a black tile for the first time since he was a child, and he did not go and look at it. The distant black tile would keep perfectly well without him. The basins would not, and the soap needed cutting, and the lino needed mopping before night shift.

He mopped the clean lino thoroughly and wrung the mop and hung it straight, and checked the precious petition once more before he finally left it to dry overnight. Three people in the building felt something cold that night and none of them knew why. Nell Ardery on the landing of number 14, with her eleven weeks of figures in her pocket. Garrick Neve at the rail, with nine years of readings behind him. The checker at the yard gate, with a docket under his arm.

In the evening Ola brought the diary up to date on the landing rail, forty pages and more, with the Review finding copied under the re-read. Beatrix came down from the yards to read it and stood a long time in front of the rail without speaking. Tessa counted the tall rack aloud in the cold store, seventeen boards, and said eighteen before nightfall, because the ward had sent another patient upstairs with a chart that did not match. Nadia held her own car at the terminus past midnight, though no one told her to, and looked out over the empty arcade with her hands on the familiar brass.

Ninety-one people knew a man in a washroom had told the truth in front of his colleagues, and had held six through a room with nine seats, and had opened a file of four thousand one hundred and twelve folios by one. The branch knew. The Vestry knew. Bellhouse knew by nightfall, and the house kept the terms down anyway, signed or unsigned, because a man who asked for the date instead of the money was worth holding.

Nobody has thanked him.

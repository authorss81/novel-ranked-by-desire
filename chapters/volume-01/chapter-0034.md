# Cordwain

On the 15th he walked the arc for the first time in seventeen days, on purpose, in daylight, which was the single hardest thing he had done since the 3rd.

The arc is yards to free clinics. It is not a street; it is a set of corridors that people walk because they are walking anyway, four times a day, on the shift changes, holding doors and carrying trays and stopping on landings to wait for somebody who is slower. He had spent four years learning to stand in the densest part of it, and on the 29th he had got on a tram at the yards and gone straight home because nineteen people was the number he had and there was no twentieth one coming.

He had not ridden since. The figure had kept moving. The faces had stopped.

So on the 15th he went back into it at ten past six in the morning, at the change, with his plate in his pocket and no plan, and let it happen to him.

By four o'clock in the afternoon he had stopped being able to count them, which was a thing he had not done before.

Twenty-two new faces in one day. A woman at the clinic door holding it for a man with a stick. Two porters at the yard gate who had a lifting argument and lost it. A man in a bread queue who moved up to let somebody in and did not go back to his place. A boy on the landing who had given his seat and was standing. Four people in a stair who took a stretcher up two floors between them because one of them had done it before. Eleven more he could not have described. All of them inside four minutes' walk of him and all of them distinct.

Forty-one. He wrote it on the back of a docket in the yard office and read it and wrote it again and thought about a woman on a tram roof saying that a pattern is a legible shape, and understood with a cold feeling that he had just made his own.

Nobody had asked him to walk it. That was the part he was going to have to look at later, in a colder room, because it had not occurred to him that the figure was the part he was hunting and that for four years he had been hunting it in the wrong place. He was not climbing. He was taking a census.

Cordwain took the arc at the yards end and the yards were there because Halvard Rees came from them, a wire-splicer off the Ferrier line, and Lin Hao had never once asked the man where he worked because it had not been the kind of question that existed between them.

He learned about the block on the 16th, from Nell Ardery, who was on the landing of number 14 at seven in the morning with a plate reader in her hand and eleven weeks of figures on a sheet in her other hand.

She was a machinist at the Cordwain works and she had a plate and a band and no reason to talk to him except that he was standing on her landing asking about the district return.

"Eleven weeks," she said. "Two hundred and forty a week, then two hundred and eighty, then one hundred and ninety. It is never the same and it is never nothing. And every Thursday the district office posts a return and my number is back where it was, and my band has not moved in eleven weeks and I have been told three times that this is because I am steady."

"That is not possible."

"That is what I said." She turned the sheet round. It was eleven columns of her own figure, in her own hand, with a ring drawn round each one. "I am not complaining to a stranger on a landing. I have been to the Correction Office twice and both times they asked me for an instrument and there is no instrument, because the man who took the number off me works in a room I have never been in and has never spoken to me, and I have begun to think he does not exist and the district simply eats it."

"Who takes it off you?"

"I have never seen him. He is not a collector. There is a line on the return that says adjusted to district and a set of initials against it and the initials are a code." She folded the sheet. "You are the man off the board."

"Yes."

"Then I am going to tell you something for nothing," Nell Ardery said, "because it costs nothing and you are the only person in this district who has admitted to a number in public in the last month. Eleven weeks. Two thousand one hundred and forty grains. That is what the building has lost."

The landing was very cold and the stair went down into a yard that was making noise at seven in the morning.

"What is the building?"

"Cutler's Row," she said. "Thirty-one dwellings, four storeys, and it is the worst-adjusted address in Cordwain and everybody in it knows it and not one of us can prove anything. There is a woman on the second floor of number six who has not been above rank two since the spring and she sweeps a stair every morning. There is a man on the third floor of number 9 whose band went down in June and who has been paying at the new rate ever since."

She looked at him.

"It is a district return," she said. "Somebody has to make the district come out. Nine thousand people, three hundred a week short, and a return is a page with numbers on it and the page has to balance. You cannot balance it by taking three hundred from nine thousand people. You take it from thirty-one."

The requisition order came off the board on the 17th at seven in the morning, in the district office's own frame, in a district office two streets from the yards, and Lin Hao read it standing in front of it with his hood up because it was raining.

It was not a long document. He had read documents since June and he could get through a requisition in about ninety seconds, and this one he read three times because he could not believe that it was doing what it appeared to be doing.

REQUISITION OF PROPERTY. CUTLER'S ROW, CORDWAIN — THIRTY-ONE DWELLINGS, AND THE YARD PASSAGE AND THE BACK STAIR ATTACHED.

Authority: Field mandate, executed on the officer's own hand, no countersignature.

Ground: premises under continuing observation by reason of an unsettled account standing against the address.

Execution: 19th, six in the morning.

And at the bottom, in the field for the officer, two names.

SATO, R., FIELD — EXECUTING OFFICER.

LIN HAO — SUBJECT. ACCOUNT CARRIED. PRESENT BY ATTESTATION 3RD.

There was no name for the man who had adjusted the return. There was no instrument for the two thousand one hundred and forty grains. There were thirty-one dwellings and his own name twice on one page, and he had done nothing on the 17th except walk into a district, and the district had been waiting for somebody to walk into it since the 3rd, and the first person it had ever had was him.

He went and found Runa Sato at eleven, on the second floor of the Fourth Branch, and she was not surprised to see him.

"You want to know why the ground is that address."

"I want to know why the ground is a row of thirty-one flats and a district return."

"Because I looked for it and I found it," she said. "You declared on the 3rd that your account is fed along an arc and that the arc runs through Cordwain, and on the 6th I walked it, and the arc goes into Cutler's Row at the yards end because a man off the Ferrier line lives at number 11 and he is one of yours. That is not a secret. You said it in a corridor. Everything that is on that wall is on that wall because you put it there."

"That is not a ground. That is a street."

"It is the only ground available and I did not write it." She had a copy of the order on the desk, which she was not supposed to have. "Here is what wrote itself. A mandate lets me execute on my own hand, so I executed, and the execution needs a subject, and the subject of an account that has been observed for seven days and that no instrument can band is the account's carrier."

"I did not choose you. You chose to declare. You chose a corridor with ninety-one people in it, and you chose to say the account moves along an arc and that the arc goes through Cordwain, and I have done the rest of it with a pen."

"And the two thousand one hundred and forty grains."

"I did not know about those until the fourteenth, when a woman on a landing told a man on a landing and I was four doors up a stair." Runa Sato picked up her copy. "It is also why I will not be stopped. The district office wanted the return balanced before the quarter and they have been eating Cutler's Row for eleven weeks, and if I requisition the row the adjustment stops on the 19th, because a requisitioned address is not adjusted."

"That is the truth of it and I am not going to pretend the truth of it does me credit. Twenty-two thousand grains out of a page in nine weeks. There were four other ways to do that and none of them are legal."

"You are going to put thirty-one people out of their homes so a return balances."

"I am going to put thirty-one people out of their homes so that the twenty-two thousand grains they were being eaten at stop being eaten, and I have not slept about it, and I did the arithmetic on your account as well and it is the same arithmetic." She put the copy down. "You want me to be softer than the machine and I am not, and there is a version of me that is, and she would have requisitioned a building with a number on it instead of a building with a name on it, and then the adjustment would have continued."

"You could have requisitioned the district office."

"Twelve thousand dwellings and I could not have found a subject for it."

He went to Cutler's Row on the 17th at four in the afternoon, in daylight, on purpose, with his name on the order and no authority whatsoever, and the door of number 11 was open because the warders had a man on the stair.

That was the part that was not in the order. Two warders had come at three with a clipboard and had gone down the row knocking, and at number 9 a man had come out onto the landing and had put his hand flat on the door frame and had said the room was his and he had been in it since March, and one of the warders had written something and gone on down. It had taken them four minutes for thirty-one dwellings because most of them are not there in the afternoon.

At number 11 the door was on the latch and the man was at work and the woman who answered it said he was not in, and Lin Hao stood on the landing while she went to get him and looked at the back stair door at the end of the passage.

It is a fire door. It is on a chain and a padlock from the inside, because it is the only way out of the back of the row, and there are eleven dwellings behind it. The chain was on the floor and the padlock was hanging open in the staple and the door was standing four inches into the passage, and it had been opened from the outside with a bar.

He stood and looked at it for long enough that the woman came back and saw him looking at it and said, in the flat voice of somebody who has already decided what to think, "It was like that on the eleventh."

"The eleventh."

"I chained it that night," she said. "On the twelfth it was standing open again and I chained it again, and last night it was standing open again, and your warders came at three this afternoon and went straight through it and did not look where they were going. I am not a fool. I have lived in this row for nine years and I know what a warder looks at."

Behind her the door came in off the landing, and Halvard Rees had his coat over his arm and a roll of wire in his hand, and he looked at the open door in his own passage and then at Lin Hao, and neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing on either side that had not already been said on a tram.

"Do not go down there," Halvard Rees said.

"It is not a cellar," Lin Hao said. "It is four dwellings and a passage and the only way out of eleven flats."

"I know what it is. That is why I am telling you." Halvard Rees put the roll of wire down on the table. "Three times in a fortnight a man has come through my back door and gone down and I have not heard him come back up. And you are going to go down there, because you have been in this row four times in three days and you have never once said why."

He picked the wire up again.

"There is a stair on it," he said, "and it has been loose since the winter, and I have been up it twice and told them, and they have a book."

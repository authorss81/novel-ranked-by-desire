# What the Board Does Not Say

The register was on a frame at the left-hand end of the Board about a foot off the ground, and by the 23rd there were a hundred people bent over it instead of forty, and a man had set out two chairs in front of it for people who could not read standing up.

Lin Hao read it for the fourth time in his life and stopped at the third line, because the third line had stopped being the most frightening sentence he had ever read and had become a different thing.

Register opened nine years ago. Thirty-one entries. Entries are accounts. Entries are not persons.

Eleven people bent over that sheet read the third line the way he had on the 10th, which is to say as a promise that the Wing did not know what it was holding.

A woman on his left said "May I," with a plate in her hand and her reading slip rolled up in the other.

He moved along.

Ines Duarte was sitting on the low wall at the end of the frame with her coat over her knees and a folder she was not using, and people had started to walk round her.

"You have not said anything in four days," she said.

"You gave me until tonight."

"I gave you until tonight four days ago." She did not look up. "It is the twenty-third, and I want to say a number to you, and I want you to let me get all the way through it, because you have that face and I have watched it come up on about nine people in four years and every one of them stopped me in the second sentence."

He sat down on the wall beside her.

"Nine years," Ines Duarte said. "About eleven unsettled readings a year in this city, and nine of the eleven come to me, and I have had the job eleven years. That is a hundred. Call it a hundred and a bit, because two of the years were bad."

"And how many are on that sheet."

"Thirty-one."

He looked at it. Thirty-one plate numbers in a clerk's hand, about half with a date, half without, and no names anywhere on the page.

"That is not what it is, then."

"That is exactly what it is, and you have it in nine seconds, which I had hoped you would." She closed the folder she had not opened. "It is not a list of people like you. If it were, it would have a hundred and something on it and somebody would have used it by now. Thirty-one is not a population. Thirty-one is a catch."

"A catch of what."

"Of accounts that were noticed." She nodded at the frame. "Look at the dates. The ones with dates were entered beside by somebody in this city. The ones without were entered before the form was printed, so the practice came first and the form came after it."

"And us."

"Us, exactly." She turned her head. "Yours is the thirty-first and it has the third on it, and it is the only entry on that page with a date inside the last eleven days, and you were not in it on the second. A plate of yours that cannot settle goes nowhere until the machine has a reason to write it down, and on the third you stood up in a corridor in front of ninety-one people and gave it one."

"So it is not a record of what people like me do."

"It is a record of what this city catches doing it," Ines Duarte said. "Thirty-one people in nine years, and every one of them got on that sheet the day somebody looked at them. About seventy of the hundred never got on it at all, and they are still out there. That is the thing the page does not say, and I have been standing here for two hours working out how to say it to you."

Runa Sato had sent one line to the Fourth Branch on the 22nd that said OBSERVATION ROOM. TEN O'CLOCK. It took Lin Hao eleven hours to work out why she wanted to see him at ten at night in a store cupboard, and about four of them to get to it: it is the only room in the Bureau with no window, and therefore no one who could see into it.

She had carried the table up the back stair herself nine days ago and there were three chairs at it now, and Ines Duarte was already in one.

"Sit down, both of you," Runa Sato said. "This door does not lock and I would rather it did not."

"You sent for her," Lin Hao said.

"I sent for the auditor. Mr Hartle at the Exchange reads a plate and a number and nothing else, and he could not tell you whose thirty-one those are in eleven years. Ines can tell you, and that is the whole reason she is in this cupboard."

"I have never had feelings about you," Ines Duarte said.

"Nobody has. Sit down."

Lin Hao sat down.

There were two things on the table. Her own file, carried since the 6th and never shown to him. And a blank page with a date at the top of it.

"I am going to tell you what I have established and then what I want, in that order," she said, "because you are a man who can tell when a person has reversed the order."

"What I have established is that the Field division and the Wing do not share systems. That page has been on a wall for nine years and I had never seen it. I have asked six officers in the division and not one had seen it either. It is not withheld from us. It is a different office's paperwork, and nobody reconciles one against the other. That is the most frightening thing I have learned this month and it is not about you."

She tapped the corner of the file.

"The oldest entry is nine years old, put on by hand in pencil before the form was printed, and it is the only one of the thirty-one still running. The oldest account on that page is not yours. You are the thirty-first and you arrived on the third."

"That is not a question."

"It is not a question yet." She put a pen down beside the blank page. "Here is what I want. I have held it since the tenth and I have not put it in a file, because I am not entitled to.

"On the third you struck five names off a board by saying a true sentence out loud.

You had no instrument against one of the five and you did not look for evidence against any of them, and this office has been treating your declaration as a finding for thirteen days, and under it there are five people counted as settled by a man who cleans washrooms. There is a word for what you did and the word is *unattested*. If it is ever tested the five go straight back on the wall and the floor goes back under the audit and ninety-one people find out in one morning what your sentence was worth. That has a date on it. The date is the twenty-ninth."

"You want me to say it again."

"I want you to write it. In your hand, dated the twenty-third, and it goes into a Field file and not into yours. You tell me what you actually know: that you struck five names by saying a thing and had no instrument against any of them. You give me the five names. And at the bottom, one line — whether you would say it again if they asked you tomorrow morning."

"That is not what you want."

"That is exactly what I want. It is the cleanest thing you could put in that file and it is worth nothing at all." She looked at him. "I have been in this cupboard for thirteen days with a pencil and I have not been able to write the version that helps me."

Ines Duarte had not moved.

"You will not get it," she said. "He is not going to write it and I am not going to let him. The reason is in a drawer in Lantern Row with my handwriting on the top of it, and you need to know what happens on the twenty-ninth when it comes out, and you know, because you have been in the Field nine years and watched it happen to four other people."

"Mr Hartle cannot—"

"I can," Ines Duarte said, and stopped, and looked at the table.

Both of them looked at Lin Hao.

There is a thing a man has, standing in a room, where two people who want incompatible things out of him have stopped arguing with each other and turned around. He has about four seconds to use it, and whatever he says in those four seconds is the negotiation, and afterwards neither of them can explain how it happened.

"I am not writing your sentence," he said. "Not the clean one, and not the one that says I did it on purpose. And you are both going to hear the rest of it standing up, because if I say it from a chair I will go round it twice.

"On the third, five names came off that board because I stood up and said a true thing, and the true thing happened to be in the same place as those five names. I had no instrument against one of them. I did not look for one, because looking would have taken four days and the audit was in eleven. I know what that is, and I would like it written down that I know, in a room with two people in it, on the twenty-third.

"And one of the five is not settled."

He looked at Runa Sato and did not stop. "His name is Emrys Vaz. He carries branch stock up from the yard at six every morning, and he had no plate at all until the morning of the third, when the reading that came back gave him one, and it read four hundred and forty-one, and it has not moved since, and he can only go down. I have known that since June. Nadia Ferrous has been telling the whole quarter since June and it took me until the third to hear her.

"And he is one of the five I struck off, because I said a sentence and he was standing in that corridor with a plate he had been handed that morning, and he followed me out with his eyes, and he has not said one word about it since, to anybody, including me. I struck him off a wall in front of ninety-one people because he did not ask me for anything."

He put both hands flat on the table.

"So you can have the document.

It is the only version of it that is true and it is the version that costs me the wall.

"Write this. Lin Hao, the twenty-third. I struck five names off the Fourth Branch board on the third of this month by declaration and not by evidence. I had no instrument against any of the five. One of the five is Emrys Vaz and he is not settled and his plate reads 441 and I knew it on the day and I said nothing. It goes into the file in your hand, Ines, and a copy goes into his, and I sign it in front of both of you, and neither of you has to do anything else about it.""

There is a rule in the Field about statements, and it is that an officer does not transcribe one. What she actually did was put a pen and a blank page in front of him and hold the page flat with two fingers while he wrote it standing up.

"You understand what that does," she said, when he had signed it.

"It puts Emrys Vaz back on."

"It puts five back on." Runa Sato did not raise her voice and did not need to. "Yours was the only thing holding the other four. You did not defend them with an instrument, you defended them with yourself standing in a corridor, and this is a piece of paper that says so in your hand. The day a clerk on the second floor reads it, all five are on that board again. And it will not be me that asks for it. The division will ask for it, and they will be right to."

"Then say it anyway," Ines Duarte said, and her voice was not steady and she let it not be. "Mr Hartle will pull the entry in an afternoon. He will want an instrument, and an instrument is a hearing, and a hearing is rank five and above. I am rank two. Your band does not take a hearing until it has survived the twenty-ninth. You know that as well as I do, which is why you have been standing there doing arithmetic for four minutes instead of asking me for anything."

"I am not asking you for anything."

"You asked me for everything," Ines Duarte said. "You said a boy's name out loud in a cupboard, and I have been carrying it since the twenty-second, and I am putting it in your file tonight. I am not doing it for you. I am doing it because a drawer is not a place to keep a person. I cannot open a hearing. I can only carry a name up a corridor, and I would rather it went up a corridor than stayed in my own flat."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and out in the corridor a man was putting the lights out one bank at a time.

"Say the number back to me," Lin Hao said. "The one on his plate."

"Four hundred and forty-one."

"Say what happens to it."

"It goes down," Ines Duarte said. "It has only ever gone down since the third."

"He is a boy and he carries stock up a yard at six in the morning, and he is the only person in that building who is not frightened of you." She put her hand on the sheet. "So write it down properly, with the word PLATE on it. A man in the Wing will not read a number on its own, and will not read a number with a man's name on it either, but he will read *PLATE FOUR HUNDRED AND FORTY-ONE* and go and find who holds it. That is what I am for."

He wrote the three extra words and initialled it.

"You will lose the wall," Runa Sato said, from the doorway, with her coat on.

"I know what it is worth."

He gave her the page and watched her put it into the file herself, at the front, above the observation of the 6th, which is a place a document does not go unless it is the reason for the rest of them. "It was worth five people on the second floor and a fortnight from a man who will not sign anything again after this one. I spent it on nineteen people in four years, and tonight I am spending the last of it on a boy at a gate, because he never asked me for anything and I have run out of other things to pay with."

"That," said Ines Duarte, "is the first thing you have said since the third that is about the other people and not about you."

She put the folder under her arm and went out through the door and down the back stair, and Lin Hao stood in a store cupboard with no window while the most dangerous operative in this district took the lights out one bank at a time around him.

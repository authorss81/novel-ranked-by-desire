# Requisitioned Rooms

The execution order is for six in the morning and it happens at ten past, because eleven warders arrive in two vehicles and one vehicle is late.

Lin Hao gets there at twenty to six because he had not slept. Cutler's Row in the rain at that hour is thirty-one doors and a yard passage and a back stair, and two thirds of the people in it are still asleep, which he finds harder to be near than anything that has happened to him since the 3rd.

Runa Sato is at the mouth of the passage in a wet coat with a clipboard she has not written on yet, and she looks at him and does not ask.

"You are not on this order," she said.

"I know."

"Then you are standing in a private passage at six in the morning in front of thirty-one dwellings."

"I am."

She wrote four words on the clipboard and did not read them back.

The warders went in at ten past and did it in eleven minutes, because eleven of the thirty-one are empty and the rest come out onto their landings in whatever they slept in, and the district clerk reads out a number at each door, and a woman on the second floor of number 6 asks what the number is for, and the clerk says it is the address, and the woman says the number is not the address.

At number 14 Nell Ardery was already dressed, and she came out, looked at the warder and then at Lin Hao and then at Runa Sato, said nothing, and went down the stair with eleven weeks of figures folded in her pocket.

They went into the back stair at seven. It went at twenty past, from the landing at the top of the yard passage, and it went the way a thing goes when it has been reported twice and written in a book.

Runa Sato was under it in about two seconds. Lin Hao did not see her go. He heard the noise of her going — a single flat sound like a sack of sand coming off a lorry — and then he was on the landing on his knees with both arms in the gap and he had Halvard Rees by the collar, because Halvard Rees had come back at six with no coat on to see what was happening to his row.

The stair came down across the back passage. It was not a lot of stone, which is the thing nobody understood afterwards, including him: about nine hundredweight of landing and a length of handrail, four seconds, and Runa Sato under it, and it did not move, because she carries weight, and that is the one thing about her that is true on a document as well as in a room.

Lin Hao got his left forearm in to the wrist and something in the hand took hold of the edge of it, and he heard Halvard Rees shouting his name from about four feet away and then heard him not shout it any more.

It took the warders nine minutes with a bar and the district clerk eleven minutes on a telephone and Runa Sato four minutes to come out from under it, and she came out on her own and stood up in the yard passage with dust in her hair and one arm hanging and said, very clearly, "Everybody back through the front."

They put her in the ground-floor flat at number 6, which the warders had taken as a post because the woman who lives there is at the works until six, and by ten past seven there was a table in her front room with a lamp on it and a mattress on the floor.

Lin Hao had the arm in a sling made out of a warder's belt. Halvard Rees had gone back up, had left the back stair door standing open, and had told two warders, in the plainest words available to him, that he had reported it twice.

Not one of them was going to a clinic. A Field operative at a requisition who goes to a clinic produces an incident form, and an incident form at six in the morning in a street of thirty-one dwellings is the end of everybody involved. So they were both awake, and both upright, and both very bad.

She got her arm up on the table and looked at it and did not put it down.

"That is not a bone," she said. "That is the other thing and it is going to be very bad on Thursday."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be sorry, it is extremely funny." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "I have been in a building with a stair on it for four years and I have signed for two, and I did not think about either of them at a quarter to seven this morning, and there is a woman in this row who told them about it, and if her name is on a form today then I have been a killer with a clipboard for four years and this is the morning it caught up."

Lin Hao put his good hand flat on the table.

"The door had been forced twice before this morning," he said. "At three on the 17th by your own warders, and once on the 11th by somebody else, and a woman in this row chained it up again both times and told me about it on the 17th and has not said a word about it to anybody. And a wireman in number 11 went up it in the winter and again since and reported it, and it is in a book."

"And you are telling me this in a flat with a door that does not lock."

"Yes."

"That is the stupidest thing you have done since the 3rd, and the 3rd was the cleverest thing anybody has done in this city this year." She put her forehead down on her forearm. "Then say the rest of it."

"I was in the stair."

"I had my arm in to the wrist and Halvard Rees was on the other side of me and he came back at six without a coat on because his door was on the latch." Lin Hao said it in the flat voice he used for reading a plate out loud in a public corridor. "That is the version with my name in it. There is another one. The stair came down at seven minutes past and the only person under it was you, and the warders' tally has me out of the passage at six and no time back in, and there is a district form that says what was under it."

Runa Sato lifted her head.

"You are offering me the falsification."

"I am telling you what it is."

"On a table with a lamp on it." She sat back. "I am going to write that the failure was unforecast, that I was under it and took command, that I was alone in the passage, and that I ordered the evacuation. That is going in tonight and it is going to be the truest document I have ever signed, and I am going to sign it knowing it is false in four places: the door, the book, the two warders at three o'clock, and you."

"And it will not save me."

"It will not save you. The warders keep their own tally — times in and times out, not mine, not the district's — and your name will be in it at seven minutes past seven this morning." She put her palm flat on the table next to his. "But the alternative has a woman's name at the bottom of it, and she asked me in the yard whether the number was the address. And you did not come here to ask me to. You told me what I would lose, which is the only currency anybody has ever taken off me in nine years."

She came round the table.

"Whatever you are about to say," she said, "there is a flat with a door that does not lock, and I have one arm, and you have one arm, and neither of us has anywhere to be for two hours."

"I was not going to say anything."

"You were going to say something kind, which is worse." She got her good hand on the front of his shirt. "You are the only person I have ever worked beside who does not know what he is, and it has taken me four years and a requisition of thirty-one flats to work out that that is a *method* and not a defect."

She got the sling off him, and got her own coat off the back of a chair one-handed and dropped it on the floor, and stood over him while he worked his good arm out of his coat.

"Right," she said. "Four things first, not one of them a condition, all of them true."

"Go on."

"One. I have wanted this since the sixth, when you told a machine it could put your name on four other people's floors. Two. I do not want to be able to say afterwards that it was anything else, and if you make it anything else I will stop. Three. We have one arm each and nowhere to be for two hours. Four. Afterwards I am going to sign something that costs me the thing I am best at, and I am telling you now so you cannot claim later that you did not know what it was."

"Four things. All true."

"All true." She got him up off the mattress edge with one hand. "Now sit down properly, because you are going to fall over and I am not catching you on that arm."

It was not gentle and it was not fast and it was not two people being kind to each other in a bad hour. It was a Field officer of the brute-force division who has spent nine years being the most competent person in every room she enters, taking the whole of a thing off a man who cleans washrooms, and it went on a great deal longer than either of them had planned for, because she had one arm and was determined to pretend she had two.

She had him against the table with his good hand flat on the wood and his forearm out of the sling by the end, and she told him what she wanted in the flat operational voice she used for briefings, and then about a third of the way through she stopped being able to and made a noise in her throat instead and put her forehead on his shoulder and kept it there.

He got his good hand into her hair and she let him, which was the only thing either of them did that afternoon that was a concession.

"You are very warm," she said into his collar. "I thought you would be cold. Everybody in this is cold."

"That is what it is."

"Yes." She came up onto her knees. "That is exactly what it is, and I am going to think about it on a tram for a month."

She came first with her teeth on his shoulder and her good hand fisted in the front of his shirt, fast and not quiet, and she held him exactly where he was with one arm and did not let him hurry it, and he went over about four seconds after she had finished and put his forehead down on the table and left it there.

Then she got up and dressed one-handed in about ninety seconds and said that the nurse was next and then the form and then a very bad conversation with a man in the district office who wanted to know why eleven dwellings were not emptied.

She wrote it at nine that night in a requisitioned first-floor flat at number 11, with her arm in a sling and the district clerk asleep on a chair, and Lin Hao sat at the other end of the table and read nothing at all.

It took her eleven minutes. She did not ask him to go and she did not offer him anything to read, and at no point in the eleven minutes did she look up.

Failure unforecast. Officer present at time of failure. Took command. No person other than the officer present in the passage. Evacuation ordered, back stair 06.58, front passage 07.04.

Then four lines in small print at the foot, because the form has a box for injuries and she filled it in correctly and completely, and both the names in that box were hers and his.

Then she signed it, and countersigned the countersignature line herself because a countersignatory was required and there was not one, and sat looking at the two signatures.

"There," she said. "Now it is in my files. Not the division's. Mine. When it goes up on the 20th it will carry my hand twice, and there is not a person in Ostara who can take it out of my hand and put it somewhere else." She squared the pages. "Do not thank me. I have taken it off you so that you do not have to carry it, and if you thank me in this room I will put this arm through the lamp."

She was not asleep when he reached the stair.

"Six hours," Runa Sato said, to the wall. "You had six hours from the stair. You spent four of them on my table and one walking past a door on your own landing, and you have got a requisition, a mandate and an arm." She turned her head. "So it goes in the warders' sheet. You know it does, and you told me what it would cost me anyway, at seven in the morning, over a table with a lamp on it. Which one of those was the decision."

He stood on the landing in the dark for about a minute and let it be a decision.

He had spent four years refusing to be looked at, and every time it had cost somebody else more than it had cost him, and the only time he had ever chosen to be seen was the 3rd of this month in a corridor with ninety-one people in it, and ninety-one people had gone home and Emrys Vaz had got his rota back and five names had come off a board. So the answer to her question was not a side. It was a door with his name on it and thirty-one households on the other side of it, and he was not going to stand in a street at seven in the morning and call it an accident.

There was one document in Ostara that answered a question about a forced door and a book and two warders at three o'clock, and it was a statement by the subject, in his own hand, dated the 19th. He would write it tonight: that he had walked the arc on the 15th on purpose, that he had known the row before the 17th, that he had gone down that passage without order and without authority, and that the stair had been reported twice and forced twice and that he had not said so before it came down.

And he would say that the account standing against Cutler's Row came from him, and that every flat that came off it after that was his to answer for.

There was not another side and he had not been offered one, and he had gone into that row at six in the morning without being asked, because a door had been standing open and a woman had told him so.

He went down the stairs with his arm in a warder's belt.

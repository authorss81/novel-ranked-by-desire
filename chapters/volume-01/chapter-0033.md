# Solveig's Arithmetic

He went back to Bellhouse on the 13th with the answer he had worked out on the tram, and Marguerite Vale had the four books on the table with the carrier buckled and a clean sheet in front of her, and she had not got up.

"Say it standing there," she said. "Not sitting down. I have watched four men say no to me sitting down and three of them changed it in the corridor and I have to write down what was said in the room."

"I am not saying no to you because of the handling charge."

"Then say the rest of it."

Lin Hao put his hands on the back of the chair and did not sit down in it.

"I have nineteen people," he said. "Nineteen. And I have a thing that only works when I put my hand on them, and it does not work at a distance, and it does not work on paper, and it does not work through a wall or a counter or a court or a woman in a good coat in Bellhouse. That is the whole of what I am. It is nineteen hands and a number attached to each one."

"Your account is worth more than the Fourth Branch's floor."

"I know what it is worth. I have read your book." He did not look at it. "I have got a floor under me worth eight thousand one hundred and forty-one and a tolerance of three grains, and if I take your line I will still have the floor, because you said you would not touch the band, and the band is the only instrument in this city that has ever been on my side. And I will still have three grains."

Marguerite Vale's pen did not move.

"And the moment after that I stop being able to do the one thing I can do," Lin Hao said, "and I will have a cupboard with a number in it, and I will be a man who keeps a cupboard. In four years somebody will come with a paper and I will have to choose between a figure and a person, and I already know which way I will go, and so do you, which is why you have offered it to me and not to a bank."

She wrote for about eleven seconds and then read it back to herself and did not read it to him.

"That is a worse answer than I was expecting and a better one than I get," she said. "The second half of it is the half Rask will be interested in. The first half is the half you should have led with, and I am going to note that you led with the other one, because it is a man who has been thinking about a cupboard and not about what is in it."

She turned the sheet round and there were five lines on it and a time and a room and his name spelled out in full, and the last line said REFUSED, and under that she wrote the words he had just said about a cupboard, which she was entitled to, and initialled it.

"That goes in the book in an hour," she said. "Rask will read it. She reads everything in the first week of the month, which is why I made you say it in a room."

Lin Hao picked up the third book from the carrier before she could close it.

It was open where it had been open the day before. The page marker was in it and the column of thirty-one plate numbers was down the left and the valuation was on the right, and beneath the valuation there were four lines that were not part of the valuation at all, and they had been added later, in a different ink, in a smaller hand, and they were a breakdown.

MOVEMENT — CONTINUOUS.
SOURCE COUNT — NOT ESTABLISHED.
SETTLEMENT RISK — NIL ASSUMED.
FIRST ENTRY, STANDING ALONE — 400.

Four hundred grains, on their own line, with a note beside it in the small hand that said *one movement, one witness, not aggregated, carried at face.*

He had carried four hundred at a time. He had carried it for a month without knowing that was what he was doing. And four hundred grains on the 23rd at four in the afternoon in the rain, with two hundred and more people watching a woman put a plate down and pick it up, was the only number in the city that he had never spoken out loud to another human being.

"There's a woman," Marguerite Vale said. "At an interchange. She put something down and picked it up again and a clerk stamped it, and Rask has written it on a page of a book in a room four floors above a chandler's, and there is one line of that page that is more accurate than anything else in it."

Lin Hao put the book down with his hand still on it.

"That's not a valuation," he said.

"No. Somebody with real books put a valuation on the facing page, and then came back two days later and wrote down the four things she could not do. The fourth is the only sentence on that spread I would put money on, and it is one line, at the bottom."

Marguerite Vale stood up and took the book out from under his hand, which took two fingers and no force.

"I am not going to tell you her name. I am not going to tell you the district. I am going to tell you that a woman who keeps books for a living has worked out that you are carrying a movement that was never aggregated, and there is a version of the city in which that is a fact about nine other people and not about you, and she has written it down, and she left it in a book she does not lock."

"What does that give her."

"Four lines." Marguerite Vale buckled the carrier. "That is the entire value of what you have just cost yourself. Four lines in a woman's arithmetic that she has never shown to her chair."

Runa Sato was on the second floor of the Fourth Branch at half past four on the 13th, at the rail by the board, and she had been there long enough to have a folder under her arm and short enough to have read it standing up.

"Say it in a corridor if you want," she said. "We are the only two people in it."

"You were at Bellhouse."

"I was outside a chandler's at half past four on the 13th because a man who declares a reading in a corridor has eleven days of unobserved time left and I spend them." She took the folder out from under her arm. "Here is what happened while you were having a conversation. On the sixth, a Field file was served on you at this address. You have had it seven days. A served file is answered by the subject in writing, or by a named sponsor, inside ten days of service."

"I know how a served file works."

"Then you know the rest of it, which is that the answer goes into the file with your name on the top of it and the file goes to the division, and a file with an answer in it is a file about a situation." She shut it. "There is nothing in yours. There is no answer and there is no sponsor and there is nothing in eleven days that will put either one there, because a sponsor is a person with standing who puts their name next to yours, and you do not have one of those, and the woman who came to this building on the 11th did not offer to be one."

"She is a broker. She trades in favours. She wanted the account."

"She wanted the account and she was going to give you a line and take a charge on it, and either of those is a sponsor, and both of them were on this table at half past one on the 12th and both of them are gone." Runa Sato looked at the wall. "It is not my business what you decided in Bellhouse. It is my business that you decided it, and the division does not care what you decided, the division cares that there is nothing in the file."

"And what does an empty file do."

"It gets a mandate." She said it flatly. "A mandate is Field authority to execute without countersignature. At the moment every requisition that touches this district goes to the district office first and comes back with a man's name on it, and that man takes about a day and a half. From today they do not. They are executed on the officer's own hand."

"You could have told me on the 6th."

"I did tell you on the 6th, and you read it and said thank you and asked me about the register on the wall." She let a second go by. "Lin Hao. I am not going to be angry at you for this, and I have decided that in advance so that you can hear how unusual it is."

"There are two things a man in your position can do with a file served on him. He can get a sponsor, and a sponsor is a person who stands next to him. Or he can do nothing, and doing nothing is a category the division has a form for, because a division that cannot read a refusal has been told no by every quiet person in Ostara."

"So you have not refused anything. You have failed to answer, and the machine has read that as an agreement, and that is the least bad reading available to it, and it is going to do about nine thousand grains of work on the strength of it."

Somebody came up the stair behind them and stopped a metre off and went back down again without a word, and Runa Sato did not turn round.

"Do you know what a mandate means in Cordwain," she said. "Because that is where it will be used. It will not be used on you. A mandate is not for people. It is for buildings."

At ten past five a man came along the second-floor corridor of the Fourth Branch with a clipboard and a bundle of tally sheets and worked down the list of contracts from the top, and he was not a clerk and he was not a Field officer and he did not have a badge, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has done this in eleven buildings and has stopped expecting to be stopped.

Lin Hao was standing by the board with the ninety-one names off it and the single line in a clerk's hand that said no further action this quarter.

The man reached the fourth line from the bottom, which was the Fourth Branch's own water and cleaning contract, and he wrote four words on the tally sheet in the supply column and did not read them back.

SUPPLY — UNSETTLED — SUBJECT PRESENT.

Then he went on down the corridor to the stair with the clipboard under his arm and did not look at a single door he passed, and that was correct, because he had counted the words.

Lin Hao stood there with the board a foot in front of his face, and the line on the board said no further action this quarter, in a clerk's hand, with a date, and the man on the clipboard had said four words in the same building and the two documents were about him.

Whatever the machinery had been doing for nine years, it had started doing this at ten past five on the 13th, and it had done it in pencil, and there was not one person in the corridor to see it happen.

Emrys Vaz came up the back stair at six with the yard's stock docket under his arm, read the tally sheet as he passed the man putting it away, and went up two steps and came back down and stood at the rail with his hands at his sides in exactly the position they had been in on the 3rd.

He read the four words twice. Then he looked at Lin Hao and did not say one word, because he had understood that the first thing in the world he was going to be asked about this was whether he had known, and that he could not prove that he had not.

"He did not say anything," Lin Hao said.

"No," said Emrys Vaz. "He did not."

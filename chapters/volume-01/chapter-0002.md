# No Interval

There was a job to be done and Lin Hao did it, because there was always a job to be done, and because the alternative was to stand in a corridor holding a wrapped plate and be a man who was standing in a corridor.

He did the fourth washroom and then the fifth, and at twenty past eleven he found a reason to go up.

The reason was real. The linen store on the third floor had been giving out towels with a hole in them for a fortnight and he had been collecting them in a sack under the sink for a fortnight, and if a linen supervisor came round he would want the sack, because a sack of evidence is a thing a man gets told off for having and not for having. So he carried the sack up one flight, along a corridor he had cleaned four hundred times, and on the way he walked the length of it at the pace of a man carrying something.

Nobody walks a corridor at the pace of a man with nothing in his hands. That is the first thing the corridor teaches you.

The Bureau of Merit published its own table. It had to. A city that ranks two million people cannot afford to be asked, once a week, by anybody standing in a public place, what the numbers actually mean — so the numbers were nailed to a board on every landing above the counters, in brass on painted board, with the band, the weekly grains it took to hold the band, and a single line of plain text under each one describing what that band bought, in the flat administrative voice of somebody describing a staircase.

Lin Hao had walked past that board four hundred times and had read the top of it, the ranks, the way you read the name of a river you cross every day of your life.

5 — 8,000 — HELD ACCOUNTS. PRIVATE OFFICE. THE RIGHT TO BE ASKED BEFORE REQUISITIONED.

6 — 20,000 — SEALED FILINGS OPEN. WARRANTS. REQUISITION OF PROPERTY. COMPELLED TESTIMONY.

Six was a line on a board.

He had spent twenty-five years wanting a number that was not on that board. Nine. He had wanted it since he was nineteen and had not really known why, and the why had been standing in a public hall three years ago with his band on the wall behind him, saying that the number was wrong, and gone by morning. Nine was the number that made the file open. That was all nine had ever been. It was a boy's arithmetic about a door.

Six was different, and he understood that in about four seconds standing in front of a brass plaque.

Six was the first rank on that board that had a verb in it.

Six was *sealed filings open*. Six was the first number in Ostara that had ever been able to open a thing that had been closed. Not all of them — nine, above, in a row on its own, with a line under it about any file in the city. But six was where the wall started, and between four and six there were two bands and a washroom and a plate and eleven weeks of proving to nobody that he could climb one.

So the want moved, that morning, in a corridor, while he was carrying a sack of bad towels.

He had never before in his life had a number tell him what to do next. It was an appalling feeling. He stood there a moment longer than the towels required.

The Correction Office kept a petition desk at every branch, which nobody expected and which was technically a sub-office of an arm of the Bureau three buildings away. The desk was a trestle table with a queue rail in front of it, staffed by one clerk who had been there two years and had the specific resentment of somebody who had discovered that she was the person in the whole Fourth Branch that the general public was legally permitted to shout at.

Lin Hao went and stood at the end of the rail where a person waiting has to stand, with the sack of towels on the floor by his foot.

"Is it open?"

"It's open till two." She did not look up. "You've got no forms."

"I'm not here for a form."

"Nobody's here for a form."

He looked at the wall behind her, which is what a man does when he wants a conversation to keep going and does not want to be the one steering it. Petitions were pinned up there in a long column of carbon copies going back years, because the Correction Office was required to publish what it had refused as well as what it had taken, on the theory that a refusal with a number on it can be appealed and a refusal with a name on it can only be feared.

Four years back, third sheet down on the left-hand column, in the small tight print of a copy that had been folded and unfolded a great many times:

PETITION 4118. I. DUARTE (RANK 2, AUDIT) — RE SCORE OF A DEPENDENT SIBLING, D. DUARTE. REFUSED. NO INSTRUMENT OF RECORD. THE ACT DESCRIBED IS NOT SCORED UNDER ANY CLASSIFICATION HEADING AVAILABLE TO THIS OFFICE.

Lin Hao read it three times.

The machine's terms were all there and they were all the terms he had been living inside for four years without knowing they had names. *No instrument of record.* The act described — somebody had done a kind thing, in a doorway, where nobody was standing with a form. There was no heading for it. The office could not score what it could not see, and what it could not see it was obliged, by twenty years of honest administration, to say so in writing and file it.

She had gone downstairs that day to be scored down, and then she had tried to fix it with paper, and the paper had gone back with a sentence on it that was, in its way, the most accurate sentence anybody in Ostara had ever written about her.

He had a sack of bad towels and a woman shouting at him from the far end of the queue rail and eleven weeks between him and a band he could reach.

Faber would be at that counter until eleven and then he would go home to whatever it was he went home to, and none of any of this would ever touch him, because Faber's own account had been settled for years and would be settled again next Thursday, and the entire apparatus of the city would pass over the head of a man with a comfort of mind that Lin Hao had only ever read about in other people's faces.

The Classification Wing intake door was at the end of the ground-floor corridor, and there was nothing remarkable about it, which was the thing he had noticed at half past five that morning and had not been able to put down.

He walked past it twice on the way to the linen store and twice on the way back, and on the fourth pass he stopped on the same side of the corridor a person stops when they are waiting for a lift, and he said, out loud, to the corridor:

"Excuse me."

A messenger came round the corner with a sealed tube under his arm — one of the ones who carried documents between floors and were not supposed to stop for anything.

"Sorry. Is that door locked?"

"Which door."

"The classification one."

The messenger looked at the door, and then, with the particular expression of a man who has been asked something he has no reason to be asked, at Lin Hao.

"It's locked," he said. "It's locked to you and it's locked to me. You get buzzed in and you get buzzed out and you don't leave your coat in the corridor, that's the only rule down there I've ever been told about."

"Who comes out of it."

"People getting taken for a reading."

"What does that mean?"

The messenger shifted the tube.

"It means they go down and they come up later," he said. "It happens about nine times a week in this building and if you're waiting for somebody who's gone down, then you'll be sat in that corridor till two in the morning, and if you're not waiting for anybody then you'd do well to keep walking."

He went, and his boots went up the stair, and the tube under his arm was sealed with the wax that Lin Hao had been mopping wax off the floor outside that door for four years.

That was the last useful thing anybody said to him that day, and he had arranged to receive it, and the arranging was the part he would think about afterwards.

A man with a purpose can be asked things. That was the whole of the lesson, and he had learned it late, in a corridor, standing beside a sack of towels with a real grievance about linen to justify his presence in it.

He had spent four years refusing to ask a single question because a question from the second floor is a question with a shape, and the shape of it goes into somebody's impression of him, and somebody's impression of him was the only thing he had.

So he asked one, out loud, at the mouth of the corridor where the pigeonholes were, to a counter clerk who did not have to stop:

"If an unsettled reading doesn't band by Friday, what happens."

The clerk stopped.

She was young enough that the branch had made her the whole of the public's official grievance, and she had been at the Fourth Branch long enough to have a first name for Lin Hao, which he had never given her, and she looked at him now the way a person looks at a man who has just said a word out loud in a building where words are money.

"You've had one," she said.

"Yes."

"Monday?"

"Today."

"Right." She put the file down, which Lin Hao noticed and did not understand the significance of until much later, when it was explained to him that a counter clerk who puts a file down during a conversation is a counter clerk who intends to remember the conversation. "Then you'll be on the Thursday list, and they'll see you on the Thursday or the Friday depending which room you land in, and there's nothing whatever happens in between."

"And if they do."

"They keep you."

"Keep me where."

"Down." She said it without any weight at all, the way you say a word you have said eleven hundred times.

And then she seemed to hear herself, and she looked at him again, and this time it was not with suspicion. It was with the particular discomfort of a decent person who has just been bored and has noticed that she was bored at something that was not boring.

"Nobody gets taken for a reading because they did anything. That's the thing people get wrong. They come out of here convinced they got put in a room for something they did."

"It isn't a punishment. It's an unsettled account. And an unsettled account has to be settled somewhere, and the only place the instruments can do that is down there with somebody who has got the time."

"How long is somebody down there."

"Long enough." She picked the file back up. "Take the plate up. Don't unpack it. And if you do end up going, take a book, because they give you a room with nothing in it and they do not have a shop."

He took the plate up.

He cleaned the fifth washroom and the sixth, and he did the row of basins on the second floor for the second time, and he stood in the middle of a room with no window holding a wrapped disc and worked out that he had made a mistake with it.

He had thought the machine was broken. He had thought it for about twenty minutes, standing in front of a brass plaque, and in that twenty minutes he had let the whole thing settle into the shape he already knew how to carry, which was: *something is wrong with me and I will manage it quietly, and it will never once come up in a conversation with a human being.* And he had been carrying it for three weeks already, since Wednesday morning, and it had not once in that time made him do a single thing.

The sack of towels was still by the petition rail. There were four people in the queue behind it. There was a corridor between the third-floor landing and the ground-floor corridor, and a room at the end of the second one, and a plaque on the wall of the first one that told him, in plain administrative text, exactly what his life would consist of if he went through the door and the reading stayed the way it was.

He was a man with a supply of nothing and a gap in him where a number used to be.

Six would open a sealed filing.

Eleven weeks. Nine thousand grains. One attested deed at a time, in public, by people who would write it down and stand in front of witnesses and say his name out loud while they did it, which was the exact opposite of everything he had ever been good at, and which he had refused to do for twenty-five years on the grounds that a man who competes openly is stealing from somebody smaller.

He looked at the stairs.

Then he went down them, all the way, past the counters and out past the pigeonholes, because he had decided — deliberately, with the whole weight of the decision on his own two feet — to be a man who asked questions where people could hear him, and it was too late in the day to start and stop in the same hour.

He had got as far as the Classification Wing door, and he had his hand up, and he was about to do the stupidest thing he had done in four years, when the voice came from behind him.

"Lin Hao."

He turned round.

There was a woman standing at the far end of the corridor with a folder under her arm and her hat still on, in auditor's grey, which is a grey nothing else in Ostara is allowed to be, and she had clearly been standing there for some time because there was a patch on the wall behind her where her shoulder had been keeping the damp out.

She had not been waiting for him. That was the thing that went into him and stayed there.

She had been standing against that wall for her own reasons, and he had walked into a corridor she happened to be in, and she had known his name anyway.

"I don't know you," he said.

"No," she agreed. "I don't expect you to. I've got your file."

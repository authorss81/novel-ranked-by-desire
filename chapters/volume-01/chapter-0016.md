# What a Band Costs

The Exchange at five o'clock was marble and steam and a queue that moved like a tram, and he took his place in it with the wet slip folded in his inside pocket and learned what four hundred grains bought.

It bought a hearing this week instead of in March. It bought an hour of a scarce machine with a name on the door. It bought a choice of district and a bed in the Vestry clinic with a name on the door instead of a bench in the hall. The clerk showed him the permissions board the way she showed everybody, patiently, one line at a time, and every line on it was time, and time was the only currency the Exchange had ever accepted.

He read the waiting column twice. Beside him in the queue a woman in a district coat was reading it too and had been for some time, and when he looked over she said, without being asked, "Eleven weeks for the machine. Four weeks for the room. I've been in this queue eleven years."

She went in and came out in under two minutes, which meant she had come to be told she could not afford it.

She stood on the marble a moment with the rain on her coat and folded the slip into three, and then she said, to nobody in particular and with perfect control, "Right," and went down the steps. He had heard that voice before in the mouths of people being reasonable about something that was destroying them, and he had never got used to it, and four years of looking through had not dulled it at all.

The line went on. In front of him a woman with her hair gone white was holding a slip she had carried since the previous winter and had come down here anyway.

"Seven months," the ticket woman said, taking the slip without reading it. "I can offer you a date in March. I cannot offer you anything before March."

"I'll take March."

"You took February last year."

"February was better," the woman said, and laughed, and it was not a good laugh, and the ticket woman laughed with her anyway, because that is what they do.

Lin Hao looked at the floor. He had cleaned this building's washrooms for four years and had never once stood on this side of the marble, and the entire machinery of his life sat in the seven months that woman had just been handed.

He read the waiting column again while she was still going down the steps.

Three thousand five hundred and eighty-eight left to run. Four hundred at a time. That was nine of them, on nine wet evenings, in front of nine sets of two hundred people, and every one of them would cost three people a cold minute a mile off, and three times nine was twenty-seven, and one of the twenty-seven was a person who kept track.

The band was not a reward. It was a queue. Rank five was the rank at which you stopped waiting, and the price of stopping waiting was that other people waited longer, and the woman in the district coat was going to be standing in that marble hall in eleven weeks being told the same thing by a different clerk.

Emrys Vaz's rota line was deleted on the twenty-second. His shift went. He would be re-posted to the sub-levels, where there is no witness box and no rail and no stamp, and he is seventeen, and he has never in his life been asked to attest anything, and nobody in the building is going to ask him.

He took a day off.

The supervisor at the Fourth Branch stared at him for a long moment, and then went and got the day book, and then came back and asked him what he wanted to put in the reason column, and Lin Hao said leave it blank, and the supervisor left it blank, because a supervisor who asks why a man took a day off in a draw-down week opens something he cannot close.

He did it because the arithmetic only closed while he was standing still. He needed one full day in which he was not earning anything at all, to find out what the plan looked like from outside it.

It looked like nine evenings, and it looked like a man learning to be looked at, and it looked exactly like what somebody else had done three years ago in a better coat.

So he spent the day off in the arcade, because the day off was not going to spend itself.

He took a ticket at the yards gate at ten, paid for it himself, rode the arc end to end and came back, and then he sat down on the wall at the bread stall at eleven and stayed there until four with his hands in his lap, doing nothing, earning nothing, while the doors of the Vestry went on being held by strangers on every side of him.

The figure came up anyway.

Not much, and not by anything he did — by a hundred things other people did within reach of him, and the machine took its cut of every one of them and put it in him while he sat there with his hands empty, and at two in the afternoon he had to turn his face away and breathe through it.

That was the discovery, and it ruined the day along with the plan. He had imagined a day off as an absence. The machine had no such thing in it. It had proximity in it, and there was no way to stop, and the only variable left to him was never the giving.

It was the going where the doors were.

He understood what that meant by four o'clock and sat with it a long time before he got up. It meant there was no version of his life in which he became poor and nobody else became cold. It meant every tram he rode to was a tram somebody else's week came out short at the far end. It meant the hoard had a floor under it, and the floor was the whole of Ostara, and a man who wanted to be a better person could not get there by doing less.

Ines came to his rooms that night and sat on his one chair and looked at his one knife and his one plate, and said, "You're doing what he did."

"Who."

"Your brother." She did not soften it and she had not come to soften it. "Converting unrecorded acts into visible ones, on purpose, in public, with a crowd. Lin, he did it because somebody told him the count was wrong. You're doing it because a band is nine evenings away and you have never once in your life been allowed to move first."

He could not deny it. The delight at the interchange had been real and hotter than anything he had felt on the tram, and the horror had been real too, and both of them were still in him, side by side, and the worst of them was that he would do it again.

He said so. He looked at her while he said it, because she had earned the true version and because there was nobody else in Ostara he could say it to.

"I would do it again," he said. "That is what I want you to have heard me say."

Ines sat very still with her hands on her knees.

"Now I know what I am sitting next to," she said.

"Yes."

"Do you know what the difference is between you and your brother?"

"No."

"He did it with a reason he could write down." She said it without any cruelty at all, which was why it landed. "He had a figure that was wrong and he could put it on a table and prove it and name the man who had made it wrong, and if they came for him he had a document. You'll have nine evenings and a crowd and no document, and in two years somebody with a seal is going to ask you where it came from and you are going to have to say a number."

She stood up and put her coat on and picked up her file.

"Good. Now you know what you are doing, and you can decide whether you can live in the building afterwards." She got to the door and stopped. "I'm not going to tell you not to. I've read the file. I have been where you are standing except that I had a seal, and the seal is the only reason anybody would have listened to me at all."

She went down the stairs without her file, which in eleven months she had never done, and he sat in the middle of the floor with his hands on his knees in exactly the position she had been sitting in.

He wanted rank six. He wanted it the way he had wanted the box, shamefully and correctly, because six was the first number that opened anything, and because the ninety-one names on the draw-down list were going to be read whether he moved or not.

He had the stairs to himself on the way up and he went slowly, because the thing he had just agreed to do was nine evenings long and every one of them had somebody's ankle in it.

Tobias Renn watched him from the filing room the next morning, bored and precise, counting. Tobias had worked out what Lin Hao was doing on wet evenings at the interchange, and had worked out why anybody with an account that would not settle was standing out there at all, and Tobias had a life that consisted entirely of one counter and the racks behind it, and he loved it in a way that made him dangerous to be around.

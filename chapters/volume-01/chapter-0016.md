# What a Band Costs

The Exchange at five o'clock was marble and steam and a queue that moved like a tram, and Lin Hao stood in it with his slip in his pocket and learned what four hundred grains bought.

It bought a hearing this week instead of in March. It bought an hour of a scarce machine with a name on the door. It bought a choice of district and a bed in the Vestry clinic with a name on the door instead of a bench in the hall. The clerk showed him the permissions board the way she showed everybody, patiently, and every line on it was time.

He did the arithmetic on the way back to the Branch, walking past the Board without looking, with rain coming down the Reading in sheets.

Four more identical deeds at four hundred grains, on four wet evenings, in front of four sets of two hundred people. Every one of them cost three people a cold minute. The ninth of those was a person who kept track, and a pattern of nine in one season could be triangulated to a source, and the source had an address, and the address was him.

The band was not a reward. It was a queue. Rank five was the rank at which you stopped waiting, and the price of stopping waiting was that other people waited longer.

He understood the rule perfectly. Emrys Vaz's rota line was deleted on the twenty-second, and his shift went, and he would be re-posted to the sub-levels, and there was no witness box at the bottom of a yard.

He took a day off. Nobody at the Fourth Branch had ever taken a day off in a draw-down, and the supervisor stared at him and then wrote it down because there was no form to refuse it on. He did it because the arithmetic only closed while he was standing still, and because he needed one day in which nobody watched him earn.

Ines came to his rooms that night and sat on his one chair and looked at his one knife and his one plate, and said, "You're doing what he did."

"Who."

"Your brother. Converting unrecorded acts into visible ones, on purpose, in public." She was not accusing him. That was worse. "You caught yourself doing it and you can't stop and you don't want to stop."

He could not deny it. The delight at the interchange had been real, hotter than anything he had felt on the tram, and the horror had been real too, and both of them were still in him, side by side, and the most frightening thing in the batch was that he would do it again.

He wanted rank six. He wanted it the way he had wanted the box, shamefully and correctly, because six was the first number that opened anything, and because the ninety-one names on the draw-down list were going to be read whether he moved or not.

Tobias Renn watched him from the filing room the next morning, bored and precise, counting. Tobias had worked out what Lin Hao was doing on wet evenings at the interchange and had worked out why a man with no settled account was standing there, and Tobias loved the branch the way only a man with nowhere else to go could love it.

# Afterward, Ines

"Say the number again."

He said it again. Nineteen grains, out of a figure with no rate behind it, on the Ferrier tram at four o'clock on a Wednesday, into the shoulder of a man called Halvard Rees who splices wire out of the Cordwain yards and had been four days from doing something stupid about a fall he had taken four years before.

Ines Duarte listened to it with her hands flat on the table.

"Nineteen," she said.

"Yes."

"That is not a large number."

"No."

"That is the smallest number in this entire business that could still be somebody's entire afternoon." She sat back. "Lin. A reading is measured in grains per week, and an interval is what a person is *doing*. You have put a figure of three thousand four hundred into a man in a coat and taken out exactly nineteen grains, and the man's shoulder has gone quiet, and the plate in your coat has gone down by nineteen, and you want me to believe you do not know what you are carrying."

"I know what it's doing. I don't know what it is."

"Then we will get there. I am going to get there." She pushed the folder to one side and put her elbows on the table. "Tell me what it costs. Not what it is. What it costs, because that is the question I am going to have to be able to answer in four years' time and I would rather have it tonight."

"A person feels it," Lin Hao said. "Cold. A second, up to four. In the hand, or the chest, or an old injury." He stopped. "That's it. That's the cost."

"And the person who feels it — do they know?"

"They think they were cold."

She looked at him for a moment and then said: "Then you have been doing something that cannot be made to work and cannot be stopped, and the instrument that started this city has no reading for it."

"A plate reads a figure and a rate." She held up two fingers. "You have got a figure. A rate is a measurement of *how often you are the one who does it*, and a man who is the one who does it without ever being recorded has no rate in him at all, because the only way to get one would be for him to stop."

"And there are people in this city who can do what you can do, and every one of them has been put in a room with no window. That is not because of what they can do. It is because of what it looks like on a plate."

She put her hand flat on the table, level with the folder, and did not touch it. "A figure with no rate behind it is a figure the instruments cannot place, and a thing the instruments cannot place is a thing somebody eventually has to write down. You have not even noticed. You have not once in four years let anybody find out what you are. It is the single most disciplined thing I have ever seen in a person who has no idea he is doing it."

"I have a rule. I don't touch anybody."

"How long has that lasted?"

"Seven days."

She laughed — one syllable, entirely without humour — and shook her head, and then she stopped laughing and put her hand over her mouth, and Lin Hao watched a woman who held fourteen years of other people's paperwork in her head have something come apart in her face very quietly, in a room four feet by nine feet on a street nobody chose.

"Ding," she said, into her hand.

"What?"

"My sister." She took her hand down.

"Four years ago. She came in at eight hundred grains, and the week before she had eleven hundred, and I have never been able to make that arithmetic work. Because she did not stop helping people. She helped people all her life. She helped people on the way to work. She helped people in doorways at seven in the morning where there is nobody to see a doorway at seven in the morning."

She was looking straight at him.

"She took somebody's child up the long stair at the Vestry. Carried it. Nine steps. There is a lamp on the third landing and it is out, so she did it by feel, and she put the child on her own shoulder, and she got to the top and sat down on the step and put her head in her hands for about a minute because of what it had cost her."

"She should have been scored for that."

Ines's voice was completely level. "Instead she lost three hundred grains in a single week, and there was no instrument of record, and I filed a petition, and the petition was refused in four days, and the sentence on it is the one you read out to me a quarter of an hour ago."

"And I have been a rank-two auditor for fourteen years, and I did not take it to a hearing, because hearings are for rank five and above. And I have been carrying that sentence around with me every day since the April it was written in, and I have not once been able to put it down."

She stopped.

Lin Hao said: "I've got some of that."

Ines Duarte looked at him.

"I don't know how much," he said, "and I don't know whose it is, and it isn't mine to give away, and I am telling you that it exists and I am telling you that it settles where it's aimed, because that is what it did on the tram and it did not have to know anything about him."

"You could put a hand on me and give me my sister's morning back."

"Yes."

"And what happens to me."

"You feel it cold," he said. "In the hand, or in the chest, or an old injury. And then it is not there any more, and you will be — " He stopped, because the word he had been about to say was the wrong one and it was the wrong one because he had watched it happen on a tram and had been sick about it since. "You will feel finished. Like you've come out the other side of something you were surviving. You will not be stronger. You will be *finished*, and that is not the same, and I am telling you now so that it can't be a surprise later."

Ines Duarte stood up.

She took her coat off.

She hung it on the back of the chair where anyone coming in the room would see it, and she did that deliberately, and she said: "The other room. The bed's in the other room and the lamp's in this one and I'll bring the lamp. Yes. Do it. Right now, before I think about it, because if I think about it for four seconds I will start being an auditor about it and I have been an auditor about it for four years and I would like twenty minutes in which I am not."

Then she said, "And you don't have to be careful with me. I have been extremely careful for four years. I would like to find out what the opposite of careful feels like and I have not got anybody else to try it with."

It took him a while to understand that she was better at this than he was, and about the same again to stop being embarrassed by it.

She was not rushed and she was not cold. She was the opposite of cold; she was hot and precise and entirely present, and she kept stopping to say things — not speeches, short flat statements, delivered while her hands were busy — in a voice he had last heard reading a refusal to the branch clerk for four years.

"Lamp on the table. Turn it down. No, down. There."

"Your hands are extraordinary," she said. "You know they are, don't you. You've never once been told that."

"No."

"Four years. You clean things." She got his collar open and put her fingers in at the back of his neck and pulled his head back off her shoulder. "You have the hands of a man who has been taking things off surfaces that other people are standing on. That is not a compliment. It is a description of the job, and I am telling you now that it is going to be the best part of tonight, and that it is going to be entirely irrelevant to anything either of us does afterwards."

She got his shirt open the rest of the way and put it down the back of the chair, and then she stopped and looked at him.

"Stop standing there being decent about it."

"I don't know—"

"You don't know because nobody has ever told you, and because you have spent four years in a building where being touched was a thing that happened to other people." She put a hand flat on his chest and pushed him, not hard, just enough to put him back a half step and get the two of them the right way round. "Nobody is going to be harmed by you enjoying this. I have read the file. There is nothing on it. Go on."

He got her blouse open.

There was ink on the side of her right hand, three fingers deep, the kind that does not come out, and she watched him find it and did not explain it. Her ribs came up under his palm and she was thinner than she looked sitting down in a coat, and he put his hand flat and felt her breathe and did not say anything about it, and she took his wrist and moved his hand four inches to the left without comment, and he understood about an hour later that he had been wrong and that she had decided not to teach him.

She was on the bed in the other room and the lamp was on the chest of drawers and it threw everything in warm and low, and she pulled him down onto her and put her hand flat on the back of his head.

"Here," she said. "Now."

He put his hand on her wrist, over the place where a rank-two auditor's pulse is, and the light in her face changed and her breath went out of her in one long piece.

It came out of him and it went into her and it was nothing like nineteen grains. He could feel it going — not as a push but as a *draining*, a steady going down out of his own chest, into her, for a long time — and Ines Duarte made a noise she had not planned and turned her face into the pillow and held on to him with both arms.

Her shoulder went first. Then something in her back. Then, a long way in, something in her chest that let go so hard that she shook from the heels up and made a sound he would hear in his own head at four in the morning for the rest of his life, and she was saying a name, not calling anybody, just saying it — *Ding* — over and over into the mattress, with her whole body under it.

Then she was quiet.

And she was warm, and she was heavy, and she was finished.

"Not like that," she said, some time later, into the pillow. "It isn't like that. I had assumed it was like that. I had assumed for four years that it would feel like being *told* something." She laughed, and it broke in the middle. "It's like being let go of. It's like being let go of."

"Sorry—"

"Don't. Don't be sorry. Don't you dare be sorry." She rolled over and got a hand on the back of his neck and pulled him down. "That is the first apology anybody has made to me for anything in four years and I did not come here for it."

Then she pushed him back off her and sat up and looked down at him, and the professional thing came back into her face without the rest of the professional woman coming with it.

"Right," she said. "That is done, and it is not the reason I said yes, and we are going to be extremely clear about that, because in about four years I am going to have to audit you, and I cannot do that if there is any part of tonight in it that I cannot put down."

He said, "I don't understand."

"You will." She pushed her hair back off her face. "You have given me something, and I have had something given to me, and those are two entirely different transactions. One of them happens in a bed. The other one happens to people in doorways. And I am not going to lie here and let you believe you got this off me."

"I'm not going to think that."

"I know. That is why I am saying it now, while I am still able to be said to."

She lay back down and pulled him with her by the wrist — the same wrist, deliberately — and put her other hand flat on the back of his neck.

"You have no idea what you are. That is the whole of what I have established about you in a fortnight, and it has taken me four years to be able to put it into a single sentence. So I am going to enjoy this, and I am going to enjoy it properly, and when it is over we are going to talk about a reading, and one of those two things is going to be the reason I remember the rest of my life, and I do not presently know which."

She took her time.

That was the part he had not expected and never forgot. She was not quick about anything, and she did not perform any part of it, and when she was enjoying herself she was completely unselfconscious about it, which was worse for him than any amount of composure, because there was nothing to be calm behind.

She put her hand on the back of his thigh and pulled him to her and said, "Come here. Closer. You are treating this like a floor."

"I am doing it exactly—"

"You are doing it exactly the way you would do a floor." She got her heels up behind him and hooked them, and pulled, and made a sound in her own throat that she did not arrange. "There. *There.* Do that again."

He did.

Her back came up off the bed and she turned her head into the pillow and made another sound, and her hand came up and found his and put it flat against her sternum, and held it there, and left it there — and he understood that she was showing him the exact place it had happened, and she was not going to explain that either.

"Higher," she said. "That's — yes. Stay there."

He stayed there. He stayed there longer than he thought he could and she let him and did not talk through it and did not let go of his hand, and neither of them looked at the lamp.

Afterwards she lay back with her chest going and put her hand over her own eyes for a moment, and then took it away and looked at him with the flat and entirely undefended face of a woman who has just been somewhere she has not been for four years.

"Again," she said.

"Are you—"

"I know what I am doing. I am not a girl and I have thought about this considerably longer than you have, and I am not going to be asked twice." She pulled him down. "And this time I want to watch you."

He watched her watch him, and it was the most exposed he had ever been in his life, and she did not let him look away, and at some point in the middle of it she put her hand over his eyes the way she had said she would.

He understood why. It was because she did not want him to see her face doing that, and she was going to be his colleague for four years, and she was not going to let him have it. He put his hand over her hand and kept it there.

They went again after that, slower, and she talked to him the whole way through in the low flat voice — not dirty, not tender either, just continuous, telling him what she felt and where and in what order.

Which was a thing no one had ever done for him. He understood about eleven minutes later that it was also the single greatest act of kindness anybody had ever committed against his own dignity.

She was asleep inside ten minutes of stopping.

Not the exhausted collapse of a woman who had been worn out. She went down into it like a person putting a coat on, and she was gone.

He did not sleep. He lay in the narrow bed in the other room of a second-floor flat on Lantern Row with the rain going on the wall eleven feet off the window and a lamp on a chest of drawers and a woman breathing beside him, and he watched the ceiling, and put together one at a time and without any hurry the things that had happened to him since Wednesday morning.

The relief had gone into her. That was real. He had felt it go and he had felt what it had cost him and he had watched a body put down a four-month weight, and the whole of the argument he had made to himself on the tram — that he would rather go the rest of his life with the whole of it sitting in his chest than hear it a second time — turned out to be worth nothing whatever in a room with the lamp down.

She had asked. She had been told what it cost. She had said yes out loud in front of him, and then she had hung her coat on the chair where anybody coming in would see it, and then she had spent a good part of an hour making sure he understood that those were two different things.

And then she had told him what she felt, out loud, in order, for forty minutes, so that he would not spend the rest of his life holding it.

He had not known that was available.

She came up out of the sleep around eleven and lay there a moment, and then she turned and put her head on his chest and her hand flat on his ribs, and said:

"In fourteen years I have audited four thousand one hundred and nine people," she said, "and there is not one sentence in any of those files about you."

"Sorry?"

"About any of them. Not a word about how it *is*." Her thumb moved once against his ribs. "Four thousand one hundred and nine people, and every single one of them got to a file because something they did had a witness or a machine in it. None of them would ever have got there otherwise. And the whole city thinks it is a fair instrument, and my sister is on the fourth floor of a building in the Vestry with a bad shoulder and a light bill she cannot pay because of a staircase."

Then she said: "Lin. I want to say something to you and I want to say it once, and I would like you to believe it, because it is true and you have no reason to believe anything anybody has said to you in a very long time."

"Okay."

"You have never once in your life been the thing that happened to somebody." She said it perfectly evenly, the way she read a refusal out of a wall. "Not your brother. Not your mother. Not the branch, not the Board, not the eighty people who walk past a man with a mop every morning and would not know his name if you told them."

"You have been the floor under the whole of it for twenty-five years, and there is not one person alive who has ever come up out of it and said *that was you*."

She let that sit.

"That is the first thing anybody has said to me in three years," Lin Hao said, "that I did not have to check."

"I know. I could tell." Her hand stopped moving on his ribs. "Oh."

"What?"

Her hand had gone still.

She sat up on one elbow in a camp bed four feet wide, with her hair down and the lamp going, and she looked at him and then past him at the wall, the way she had done in her chair two hours ago, and her whole face changed into the professional stillness that Lin Hao was beginning to understand was not a performance but an accommodation — the face of a woman making room for a record.

"There's more of you," she said, "than there is."

"What?"

"When you—" She stopped and started again, which she had never done all night. "When you were inside me I could feel it. Not you. Something else. It is like standing in a crowd and being able to tell how many people are in it without counting them." She put her hand on her own sternum, which was a professional gesture and a frightened one. "I have held four thousand one hundred and nine files in my head. I know exactly what a document feels like when I hold it. And that was not a document. That was people."

The room went completely quiet.

"How many," Lin Hao said.

"I counted nine."

"Nineteen grains."

"Nineteen grains." She said it slowly. "Out of *nine people*. Lin, that is not a supply. That is nine separate human beings who each put a small amount of something into you. And it has been happening for months, and it has been happening to you because you are the one person in that building nobody looks at, and that is not an accident. That is your *life*. You have built a life where nothing can find you, and the nothing has been feeding you ever since you worked out how to be invisible."

"And it moves when I touch them."

"It moves when you touch them, and only them, and it goes in as *relief*, which means it goes to whoever is owed something and it goes there because you put your hand on them." She stopped. "It is an account."

"Yes."

"And how many do you have."

"Fourteen lines."

"And two of them have been spent twice." She said it like a number she had been given. "A turned line is a person who has felt the cold more than once. It is not a complaint. It is not a condition. It is a *pattern*, and a pattern is reportable, and a reportable pattern opens a file on the person it was spent out of, and that person has never done anything except be near you."

"I know."

"You do not know." Ines Duarte was already out of the bed and had her coat on, unbuttoned, and she was reaching for the shelf where the folder was, and she stopped with her hand in the air and turned round. "You know the count. You have not done the multiplication. Two turned out of fourteen is not a scandal. Two turned out of fourteen is a man who has been in a building for seven days."

"Nine names in a single night is not a supply. It is a *source*, and a source has a location, and a location has an address, and the address has a woman in it who is writing it down."

She went to the shelf.

She pulled down a black notebook — the size an auditor keeps, ruled, with a rubber band round it, and the rubber band had perished and was stretched — and opened it on the table in the other room and turned pages with a thumb that knew exactly where it was going.

"Nine of them she could name," she said. "She went down the column the way she went down a list of settled accounts, fast, and read off nine names, and then she stopped."

"What."

"Not one of the nine." Ines turned the notebook round so he could see it. It was the Thursday list, forty names, in her own hand, with the branch stamps down the side. "She has audited every unsettled account this city has produced for four years. She knows what a name looks like when it is on a list. None of the nine was on this one."

She tapped the page.

"And that is not because your people are hiding. It is because you cannot get a file for a person with no reading to dispute and no instrument behind them, because they are not *doing* anything the city can see — and that is exactly why you are full of them. The Bureau has no category for them. They have no dispute and no instrument and no weekly plate, because a plate is issued when you are born and issued to an address, and an unrecorded good deed has no address, so the person it went to has none either."

She had a pencil out. She had it uncapped before he noticed she had moved.

"Give me the next one."

"I can't. It's not a list. It's a—"

"It's nine and you have the eighth." She was not looking at him. "Give me the ninth name and I will tell you, in about four seconds, whether she has a file anywhere in this building. And if she has not, that is the most dangerous fact either of us has tonight, because it means there is a class of person in Ostara who can be spent out of and cannot be complained about and cannot be traced and cannot be protected. I have spent fourteen years establishing that the one thing this city cannot do is leave a person unrecorded. There is one. And I have been sitting on top of it for four years."

Lin Hao looked at the notebook.

"There isn't a ninth," he said. "Not tonight. There isn't a tenth one. It's—"

"It is not a list of the last ten," Ines said, looking up, and her voice went cold and flat in a way he had not heard on it before. "It is a ledger. You said so yourself twenty minutes ago. It keeps every one of them."

He shut his mouth.

She wrote a single word at the top of a clean page and turned the notebook so he could read it upside down.

She had written: BRIGHT.

"Eleven times a year this city produces a reading that will not band," she said, "and I have audited nine of them, and I know the name and the address and the brother of every single one. And not one of those names is the name that just came out of your chest in this room."

She wrote nothing else. She just left the word sitting there in the middle of a blank page, in pencil, in a room four feet by nine feet on a street nobody chose.

It was not a name he knew either.

It was, in her mouth, the most ordinary sound in the world — two ordinary syllables, said by a woman in her thirties in a lamp-lit room on a Friday night, the kind of name you hear every hour of every day in every room in Ostara. A woman in a kitchen. A woman on a rota. A woman who had a plate somewhere or did not have one at all and had never once in her life been asked by anybody which.

And it went into him like a stone into water.

# What the Clinic Files

The Vestry clinic keeps a paper record, which is unusual, and the reason it keeps a paper record is that nobody funds it.

The Bureau's free clinics file by headcount. The Vestry clinics file on paper because a headcount file cannot hold a person's temperature at four in the morning and a headcount file is what the Bureau reads, and the Bureau has never once asked to see a Vestry paper file, because asking would mean acknowledging that there is a Vestry.

Tessa Okonkwo ran the largest one. She had a treatment room with four beds, a kitchen, a bench outside for people waiting to be not-ill, and a cold store off the back that held bodies at two degrees on a shelf with a ledger nobody was supposed to keep.

Lin Hao had been in the building before, once, in the small hours, and had asked a question at four in the morning and been told yes.

She was at the front desk when he came in on the 31st and she did not pretend to remember him and did not pretend not to.

"Sit," she said. "You are the worst thing that has ever happened to my intake sheet and I have been waiting three weeks for you to come back."

"You knew I'd come back."

"I knew somebody would." She put a chair out with her foot. "Sit. You look like a man who has been walking."

He had. Three days. Forty-one minutes each way instead of eleven on the tram, because on the 29th he had stood at the top of a lift on the Vestry side and done the last sum he had available to him and it had come out flat and final and it was that: the figure kept moving wherever he stood and the faces stopped coming, and nineteen was all he had, and two of the nineteen were on a printed sheet with a time against them.

He had given up the only place in Ostara where the account filled fastest, and it had bought him a thing he did not yet know the name of, and his hands were shaking by the time he got there, which was new, and which was not the shaking from Ch 20.

"Show me the charts," he said.

She brought four. Not the patient files — the intake index, the one-page summary each person gets on arrival, which is what the clinic has ever been able to defend.

"They are all correct," she said. "Read them."

He read them. Four intake sheets, four sets of numbers, four ordinary arrivals in the last month with unremarkable complaints, and everything on them matched the bodies that had come through the door.

"Then show me the ones that do not."

"There are none," Tessa said. "That is the problem, and it has been the problem for two years, and you are the first person I have said that to, so I am going to say it slowly." She put her finger on the fourth sheet.

"This man came in on the ninth with a chest complaint and a plate reading of two thousand one hundred and forty. He left on the eleventh with a plate reading of two thousand one hundred and forty. His rate never moved. His chart is perfect." She tapped the paper. "But on the ninth he came in grey to the lips and could not get up the two steps to the bench, and on the eleventh he walked out carrying a case."

Lin Hao looked at the sheet. "What happened to him?"

"Nothing happened to him. He was relieved of something." She turned the sheet over and there was nothing on the back. "That is my word for it and I have never written it down. A body arrives carrying a debt of exhaustion that has three days left to run and it walks out without it. The chart agrees. The chest does not agree with the chest from the ninth, and the colour does not agree with the ninth, and nothing in this building did it, because I have watched every instrument in here and none of them fired."

"You think I did it."

"I think somebody has been walking through my waiting room for two years handing out things nobody signed for," she said, "and I think they never once came in at the front, and I think there are about two hundred people in this quarter who have been relieved of something and have told nobody, because telling nobody is what you do when a thing happens that cannot have happened."

He put the sheet down. "How many?"

"That's the part I am not going to answer in a treatment room at two in the afternoon." She went round the desk and unlocked a door with a key off a nail. "Come and see."

The cold store was eight feet by eleven and colder than the yard that morning. There was a shelf along the back wall with a blanket over something long and flat, and there was a rack of clipboards on the left, and the clipboards were full of intake sheets in her own handwriting.

She took the first one off and gave it to him, and then the second, and he read them standing up in a cold room in his coat.

Nine charts. Nine intake sheets. Every single one an ordinary arrival, every single one internally consistent, every single one describing a body on arrival and a body on departure that did not belong to the same week of the same life.

"And this," she said, and pulled the tenth off the rack, "is the worst of them."

He read it twice. It was the worst of the ten and it had nothing to do with a number.

Both boxes said the same complaint, the same duration and the same figure, and they were four hours apart on the same page. The only thing that differed was the free-text box at the foot of each, and in the arrival one Tessa had written on a cart, could not rise unaided, cyanotic, and in the departure one she had written nothing at all, because a woman who had arrived on a cart and left carrying her own coat needed no note.

"I did not write both of those," she said, and put it flat on the table. "I wrote the arrival one. The departure one is mine too, four hours later, in the same hand, and between the two of them I have entered into a document a woman who walked out of here unable to walk in."

She put the tenth chart back on the rack and shut the rack.

"That is the box," she said. "Ten charts, and I have never counted them in front of another human being, and it has never been in the clinic's own ledger, because the clinic's ledger is the paper the Bureau could ask to see. It is in the cold store, in my hand, on clipboards, and if I am ill for one winter somebody will find them or burn them."

Lin Hao looked at her for a moment. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because you asked me at four in the morning three weeks ago whether a body could be found in the records that did not match the body in front of me, and I said yes, and you went away, and you have been gone twenty-two days." She put her back against the shelf. "I have decided that you are either the man who did this, or the man who can stop it, and either way you are the only person in Ostara who has a reason on earth to care about a body that does not match its chart. Now. What have you come for."

He had come for a thing he could not say out loud in that form, so he said it in a different form, which was still true.

"I can put something into a person. It costs them a cold minute and it gives them back what they owe, and it makes them finished, and it does nothing anybody can see." He watched her face while he said it. "It has gone into about nineteen people in four years and I have never been able to choose between them, and two of them are on a list that is going to send them to a room on Thursday, and if I spend on either of them I mark them, and if I don't spend on them I watch them go."

"That is a poor problem."

"It is the whole of my problem."

"No," Tessa said. "It is the whole of your problem as you have described it, and you have described it with one arm behind your back. You have not said whether you can aim it."

The room was very quiet. There was a shelf with a blanket on it behind him and a rack with ten clipboards on it and a woman looking at him with an expression he had not seen directed at him before, which was professional.

"I don't know," he said.

"Then why are you here."

"Because I put my hand on a man called Colm Deveraux in a corridor six weeks ago to shove a door," he said, "and he went from two thousand one hundred and sixty-eight to three thousand one hundred and sixty-nine in three days, and his rate did not move at all, and he has not spoken to me since and he has a locked office."

Tessa Okonkwo did not move. She did not go white and she did not laugh and she did not do any of the three things he had been braced for.

"You put merit in a figure instead of in a body," she said.

"Yes."

"And the body got nothing."

"The body got nothing," Lin Hao said, "and the figure went up by a thousand and a hundred and one, and I have had six weeks to work out what that means, and what it means is that it is not a question of whether it goes in. It is a question of *aim*."

He put his hand flat on the shelf.

"I want to try it on purpose," he said. "Not on Colm, not by accident, in a room, with somebody who has said yes to it, and I want to see whether the figure takes it cleanly when I am the one doing the aiming. Because if it does, then I have a way to put merit where it is counted, and the two people on that list do not have to be in a room on Thursday."

Tessa picked up a clipboard, looked at it, and put it down again without reading it.

"No," she said. "Not in the ward."

"Tessa—"

"Not in the ward." She said it the way Ines moved people's hands off documents. "The ward has four beds and eleven people in it this afternoon and a kitchen queue out the back, and you have never done this on purpose in your life, and I am not going to discover what happens when you miss by a foot with nine strangers in the room. No. Not the ward. Not the treatment room. Not the waiting room with the bench outside."

"Where, then."

She looked at the door she had unlocked, and at the shelf, and at the rack of ten clipboards in a room at two degrees.

"The store," she said. "After hours. Nine o'clock, when the last of the kitchen queue has gone and before the morning intake lands. It is cold, it is locked, it is mine, and there will be two of us in it and nobody else on the floor. If you are going to be the first person in Ostara who does this on purpose then you will do it in the one room in this building where nothing belongs to anybody else."

"And the patient."

"There is a patient." She went to the treatment-room door and looked through it. "Wenna Ost came in on the twenty-eighth with a loading-bay crush across her chest and a scar on her forearm that was scored against her four years ago by a supervisor who wanted the shift count to come out. She has been in and out of this room nine times and I have told her the truth nine times, which is that I cannot re-score a scar and that the only instrument in Ostara that can re-score a scar is a hearing, and that hearings are rank five and above, and that she is rank two."

Lin Hao looked up. "She said yes?"

"I have not asked her anything yet and I am not going to." Tessa came back and sat on the bench under the window with her arms folded. "What I am going to do is ask her whether she will be present in a room at nine o'clock at night with the two of us, and be told exactly what I am going to tell her, and be given the right to say no and the right to stop it."

She looked at him.

"And she is to be told that if she says yes she will be the first person in this city who has agreed to be on the receiving end of something nobody has ever done on purpose. If she says yes, then you have your patient. If she says no, then you do this to me, or not at all, and I would think a great deal better of you if it were not at all."

She looked at him steadily.

"Ten o'clock," she said. "Not nine. I want an hour to get the room right, and I want to be able to stop it, and I want you to understand that I am not frightened of you and I am not impressed by you and I have been a doctor in the Vestry for eleven years and I know precisely how badly this can go, and I am allowing it because in two years I have filled a rack with bodies that got better and no explanation, and I have run out of other rooms to think in."

He wanted to say thank her. He did not say it, because she had not thanked him for anything in twenty-two days and neither had anybody else, and he understood by then that in this city gratitude was a receipt and receipts got filed.

"Nine forty," she said. "Bring nothing. Don't bring your plate, don't bring a notebook, don't bring that leaf of paper in your coat with the name on it, and if you write that boy's name down in this building I will take the clipboards out of the cold store and you will never find out where."

He went out into the yard at half past two with his hands worse than when he came in, and the Vestry was full of afternoon light and the long stair up from the yard was where a woman had once carried a child nine steps in the dark and lost three hundred grains in a week, and he stood at the bottom of it and did the arithmetic he had done for nineteen days.

He had a room, a time, and a woman who was going to hand him one of the few people in Ostara who could say no and mean it.

And in four hours he was going to try to aim the one thing he had, and he had no idea at all whether he was doing it in order to help two people on a list, or because it was the first door in nineteen days that he had found which was not somebody's back.

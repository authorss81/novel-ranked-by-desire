# Field Order

The Branch was quiet the next morning in the way a building is quiet before a list is read, and Lin Hao went to the Exchange counter with four thousand six hundred and twelve grains on his plate and a plan that did not close.

The floor was doing what a floor does in the week before a list, which is early, quiet, and extremely polite. Somebody had pinned the list itself to the board by the door in the second column of print: ninety-one names, a tick column, and nothing anywhere explaining what the tick was for. His name was on it, thirty-eighth down. So was Emrys Vaz's, at the very bottom, under a heading for staff who do not hold a plate.

Three thousand three hundred and eighty-eight short, and nine evenings of a plan that needed a body to be wrong at the kerb, and he had got as far as one.

He could not get to eight thousand without another interchange. One interchange was a deed. Two was a habit. Three was a man standing at the same kerb waiting for someone to step wrong, and Tobias Renn had already put those two facts together and had not gone home.

Tobias did not do it to destroy him. Tobias did it because four evenings at an interchange from a plate that would not settle is a discrepancy on its face, and the deputy warden was the branch, and Tobias had never in his life worked anywhere but here and had no idea that a man could want a thing the building did not offer.

He went to the one person in the building who could act on it, with his file and his counts, and he did it bored and precise and entirely honestly.

Lin Hao learned this later. At the counter that morning he learned only the figure.

The clerk pressed his plate to the socket and read it, and then took it off and read it again, which nobody at that counter had done for him in four years, and then she called her supervisor with one finger without looking away from the plate.

The supervisor was a heavy woman with grey hair called Vole, and she read it a third time, and then she turned the plate over and looked at the back of it for a while, and then she said, "Come with me, and bring that."

He went with her into a room with a table and two chairs and a wall of pigeonholes, and she shut the door, and sat down, and put the plate flat on the table between them.

"There is an aggregate on you," she said. "There has been one for four years."

"I don't know what that is."

"You wouldn't." She turned it so he could see it. "Every plate in this building carries two figures. One is yours. One is the floor's. The floor's figure is what the Fourth Branch produces, week on week, out of four thousand staff, and it is pooled and it is held, and it is released in slices to anybody in the building who does a thing that somebody else stands in front of and attests."

She reached over and pulled a pigeonhole out of the wall without looking at it, which told him she had done this a number of times.

"That is June's. That is the last quarter. If you were any kind of use to me I would have shown you the whole row." She pushed it back. "Ninety-one of those boxes are open on this floor this week because of the draw-down. Every one of them has a name on the front and a number on the spine, and every one of those names belongs to somebody who cleaned a floor or carried a file or carried a tray for four years and never once stood in a box."

She drew a line down the middle of a scrap of paper.

"Your side moved four hundred on the twenty-third, in public, in front of two hundred witnesses, attested and stamped. The moment that landed, the machine had to look at you properly, because it cannot leave a witnessed deed sitting on a plate that has not been reconciled. So it went and looked at the other side."

"And the other side was three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine."

"It was."

He looked at the paper.

"The Branch carried that for four years," Vole said, "because there was nothing on your plate to justify spending it. Doors held. Trays carried. Six floors of washrooms cleaned by one man while the office filled up around him. It is in the aggregate. It has been in the aggregate the whole time. It was not withheld from you out of malice. It was withheld because there was no instrument that could say who had earned it, and you are not a name on this building's books in any capacity other than a mop."

Three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine grains, four years of doors and trays and washrooms, settled to him at once because somebody had finally stood in a box and written his name down.

His figure crossed eight thousand mid-week on the strength of a single public deed and an aggregate the branch had carried for four years. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one.

The band appeared. White tile, rank five. Held accounts. A private office. The right to be asked before requisitioned. It was the first tile that had ever moved, and it was the first time a number had been the answer to a question rather than the measure of a distance from one.

He felt elation, exactly once, for about ninety seconds, in a corridor with the tile slip in his hand, grinning like Ola.

Two people on the second floor said good morning to him in a different voice and did not know why they had, and both of them had watched him mop that landing for four years without ever once being asked to think about him.

He went down and out into the Reading and stood at the rail. The board was dry and the painters had knocked off, and he found his own tile without difficulty — low down, black, still four — because the Board publishes once a week and the day had not come round, and he had wanted it now.

Then he understood that the white card in his coat was the entire of the law, and that nothing else about him had changed at all.

He went back up to the second floor to tell somebody, and there was nobody he could tell, and that was the second thing that happened to him that morning and the second thing that followed from the first. He had an account that had jumped three thousand five hundred in one morning and there were four thousand people in that building and every single one of them would have to be told by somebody else, on a Thursday, from a board in the open.

Then it went, because on the same plate, in the same morning, in the second column where a branch records what it wants done to itself, the deputy warden's office had written a request for a Field observation, and Lin Hao knew what a Field observation was because he had cleaned the corridor they come in through.

The Bureau sold permissions at the Exchange under a schedule of fees and waiting times, ranked by band, administered by clerks who stamped triplicate and kept a carbon. A rank-five man did not wait. That was the whole machinery, three counters and a rail and a printed sheet, and it existed so nine thousand people would believe waiting was fair.

He stood there and did the only sum he had, and it came out the same as every other sum he had done in a month. Four hundred grains at an interchange, minus three cold minutes a distance away. Nine evenings of that against ninety-one people on a list by a door, and a fortnight he had just paid for with the only deadline anyone had ever given him.

Ditta Halse waited longest in that arrangement. She had been on the Vestry benches since six on the Friday, sleeping there twice, working a night shift and coming back, and no permission on that board would bring her brother up the stair any sooner.

He took the band and did not celebrate. He put the slip away and went up to the fourth floor and stood a moment outside the locked supply office, remembering a thousand and one grains and a frightened face, and then he went down, because Cassius Rhee wanted to see him.

On the second landing Tobias Renn came the other way with a bundle of files under his arm, and stopped, and looked at the tile slip in Lin Hao's hand where it was not quite out of sight.

Tobias did not say anything for a moment. He had been ready — he had a face for it, and a sentence, and Lin Hao could see both of them waiting in his mouth — and instead Tobias looked at the slip, and at Lin Hao, and then went on up the stairs with his files held higher than they needed to be.

At the turn he stopped and said it to the wall. "Eighty-two of them settle. Nine do not. I have had all nine on a sheet since the day the list went up and I file them Friday, and you are number four on it, and I did not put you there."

"And you filed it anyway."

"I filed it correctly," Tobias said, "which is the same thing and is not the same thing, and if you want me to have done the other one you will have to explain to me what the other one is." He went up. His voice came down once more, very flat. "You have got about six weeks before it matters. That is what I have got for you. Do not waste it."

Lin Hao stood on the landing and let him go, because there was nothing available to say to a man's back about a thing he had done honestly and for the right reason, and because the six weeks was the only thing anybody in that building had ever handed him that was not on a list.

He had forty minutes. He had never once in four years been asked to sit down in the Fourth Branch.

# Field Order

The Branch was quiet the next morning in the way a building is quiet before a list is read, and Lin Hao went to the Exchange counter with four thousand six hundred and twelve grains on his plate and a plan that did not close.

He could not get to eight thousand without another interchange. One interchange was a deed. Two was a habit. Three was a man standing at the same kerb waiting for someone to step wrong, and Tobias Renn had already put the two facts together.

Tobias did not do it to destroy him. Tobias did it because a man with no settled account standing at an interchange four evenings in a row was a discrepancy, and Tobias loved the branch and the deputy warden was the branch. He went to the one person in the building who could act on it, with his file and his counts, and he did it bored and precise and entirely honestly.

Lin Hao learned this later. At the counter that morning he learned only the figure.

The clerk pressed his plate to the socket, read it twice, called her supervisor, and the supervisor read it a third time. The four-year office aggregate the Branch had carried for him, split by a formula nobody published, had been held pending a witnessed deed. His interchange attestation forced a re-read. The held share applied in one movement.

Three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine grains, four years of doors held and trays carried and washrooms cleaned while the office filled, settled to him at once because somebody had finally stood in a box and written his name down.

His figure crossed eight thousand mid-week on the strength of a single public deed and an aggregate the branch had carried for four years. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one.

The band appeared. White tile, rank five. Held accounts. A private office. The right to be asked before requisitioned. The first time in his life a tile had moved and the first time in his life a number had been the answer to a question rather than a distance from one.

He felt elation, exactly once, for about ninety seconds, in a corridor with the tile slip in his hand, grinning like Ola. Then it went, because on the same plate the deputy warden's office had written a request for a Field observation, and Lin Hao knew what a Field observation was because he had cleaned the corridor they came in through.

The Bureau sold permissions at the Exchange under a schedule of fees and waiting times, ranked by band, administered by clerks who stamped triplicate. A rank-five man did not wait. That was the whole machinery, three counters and a rail, and it existed so nine thousand people would believe waiting was fair.

Ditta Halse waited longest in that arrangement. She had been on the Vestry benches since six on the Friday, sleeping there twice, working a night shift and coming back, and no permission on that board would bring her brother up the stair any sooner.

[Ledger. Nineteen lines. Three turned.]

He took the band and did not celebrate. He put the slip in his pocket and went up to the fourth floor and stood a moment outside the locked supply office, remembering a thousand and one grains and a frightened face, and then he went down because Cassius Rhee wanted to see him.

He had forty minutes. He had never once in four years been asked to sit down in the Fourth Branch.

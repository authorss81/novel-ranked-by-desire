# The Draw-down

The morning of the 29th the Fourth Branch cleaned itself, and not one of the ninety-one came in early.

Lin Hao had worked in the building four years and had never seen it do this. At seven the floors were already being done by people who usually came at eight, and by nine the second-floor landing smelled of the solvent they used on plates and there was a queue along the counter rail twenty deep, and the queue was not for the plate office. It was for the board by the door, where a printed sheet with ninety-one names and a column of times had been standing for a day and had overnight acquired a small crowd.

He counted the people who looked at the board and then went away having looked at it. Eleven in twenty minutes. Every one of them had just been told a date and a time by a machine in a corridor, and had come in early to stand in front of it and let the fact settle, which he understood in his body because he had done exactly it in a corridor on the 27th with a tile slip in his hand.

He went down to the ground floor and did the thing he had decided on the stairs, which was to walk the floor with the list.

The list was on a clipboard on the second-floor rail, copied out by a woman from the second column and left there for anybody who wanted it, which was almost certainly an error. He read it standing up, the way he had read staff notices for four years, and he found two names in ninety-one that he already knew the shape of.

Emrys Vaz was last again, under the heading for staff who hold no plate. His line had a time and no room against it, because the sub-levels read on the ground floor and the sheet had been printed on the second.

Eleven names above Emrys's were on the fourth floor, and one of them was a name he had been reading for four years off a rota in the depot, and Lin Hao stood on the landing with his hand on the rail and understood what he was looking at.

He had nineteen lines and nineteen people in four years, and the Bureau had independently assembled ninety-one, and the two lists met.

That was the whole of the draw-down. Not the Branch's numbers, not the aggregate, not a shortage. Ninety-one people whose entire occupation was being the person nobody looked at, all of them in one building, put on a list because the machine could not find a settled figure on any of them, and a man with no settled figure is not a person to the Bureau. He is an exception, and exceptions get a room.

Ottoline Barret was forty-second down and had held a door on the fourth-floor stair for eleven years. He had felt her there every morning at seven, for the same two seconds he felt everybody who holds a door, and her name was on a printed sheet with a time against it.

He went and got his coat.

The arithmetic was short and he did it standing at the top of the Vestry lift because that was where the wind came from. He had tried, over nineteen days, every version of standing somewhere different and being a different sort of man, and the number had gone up every time he rode the long way, and it had gone up on a day off against a wall. That was the mechanic, and the nineteen he had were the nineteen he was stuck with.

And two of the nineteen were on the list.

He stood in the wind and understood the trap, and it was a clean one, and there was no way round it from where he was standing. He could not help a person on the ninety-one. The relief was invisible; the cold was not. The cold was one to four seconds in a hand or a chest, and a man on a list who feels a cold and then settles on a reading is a man with an anomaly, and the Bureau's threshold for an anomaly is three episodes in a season, and this Branch had a draw-down in five days.

Spending on somebody listed marks them.

He had been telling himself for four years that the cost of this was a cold minute, one at a time, borne by strangers who would shrug it off and think they had caught a draught. It had never once mentioned the part where he did it on purpose to somebody he had already marked by accident.

He got the lift down. Nadia Ferrous was in it. "You're the Fourth Branch one," she said, which was not a thing she knew and which she had worked out from nothing on her own. He said yes. She said, "My sister's kitchen lady says they put a hundred names up on a wall." He said ninety-one. She said, "Ninety-one's not a hundred," and the doors closed and she took it down four floors with her hands.

Lantern Row at four in the afternoon was light through a window above a chandler's shop and a woman on the landing with her coat already off, which Ines never did.

"Give it to me in writing," she said.

That was the whole greeting, and it took him four minutes to understand she had been waiting two days for him to arrive and had decided not to be gracious about it.

"I could have taken it off you in the street in four seconds," Ines said, "because I have read a file of yours the Fourth Branch has never been sent. I chose not to. Waiting was a choice and it was mine."

"All right."

"You asked a signal operator for something," she said, "and you got it, and you have not told me what either half of that was."

He got the notebook out. He had bought it for eleven cents in a shop on Ferrier Street, and he had bought two, and he wrote it out on the second while she watched, because she would have checked the paper and he had known that when he chose the shop.

"I asked a woman who feels the city's current for the locations and the dates of everyone in Ostara who can feel a merit spend," he said. "I asked her for the turned lines. A turned line is a person who felt a cold and did not put it back. There are three of them in four years."

"Three."

"Three, and I have two of them already on the ninety-one."

Ines stopped moving. She was standing by the window with her hands on the back of the chair and she put them there deliberately, which she did when she was about to need them to be steady.

"Name them."

"No."

"No," she agreed. "No, and correct. Go on."

"What I was given was four years of the book," he said. "All of it. Every cold minute she has felt and inferred, by car, by hour, by street, and a column at the back she ruled herself with three ticks in it and nothing written beside them."

He stopped there, and it was the truthful place to stop, and Ines Duarte, who had read four hundred files and could read a man the way he read a room, looked at him for four full seconds and then said, very evenly:

"And what did it cost."

And there it was. The thing that was actually uncomfortable, and it was not the book and it was not the three names. He had sat in a cab in the rain at eleven at night and agreed to a price out loud, before anything started, because she had made him do it and because that is the kind of man he is.

"It cost me myself," he said. "She set the terms and I said yes out loud before she took her coat off. That is the price of the book, I paid it in a cab, and I have not mentioned it to you in two days. You are going to want me to be sorry for that and I am not going to be, and you are also going to want me to have refused, and I would not have."

"You told me a thing bought in the rain at eleven at night is worth nothing."

"I did."

"And you bought it in the rain at eleven at night." She let go of the chair and got the second notebook off the table and read his handwriting standing up, all of it, and he let her, because that is what the paper was for.

When she had finished she read the last four lines again and then sat down, which she had not intended to do.

"You are telling me a woman gave you her four-year count of people feeling cold," she said, "and that the transaction had a physical component disclosed before it happened because she insisted on disclosure, and that neither of you has any protection in it, and that the price was set out loud."

"Yes."

"And you are telling me this on the day the Branch has put ninety-one names on a wall."

"Yes."

Ines squared the notebook to the edge of the table with two fingers.

"I have spent two days imagining being angry about a book," she said. "It turns out I am not angry about the book. I am angry that a woman on a tram has been keeping a four-year count for nothing, and has been called a condition for it, and that you were the first person in four years to ask her for it." She looked up. "The part I am angry about is that the use was available and it was mine to ask for first, and I did not ask, because I am rank two and could not have moved a page of it, and you could."

He had not thought of that. In nineteen days he had not once thought of it.

"The name at the back of her column," he said. "The one on a rota in this building."

"You should have it. Not today. In a month, when you are not holding anything at me."

She wrote a date on the corner of the page and initialed it, which he recognised, because he had watched her do it to other people's evidence for four hundred files.

"I am not going to put that name in anything you give me," she said. "Say it out loud tonight while there are two of us in it. Tomorrow it is an office and I am an auditor and it is a line on a sheet, and the reason I will not do it is that I am protecting the man who put a supply on a board this morning, and I would like that written somewhere that is not my memory."

He wrote it out, signed it, and gave it to her, and she put it in her coat against her chest without looking at it.

"And the ninety-one," she said.

"Two of them are mine. Emrys Vaz, and a woman on the fourth floor called Ottoline Barret who has held a stair door for eleven years."

"Ottoline came in twice in four years and declined a hearing both times because she could not afford the wait." She put her hand over her eyes for exactly one second. "What did she decline it for?"

"She never said. I have never spoken to her."

"You have never spoken to her, and you can feel her in a stair every morning at seven for eleven years, and you have been in that stair at seven every morning for four of them."

He stood in the window light on Lantern Row and felt the last of his wishful thinking go out of him, and it went quietly, the way a held breath goes.

He knew what he wanted. Not rank. The ninety-one, by name, and specifically the thirty-eighth on that sheet. He would have taken the whole fortnight's worth of somebody else's fear to get it, and he was not going to get it, and on the third of next month a man on the second floor of the Fourth Branch would find out whatever the machine had for him.

She walked him to the stairs and at the door she said the only useful thing she said all afternoon.

"There is nothing I can do with ninety-one names," she said. "It is not a legal question and it is not a document question. It is arithmetic. Those people are invisible, the machine cannot score invisible, and it will keep producing lists until somebody legible takes the whole thing onto himself." She looked at him on the step. "The only instrument that works on a floor like that is a person standing in it saying a true thing out loud. I can tell you exactly why nobody ever does it, and it is not cowardice."

"What is it."

"By the time they have worked out that they have to, they have already spent the fortnight." She shut the door gently.

He went down three flights and stopped on the half-landing, because on the second-floor rail there was a person.

"Tobias Renn," he said.

Tobias had a folder under his arm and his sleeves were buttoned at the wrist, which Lin Hao had never seen him do, and he was standing by the clipboard list of ninety-one names with a pencil in his hand.

"I've got a question," Tobias said. "I've been working on it since the 27th and I'm asking now because I've decided that if I write it down first I'll write it wrong." He tapped the folder. "I file everything that arrives in this branch. I have got a category for a man who cannot settle. The category has a box, and the box is four lines long, and one of the four lines is *probable cause*."

"Tobias—"

"I have not got a probable cause. I have got an order, and a file with a shape in it, and a Field report saying the same shape is in four other files in other buildings, and every one of those four names a man or a woman who has been in a position to do kind things where nobody was looking. The box wants four lines and I have got nothing to put in it." He held the folder out flat. "So I would like you to tell me what you are."

He stood there with his pencil out and waited.

Lin Hao had nineteen lines in his head and two of them were on the list behind him, and he had an arc and a cupboard and a book and a woman with a pen, and he had about four seconds in which to decide what a filing clerk is worth.

He said, "I don't know."

"Write that down," Tobias said, "and I will file it, and you will have said it on the record and I will have done my job and one of us will be able to sleep. Say it into the page. Not to me — into the page, so that when this goes up, somebody above both of us has your handwriting on it and not just mine."

And Lin Hao understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that Tobias was not torturing him.

Tobias was asking to be told.

And every true sentence available to him contained the word he could not afford, and he stood on the second-floor landing of a Bureau building and understood for the first time in his life that the reason he had survived four years by being unreadable was that nobody had ever once asked him a question with an honest short answer.

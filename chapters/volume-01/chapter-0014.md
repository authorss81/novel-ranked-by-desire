# Two Witnesses

The day of the repaint he went looking for a witness who would stand in a box in front of him and be written down.

The Board was being painted tile by tile in the Reading Square, and the Thursday crowd stood four deep in the wet watching names go up.

The painters worked from the top down on a scaffold with a bucket on each plank, and the names went up in rank order, and a man in a wet hat read each one out as it was set so that the crowd could find it without reading the whole board, which was a kindness and also an enormous power, and Lin Hao stood at the far rail and thought about the man in the hat for longer than he thought about his own name.

The board had four thousand tiles on it. His was low down and black and it said LIN HAO and RANK 4 and a figure he already knew, and every Thursday for four years he had ridden past it on the tram without turning his head, and today the man in the hat read a name four rows above it that he knew — Dereck Hain, fourth floor, counter, worked there eleven years — and the crowd made a noise, and Hain's tile came off the board and a white one went up in its place.

A woman in a green coat came along the rail looking for her son's name and found it and put her thumb on it and said something to him that he did not hear, because he had turned away by then.

He had wanted to be looked at since the bread stall, and it turned out that what he wanted was a white tile and a man in a wet hat, and that standing four feet from it in the rain was not enough to hurt.

He went to the Vestry instead. The arcade car sat at the terminus with steam coming off its roof and the conductor checking the blind, and Nadia Ferrous was on the step with her hands in her sleeves laughing at a passenger who had tried to pay with a button.

She would do it instantly. That was the problem. She said so before he had finished asking, loudly, for nothing, with both hands up, and the whole terminus heard her.

"I'll stand in your box, Lin. I'll stand in it all day. I'll tell them about the cold, I'll tell them about the lift, I'll tell them about the third one—"

"No," he said, gently, because there was no other way to say it to her. "Not you."

She was hurt for about four seconds and then she wasn't, because she wanted him, and she said so the way other people said good morning, and being refused once did not move her. She put her chin up and turned back to her passenger and carried on, and he stood on the step beside her while the conductor blew his whistle and the car went out with her on it, and she did not look back at him, and that was worse than if she had.

Using her would have put her name next to his on a filed attestation in the week of the draw-down. A filed name could be read. A read name could be taken.

He did the other three that week and they were all the same, and being all the same was the answer.

Ines could do it and could not. He asked her that night in her rooms with the lamp down, and she told him plainly why, in the voice she used for filings she knew would stand up in an office for twenty years.

"An auditor attesting a drawing account is the end of my seal," she said. "Not the end of my job. The end of my seal. I file true things. Every figure I have put up in eleven years goes into a room and is checked against me personally. If I stand in that box and stand next to you, everything I have filed about your reading becomes a lie they can prove, because you will be in the box and I will have put my name to it."

"You'd be cleared. There's no rule against two people—"

"There is a rule against a person who certifies the thing they are inside of." She turned the lamp down further. "I want to. I want it the way you wanted the box. I am not going to be able to have it, and I would like you to notice that I said that out loud, because I would not have said it out loud a year ago."

He nodded. He had known before he asked. Asking had been the form of it, the way standing in the box alone had been the form of it.

Ola could not. She had no plate and could hold none — a premises registration for a kitchen and a name on a lease, which opened a cupboard and nothing else — and the city could not record the one person genuinely willing.

He went back to her about it on the third day, because he could not leave it alone, and she put the book down on the wall between them and listened to the whole of it with her mouth slightly open.

"So I can't do anything," she said. "I can't even be bad at it. That's the joke, Lin. I can hold a door and write it in a book and that is the entire extent of what I am allowed to do with my whole life."

"You don't need a plate to stand there. Only the man in the square does. You could stand beside me and say out loud that you saw it happen."

"And they'd write *Bright*." She said it with a lot of satisfaction. "And about forty minutes later somebody would come and ask me why the name's on there, and I would have to say it was me, and I would have to say how I know you, and then I am a woman who was standing next to a man who cannot show a settled account, and I am finished as a person who works with children." She shrugged. "Mord would have to change his crossword. It is not a small thing."

He sat with that a while. She was, he was finding out at a rate that was embarrassing to him, not the simple case. Every time he thought he had her measured she would turn over and be a person with a flat and a flatmate and a rota and a sister who put her on it.

Association is a public act. Two people can be seen together and read as one, and rank is a social fact before it is a number. He had run out of people who would stand next to him, and he had done that to himself deliberately over four years, and now he needed the one thing he had refused to have.

He felt lonely then, specifically, not generally. Not the loneliness of an empty room. The loneliness of a man who clears every room he enters and now needs one to stay.

So he did not use Nadia. He went and found the one person in Ostara who sees forty thousand people moving and therefore knows which witnesses are about to do something.

Beatrix Halm drove the arcade car to the yards four days a week. Her roster was pinned in the depot office at the yards on a board with a glass over it, and he read it while standing just inside the door with a cloth over his shoulder, which is where cleaners stand and which is the only place in that building where a man may look at a board without being asked why.

It took him four minutes to write the pattern down on the back of his hand with a pencil and then wash it off at the standpipe. End of shift, eleven at night, the yards. Rain most evenings. A gate unlit on the north side, and a lamp out over it since the winter, which he checked by walking round to look at it.

There was a shed man doing the rounds with a lamp and a man in the office who did not look up. Neither of them asked him anything, and he was not asked anything, and he walked out through a gate that was not guarded, and stood in the street in the rain and found that his heart was going.

That was not an ally yet. It was an address.

Nadia found him on the way out of the depot and caught his sleeve, still cheerful, still loud, with a paper bag in her other hand.

"You'll come back, though," she said. "When you've got your box full. You'll tell me."

"I will," he said, and meant to, and knew the promise was worth nothing until he had something to put in the box.

"Here." She put the paper bag into his hands. It was a roll, warm, wrapped in a cloth. "Ola said you'd come at the end of the week. It's not the end of the week. Tell her it's not the end of the week."

He carried it home in both hands the whole length of Ferrier Street, which was four minutes he could not account for, and did not eat it until Thursday, and it went to pieces by then.

That evening Ola showed him the back of her book, shy for the first time since he met her, and there were four years of somebody else's handwriting copied out in pencil, dates and car numbers and the word headache underlined so often the paper had gone soft.

Beatrix Halm did not write the cold minutes down because she was frightened. She wrote them down because not one person in her own division had ever asked her what she feels, and she had been keeping the real figures in a book for four years under the only heading anybody would give her, which was a headache.

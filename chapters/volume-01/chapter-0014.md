# Two Witnesses

The day of the repaint he went looking for a witness who would stand in a box in front of him and be written down.

The Board was repainted that morning, tile by tile, with the Thursday crowd standing in the Reading Square watching names go up, and he did not go and look at it. He went to the Vestry, where the arcade car sat at the terminus with steam coming off its roof, and found Nadia Ferrous on the step with her hands in her sleeves, laughing with a passenger.

She would do it instantly. That was the problem. She said so before he finished asking, loudly, for nothing, with both hands up, and the whole terminus heard her.

"I'll stand in your box, Lin. I'll stand in it all day. I'll tell them about the cold, I'll tell them about the lift, I'll tell them about the third one—"

"No," he said, gently, because there was no other way to say it to her. "Not you."

She was hurt for about four seconds and then she wasn't, because she was twenty-three and wanted him and said so the way other people said good morning, and being refused once did not move her. But using her would have put her name next to his on a filed attestation in the week of the draw-down, and a filed name could be read, and a read name could be taken.

Ines could do it and could not. He asked her that night in her rooms, with the lamp down, and she told him plainly why.

"An auditor attesting a drawing account is the end of my seal," she said. "I file true things. If I stand in that box, everything I've filed about you becomes a lie they can prove. I want to. I can't."

He nodded. He had known before he asked. Asking had been the form of it, the way standing in the box alone had been the form of it.

Ola could not. She had no plate and could hold none, a premises registration and nothing else, and the city could not record the one person genuinely willing. That was the arithmetic of the whole volume in one sentence, and he sat with it a long time.

Association is a public act. Two people can be seen together and read as one, and rank is a social fact before it is a number. He had run out of people who would stand next to him, and he had done that to himself deliberately over four years, and now he needed the one thing he had refused to have.

He felt lonely then, specifically, not generally. Not the loneliness of an empty room. The loneliness of a man who cleared every room he entered and now needed one to stay.

So he did not use Nadia. He went to the one person in Ostara who sees forty thousand people moving and therefore knows which witnesses are about to do something.

Beatrix Halm drove the arcade car to the yards four days a week. Her roster was pinned in the depot office, and he copied it without asking, standing where cleaners were allowed to stand. End of shift, eleven at night, the yards. That was not an ally yet. It was an address.

Nadia found him on the way out and caught his sleeve, still cheerful, still loud.

"You'll come back, though," she said. "When you've got your box full. You'll tell me."

"I will," he said, and meant to, and knew the promise was worth nothing until he had something to put in the box.

That evening Ola showed him the back of her book, shy for the first time since he met her, and there were four years of someone else's handwriting copied in pencil, dates and car numbers and the word headache underlined.

Beatrix Halm did not write the cold minutes down because she was frightened. She wrote them down because nobody in her own division had ever asked her what she feels, and she had been keeping the real figures in a book for four years and calling it a headache.

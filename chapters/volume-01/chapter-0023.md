# Two Kinds of Clean

The witness book at the Fourth Branch is kept on a lectern by the plate office door and anybody may read it, and Lin Hao had read it four hundred times in four years without ever once looking at the column on the left.

The left column is the automatic column. It is where the counter records a release that did not need a deed behind it — a held floor aggregate moving to a plate because a plate crossed a band, or a correction, or a re-read forced by something else landing. It has a heading that says so in nine words and it is signed by the counter clerk, not by the beneficiary.

Underneath the heading there were two lines in the book on the morning of the 30th.

The first said: 27th. Re-read forced by attested deed 15:04. Fourth Branch floor aggregate released in full to LIN HAO. Clerk V. Countersigned.

The second said: Same entry. Attestation entered to satisfy reconciliation. T. RENN.

There was a column for it. He had never noticed the column because it was narrow and because the column was, until the morning of the 30th, empty.

Lin Hao read the line three times and then went and stood with his back against the wall beside the lectern for a while, and what he felt was not fear. It was the specific, extremely clear understanding of a man watching a piece of paper burn and being able to reach it.

Tobias Renn had not forged anything. That was what made it work. The release was true; it was a real release of a real 3,529 on a real day, and it had been permitted by the machine because the machine does not ask a man whether he wants a thing that has already happened to him. What was false was the attestation in the narrow column, and the attestation was not a fact about the release. It was a fact about a deed, and the deed Tobias Renn had attested was a witnessed deed at the Ferrier interchange on the 23rd, which he had not been at, had not seen, and had never been asked about.

Tobias had written, in his own hand, that he had stood in front of that deed. Which he had not.

The only reason it existed was a draw-down. In an audit, every plate in the branch is reconciled against the aggregate it carries, and if the aggregate has moved without an instrument behind the movement, that is an irregularity in the books, and an irregularity in the books on a draw-down week means the reconciliation fails, and a failed reconciliation means the branch does not have a settled figure for anything, which means the ninety-one stop being a list and become a process.

Tobias had gone into the witness book on his own initiative and put a false attestation on a true release, in order to protect ninety people from a schedule he could not move.

And it was beautifully done, and it was exactly what a careful clerk does, and it had been done in the left-hand column on a working day in front of the whole plate office.

He could stop it. That was the part that stayed with him for the rest of the day.

A retraction before the reconciliation runs is a correction. Every counter in Ostara will accept a clerk saying I entered that in error, strike it and nobody writes it up, because a correction made before anybody asks is not a discrepancy, it is a man fixing his own work at eight in the morning on an ordinary day, and there are ten thousand of those in this city every week.

He had until the reconciliation at four.

He could take it to Vole, who would strike it and say nothing, because Vole had read his plate three times and had told him about the aggregate herself, and Vole had shown him the pigeonhole.

He could take it to Ines, who would strike it and then, being Ines, look very hard at what the entry underneath it said.

He could take it to Rhee, who would strike it and would then know, permanently, that a rank-five plate on his own staff carries a floor aggregate that moves without instruments, which is the sentence that turns a portable merit source into a requisition.

All three roads ended in a document with LIN HAO written on it.

He stood at the lectern for forty minutes and did not take it anywhere, and he was entirely clear, the whole time, about what he was doing. He was not confused. He was not caught between a man and a machine. He was choosing, with his eyes open, to let a junior filing clerk carry a false attestation on a plate that had his name on it, because the clerk's name was in the narrow column and his was on the line above it.

He went up and did the second floor. He did it badly, because he could not do it well, and at two o'clock he stood on the fourth-floor landing for eleven minutes while Ottoline Barret came up with a stack of trays and said, "You're in my light," and moved him along the wall without waiting to find out whether he would move.

At half past two Tobias Renn came round the corner with a folder and stopped dead.

They looked at each other across four feet of landing.

"I know what you've done," Tobias said.

He had his sleeves buttoned at the wrist again and there was nothing in his face at all, which was much worse than the face he had worn on the 27th.

"Not yet you," Tobias said. "Sit down. No — stand, then. I've got about an hour before the reconciliation and I am going to use all of it, and if I don't use it now I'll go and put my name on the form that suspends me and I'll have used the last of it on that instead." He put the folder under his arm. "You saw it."

"At the lectern."

"And you had from eight until four and you are still here." Tobias's voice went up half a tone and then came back down, controlled. "I have been in the filing room for four years and in that time exactly one person has ever come and read that book, and it is you, every morning, on your way past, for four years. So you saw the line. You had the whole day. And you are here, on a landing, and I have to come and find you, and I want you to tell me why."

Lin Hao could have said that he had wanted to protect him. It would have been almost true and it would have been, in the way that matters in this city, an act of theft.

"The release is mine," he said. "A query about that line is a query about me. If you strike it and it goes up, then a floor aggregate in this branch moved three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine grains onto a plate with no settled reading, and somebody above you is going to be interested in that plate, and it will not be me they ask."

Tobias nodded slowly. "Right."

"And I could not say that to you," Lin Hao said, "because saying it means saying why the plate has no settled reading."

"Yes."

"So I let you carry it."

"You let me carry a false attestation." Tobias put his hand flat on the wall, which he had never done in front of him in four years. "Do you know what the punishment is? I looked it up this morning, because I am a filing clerk and I look things up."

He said it flatly.

"It is not the sub-levels, which I had assumed, and I want to be clear that I assumed it and I was wrong, and I have not got a moment's use for being wrong about that. A clerk who attests a deed he did not witness comes off the filing register. Not suspended. Off. Every attestation he has ever signed is re-opened and re-checked by a man two grades above me, one at a time, by hand, and it takes about two years." He took his hand off the wall. "And then they take the register away and you are an enquiry clerk, which is a person who files other people's boxes and does not open them."

"Two years of re-checking and then the boxes."

"Two years of re-checking and then the boxes," Tobias said, "in a room in the sub-levels' annexe, for as long as they like. And I have twenty-nine months of filings behind me, Lin Hao. Twenty-nine months and every one of them correct. That is not a boast. That is the reason I could sign your line and sleep about it, and the reason nobody is going to believe me for about four years."

He took the folder out from under his arm and opened it.

"I am going to do it now," he said. "Not at four. Now. Because at four the reconciliation runs, and if I do it now then what I did about it sits in the front of the file instead of the back, and a man who files himself in the front of his own file is a man who was straight. A man who gets caught at four was a man who tried it."

He wrote on the top sheet with a pencil and then signed it.

"I am going to state that I entered the attestation, that I had not witnessed the deed, that I entered it to protect the branch's reconciliation on a draw-down week, and that no other person instructed me or knew of it. All true. All four lines of it."

He held it out.

"There is a witness line," he said. "You may sign it or not sign it. If you sign it, my account is straight and yours has a witness in it. If you don't, my account is still straight."

Lin Hao took the pencil.

He held it for about eleven seconds with the point a quarter of an inch above the witness line, and in those eleven seconds he ran the only calculation available to him, which was the calculation he had been running for four years: that a man who is looked through is a man who is not used, and that everything he had in the world was the product of that, and that there was no version of himself that could sign a witness line under a false attestation without becoming a different man by the ink's end.

He put the pencil down on the folder, un-signed, and Tobias Renn looked at it for a moment and then took the folder back and squared it against his hip.

"No," Tobias said. "No, that's fair. I wouldn't either."

"Lin—"

"Don't." Tobias turned and went to the rail. "I'm not going to stand here and be argued into it. I have been argued into one thing this week and it has cost me the register, and I would like to keep the one thing I actually did, which was get here on my own."

He put his hand on the rail and looked down the stairwell.

"You know what the worst of it is? You were going to come. I can tell. You've been in that book every morning for four years and you read the whole page every morning, and you got to the word Renner at about eleven minutes past eight, and you had seven hours and forty-nine minutes."

Lin Hao did not say anything, and the silence went on for long enough that it stopped being a silence and became an answer, and Tobias Renn heard it and nodded once.

"Right," he said.

He went down the stairs with the folder under his arm and Lin Hao listened to him all the way to the ground floor, and then he stood on the fourth-floor landing in the light of the stair window with the wind coming off the Reading Square and could not have told anybody, including himself, what exactly he had been protecting.

It was not himself. He went over that for an hour on the second floor, going through it the way he went through everything, checking the arithmetic twice, because he had known for four years that he hoards and had never had the nerve to look at a real instance.

It was not the plate and it was not the band and it was not the ninety-one and it was not his brother. It was that a filing clerk who had carried it for him was standing in a room in an annexe with somebody else's boxes in front of him, and Lin Hao had chosen that, on purpose, at eleven minutes past eight in the morning, with seven hours and forty-nine minutes available.

He wanted to go and find Beatrix and ask her to go back to a date. That was the shape of it. Four years of a woman's headache diary and a column she ruled herself with three ticks in it, and one of the three was a name on a rota in this building, and he had been carrying a version of that name in his coat for three weeks without writing it down, because a man who writes a name down owns it.

He wrote it down now, at half past four, on the back of a leaf from the notebook he bought on Ferrier Street, standing at the lectern where the witness book was.

EMRYS VAZ.

The yard rota, six in the morning, the line that had been struck out on the 22nd and then rewritten to the sub-levels. The boy who carries stock up from the yard and has never in his life been asked a question by anybody above him. Beatrix had never met him and had seen the name on a rota from the far end of a yard at six in the morning, four or five weeks ago, on a night when the branch aggregate had been doing something she could feel from a hundred yards off.

He had told Beatrix in a cab, twenty-two days ago, that he would keep her three ticks where they were and spend nobody new. He had bought that with the three,529.

And the boy was on the list. Last again, under the heading for staff who hold no plate, with a time against him on a sheet printed on the second floor for a reading that happens on the ground floor.

Nineteen lines. One of them was a boy who had felt a cold minute in a tram yard from a hundred yards away and put it in a column of his own because a man in a good coat had called it a condition, and who was going into a room on the third of next month for it.

Lin Hao put the leaf in his coat and stood in the corridor by the second-floor door for a long time with a mug of cold tea belonging to somebody else.

The filing room was empty and the chair was out from the desk at the angle a man leaves it in when he means to come straight back, and there was a mug on the desk with the tea gone cold in it, and there was a sheet of paper weighted under a stone, and on the sheet, in Tobias Renn's small upright hand, was the name of the man who had been sitting here for four years, and under it, because he had been raised to file the truth even when the truth was his own destruction:

T. RENN — RELIEVED OF FILING REGISTER, EFFECTIVE TODAY. NO DEPUTY ARRANGED.

The chair was still warm.

# Nineteen Grains

He went home the long way, on the tram, which was the wrong way by a quarter of an hour, because a quarter of an hour on a moving vehicle with your hands full of a wrapped plate is the cheapest privacy in Ostara and he had four years of practice at not needing more than that.

The Ferrier line came into the Reading from the Cordwain yards, ran the length of the Square with the Board on its left, and then went down through the Slough in a long descending sweep past the free clinics and the long staircases, and by the time it reached Ferrier Street the car was empty enough to sit down in and the conductor had stopped offering you a seat and started offering you a look.

He had not eaten. He had had the staff kettle at eleven, a heel of bread at four, and the wrapped plate against his ribs since the middle of the morning, and by five o'clock he was doing the thing he did when he could not stop thinking: he was sitting very still in a hard seat watching the wires go over the roofs, letting the thinking happen at a distance where it could not touch anything.

At Cutler's Row a man stood up too fast.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing he would think about afterwards and could never get past: there was no fall, no sound, nothing that anybody else on that car would have looked up from their evening for. The man simply put his hand up to the strap and missed it, and his knees came up, and he went down along the side of the car with his shoulder into the partition, and his right hand closed around Lin Hao's forearm above the wrist with a grip that had nothing wrong with it and everything wrong with the arm it came from.

Lin Hao did not decide to touch him.

His hand was already there. It had gone the way his hands always went, which was *before*, flat over the man's wrist where the sleeve had ridden up, and he felt the whole thing arrive through his palm like a word said in a room he was not in.

What came out of him was cold and it had weight and it had direction.

It ran up his arm and out of his shoulder and into a man he had never seen before in his life, and it went on for a length of time that he would afterwards have guessed at two seconds and which was, by the time the tram came into the next stop, considerably more than that.

And in the same instant, on the other side of the contact, the man went cold.

Not cold the way a car is cold. It came up through the wrist Lin Hao was holding and went into the old place — the pylon, the shoulder, four years of it — and sat there, and Halvard Rees made a sound like a man who has put his hand on a hot plate and cannot decide whether to take it off.

It lasted about three seconds. Lin Hao watched it happen from the outside, which was the part that undid him: he could see the exact instant it arrived, because the man's whole body registered it, the shoulders coming up and the breath going short and the hand on his forearm tightening to a grip that had nothing to do with holding on.

Then it was over.

The man was not well. Nothing about him was well. He was an ordinary man in his forties on a tram at four in the afternoon and he was grey and shaky and out of breath and something in him had gone quiet that had not been quiet for four years.

But the tremor went. It went inside the same two seconds the cold arrived, and it went completely, and Halvard Rees sat down on the bench opposite without appearing to decide to, heavily, the way heavy men sit when they have decided to trust a seat. He put his hands on his knees.

"You went cold," Lin Hao said. "Just then. Your hand went cold."

"Did it." Halvard Rees looked at his own hand as though it belonged to somebody else, and then he laughed at nothing in particular and shook it out, the way a man shakes out a hand that has been in a bucket. "That's the cold, that's the tram. There's a draught in this car that comes in at the junction and it's been doing it since we were kids. You get four or five an hour on this line if you're stood by the door."

"Four or five an hour."

"Aye." He was already looking past it. He had decided, in about a second and a half, that it had been the tram, and Lin Hao understood that he was not going to be talked out of that, and understood also that it had been the correct decision, and felt something in him go cold that had nothing to do with the draught.

Lin Hao took his hand off the wrist.

"Are you—"

"I'm all right." The man said it in the voice of a man reading a sign that says a station has been rebuilt. "I'm all right. I'm — that's better. That's a good deal better than it was."

He was grey. He had wire-splicer's leather on his forearms and a rail-dust in the creases of both elbows that no washroom in Ostara would ever get out, and he had the particular shoulders of a man who has spent twenty years reaching above his own head.

"Halvard Rees," he said. "Splicer. Ferrier line, out of the yards." He was looking at Lin Hao's hands. "You've got a good hand. Sorry — I don't know you."

"Lin Hao."

"You're not on the line."

"No."

Halvard Rees flexed the shoulder that had been going, and made a sound through his teeth that was half a laugh and entirely surprise, and then he stopped, because the surprise had caught up with him.

"That's the pylon," he said.

"What?"

"That shoulder. Cutler's yard, four years back, I came off a gantry four years back and I've had it ever since." He put the hand flat against it. "It's been going since about Sunday. Four days. It's been going in a tram and in a shop and in my own kitchen and I've been sitting on it, do you know, I've been sitting on it. And you put your hand on it and it's *quiet*."

Lin Hao had not put his hand on his shoulder.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't." Halvard Rees put his hands flat on his knees, and then took them off again, and then put them back, which was the first thing Lin Hao had seen him do that did not look like a man who was sure of his own body. "I'll say this once and then I'll say nothing else about it, and you can go and think about it on your own time and come to whatever you think."

He looked out of the window at the wet roofs going past.

"I didn't ask you for anything," Halvard Rees said. "That's the part I want understood. I didn't ask you for anything and I don't know what you did and I'm not going to stand here in a moving tram and thank a man I don't know for a thing I can't name." He got up. "Eleven Cutler's Row, second floor back, and I'm not writing it on anything, because I don't write things down. But if you were the sort of man who did that on purpose, I wouldn't want you doing it to me twice."

The tram came into Ferrier Street and he got off, and he got off *easily*, without the hand on the strap, and he went down the steps two at a time, and Lin Hao watched him go through the window until he turned into the chop-house at the bottom.

Lin Hao sat in the hard seat on a moving tram with his hands in his lap.

He had not done anything.

That was the sentence, and he turned it over, and underneath it there was a second sentence that he had not yet earned the right to say and which he therefore said anyway, in his own head, twice, like a man testing a floor with his heel: something had come out of him. There had been a place in his chest where there had been something and now there was not, and the not was *lighter*, and the lightness was sickly, and it felt exactly like the feeling of a room after somebody has been in it and left.

Nothing had come in. The man had not given him anything. He had put a hand on a stranger's wrist on a tram and nineteen grains' worth of something had gone out of him into the middle distance, and the stranger had felt it arrive as three seconds of cold in an injury that had been hurting for four years, and had decided it was a draught, and had gone home.

He understood then, sitting there with his hands in his lap, what he was actually holding.

Not the thing in his chest. The other thing. That man was going to remember the cold. He was going to remember the *shape* of it — where it had gone, how long, what it had been sitting on — because everybody remembers the first time a thing goes quiet, and it had gone quiet four years early and he had been standing in the middle of a moving tram for it.

In four years, or five, or whenever, that man would be on the Ferrier line in the cold and something would bring it back, and he would stop, and he would work out that it was not a draught, and he would start keeping track.

He had made his first turned line on a tram at four o'clock on a Wednesday, in under two seconds, by accident, out of the smallest possible opening.

He got off at the Square because he wanted to walk, and walked under the Board without looking at it, which he did every Thursday and was doing now on a Wednesday, which was the kind of small dishonesty he permitted himself exactly one of per week.

The concourse under the Reading was where the plates came up from the sub-basement in trays and went out into the city in pockets. There were four hundred people in it and a pigeon-note overhead that never stopped, and it was the first place Lin Hao had ever stood where he was completely alone in a crowd.

He took the plate out of his coat.

The oilcloth came off in two pieces because he had not undone the flap properly, and the disc inside was cold, and the face of it was cold, and he held it up in the flat white light of the concourse and saw nothing at all, because a plate does not carry a figure on its face. You press it to a socket. That is the entire mechanism.

He almost put it back.

And then he found the branch plate office on the concourse's west side — a little kiosk of the kind every district has, staffed by a girl who was eating something out of paper — and he put the disc against the socket and turned it, because he could not not do it, because four years had taught him to read a slip.

He stood there.

The interval line was a dash. It was still a dash. Nothing else about the slip had changed, not the header, not the cycle number, not the figure, which was to say that the machine had gone to the trouble of producing an entirely fresh piece of paper about a man standing in a public concourse.

He looked at the figure for a long time.

Three thousand four hundred and twelve grains in a week.

Three thousand three hundred and ninety-three.

He counted it twice with his finger on the edge of the kiosk, on the damp, in the bad light, and the digits were the same and he had not misread a nine for a three, and he had been reading plates since he was a boy.

It had gone down by nineteen.

He had spent nineteen grains of a figure that had no rate behind it on a man on a tram, and the machine had noticed, and the machine had a column for that, and the column was on the slip in the kiosk window in front of him, and the man on the tram had got four years of a shoulder for it.

[Ledger. Three lines.]

It came into the space behind his eyes the way a sound arrives in a room with a stone floor, and it did not come from anywhere, and he did not turn round, and he understood that it was not going to explain itself and that if he stood here long enough it would go away again and probably come back with a second line.

Three lines.

He had known exactly one person all day. One stranger, for nine or ten seconds, on a moving tram, at the far end of a car from everybody else, and that man's name was Halvard Rees and he worked out of the yards and he had been four days from doing something stupid about a shoulder.

Two of the three were people he had never met.

The concourse went on around him, four hundred people crossing a stone floor to nowhere in particular, pigeons going up in a spiral under a skylight, the Board standing nine streets away with Thursday's paint drying on it, and Lin Hao stood at a plate kiosk in the middle of Ostara with a slip in his hand and the specific, entirely physical understanding that he had not been carrying a broken plate.

He had been carrying three people.

And he had spent one of them on a man on a tram at four o'clock on a Wednesday, and it had worked, and the man had felt it happen, and had gone home and told nobody, and would remember it in the cold for the rest of his life.

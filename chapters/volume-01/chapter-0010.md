# No One Keeps the Diary

He did not sleep and neither did Ines, and they went into the vestibule at six because the shelter hall opened at six and if you wanted to find out how long thirty people had been sitting on a bench you had to be there when they started counting.

She was exactly where he had seen her. Front bench, end, woollen hat, a paper cup gone cold in both hands, and a school exercise book open on her knees with the elastic gone from it.

"Ola Bright," Ines said.

The woman looked up and was not frightened. That was the first thing, and it was the thing Lin Hao had been dreading for two hours, and it went through him like cold water. She looked up and she was *delighted*, because two people had come in at six in the morning and one of them had said her name.

"You're the man from the landing," she said. "The branch. You put your hand on my arm and I lost about six inches of height." She laughed. "I've been telling everyone. Have I not? I did tell everyone. It's my best story."

"You have a notebook."

"I have a notebook," she agreed, and held it up, and it was a school exercise book with a marbled cover and forty-one pages gone soft at the corners, and she held it the way a person holds a thing they are proud of rather than a thing they are frightened of.

Lin Hao took the four sheets out of his coat and unfolded them on the bench between them.

They looked at each other for a while.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's *lovely*. You've kept them." She was genuinely moved. "I gave those to the tray and I thought they'd go in the paper." She took them and looked at them with affection. "I've a proper book now. Forty-one pages. It isn't the same, it's less tidy, but there's more of it."

"Nadia Ferrous told you about the cold."

"Nadia tells *everyone* about the cold." Ola Bright said it without any weight, the way you say a thing about a friend who is loud. "She'd tell a stranger on a tram. She told a man from the Church of the Quiet Number for twenty minutes on the Thursday and he took notes, and I said, Nadia, he's taking *notes*, and she said, he wants to help, and I said, Nadia, nobody wants to help, they want to write it down."

Ola Bright put her head on one side.

"And she said — does he though?"

Lin Hao had to look at the wet pavement for a second.

"She's not turned," Ines said, from behind him. "Ma'am, I am an auditor and I am going to ask you some questions in order, because it is easier that way and because you will not like them in any other order. How many times since the fourth of June."

Ola Bright blinked. "Eleven."

"How many in one week."

"Three. Once. It's never been four."

"Have you ever written it down anywhere except that book."

"No. Should I have?"

Ines took out her notebook, wrote eleven, and looked at it for a while.

"You have done nothing wrong. I want to be very clear about that, because I have read a great many statements this month written by frightened women in this hall, and I have read exactly one statement this morning written by a woman who is not frightened, and she has done nothing at all wrong."

"That's what I've been saying."

"Yes," Ines said. "And it does not help you." She closed the notebook. "It is a record. It is the only record there is going to be of that lift for four months, and it has a place in it and a date in it and a word in it that means cold, and one day somebody is going to put those three things next to each other. That is not a threat. It is what happens to paper."

Ola Bright looked at her for a moment, and then said, quite mildly: "You're frightened for her."

"Yes."

"Why? She's a lift hand with a cold. I've had a cold since the fourth of June. It isn't interesting."

"It isn't interesting," Ines said, "except that it is nine-tenths the way to interesting, and the last tenth is a room with no window in it, and I have spent fourteen years learning exactly how far that is and I am telling you the distance so that you can decide what you think you are writing in."

And then Ola Bright said the thing that took the rest of the chapter out of Lin Hao's hands.

"I know," she said. "I've worked it out. Nadia worked it out before me, she worked it out on the Friday and she was frightened for a day and then she wasn't, because I said — " She looked at the four sheets on the bench. "I said, who writes it down? And she said, you do. And I said, well, somebody has to, and then we both had a terrible time, and then she went back to work."

Lin Hao stood in a cold vestibule at ten past six in the morning.

*Somebody has to.*

A hall with no window in the basement of the Fourth Branch, a hundred and forty people, and a man with his score on the wall behind him saying that a number was wrong, in the ordinary voice of a person stating a thing everybody in the room should have seen. And then gone by morning.

And on a bench in the Vestry at ten past six on a Saturday morning, a woman with a cold in her hand and an exercise book, saying *well, somebody has to*, in exactly the same voice, four years later, about a completely different thing, and being right.

He sat down on the bench next to her. He did not say anything for a while.

Ines, standing behind them both, said quietly: "Lin."

"I'm all right."

"You've gone grey. You were grey last week as well."

"I know."

The woman at the public counter of the Classification Wing was called Hester Quill and she had been doing it for twenty-nine years.

It was not in the Vestry. It was a brass-fronted counter with three stools and a bell and a form rack, four streets away at the bottom of a stair, and it was the only part of the sub-level that ordinary citizens were allowed to stand in front of.

Hester Quill was not cruel. Lin Hao had been ready for her to be cruel and she was not, and it was worse. She was a small woman with reading glasses on a chain and a cardigan with pen marks on the cuff, and she took his name, and she took Ines's name, and she wrote both of them down without asking why.

"You're not here about yourself," she said, reading what she had written. "Auditor's with you. And you're not on a notice." She looked up. "What do you want to know."

"Fourth Branch. Last Thursday. A stock clerk off the fourth floor."

She looked at him for a moment and then she put her hand flat on the counter, and he understood that she had been waiting all morning for somebody to ask her that, and that she had not been asked once in six days.

"Bernt Halse," she said. "Fifty-one. Fourth floor stores, twenty-two years. He came down at ten past ten on the Thursday with an escort and a sealed notice and he was very calm, and he asked me — " She stopped. "He asked me what time it would be finished, and I told him I did not know, and that was honest, and I have thought about it since."

"Is he finished?"

"He is not finished." She turned round and pulled a card out of a rack of about two hundred and put it in front of him upside down, because that is what they do. It said HALSE B. R4 STORES. SUB-LEVEL. ACCOUNT UNSETTLED — CLASS 2. There was a date on it, Thursday, and a time, ten past ten, and a column marked with a single stroke. "His account did not settle, and he will be there until it does, and that is all I can tell you and I have told you all of it."

"Did he do anything?"

Hester Quill put her pen down.

"No," she said, and she said it kindly, because she believed it. "Nobody is taken for a reading because they did anything. That is not how it works and it never has been."

"People come out of here saying they were punished, and it is the single most common misunderstanding in the city, and I have been explaining it for twenty-nine years."

She turned the card over and drew a small box on the back of it with her pen.

"Listen. I am going to tell you how this works and then you can decide what you think of it, and I would rather you decided than stood on that stair making a face."

A person's account is a set of numbers that agree with each other. Every week the figure comes back and the rate behind it comes back, and if the two go on agreeing, the account *settles*, and a settled account is closed, and you never hear about it again.

That is ninety-nine people in a hundred and nine. And every so often the figure and the rate will not agree, and we cannot find an instrument behind it, and then the instrument has nowhere to put the question. So the person comes down. That is all. They come down so that a room with a man who has got time can find out where the missing grains went.

"And then?"

"And then they stay until somebody can close the book." She put the cap on her pen. "Some of them are there a fortnight. Some of them are there a year. Nobody decides that. It is not a sentence, it is an addition. When they find the number where the two part company, he goes home."

"And if they never find it?"

"Then he is there when we have finished being able to look."

"Bernt Halse has a sister," she said. "She was in on the Friday night. She was in on the Saturday. She was in this morning at twenty past five, before I unlocked the door, and she is in the hall at the Vestry at this minute with thirty other people."

She took her glasses off.

"She has been in that hall since six o'clock on Friday evening. She has slept on those benches twice. She goes to work at eight and comes back at six, and in three weeks it will be a month, and in a month she will not be able to remember what the flat felt like."

"I have written her name in a book, because a person has to write it down somewhere. That is all the writing down does. It does not bring anybody back. It just means that if he ever does come out of there, there is one piece of paper in this city that says his name was on the front of a form."

And on a bench in a vestibule four streets away, at twenty past six in the morning, Ditta Halse had come back from a night shift in a Cordwain laundry and was sitting in the second row with her hands in her sleeves, and she had a bag with a jumper in it, because she had made her bed on that bench on the Friday and had decided that if she was going to be here all week she would at least be comfortable.

Ines pulled him out of the queue by the sleeve and down the stair and into the street.

"Stop."

"I have not done anything."

"You are doing arithmetic with your face and I can see the numbers going past it." She was breathing hard. "You put your hand on four people since Wednesday and one of them was a supply supervisor you wanted a key from. Bernt Halse was taken on Thursday at ten past ten and you were on the second floor with a bucket at ten past ten."

"Whatever you are, you did not do that to him. Say it out loud, because I can see you deciding that you did."

"He was taken on the Thursday," Lin Hao said. "Two hours before I put a hand on Colm Deveraux and got him a thousand grains he did not earn. Two hours." He looked at the wet street. "And I have spent six days trying to work out whether those two facts are in the same sentence, and they are not, and it is not going to stop me at four in the morning for the rest of my life."

"Good."

"And a man on the tram got four years of a shoulder out of me on the Wednesday, and nobody put him in a room, because nobody could find out, because *I* could not find out." He put his hands in his coat. "That's the whole of it, Ines. It runs in one direction. The moment somebody can find out where it went, it becomes a room with no window, and the room has no window so that nobody outside it can find out either."

Ines stood in the street and did not argue with him, which was how he knew she had understood.

"What do you want to do," she said.

"I want to know what a relieved body looks like from the outside."

"Say that again in words a doctor would understand."

"I want to know whether a body can be found in the records that does not match the body in front of her."

"That," Ines said, "is either the stupidest question anybody has asked me this year or the only one that matters, and I am not an auditor any more, I am going to go and be bored in a clinic for an hour while you find out."

The Vestry clinic ran on a corridor and a waiting bench and a smell of carbolic and boiled linen, and the woman at the end of it was standing over a tray of instruments with her sleeves pushed up, and she had the hands of somebody who has put a person back together and is thinking about the next one.

She was called Tessa Okonkwo and she ran the largest free clinic in Ostara for the gifted, which meant that when a rank-five body failed at two in the morning there was one number she was given, and it was her.

"Can I help you."

Lin Hao had prepared eleven sentences on the walk from the street and every one of them went out of his head in the doorway.

"I'm not ill," he said.

"Nobody who comes in here at a quarter to seven in the morning is ill. Sit down, you're grey." She looked at his temple without touching it. "How long."

"Since the Thursday before last."

"That's not a colour, that's a symptom. What have you been doing."

"Nothing."

She looked at him for slightly longer than a doctor is required to.

Then she said: "Ask me your question. You didn't come down a corridor at seven in the morning to be told you're anaemic."

"Can a body be found in the records," Lin Hao said, "that does not match the body in front of her."

Tessa Okonkwo put down a piece of steel she had been holding and wiped her hands on her apron, twice, slowly, the way a surgeon does before she says something she means.

"Yes," she said.

"How."

"A person who has been relieved of something they should still be carrying presents as *too well*." She said it the way she would say a drug dose.

"That is the whole of the clinical difference and it is not subtle, it is just rare. Their chart is a long story. Their chest is clear. Their colour is four shades better than it should be for a person who has come in on a wet morning after no sleep. They tell me they slept. They tell me they slept *beautifully*."

"And every instrument I own says they are well. And I know they are not well. I know they are a person with something removed, because I have been taking damage off the gifted for nine years and I know exactly what a body looks like when it has been let go of something."

"How many."

"That I don't count." She picked the steel up again. "And that is the part that should frighten you, because it means it is not rare enough for me to count. I have a box, Lin, I have a physical box in the cold store with the ones I could not put a name to, and it is not full, and it is not empty, and I have never once been able to explain one of them to the person in front of me."

Ines said, from the doorway: "Dr Okonkwo. If I file a report on an unsettled reading—"

"You can't. You don't have the standing."

"I know that I don't have the standing. I am asking what the file would say."

Tessa Okonkwo looked at her for a moment, and then said: "It would say that a woman came in with an account that had a mass and no rate, and that it is the only category of person in this city whose body can be wrong on its own paper. And if the city ever wants to know how many of us there are, then the ones who are wrong on their own paper are where you look."

"And the ones who are wrong on their own paper are not hiding. They are sitting in front of me with their coats on, telling me they slept beautifully, and asking me whether the records are any good."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"If I hold a body," Tessa Okonkwo said, "I write the file. That is not a complaint, it is the only thing I have got. I do not have the thing you have, whatever it is — the ability to do it and not have it written down anywhere. If you ever need a room with no window in it that has a door in it, come and find me and I will give you one, and I will fill in the paper, and it will be the only honest paper in that building."

Lin Hao said thank you and went out.

In the corridor outside the treatment room there was a rota board on the wall, because the Vestry kitchens fed the clinic and the clinic ran on volunteers, and the board had a grid on it of days and kitchens and initials, and it was the kind of board nobody in a hurry ever reads.

MON WED FRI — KITCHEN RUN — O. BRIGHT

He looked at it the way you look at a board.

He did not see it. There was a piece of paper folded in his coat with forty-one pages of pencil in it and a signature at the bottom of the fourth sheet that consisted of the word *again* underlined twice, and there was a name in his own ledger he had never been told, and the board was a board.

He went out into the street at ten past seven and it had stopped raining.

Across the road there was a chapel with a plain door and a board outside it, and the board said that the Church of the Quiet Number held no ranks and asked for no reading and kept no register, and that it was eleven thousand strong, and that the whole of its doctrine was that a deed with no witness in it is a deed, and that a city which cannot count it has no business saying that the person who did it was worth nothing.

He had walked past that door every Thursday for four years.

He stood on the pavement and looked at it, and then he looked down the street at the brass front of the Classification Wing's public counter, where the queue had got to about nine people by now, and he thought: somebody is going to stand at that counter today and ask what is in those nine rooms, and the answer will be *accounts*, and the queue will go back down the steps.

"Lin."

Ines was beside him with her coat on and her file under her arm.

"That chapel," she said. "Do you know it?"

"No."

"I have been into it four times. It is the only institution in Ostara whose theory is correct and whose practice is therefore illegal." She was looking at the door. "And there is a woman who stands on that step in grey on every single morning I have been in this district. I asked a porter about her on my third audit. She has been there three years. She is not begging and she is not a member — she is a rank-five clerk who went to the Correction Office the week after your brother disappeared and filed a petition, and it was refused, and she has filed one every week since, and it is always the same petition."

"What does it ask for."

"It asks the City to count the ones that nobody counted." Ines turned and looked at him. "She wants it published. Not the names — the *count*. How many good deeds happen in this city every week that no instrument ever sees. One line on the Board under everybody's band, and that is the whole of it, and it is the single most dangerous document anybody in this city has tried to file in three years, and she puts it on that step every morning and asks every rank-five and above who walks past to put a seal on it."

"Has anybody?"

"No." She said it flatly. "Not in three years. Not once. Because putting a seal on that petition is a statement that the Board is wrong, and the Board is not wrong, Lin, the Board is *incomplete*, and a clerk who can tell the difference between those two things cannot put a seal on it, and so she is still on that step."

He stood in the wet street with his hands in his coat.

Eight days to the draw-down, and a plate in his coat with a dash on it, and a figure that had gone up one hundred and fifty-six grains in nine days because he had walked through a city being kind to each other without once being seen doing it, and no standing whatsoever in the world.

He could not open his brother's file. He could not answer that woman on the step. He could not stop nine people in his own building from going into a room, and he could not do it by being good, and he could not do it by being invisible, and there was not one single thing available to him on this street on a Saturday morning except a hand and a supply and a number of strangers who would go cold.

The chapel door opened and a woman in grey came out and looked up and down the street, the way people do when they have been standing in one place for three years, and her eyes went past him and stopped on Ines Duarte, and stayed there.

"Oh," she said. "You're the auditor from the Fourth Branch."

"Yes."

"Then you're the fourth one in three years." The woman in grey did not smile and did not not smile. "Will you read it? It takes four minutes. It's the same four minutes as the last thirty-six, and I would like somebody in a coat to stand on the step and read it."

# The Wrong Direction

The cold store at ten to ten at night is eight feet by eleven and two degrees and it has one lamp on a cord, and Tessa Okonkwo had spent an hour on it before he arrived.

She had brought the couch in off the treatment room, which was the single piece of furniture in the building that could be moved by two people and it had taken both of them plus a chair under the door to get it round the corner. She had brought the table. She had put the rack of clipboards face to the wall, which he understood was not for her. She had brought a hot-water bottle in a woollen sleeve and a blanket and a jug of water and a plate of bread and cheese she put on the table corner and then said he was not to touch it until it was done.

Wenna Ost came in at nine forty exactly in a knitted hat and somebody's slippers, and she was frightened and she was not stupid about it.

She said she had been asked, she said she had been told there was something in this city she had never heard of and that it could not be explained and that the only way anybody was ever going to find out whether it was real was if somebody let somebody try it on them, and she said that being a laundress off the Vestry night shift for nineteen years, she had spent nineteen years being the person who did the thing nobody saw.

Then she said, "If it does nothing I want it written down that I asked."

"It will be written down," Tessa said.

Teodor Mace was already in the room.

He was on the couch with his boots off and his back against the wall and a blanket over his knees, and he was awake, and Tessa introduced him as a patient and did not introduce him as anything else, because she had not had time.

He had come in at seven with a chest that would not settle when he lay down. His plate read twenty-two hundred and forty grains. He worked in a counting house at the Exchange. And at eight o'clock he had agreed to sit through Tessa's intake test, which was a new thing she had started in the week before the draw-down, and which he had volunteered for because it took twenty minutes and he could not sleep anyway.

The lamp was on the table. The couch was against the back wall and the chair was on the far side of it with Wenna in it. Tessa had the clipboard. Lin Hao stood by the door with his coat off and his hands not steady and no idea what he was about to do.

He had been awake for two nights. He had walked forty-one minutes twice that day in a row to get here, on the theory that the count would stop, and the count had stopped, and his hands had not.

"Listen," he said. "I am going to hold her wrist and I am going to try to put the figure into the account and not into the body. It may feel like draught. If you want it to stop, say stop. Either of you. Say stop and I let go."

"Write that down," Wenna said.

He did write it down, on the back of his hand, which was not what she had meant, and she looked at it and said, "That's the wrong end of the pen, but it's down," and he wrote it again on the leaf he had brought and was sorry about.

Then he went round the table.

The thing about doing it on purpose is that there is nothing to react to. Every other time in four years his body had gone before his head had finished deciding, because there had been a stranger in the path of a tram or a door that would not shut on its own or a woman's arm across his chest, and there had been a second in the middle of it where the Exchange was not involved at all.

There is no stranger here. There is a woman in a knitted hat who has agreed to it and a man on a couch who has agreed to something else and a doctor with a clipboard.

He got hold of Wenna Ost's wrist and he pulled, the way he pulled on Colm Deveraux's chest in a corridor with his whole weight, and this time he put the direction into it deliberately, deliberately and hard, aimed at the account and not at the woman.

Four hundred grains went somewhere.

He felt them go. He felt the shape of it go the way you feel a coin drop down a grating, down and away and *sideways*, and the cold came back up his arm at the same instant in his wrist and in his left temple where the grey is permanent.

He let go.

"Say it," he said. "Both of you. What did that feel like."

"Nothing," said Wenna Ost.

Teodor Mace had taken his hand off his chest and was looking at his own hand.

"Cold," Teodor said. "In here." He put the hand flat on his sternum and moved it once, the way a man checks whether a thing is still there. "Right in there, that's where it's been sitting since the 28th. That's gone cold and it's still there."

"Say the rest of it."

"I've been short of breath since the 28th," Teodor Mace said, "and it's still exactly the same, and I have been sitting on that couch for two hours and one of you just did something to me without asking me, and I would like it noted that I agreed to the intake test and not to that."

And Lin Hao stood in a cold room at ten past ten at night and understood that he had not done a thing to Teodor Mace by accident.

He had done a thing to Wenna Ost on purpose, and he had looked at the lamp and at two people and put his hand on the wrong wrist, because the chair was on the far side of the couch after they had moved the couch in, and from the door he could see Teodor's face and not Wenna's, and she had a knitted hat and he had a hand up against his chest.

Four hundred grains, a cold minute, and a man who had agreed to something else.

Tessa put the clipboard down.

"Plates," she said. "Both of you. In your bag."

There was a plate reader on the shelf above the door, the one the Vestry keeps for free-clinic headcount, and it had not been used in nine weeks. She read Wenna Ost's first. Two thousand and ninety. It had been two thousand and ninety when the woman came in on the 28th.

Then Teodor Mace's.

"Twenty-two forty when you came in," Tessa said, to him, not to the machine. "What's it say."

"Twenty-two sixty-four." Teodor read it off the disc and then read it again. "I don't—" He stopped. He is a man who counts for a living and he stopped in the middle of counting. "That's four hundred and twenty-four."

"You were twenty-two forty at seven o'clock this evening. Are you certain?"

"I'm certain, because I press it every night at the gate or I can't get into the yard." He held the plate out to her and his hand was not steady either, and he said, in a voice that had gone very flat, "Nobody has paid me anything."

Wenna Ost had said nothing for some time. Then she stood up off the chair.

"No," she said. "That's mine."

"Sit down, please."

"That is my figure. I've had two thousand and ninety for two years and it has not moved a grain, and I have been told by this man," she looked at Lin Hao, "that whatever he does can go into a plate. He said forty seconds ago it would either happen or it wouldn't, and it happened to somebody else, and it is on that man's plate, and it came out of me, because I am the one he was aiming at."

And she was right. That was the part that took Lin Hao's legs out from under him, standing up, in the dark, in a cold room: she was entirely right, and he had not thought about it once, because the thing he had spent six weeks trying to understand was a number going up on a disc, and the number had gone up on a disc, and it had gone up on the wrong disc, and in the meantime the woman who had agreed to it had been drained and had got nothing at all.

"Write that down as well," Tessa said quietly, to him. "Write it down in your own hand and do not decide for a minute that it does not count, because it counts."

He wrote it down.

Then Tessa Okonkwo did the thing she had actually been trained to do, and did it in about nine seconds flat, which told him that whatever else she was, she was not frightened.

"How long," she said to him.

"Four or five seconds."

"Standing there?"

"I don't know."

"Then sit down." She was already at the rack on the wall. She turned it round off the face of the wall and went through the clipboards from the bottom up with the lamp in her left hand, and she stopped at the fourth, and then went back one, and then stopped for a good deal longer.

"March," she said.

"What?"

"The second of May." She had the clipboard in both hands and she was not reading it so much as checking it against what was in front of her. "Chest. Complaint of waking every night with his hands shaking. Figure on arrival two thousand and six hundred and forty. Figure on departure two thousand and six hundred and forty. Rate unmoved. Body unmoved. He was here for six hours and he went out shaking, and I wrote unchanged, refer at the bottom in my own hand, because I had run out of ways of writing down that a thing had failed to happen."

She looked up at Lin Hao.

"Two thousand six hundred and forty," she said. "He is sitting on that couch with two thousand six hundred and forty on his plate."

Teodor Mace said, from the couch, in a voice that had gone entirely flat: "That is my number."

"Yes."

"That is my number. That was his number in May." Teodor got up off the couch with the blanket sliding off his knees and did not appear to know what to do with his hands. "You have got a shelf in your cold store with a plate on it that has my figure on it."

"I have got a shelf in my cold store," Tessa said, "with ten of them, and I told you two years ago that I did not count them, and I have not counted them until tonight, and I counted ten the day I moved the couch in, and now it is eleven."

She put the clipboard back and turned round and looked at Lin Hao.

"You are not the first," she said. "Somebody did this in this quarter before you knew what you were, and they came in at the front, and they stopped, and they left a number on a disc in a counting house somewhere in this city and a man with shaking hands who thought it was his fault."

The room was very cold.

Lin Hao stood with his back against a wall at two degrees and did not think about Teodor Mace, and he did not think about the man who shook, and he thought about a woman on a tram roof four weeks earlier saying nine turned lines in one season is a legible shape, and he thought about his own plate on the first of June, and the seventh, and the twelfth, when it had gone up by eighty-eight grains in two days while he had not touched anybody, and had gone up another eighty-seven in the next five.

It had not started with him. It had not started when he first drew nineteen grains out of a wire-splicer on the tram. It had not started when he first put his hand on Colm Deveraux in a corridor.

The count had been running the whole time he had been working out what it was.

He had been taking it for granted for nineteen days, and it had been running for months before that, and somewhere behind him there was a woman on a couch whose number was four hundred grains higher than it should have been and a shelf full of clipboards with other people's figures on them and a date, the second of May, four months before he had ever deliberately aimed anything at anybody.

He did not tell her that the count had not moved since the 29th, and he did not tell her why, and that was the first thing he kept back from her in a room where he had promised to write down everything, and he would go on thinking about it for a long time.

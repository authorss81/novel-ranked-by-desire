# The Second Basin

His plate had to be in the socket at the plate office before eleven, and the office shut at eleven, because a plate that is not read does not publish, and a reading that does not publish is read as nothing at all.

Nothing at all was a white tile, and a subsistence guarantee, and assigned work he did not choose. He had four years and eleven weeks of a posting at the Fourth Branch and no other way in the city of being certain of being there tomorrow. Everything else in his life fitted inside that, including the fact that he had been awake since five.

He had one plate. He had had it four years. It was a plain steel disc with nothing on the face of it except his registered number stamped small near the rim, and he had never lost it and never lent it, and the reason for both habits was the same: a man without a plate in Ostara is a man the city has decided does not exist yet.

He washed his hands in the second basin on the second floor and went down the back stair with the plate in his fist, and the plate office had four counters, three clerks, and a long rack of sockets along the back wall where people who already had their reading could look at it without talking to anybody, which was most of them. There were nine people at the sockets and he had time.

He pressed the plate flat and turned it the way you turn a key, because that was the instruction, and the machine behind the wall made the noise it made, a clicking that went up in pitch and then stopped.

A slip came out of the slot.

The interval line was a dash. Not a zero. A zero would have been a finding. A dash was the machine declining to write down a thing it had been given and could not place.

He had a figure. He had had one every Thursday for four years, and it was simply what the branch's aggregate gave a man for standing in a washroom on six floors for eleven hours a day, split by a formula nobody had ever published.

The interval was the part that made the number mean anything: the rate, the thing that made a figure a rate and a rate a band. On every slip he had ever taken out of that slot that line said something like 291 PER DAY, or 4,120 PER WEEK — HOLDING.

His figure had no rate. There was no column for it.

And a reading with no rate does not publish, and a reading that does not publish is read as nothing at all, and nothing at all was a white tile.

He looked at it for long enough that a woman behind him said, "She's free, if you're not going to."

Two things happened in his chest, and he noticed them separately, the way he had taught himself to notice things, which was to say: he noticed them and then put them down and looked at them later, usually at three in the morning. The first was a dread with a floor under it. The second was a heat, and it was shame, and it was the specific shame of a man who has spent four years being walked through and has just been, for one second, at the front of a queue.

He had three hours until the counter shut.

He turned round and went to the counter, which was the first decision of his working life that he made on purpose, and had been about to make for four years without knowing there was a decision in it.

Faber did not look up for the first eight seconds, because Faber never looked up before he had the slip in his hand, and getting the slip into his hand was the whole of his professional courtesy and he offered it to nobody for free.

Lin Hao put the slip on the counter flat. Faber read it. Read it again. Then he did something Lin Hao had not seen him do in four years, which was put the slip down and take off his glasses and clean them on the sleeve of his coat.

"Sit there," Faber said, and nodded at the little bench along the wall that people sat on when they were going to be told something that took a while.

Lin Hao did not sit. "What does it say."

"It says what it says." Faber put his glasses back on. "You want me to say a number to you, and I can't, because there's no interval, and without an interval I can't put you in a band, and without a band the plate doesn't publish, and without publishing you don't have a reading on Thursday. That's arithmetic. I'm not the one who invented it and I'm not the one who can go round it."

"What does that do to my posting."

"It doesn't touch your posting. Your posting is employment, and employment is a form, and the form doesn't care." Faber opened a drawer and took out a square of oilcloth, the kind that had been cut from the roll for twenty years and had a frayed corner on every single one of them. "What it touches is Thursday. And I've got to put you on the Thursday list, because the list is what an unsettled reading goes on. That's the procedure. That's all that is. If I don't put you on it, you don't publish, and if you don't publish you're read as nothing, and nobody upstairs will ever know there was a question."

"What is on the Thursday list."

"Forty names this week. I've got the list here." He did not have the list there; there was a sheet of carbon paper inside a clipboard on the floor behind his knee and he did not go and get it. "It's not a punishment, it's a review. Somebody reads it. You probably stand there for twenty minutes while a person from the Reading asks you where the figure came from and whether you've done anything unusual, and then they write something down, and then you go back to work. That's the whole of it."

"Who reads it."

"Not me." He was wrapping the plate. Oilcloth, twice, then the flap folded over and the corner pressed down with his thumbnail until it stayed. "Forty-one offices up. The list goes up on Wednesday night, it comes back stamped on Friday, and the person who reads it is whichever auditor is on the rota that week, and I don't know who that is and I am not paid to know."

The plate came across the counter in the flat of his hand, wrapped, and heavier than it had ever been in his life.

"So it went somewhere," he said.

"They all go somewhere."

"No." He said it more sharply than he meant to, and heard himself, and put the plate inside his coat against his ribs like a man putting a hand over a wound. "Thursday. What time."

"The Board repaints at nine. Your auditor could see you any time after ten." Faber had already turned back to his window and the next person in the line. "Take the plate up. Don't open it before Friday. It's not going to do anything different if you look at it."

He got as far as the door of the plate office and stopped, with two people behind him and a queue on the other side of the rope, and the thing that stopped him was not an idea.

It was a wall he had walked past every day for four years.

Behind the last counter, where the wall was painted a different colour to mark the part of the room the public did not stand in, there was a door with a brass plate on it, and the brass had been polished so often by shoulders going past it that the second line of the engraving had gone soft, and Lin Hao had read that plate every morning for four years because it was at eye level and there was nothing else to look at while waiting, and he had read it this morning and it had told him nothing.

He had read it a thousand times. He had thought it meant a basement where the building kept its spares, because that is what it had always looked like, and because a man reads what he expects to find.

The door was shut. Behind it was a stair that went down, and somewhere under this building there were rooms where the Fourth Branch put the questions it could not ask at a counter with a clerk who had to look people in the eye.

His reading had no band on it.

Somewhere under this building, there was a place where a figure with no rate behind it went to be given a form.

He went up to the second floor and cleaned the row of basins for the fourth time that morning, and he was eleven minutes late starting the fourth washroom, and at eleven o'clock the counter window in the plate office closed and the machine behind the wall stopped clicking, and the city of Ostara went about its Wednesday.

The second basin on the row had been running since before he started, and nobody had ever reported it, and he had never fixed it, because a washroom attendant who fixes things is a man who has been seen with a screwdriver. He turned the tap off anyway that morning. It was the only one on the row with a plug in it, and he had put the plug in himself on his second week, because he could not stand the sound.

The gate scan was at seven and everybody came through it: the counters, the filing room, the reading clerks, the messengers with the sealed tubes, the two men on the exchange counter, the supervisor of the sub-basement whose name Lin Hao had never once been told. Ninety-one people. He worked at five forty because the floor had to be clean before the people who would use it arrived, which made him the only one of the ninety-one who came in after the doors opened, and which was the whole of what his life was built on and he had never once had to say so out loud.

Lin Hao stood in a corridor with no windows holding a plate wrapped in oilcloth against his chest.

Somewhere above him in this city, tonight, a piece of paper with forty names on it was going to be carried up a hundred and forty-one steps, and on Wednesday night somebody was going to take a piece of chalk and mark one of them, and she would not know who he was, and she would not know what the number on his slip meant, and by Thursday she would stand up and ask him a question and he would have to decide, in front of a person whose job it was to write down what he said, whether the answer was true.

He had four years of answers that were true and about six seconds to get to the fourth washroom.

Forty-one offices up, on Wednesday night, a list of forty names went into a tray on a desk that had a drawer with a broken handle and had to be opened with a spoon. Ines Duarte read it with a pencil in her hand, the way she read everything, and it took her nine minutes because she did not skim records, and she had never once in fourteen years made anybody wait for a thing she could have done faster if she had been willing to do it worse.

Her own name was not on it. It never was; the rota had learned that about her the same way it learned everything else about her, which was slowly and against her will.

At the bottom of page two, under a column headed for the Fourth Branch, there was a name she had seen on an employment register for four years and had never once had cause to look at:

LIN HAO. ATTENDANT. FLOOR 2. UNSETTLED — NO INTERVAL.

She read it twice, which was unusual for her, and then she put the pencil down and sat with her hands flat on the desk, in a room on the fortieth floor with four windows and a view of the roofs of the Vestry, and she thought about the shape of the figure for a while.

An unsettled reading was supposed to be rare. Everybody in the building believed it was rare, and the belief was the most expensive thing on the forty-first floor, because the only way to be rare is to be rare, and the alternative to being rare is a number that somebody upstairs would have to be told about.

There had been eleven of them in a year. She had audited nine of them herself, and she knew the shape of every one, and it was the shape of a person whose account had a mass behind it and no way of getting from the mass to the rate, and it was never once, in nine cases, the shape of a mistake.

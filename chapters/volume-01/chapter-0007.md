# Lantern Row

He was in his room on Ferrier Street on the Friday evening with the four pages in his coat pocket and rain coming sideways off the roofs, and he did the thing he did when he could not think: he cleaned.

There was nothing in the room to clean. He had one chair, one knife, one plate wrapped in oilcloth, and a bed. He cleaned the sill. He wiped the table with a damp cloth and left it worse.

He stood in the middle of four feet by nine feet of rented floor and understood that in nine days ninety-one people were going to be read from a list by a man with a clipboard. And that nine of the forty who had gone through that room with the man with the cough in the week before last had come out of it in under twenty minutes with a stamp on a form, and that the rest had not, and that nobody in the building knew which.

He had four pages of pencil in a stranger's handwriting and a name in his own ledger that he did not know, and a wrapped plate with a dash on it, and a rule that had lasted seven days, and a key that was in a drawer on the third floor belonging to a man who had locked a door.

At seven he put his coat on and went to Lantern Row.

Lantern Row was four streets behind the Vestry and it was not a street anybody chose. There were nine rooms above a lock-up and a chandler's and a place that did nothing but hold other people's furniture, and the stair was lit by one bulb on the second landing that had been failing for a year, and four of the nine rooms were let by the week to people who had not got a plate of their own.

Ines Duarte's name was on the second one.

He had found it in the branch register — an auditor of rank two, requisitioning against the office, living at that address at a rent that a rank-two salary did not cover and had never covered. She had been there eleven months. There was a second name on the same lease, struck through, and the striking-through was in a different hand from the entry and much older.

He stood on the landing in the rain coming off his coat and found that his heart was going, and that he had been walking towards this door for four days without deciding to.

She opened it before he knocked.

She had her hat on and her coat on and a folder under her arm, and she stood in the doorway and looked at him for about two seconds with an expression of such total professional disappointment that he felt it in his stomach.

"Nineteen minutes," she said.

"What?"

"You are nineteen minutes later than the last time I decided you were going to come." She turned and walked back into the room without closing the door. "That is the third time I have said that out loud to a piece of furniture. Come in. Wipe your feet, I have mopped."

It was two rooms and a landing, and the tap on the landing was the reason it was two rooms, and the second room had a camp bed in it and a stack of paper on the camp bed that she was using as a side table. She had a lamp, a table, two chairs, a kettle, and a wall of shelves made out of two tea chests and a plank, on which sat some four hundred files tied with tape.

The window faced another building eleven feet away and gave onto a wall.

"Sit," she said, and put the folder on the table, and did not sit herself, and did not take her coat off.

"I'm sorry it took me three weeks."

"It took you a fortnight because you are a man who has spent four years making sure that nothing in a building can reach him, and something reached you, and you have been standing in a corridor ever since working out whether it can reach you again." She put her hat on the shelf. "Do not apologise to me for that. I have been an auditor for fourteen years. That is not a complaint, it is a description of the shape of the man, and it is the single most useful thing I know about you, and I have known it for a fortnight, which is a fortnight longer than you have known about anything."

Lin Hao sat down.

The room was warm and it smelled of paper and lamp oil and a cold cup of tea from some earlier point in the evening. There was nothing else in it that was not strictly necessary.

"Your reading came back on the fourth," she said, sitting down then, and putting her hands flat on the folder. "Figure 3,412, no interval. I read it on the Wednesday night and I read it again on the Thursday and on the Friday and again this morning, which is four times, and I want you to know that because I want you to know exactly how much of my professional life I have spent on your slip."

"Why?"

"Because an unsettled reading has a shape, and there are eleven of them a year in the whole city, and I have audited nine." She turned the folder round and pushed it across the table. It was four sheets, a corner cut off, and a card. "Read it. It is your file. Four pages and a card, and I have had it for a fortnight, and there is nothing on it."

He read it.

Four years of employment. The Fourth Branch, second floor, attendant, entered on a form with a date. A posting letter. The annual attestation, four of them, signed by a supervisor whose name he recognised and could not have told you anything about. A disciplinary note from four years ago about a mop.

And in the middle of it, between the second and the third attestation, two years in which he did not appear at all. Not employment elsewhere — nothing. No plate stamped, no address, no reading on the Board, no entry anywhere, as though he had gone out of the world on a Tuesday and come back on a Thursday and nobody had found it worth a line.

He had been twenty-three. He had been at a cousin's on the Vestry side doing nothing that anybody anywhere would have put in writing.

At the bottom of that gap there was one line, and it was not in the Fourth Branch's hand. It was a cross-reference, typed on a different machine in a different typeface on a card the colour of old bone:

RELATIVE OF RECORD: LIN WEI. R.6. SEE ALSO — READING OFFICE TRANSFERS, CLASS.

He read it four times.

And the room went on being exactly the same room, and the lamp went on being the same lamp, and a woman in a coat across a table was watching him without appearing to, and he felt the floor of his stomach go out of him and come back, and what came back was not a thought. It was a want. It came up out of him with a force that made him put his hand flat on the table, and it was so plain and so old and so completely undisguised that he was ashamed of it in front of a stranger.

The file. Six opened sealed filings. He had wanted nine for six years and he had been wrong for six years, and the wrongness was standing on a card in a different typeface.

Six.

"There's a cross-reference," he said.

"There is."

"To my brother."

"To a man named Lin Wei, rank six, Reading Office, whose last recorded act in the public record was a transfer three years ago into a classification I do not have the standing to open and have not attempted to open, because I am rank two and a rank-two auditor who goes fishing in a rank-six file loses her seal inside a month." She said it perfectly evenly. "I have read it four times. I have not touched it. I will not touch it."

"But you're reading it to me."

"I'm reading it to you because it is on your file, and your file is the thing I was asked to audit, and there is nothing irregular about an auditor showing a man his own page." She turned her hands over on the table. "Do you want to know what I have not been able to stop thinking about since Wednesday?"

"Yes."

"Nothing has touched it."

He looked up.

"In three weeks," she said, "not one person has gone near this file. Not the Registration side, not the deputy, not the counter. Nineteen instruments, four of them manual, and the last access entry on your page is the attestation you signed yourself eighteen months ago."

She leaned forward a little.

"That is not how a file behaves. A file with a settled account and a four-year employment history and a disciplinary note for a mop is a file that nobody looks at, and I would not be in this room if that were what I was looking at."

"Somebody has been to the trouble of keeping your file clean. And they have been doing it a long time, because three weeks is nothing. That is not somebody being careful this week. That is somebody being careful for years."

She let that sit in the room for a while.

"That is a bigger fact than anything else on the page," she said. "And you have not asked me a single question about it, which tells me you already know who."

"I don't know anybody in the Bureau."

"No. And I am not going to speculate about it out loud, in a room, on a Friday, because I am an auditor and that is the line I do not cross for anybody." She sat back. "Now. You did not come here for your file."

"I came here for the nineteenth."

"So did ninety other people." She said it patiently. "Nine of them will go down. That is what a draw-down is: ninety-one plates and nine that do not settle, and the nine go into a room in the sub-basement of this building with a table and a clipboard and a person writing down what they did for three years, and the room has no window because a window is a thing you can look out of and they do not want you to. The nine come out when the arithmetic closes. Some of them come out on the Wednesday. Some of them are there for a year."

"How do you know which nine?"

"I don't." She spread her hands. "That is the entire design of it. Nobody knows, including the nine. If you knew, you could prepare, and a person who can prepare is a person who can cheat, and the whole of the Bureau rests on a person not being able to get ready."

"Ninety-one people," Lin Hao said, "and nine of them, and nobody in that building knows which nine."

"Correct."

"And there is no form for the whole floor."

"No. There is a form for a man." She said it without any pleasure at all. "There is a form for a man and a room for a man and a corridor for a man, and there is nothing whatever in the city that was ever built for a floor. That is not a conspiracy, Lin, that is an arithmetic. Ninety-one names and nine rooms, and the arithmetic is comfortable, and the only person in this city who has ever lost sleep over it is a woman on a second floor with four hundred files and a landed kettle."

She stopped.

And Lin Hao sat there in the lamp light with the want in him going like a hand round a throat, and what he felt was not the file and was not the nine and was not rank six at all.

He felt, for the first time in four years, that he was in a room with a woman who had looked at him on purpose.

It was the single most exposed he had ever been, and it was not remotely like being afraid. He had been afraid for seven days and he had got quite good at it.

This was the other thing. It was being *wanted* — not by him; he had not asked, he could not have stopped it if he had asked — by a woman who had the single legal instrument in Ostara in her hand and had brought it to his table and shown it to him and was sitting there in her coat with her hat on the shelf.

And he understood, with a clarity that made him dizzy, that if he had said the wrong sentence in the last ten minutes she could have closed the file and left the room, and that it would have been the correct professional action, and that it would have taken her four minutes.

She was not going to do it.

That was the vertigo. Not that she had the power. That she had the power, and had put it down on the table between them where he could see it, and had asked him a question instead.

"One question," she said. "I have been at this for three weeks and it is the only one. I am going to ask it and I would rather you did not lie, and I want to be clear with you about the reason I would rather that: I am about to spend four years of my own memory on this, and I do not intend to spend it on a false answer."

"What question."

She tipped her head slightly, the way she had done on the corridor outside the plate office, three weeks and a lifetime ago.

"Who helped you."

The room was very quiet.

The lamp. The rain on a wall eleven feet away. The camp bed with four hundred files on it. A woman in a coat who had asked him the only question that had ever been asked of him that he could not answer, and she was not going to ask it twice, and she had said so by the way she sat.

Lin Hao looked at the four sheets of his own file on the table, and at the bone-coloured card with his brother's name on it in a typeface that had not been set in this building for eight years, and he thought about a floor of ninety-one people and nine rooms with no windows and a rule of his own that had lasted seven days.

He did not answer the question.

Instead he said: "There is a petition on the wall at your branch. Petition 4118. Four years back. Filed by a rank-two auditor, Ines Duarte — re score of a dependent sibling."

Ines Duarte did not move.

"Refused," he said. "No instrument of record. The act described is not scored under any classification heading available to this office." He looked at her. "There is no heading for it. That is not a thing I read in a manual. I have read every manual in that building and there is no heading for it, and I have spent four years standing in corridors with the clerks and the messengers and the girls on the pigeonholes, and I know what gets written down and I know exactly what does not, and your sister was kind in a doorway where there was nobody, and it came off her plate on the Thursday and it has never gone back on."

Ines Duarte sat absolutely still in her coat, in her own rooms, on Lantern Row, in the rain.

"Say that again," she said, very quietly, "and then take it back, because I am going to have to decide what to do with my face in about two seconds."

"I'm not taking it back."

"Then look at me and let me decide what my face does."

He looked at her.

It took rather longer than two seconds. She put one hand flat on the table over the bone-coloured card, and her eyes went somewhere that was not the room, and her lips moved once without any sound, and then she came back, and when she came back she was perfectly composed and her eyes were wet and she did not blink.

"That is the first time in four years," she said, "that a stranger has told me something about my sister that I did not already know and did not have to be a document to hear."

She wiped her face with the back of her hand, briskly, the way you wipe a counter.

"It also tells me," she said, "that you have spent four years standing in corridors learning what gets written down. Which means you already knew what the refusal said before you ever came up to this room, and you let me ask you a question three times first."

"Yes."

"All right." She put her hand flat on the bone-coloured card. "Then we both know what you came for, and I am not going to make you say it out loud in my own rooms."

Lin Hao sat on a chair in a room four feet by nine feet above a chandler's on a street nobody chose, with the audit in nine days and the ninth room with no window in it being, on any reasonable estimate, his.

"Who helped you," Ines Duarte said.

She asked it again.

She said it much more quietly, and she had not taken her coat off, and she had not stood up, and she was not going to make him say it, and she was not going to leave either, and both of those things were happening at the same time, in the same woman, in a chair she had only just sat down in.

# Nine Seats

On Thursday morning in the second week after the re-read Lin Hao stood in the Exchange box for the third time, with his plate in his hand and thirty people outside the rail, and Garrick Neve read the figure without looking up.

“Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-nine,” Neve said. “Two grains down on the published standing. Two grains inside tolerance. Band holds.”

He wrote it down and read the weekly question.

“What instrument do you hold for the figure on that plate.”

“The requisition order of the 19th and my own declaration of the 23rd,” Lin Hao said. “No other instrument.”

Neve wrote that down and read it back, and Lin Hao read it back after him, and Neve stamped the sheet and gave him the counterfoil. Three Thursdays, three counterfoils, three true answers with no instrument behind any of them.

Outside, the Board had been repainted again and his tile was still white. The painters knew it now and painted round it without being told. He stood and watched them for a minute and then walked back to the Fourth Branch, because there was nowhere else to be at nine in the morning on a Thursday when the whole city was being published.

The terms were waiting on the counter beside the soap in the second-floor washroom, in a sealed envelope with a broker’s mark on it, and he knew what it was before he opened it because there was only one house in Ostara that wrote to a man in a washroom.

Marguerite Vale had kept the terms down after he refused her on the 13th. That was what Runa had told him and what the corridor had told him and what he had believed without checking. The envelope proved it had not been the whole of the truth. She had kept something else down as well, and now it had come back.

Three sheets. The first sheet was the offer, rewritten in a clerk’s hand. The second sheet was the date and the room and the time of the first offer, in writing, with his initials on it where he had put them. The third sheet was new.

He read all three standing up with his coat still on.

The offer was the same and not the same. The figure off the Board’s arithmetic into a line held in her personally, untouchable by court, at one grain in four off the top for five years. That part had not moved. What had moved was the favour. The first time she had wanted to be the broker who found it before her chair turned over. This time the third sheet named no favour at all. It named a date six weeks ahead, a room in Bellhouse, and nine seats.

The Winter Review sat nine. He knew that from the queue sheet. Twenty-two candidates ordered by published standing, seated as many as were seated, in front of a panel with nine chairs behind the table.

The letter said she would be in the room. Not on the panel. In the room, in the public seats, with the house behind her. It said the house had told the terms round after he asked for the date instead of the money, and that asking for the date had been worth more to the house than taking the money would have been. It said the terms came back because a man who asked for the paper instead of the payment was the only kind of man the house could hold a line for.

At the bottom, in her own hand, four lines.

Come and hear them read aloud. Nine seats and one of them is yours if you hold the band. I am not asking for an answer in this room. I am asking for an evening.

He folded the three sheets up and put them in his coat pocket and finished the basins, because the basins did not care about brokers’ houses, and then he went down to the yard gate to think.

Emrys Vaz came up with the crate at six minutes past six in the evening, though this was morning, and Lin Hao corrected himself standing by the gate. Morning. The boy came through at ten past seven with the docket, because the rota had changed after the requisition, and he nodded at Lin Hao the way he always did now, kindly, at a distance.

Lin Hao did not stop him. There was nothing to stop him for. Another cold into a turned person was how you got them read, and Emrys had never felt one and knew all of it anyway, which was worse.

He went to Lantern Row at noon. Ines was at her table with the folder open and the second of May clipboard on top of it, and she looked up when he came in and put her pen down.

“Terms,” he said. He put the envelope on the table. “Read them. Tell me what they are worth.”

She read all three sheets twice and put them down.

“They are worth a room,” she said. “Nine seats in the sixth week with a broker in the public seats who has told her house you are worth holding a line for. That is leverage and it is the only leverage you have ever been offered that does not require you to spend anybody.”

“What does she want.”

“An evening. She says so.” Ines looked at him steadily. “You refused her standing up on the 13th with her own words initialled. She kept investing after you refused her. A broker in Bellhouse told the house a man in a washroom asked for the date instead of the money. That is why these are here.”

“Should I go.”

“That is not a question I can answer for you.” She squared the sheets. “Your paper with me is discharged. The month ran out on the 20th and I said the name out loud and you have not given me the word. I am not carrying that into this. Go or do not go, but do not go because you think it buys an instrument for the query. An evening with a broker is not an instrument.”

He took the sheets back and put them in his pocket.

“I know,” he said.

He walked back to the Fourth Branch and worked the rest of the shift with the envelope in his pocket, and at noon he took it to Runa’s cupboard, because there was one person in the building whose trade was reading offers.

She read all three sheets once and put them down.

“A line held in her personally,” Runa said. “One in four for five years. No road this time. That is cleaner than the 12th and it is also dearer, because a line with no road in it has nowhere to hide. Solveig has taken the column off the table. That frightens me more than the column ever did.”

“What does the house want.”

“To hold you.” She tapped the third sheet. “Nine seats. The Review sits nine and she will be in the public seats with the house round her. That is not a panel seat and it is not a bribe. It is a room that knows which way the house is leaning before the panel reads the figure.”

“Is it an instrument.”

“No.” She looked at him directly. “Nothing in Bellhouse is an instrument for a Reading Office query. A broker attests money. A query wants a hearing or a deed or a finding. Do not mistake one for the other.”

He put the sheets back in the envelope.

He stopped at the Reading Square on the way back, because the Board was being repainted and his tile sat white among the covered ones. The painters worked round it without comment. A child asked her mother what provisional meant and the mother said it meant waiting to see. He stood there a while with the envelope in his pocket, waiting to see, and then he went on.

At four he went to the Vestry because Tessa had sent word the rack had gone to fourteen, and he stood in the cold store doorway while she showed him the new board. A ward patient two floors up, temperature not down, chart and chest not agreeing, going home in the morning looking well. The stippling had crossed further past the elbow, pale blue above the joint, and the right forearm had thickened.

“You are holding,” she said. “Two grains down and the band holds. That is the drift and not a spend, and the drift is what the re-read measures.”

“How many faces.”

“More than forty-one.” She squared the new board against the thirteen. “You stood in the box and in the queue and in the corridor. Faces come back whenever you stand still in a crowd. Say the number to me.”

He looked at the rack and said fourteen out loud to her, once, the number and not the names, because that was the thing she had asked for once a month on a day he would not have come anyway, and she nodded and put her sleeves down.

At six he stopped at Cutler's Row on the way home, because Halvard was on the wall with the wire and the evening was warm. Halvard asked about the sling and he said it was off, and Halvard nodded and said a man with two arms could carry more, which was his way of saying he was glad.

At seven he went up to his room and washed and changed his shirt, because the sling had come off that morning and his forearm could bear a sleeve now if he was careful.

The anger from the box had gone. In its place was a different feeling, one he had not had since the 3rd. Fear, plainly, of a room with nine seats in it where a provisional band would be struck off a figure on the day.

For Marguerite Vale, who kept the guild’s books for money and had been direct with him in Bellhouse on the 12th, the terms meant putting her house behind a man who had refused her in writing.

He wanted, concretely, to hear the terms read aloud in the room they were written for, with the date and the time on them, before the Review took the band off him — and he knew, standing in his room with a clean shirt on, that wanting leverage was a worse reason than wanting money, which was why he went. He checked the envelope was sealed and dry before he set out, because Bellhouse deserved paper in good order, and he walked the long road so the rain would not touch it.

# The Junior's Return

The filing room on the third floor had windows, which was the only reason Lin Hao ever went into it.

He had a reason that morning, and the reason was genuine: supply room three had issued him four rolls of paper towel and no soap for six weeks, and a branch attendant could not argue for soap, but an attendant returning a requisition slip could ask a clerk to initial it, and a clerk who initials something has agreed with you in writing.

He came in with the slip and the queue inside the door was down to nine people, and the heat in there came up off the floor like the floor was a stove, and it smelled of hot paper and carbon paper and the particular sourness of triplicate.

Tobias Renn was at the second bench.

"Attendant." He did not look up. He had a jacket on with a badge on it, a cloth one, the kind a man pins on when there is no regulation jacket, and the badge said FILING ROOM in a typewriter face under a stencil of the Bureau's device. "You're the wrong counter. It's the fourth bench for returns."

"It's signed out. The line's been over three months."

"Four months."

"It's been six."

"Four." Tobias Renn wrote something down, and it was not what he was writing it about, and the corner of his mouth was doing a thing Lin Hao had seen four hundred times from four floors down. "Six weeks of soap. You're the second floor?"

"Second floor."

"Then you're not short, you're late. There's a difference and it's in the manual." He turned the form round and slid it into a wire tray. "Sign under the second line and don't push it. If you push it, it goes to the warden and then it becomes a thing somebody has to look at."

Lin Hao signed under the second line and did not push it.

That should have been the end of it. Tobias Renn had a tray with forty things in it and a queue of nine and no interest whatsoever in a man with a paper-towel requisition, and Lin Hao had a rule about the floor that had held him together for six days, which was that he had never once in four years been in a room with a man who had a file in front of him and a reason to open it.

He stood there anyway.

"How's the reading?" Tobias said.

"What?"

"Your reading. It went on the list. Thursday the fifth." Tobias put the pen down, and turned on the stool, and put his chin on one hand, and it was the most pleased Lin Hao had seen anybody in the Fourth Branch since the year started. "Sit down if you're going to stand there. No, don't sit down, you'll hold up the queue, that's the queue, that's the whole queue, you're a man who costs me ninety seconds every time."

Lin Hao stayed where he was.

"The audit's come forward," Tobias said.

Lin Hao had already read it on a noticeboard. It was on a sheet of typing paper with two drawing pins in it and it had been up since before he started.

"Monday the nineteenth," Tobias said. "Draw-down. Whole branch. Ninety-one names off the staff register and a fresh plate on every one of them."

"You'll know."

"I'll be on the desk. That's the whole point of a draw-down — the local file stays local." He said *local* the way a man says his own house. "I've done two now. Cordwain last autumn, and the Saltworks office in the spring."

"And I'll tell you the thing nobody tells you, which is that it's the *files* that matter, not the plates. Anybody can be unsettled on a plate. There'll be six, eight of them, they always get six or eight, and every one of them gets a form and a chair and a stamp and they go back to work on the Wednesday."

"It's the file that's heavy. A file with a discrepancy in it that goes back three years and can't be reconciled is a file that goes up."

"Where."

"To the Wing."

The room went on around them: the queue, the heat, the eight machines, the girl on the far bench doing the Thursday repaint sheet by hand because the department had not yet trusted the machine with a Board.

"What happens to the Wing?"

"What happens to anybody's account that doesn't settle." Tobias Renn put both hands behind his head.

"It gets a room. It gets read. Somebody in there with a clipboard writes down what you did for three years and works out what you should have scored and what you did score, and if the two numbers agree, you go home. And if they don't agree, you stay until they do."

He came down off the pillow of his own arms.

"Nobody gets punished. That's what it is. Everyone thinks it's punishment because there's a corridor and no window, but it's an *account*, it's arithmetic — it's how many grains you should have and can they find out where the missing ones went."

"You go down with a settled account and you're out the same morning. You go down with an unsettled one, you're in there until somebody can close the book. That's it. There's nothing else in it."

"Is anybody at this branch going to be in trouble."

"Everybody at this branch is going to be re-read. There's a difference and you'll find it out on the nineteenth." He picked up his pen and then put it down again, because he could tell from across the bench that Lin Hao was not going anywhere, and there is nothing a junior filing clerk wants more on a Wednesday morning than a conversation with somebody who cannot get out of it. "You're frightened. Why are you frightened, you've got four grains a week and a sink to clean. Your account's four thousand and you haven't done anything wrong in four years."

"No."

"You're in. You're on the list." Tobias Renn leaned forward. "You were on the fifth. I saw you. I see the list, it goes through my desk, it's on my desk, it comes up on the Wednesday night and it goes down on the Friday with the stamps on it. 'Unsettled, no interval.' And then, what's the second line? Two lines. Yours had two lines. Do you know what the second line was on yours?"

Lin Hao did not.

"'Common origin.'" Tobias Renn said it the way a man reads out the last line of a joke. "That's the standard second line. It's the line they put on when the instruments can't tell where a figure came from and they need to, because common origin means it's shared."

"And shared means it isn't one man's problem. It's a supply. And a supply has to be *placed* somewhere."

He looked at Lin Hao with the frank, uncomplicated enjoyment of a man who has found something good.

"You've got a supply. Do you know what a supply is? A supply is a thing that can be requisitioned. That's the word. Requisition. You can requisition a machine, you can requisition a flat, you can requisition a *supply*."

"And there's a man in this building whose entire job is finding supplies in districts that are three hundred grains short, and the Fourth Branch is four hundred and twelve short, and I have seen the number on his desk."

"So they'll take it."

"They'll want it classified first. There's a process. Everything's got a process." Tobias Renn turned back to his tray. "Attendant, you're holding up the queue, and the queue is the whole of what I do."

"Is anybody at this branch going to be in trouble."

"Everybody at this branch is going to be re-read. There's a difference and you'll find it out on the nineteenth." He picked up his pen and then put it down again, because he could tell from across the bench that Lin Hao was not going anywhere, and there is nothing a junior filing clerk wants more on a Wednesday morning than a conversation with somebody who cannot get out of it. "You're frightened. Why are you frightened, you've got four thousand grains a week and a sink to clean, and you haven't done anything wrong in four years."

On the fourth floor a man called Bernt Halse — twenty-two years in the branch's stores, a plate of his own with a reader's thumbprint worn into the face of it — was putting away carbon, and would be told on Thursday that his account had not settled, and has not been told yet, and does not know.

He has a sister who eats at the Vestry kitchen on Thursdays in a queue that goes out into the street, and every Thursday for four months she has asked him, in the way people ask a thing they have asked before, whether his branch was the one being audited. He has told her no four times.

He does not know that a woman in the Vestry arcade has been writing down the dates on the days it happens to him.

Lin Hao put his hand out to take the requisition back.

He did not do it on purpose. He did it the way a man reaches across a counter, and Tobias Renn turned the form round at the same moment and put his own hand into it, and for about a second and a half the two of them had hold of the same piece of paper, and Lin Hao felt the exact moment the floor came up through him and into the man.

Nothing happened.

That was the worst of it. Nothing happened at all — no cold in his own chest, no lightness, nothing went anywhere — and a black shape of vertigo came up through the floor instead, and his ears went, and he was grey at the left temple before he had got to the second floor.

Tobias Renn said, "—are you all right?"

"I'm all right."

"You're not all right, you're the colour of a wall." Tobias Renn put the pen down and actually looked at him, which was the single most frightening thing that had happened to Lin Hao in four years. "Are you ill? Because if you're ill that's an attendance matter and I'd have to put it in."

"I'm fine."

"Sit down for a minute."

"I can't sit down, I've got four basins."

He went out into the corridor holding the side of the door frame, and the reel went on turning behind him the whole time, and the girl on the far bench doing the Thursday repaint sheet by hand looked up once and then went back to it, because nothing that happens on that floor between seven and four is any of her business.

He cleaned four basins with a head full of noise and then went up to the plate office and pressed his plate into the back rack and read the slip standing up.

Up again. Eighty-seven grains in the five days since he had not touched a living thing, and the dash still under it, and the dash was going to outlast every one of the other numbers he had ever cared about.

He stood there with the slip in his hand and could not make his mind get round to the number, because it kept sliding off him and going back to a filing room on the third floor where a man in a cloth badge had said the word *supply* with his chin on his hand, and had not been enjoying it maliciously, and had not known a single thing about it, and had said it because it was the most interesting thing in his week.

That was the anger, and Lin Hao stood on the second floor of the Fourth Branch and found that he had it and did not know where to put it.

It was not a large feeling. It did not make him want to hit anybody. It was much worse than that. It was the specific rage of a man who has spent four years being the person in the building that nothing happens to, watching a decent, cheerful, entirely ordinary junior clerk enjoy the idea of ninety-one people being sent into a corridor with no window, and finding that there was nothing whatever he could do about it, and that he was not going to be able to do anything about it for eleven days, and that the only power he possessed in the entire city was a hand that cost him his sleep.

The anger came up in him and had nowhere to go and it came out in the wrong place, which was the requisition slip in his hand. He had crumpled it without noticing. He smoothed it out against the rack with two fingers, and it was still legible, and the second line was still on it in Tobias Renn's type:

COMMON ORIGIN. SOURCE UNPLACED.

He put the slip back in his pocket.

The rota for the night shift was on a board by the back stair and he went to read it because there was nothing else in his evening, and the supply rota had been altered.

Supply room three — the room the whole branch's cleaning stock came out of — was shut. Closed pending audit, it said, from the sixth. Key with the supervisor.

Six floors of washrooms, ninety-one people, and from the sixth there was soap and linen and paper coming out of a room with a locked door and a man holding the key who was going to be extremely careful with it, because in a draw-down month a supply supervisor who signs out stock without a counter-signature is a supply supervisor with a discrepancy in his file, and that is the *heavy* kind, and Tobias Renn had told him exactly what the heavy kind does.

From the sixth, Lin Hao had nine working days of stock to move.

He read the board for a while. Then he read it again, because he had understood the arithmetic on the first pass and did not want it to be right.

If the stock stopped, the washrooms stopped. A washroom that has stopped is not a scandal. It is a *finding*, and a finding goes into the branch's own file before the nineteenth, and the branch's file is what the draw-down reads on its first morning, and a branch that arrives at the nineteenth with a hygiene finding is a branch whose ninety-one names are read twice.

And there was a boy called Emrys Vaz, seventeen, on the yard rota, who carried stock up from the yard at six in the morning and was paid four hundred grains a week for it and whose rota line would be deleted in a moment if the stock had nowhere to come from, and who would be re-posted to the sub-levels, which are unheated and unlit and where nobody had ever stayed a whole shift.

Lin Hao put his hand flat on the rota board.

Twelve hundred grains a week, and nobody in the city knew his name, and a form somewhere said he could be reassigned at a supervisor's convenience, and in three days there would not be anything to carry.

He stood on the back stair of the Fourth Branch with a crumpled requisition slip in his pocket and the grey at his temple and eleven days on a board, and the thing he wanted, with a clarity that arrived in him like a slap, was not rank six, and it was not his brother, and it was not the file at all.

It was that Emrys Vaz should not be sent down into the sub-levels. That was it. That was the want, on that stair, on that Wednesday, and it was the first want he had ever had in his life that had a face on it that was not his own.

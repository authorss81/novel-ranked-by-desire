# Zero

The queue sheet was still up on the permissions board wall when Lin Hao left the Exchange on the morning of the 26th, and by the time he reached the Reading Square the rain had gone through his coat at the shoulders and his left forearm was aching inside the sling in the particular way that meant he had been holding it wrong for an hour.

He did not go back to look at it. He had read it twice and the second reading had put three numbers in his head that would not come out again, and he walked the long way back to the Fourth Branch because the tram would have put him past eleven doors he had already counted and he could not afford to count another one.

Forty-one minutes. He had timed it four times now and it was forty-one in the rain and thirty-eight when the pavements were dry, and the difference was the queue at the Ferrier crossing and not his leg, and he spent the whole of it doing the same division he had done on the pavement outside the Exchange.

Eleven thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine divided by four hundred. Twenty-nine and a fraction. Thirty acts in six weeks, each one in front of witnesses, each one something he had chosen rather than something that had happened to a stranger holding a door for him.

He had nineteen people and no route to a twentieth. He had known that since the first week of June and he had proved it on the 15th by walking the arc on purpose and taking twenty-two new faces in one day, which was how the count had gone to forty-one, and the count had not moved since because there was nowhere left to walk that he had not already walked.

The corridor board on the second floor had five names on it again and a gap at the top where the reason should have been. He stood in front of it with water running off his coat onto the lino and read the five the way a man reads a list he wrote himself.

Ottoline Barret. Emrys Vaz. Tobias Renn. Two he had never asked the names of and now could not ask without making it worse.

A clerk came past with a bucket and nodded at him and went on. Nobody stopped. That was the part he had not expected when he stood up on the 3rd and said the sentence out loud in front of ninety-one people. He had thought being looked at would feel like being watched. It felt like being filed.

He went into the washroom and took the sling off and hung his coat on the back of the door and ran the cold tap over his forearm until the ache went down to something he could work with. The basins were clean. He had cleaned them at seven that morning before going to the Exchange, and the soap was where he had left it, and the petition slip he had been refused at the Correction Office rail was still folded in his coat pocket with the deposit figure on it in pencil.

Four thousand one hundred and twelve folios. He had asked to see them and been told to come back with standing, and standing meant six, and six meant twenty thousand, and he had eight thousand one hundred and forty-one published on a wall in the largest type in Ostara.

He put the sling back on and went down to the yard gate at half past two to watch Emrys Vaz come up with the stock docket.

The boy came through the gate at six minutes past with a crate on his shoulder and the docket under his arm, and he put the crate down on the wall and signed for it and picked it up again without looking at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao understood in the space of that one movement that the thing he had come down to do was not available.

He had come down to look for a twentieth person. That was the shape of it and he admitted it to himself standing by the gate with rain on his face. A new face would have meant a new line, and a new line would have meant one more pair of hands that could be relieved without killing anybody, and he had walked down here to see if the yard had brought anyone new since the 15th.

It had not. The same driver, the same checker, the same boy with the same plate reading the same figure. Four hundred and forty-one, going one way. Down.

Emrys looked at him then, for about a second, and nodded, and went up the ramp with the crate. The nod was not a greeting. It was an instruction to leave him alone, delivered kindly, by a person who had understood all of it in a corridor on the 3rd and had said nothing since.

Lin Hao went back up to the second floor and took the bucket off the clerk and finished the landing himself because his hands needed something to do that was not arithmetic.

At four he went to the Vestry to count what he had.

Tessa Okonkwo was in the clinic with her sleeves down and a queue out to the door, and she saw him over the heads of the queue and shook her head once, which meant not now, and he sat on the bench in the arcade and waited until the queue went down.

Ola Bright came through with a tray and stopped when she saw him and put the tray down on the bench beside him.

“You are counting,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Do not count me twice. I am in there twenty-two times since the 18th and every one of them is the same one.” She picked the tray up again. “Nadia says you are going to do something stupid before the 29th and she says she is going to stop you.”

“Tell her I am still deciding what it is.”

“I will tell her you said that and she will say that is worse.” Ola went on with the tray.

He waited another forty minutes and Tessa came out wiping her hands and sat down on the bench beside him without looking at him.

“How many,” he said.

“You know how many.” She held her left arm out for a second and then put it down again. The sleeve was down but he knew what was under it. Above the elbow and pale blue where it had crossed, a hand’s width into the upper arm, and the right forearm begun. “Four yesterday. Two this morning. All of them between six and ten at night because that is when the shifts are.”

“Are any of them—”

“None of them is mine. All six were in a ward and every one of them went home looking well.” She looked at him at last. Her anger was tired and it was the tiredness that frightened him. “You came here to ask me whether there is anybody in this building you have not touched yet.”

“Yes.”

“There is not.” She said it plainly. “Nineteen people in four years and you walked the arc on the 15th and took twenty-two more in one day, and five of the forty-one have kept something they should have put back, and the five are Nadia and Ola and Emrys and Teodor and me. You know the list because you went and found all five of us on the 20th.”

“I know.”

“Then there is your answer.” She stood up. “You have nobody left to reach that you can reach without putting a cold into a person who has already kept one, and putting another one into a person who has kept one is how you get them read. Three in a season gets them a file. Nadia has had three since June. So has Ines now, as of the night you stood in her room and did nothing. Do the rest of it yourself.”

She went back into the clinic.

He sat on the bench and felt the want come up in him the way it always did when somebody told him the truth without padding it. The want was not to be six. The want was to find one person in this city who could be relieved without being marked, and to do it once, properly, with witnesses, and to have the four hundred grains scored and written down so the distance would come down by one.

It was a small want and it was impossible, and that was why it sat in his chest like a stone.

He walked back to the Fourth Branch in the rain and went up to the corridor board and stood in front of Emrys Vaz’s name for a long time.

Four hundred and forty-one. The figure was written against it in the clerk’s upright hand, and at the top of the list was the date of the next reading. The 29th. Three days away.

The clerk who kept the board came out with a fresh slip and pasted it over the date without looking at him.

“You are on the queue,” she said. She did not mean it unkindly. “Twenty-second of twenty-two. My sister saw it.”

“Yes.”

“You will want to be early for the box. Mr Neve reads the question first and the queue listens.”

He nodded and she went back into the office.

He stood there after she had gone and did the division one more time, because not doing it felt like cowardice and doing it felt like work, and both of those were better than standing still.

Eleven thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine. Twenty-nine deeds. Nineteen people. No twentieth.

For Emrys Vaz, who was second one down on a board with a gap at the top, the arithmetic meant a man standing outside a washroom doing sums about his plate.

He went into the washroom and bolted the door and sat on the edge of the bath with his head in his good hand until the light went.

He was afraid, and the fear had a shape now that it had not had in June. In June he had been afraid of being read. Now he was afraid of reading somebody else, because the best remaining lead he had was already turned, and he knew what another cold would do to a turned person.

That was the evening of the 26th, and he had zero to spend, and the Board would repaint on Thursday, and the only instrument in the city that could answer a query from the Reading Office was one he had not got.

# Ola Bright

Lin Hao found Ola Bright in the corridor outside the Vestry clinic at ten past nine in the morning, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and a pencil behind her ear, and she looked up and grinned at him like he was the continuation of a joke.

"You," she said. "Landing man. Tray man. Cold hands."

He had come to take the notebook out of circulation. He had rehearsed it on the tram, all the way down from Ferrier Street with the doors sighing open at every stop and strangers steadying each other without looking, and every version had sounded like a man from the Branch explaining a regulation to a woman in a kitchen apron.

None of them survived her face. She was delighted to see him, completely, without any of the calculation he had learned to expect from people who had something he wanted, and he felt the familiar shame of wanting to be looked through rise up and then go flat, because she was looking straight at him.

"I want to read it," he said. "All of it. Properly. Sitting down."

Ola Bright blinked. "The book?"

"Forty-one pages. Eleven entries. All of it, for as long as it takes."

She pulled the school exercise book out of her apron pocket and held it against her chest. "Nobody's ever asked that. They ask to have a look. They want the funny bit about the lift. You're asking like it's work."

"It is work," Lin Hao said. "Yours."

That landed. He watched it land, and he was glad and then immediately unsure whether he should be glad, because using the fact of her four months as a fact in her life rather than a problem in his was the most adult thing he had done in four years and he had no idea if it was kind.

Behind her the clinic door stood open on the corridor, and the queue that was always there in the morning went in and came out again in about the time it takes to read a chart: a man with a holdfast strapped to his forearm, a woman carrying somebody else's boots, two boys off the yard school who had come for the free meal and would not look at the food.

Ola was sitting outside all of it with her back to the wall because she had started at seven and had done her three mornings, and the pencil behind her ear was a stub worn down to a flat end.

He had a sheet of his own paper in his coat with four columns ruled on it, and a case with two pencils in it, and he had brought both because the plan was to sit down beside her and copy the entire thing while she talked. He looked at the shape of the paper through the cloth. He put his hand flat over it.

The plan was four hours of her attention and his handwriting in a book she could reach whenever she liked. There was a version of this morning in which she never once noticed him doing it.

That was the whole trouble. He could have done it. She was on the floor and tired and had no reason at all to be suspicious of a man holding paper.

Nadia Ferrous came through the corridor then with a bucket and a laugh, on her way from the arcade car to the kitchens, and she stopped when she saw him and said, "Oh, you're in trouble now, she's going to read you the whole thing," and went on through, cheerful and loud and entirely herself, and Lin Hao kept his hands still and did not look after her longer than he had to.

He could not be in a room with Nadia Ferrous. Not because she frightened him. Because she had felt it three times in a week and had told everybody, and every telling put a line under his name he had not written.

Ola shouted after her, "He's here about my book," and Nadia shouted back over her shoulder, "It's about your spine," and went through the kitchen door laughing, and Ola turned to him with her eyebrows up.

"She's decided you're mine now," she said. "That took eleven seconds. I'm very quick."

He smiled, and the smile was real, which he noticed with the part of himself that was still doing arithmetic.

He walked her to the arcade and let her sit him down on the low wall by the bread stall, where the morning crowd moved past in a steady wash of coats and baskets and held doors, and she opened the book at the first page and read it aloud to him, fast, funny, without malice, stopping to act out the faces.

The arcade was the widest room in the Vestry and it was not a room at all. Doors were the business there. A man held a door for a woman with a pram. A girl took a parcel up eight steps for somebody who could not manage steps. Two porters moved a trestle table out of the path of a queue and put it back afterwards at the correct angle. Nobody made a mark of any of it, because there was nothing to say about a door held by a man who would have held it anyway.

"Cold," Ola said, pointing. "Arcade. Lift. Again. That's my whole vocabulary. Cold, arcade, lift, again. I never asked anybody to explain it and I don't want to. If somebody explains it, it stops being mine."

She turned the page and did the face, and then she did the one after it, and the one after that was the same face done smaller.

"Nadia does the one where she laughs first," she said. "I do the one where I say something about charging rent. Emrys does the one where he goes very still and says thank you, and then looks at his hands, and I have written *thank you* in the book about nine times and underlined it twice, which I know is not a thing I should have done."

Her timetable came out between entries, the way a timetable does when somebody is too busy to be asked for it. Four afternoons in the kitchens, the Friday supper run because she liked the tram, three mornings on the clinic rota because her sister made her. A third-floor flat above a chandler's with two names on the lease, a flatmate who talked, a rota that put her in the arcade four days a week.

"He asks me about the flat," she said. "Every time. He wants to know if anybody else has it and I say not yet and he says right, not yet, and he goes back to his crossword. His name's Mord and he's not a spy, he's just nosy, and I like him for it."

Lin Hao wrote nothing down, and the not writing was the hardest thing he had done that morning, including the walking in.

He asked about the man from the Church. She waved the pencil.

"Him? He stood there for twenty minutes while Nadia talked. He took notes. I said, Nadia, he's taking notes, and she said he wants to help." Ola shrugged, generous and bored at once. "He was nice. He had a nice coat. I told him he could have the funny bit if he wanted. It's already somewhere it shouldn't be, isn't it?"

"It is," Lin Hao said. "Which is why I'm asking and not telling."

"Ooh." She sat up straighter against the wall. "That's a different sentence. That's the first different sentence anybody's given me about this."

She looked at him then, properly, for the first time that morning, and he saw the whole of her face at once, the quickness that was not stupidity and the generosity that was not carelessness, and he understood that the danger she was in was the danger of being interesting to the wrong room.

"Third one's different," he said. "You know that. You wrote it down and you didn't say why, but you know."

Ola stared at him. Then she flipped to the back page and wrote it down, in pencil, under the last entry, while he watched her do it and did not have to be asked to.

Third one different — L says.

"You'll get me in trouble," she said cheerfully, and tucked the book back into her apron. "Good. I'm bored out of my skull. There hasn't been trouble in that flat since Nadia put a bucket on the lift roof."

He left the book with her. That was the decision, made sitting on a low wall with bread smell all round him and a tram bell going outside, and it felt like stepping off a stair. Taking it would have been easy and would have finished her. Leaving it meant trusting a woman who told strangers everything.

He stood up with the ruled sheet still folded against his chest and did not take it out of the coat. Whatever was in that book he was going to have to ask her for a second time, and she would be the one deciding whether to give it, and that was the price of the four columns and it was a fair one.

He walked her back to the kitchens and left her at the door with the book in her pocket and his one true thing under it, and the door went shut behind her and he stood in the corridor and heard the whole of the vestibule go to noise again: four trays off the rail, a knife going through a stack of bread, somebody's cousin being let in at the back.

She called after him through the glass. "Come at the end of the week, I'll save you a roll," and he said he would, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

Ines was waiting in the arcade with her file under her arm, and she had that look she got when paper had done something she respected.

"You didn't take it," she said.

"I asked to read it. All of it. Sitting down."

"And?"

"Forty-one pages. Eleven entries. Two places, one lift, one arcade. A fortnight of supper runs and a rotation that follows the cars." He stopped. "It's dated. It's mapped. It's an intelligence source that doesn't know it is one."

Ines nodded slowly. "And the names?"

He had been saving it. He had not wanted to say it in the kitchens, with the steam and the clatter, because saying it there would have made it a kitchen thing and it was not.

"Beatrix Halm," he said. "Eleven times. On four of them it's spelled Bright."

Ines went very still. "Somebody in this city has been writing down which cars she has driven."

"Where does she live?"

"Yard Row. Over the depot store, two rooms." Ines had her chin on the file. "Lin. A signal operator has a roster and a shift and a car number stamped on the bulkhead. She does not need anybody to write her down. Somebody is doing her work for her, and doing it badly, which means doing it out of pencil and memory."

"Then it's a person, not an office."

"It is a person who has been at it a long time and has not been asked once." She started walking and he walked with her. "And you have just been in a kitchen telling the one person writing it down that you want to read her work properly. Do you know what that is?"

"Telling her somebody's reading it."

"Admitting you," Ines said, "and being much better at it than anybody expected."

He felt cold then, with the morning crowd moving past them both, because a notebook that held his feeders also held the woman who felt them, and the woman who felt them drove a tram.

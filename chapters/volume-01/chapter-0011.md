# Ola Bright

Lin Hao found Ola Bright in the corridor outside the Vestry clinic at ten past nine in the morning, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and a pencil behind her ear, and she looked up and grinned at him like he was the continuation of a joke.

"You," she said. "Landing man. Tray man. Cold hands."

He had come to take the notebook out of circulation. He had rehearsed it on the tram, all the way down from Ferrier Street with the doors sighing open at every stop and strangers steadying each other without looking, and every version had sounded like a man from the Branch explaining a regulation to a woman in a kitchen apron.

None of them survived her face. She was delighted to see him, completely, without any of the calculation he had learned to expect from people who had something he wanted, and he felt the familiar shame of wanting to be looked through rise up and then go flat, because she was looking straight at him.

"I want to read it," he said. "All of it. Properly. Sitting down."

Ola Bright blinked. "The book?"

"Forty-one pages. Eleven entries. All of it, for as long as it takes."

She pulled the school exercise book out of her apron pocket and held it against her chest. "Nobody's ever asked that. They ask to have a look. They want the funny bit about the lift. You're asking like it's work."

"It is work," Lin Hao said. "Yours."

That landed. He watched it land, and he was glad and then immediately unsure whether he should be glad, because using the fact of her four months as a fact in her life rather than a problem in his was the most adult thing he had done in four years and he had no idea if it was kind.

Nadia Ferrous came through the corridor then with a bucket and a laugh, on her way from the arcade car to the kitchens, and she stopped when she saw him and said, "Oh, you're in trouble now, she's going to read you the whole thing," and went on through, cheerful and loud and entirely herself, and Lin Hao kept his hands in his pockets and did not look after her longer than he had to.

He could not be in a room with Nadia Ferrous. Not because she frightened him. Because she had felt it three times in a week and had told everybody, and every telling put a line under his name he had not written.

Ola led him to the arcade and sat him down on the low wall by the bread stall, where the morning crowd moved past in a steady wash of coats and baskets and held doors, and she opened the book at the first page and read it aloud to him, fast, funny, without malice, stopping to act out the faces.

"Cold," she said, pointing. "Arcade. Lift. Again. That's my whole vocabulary. Cold, arcade, lift, again. I never asked anybody to explain it and I don't want to. If somebody explains it, it stops being mine."

Her timetable came out between entries. Four afternoons in the kitchens, the Friday supper run because she liked the tram, three mornings on the clinic rota because her sister made her. A third-floor flat above a chandler's with two names on the lease, a flatmate who talked, a rota that put her in the arcade four days a week.

He asked about the man from the Church. She waved the pencil.

"Him? He stood there for twenty minutes while Nadia talked. He took notes. I said, Nadia, he's taking notes, and she said he wants to help." Ola shrugged, generous and bored at once. "He was nice. He had a nice coat. I told him he could have the funny bit if he wanted. It's already somewhere it shouldn't be, isn't it?"

"It is," Lin Hao said. "Which is why I'm asking and not telling."

She looked at him then, properly, for the first time that morning, and he saw the twenty-six years in her face, the quickness that was not stupidity and the generosity that was not carelessness, and he understood that the danger she was in was the danger of being interesting to the wrong room.

"Third one's different," he said. "You know that. You wrote it down and you didn't say why, but you know."

Ola stared. Then she flipped to the back page and wrote it down, in pencil, under the last entry, while he watched.

Third one different — L says.

"You'll get me in trouble," she said cheerfully, and tucked the book back into her apron. "Good. I'm bored out of my skull. Nobody's been in trouble in that flat since Nadia put a bucket on the lift roof."

He left the book with her. That was the decision, made sitting on a low wall with bread smell all round him and a tram bell going outside, and it felt like stepping off a stair. Taking it would have been easy and would have finished her. Leaving it meant trusting a woman who told strangers everything.

He walked her back to the kitchens and left her at the door with the book in her pocket and his one true thing under it, and she called after him, "Come at the end of the week, I'll save you a roll," and he said he would, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

Ines was waiting in the arcade with her file under her arm, and she had that look she got when paper had done something she respected.

"You didn't take it," she said.

"I asked to read it. All of it. Sitting down."

"And?"

"Forty-one pages. Eleven entries. Two places, one lift, one arcade. A fortnight of supper runs and a rotation that follows the cars." He stopped. "It's dated. It's mapped. It's an intelligence source that doesn't know it is one."

Ines nodded slowly. "And the names?"

He had been saving it. He had not wanted to say it in the kitchens, with the steam and the clatter, because saying it there would have made it a kitchen thing and it was not.

"Beatrix Halm," he said. "Eleven times. On four of them it's spelled Bright."

Ines went very still. "Somebody in this city," she said, "has been writing down which cars she has driven."

He felt cold then, with the morning crowd moving past them both, because a notebook that held his feeders also held the woman who felt them, and the woman who felt them drove a tram.

# A Cold Minute, Recorded

At ten past twelve, with the lamp off and the room cold and one plate on the table between two sets of handwriting, Ines Duarte put on her coat and became an instrument.

She weighted the corners of the four pages with the sugar bowl and the inkwell and a book, and she read them standing up, and she read them in a sequence — the fourth sheet, then the second, then the first, then the third — because, he understood after a while, she was reading them in the order the cold had happened.

"Eleven entries across four sheets," she said, "going back four months. It begins on the fourth of June, which is a Tuesday, and it does not stop. That is the part that matters." She put her finger on the top line. "It is not grief. It is *enthusiasm*. Look at the intervals — nine days, five days, three days. A woman who is frightened of something writes it down for a week and then stops. A woman who has found a hobby writes it down for four months."

"She's not frightened."

"She should be."

He said: "She's bored."

Ines looked up. "Say more about that."

"Nineteen," he said. "Well. Thirty. She did it again on the Friday. She had a tray of suppers, she caught my arm on the step, and she made a joke about it before she'd let go. She hasn't been frightened once. She put the word *again* at the bottom of the fourth sheet and underlined it twice."

Ines sat down.

"Then she is not a victim of a pattern," she said. "She is a documentarian. Do you understand what that is? A frightened person with a third cold minute goes to a doctor or to the intake desk and it is a complaint. A bored woman with a third cold minute writes it on the back of a supper list and *keeps going*, and the reason she keeps going is that it is the only interesting thing that has ever happened to her."

"She gave me the sheets."

"She gave you the sheets," Ines said, "because you are the first person who has ever picked them up."

She wanted the ledger and he could not give it to her, and the ten minutes that followed were the least pleasant of the night.

He tried four ways. He said he could not describe it. He said it was not written. He said it did not have an address, and then he said the address was him, and watched her face close while he was saying it.

At the end of it he put his hands flat on the table and said: "It counts people. It has never once told me what a person did, and it is never going to, and if you are asking me to use it as evidence then you are asking a man to invent a document that does not exist, and I have watched a person invent one in this building, and it was three years ago, and he was my brother."

Ines looked at him for a while.

"Then I will work with the count," she said. "How many lines."

"Fourteen."

"Turned?"

"Two."

She wrote it down. She wrote both numbers down in the black notebook, in a column, and underlined them, and he watched her do it and understood that those two figures were now in the only handwriting in Ostara that could not be stopped.

"And how many of the fourteen have you put your hand on?"

"Three."

She stopped writing.

"Three," she said. "Out of fourteen."

"One of the three is a woman called Nadia Ferrous. One is a man called Halvard Rees who splices wire. One is you."

"There are eleven others."

"There are eleven others." She put the pen down very carefully. "Lin. I am going to ask you a question and I want the answer before we go anywhere, because it changes what we are walking into. Those eleven. Where were they?"

He thought about it, properly, for the first time.

"They were on the tram," he said. "They were in the building. They were at the branch."

"You put your hand on them."

"No."

"Then put this to yourself and sit with it." Ines came round the table. "You have understood that you can give a person back something they are owed by touching them. What you have not understood is that touching them is not what fills you up. Standing in the same carriage as them is."

He did not say anything.

"They were on the tram with you," she said, "and you did not touch a soul. Eighty-eight grains in two days at the start of that week, and one hundred and fifty-six in the nine days after, and in both of those weeks your hands stayed in your pockets. Every day you spent on a tram and a lift and a building in which the doors are held open by strangers for people who cannot manage them alone. You have been feeding it all your working life by *not being the sort of man anybody looks at*, and the reason it filled is that nobody on that tram could have told you afterwards that you were on it."

She stopped, and her voice changed.

"That is the part I want you to sit with," she said. "It does not need your permission. It does not need your hand. It is not asking you for anything. It has been running for months on the strength of the fact that you are invisible, and the moment you become visible to any of them — the moment you are a known quantity to the eleven of them, or to one of them — the rate drops, and you will feel it drop, and it will feel exactly like being hungry."

"I know," Lin Hao said. "I worked it out on Thursday, on a piece of typing paper with two drawing pins in it."

The argument came at one in the morning and it was short and it was ugly.

"You are going to be alone with it."

"Go to bed, Ines."

"No, listen." She was not angry. That was the trouble with arguing with her; she did the thing she did with a file, which was to keep it open.

"Here is the arithmetic and you have had a week to do it and you have not done it. A turned line is reportable. That is not a Bureau opinion, that is a regulation, it is the fourth subsection, and the reason it exists is that a city which cannot classify an unmeasured kindness still has to be able to say *somebody did this to her* when a fourth one happens."

"The moment a diary with four entries and a place in it reaches a desk, the desk does arithmetic. And the arithmetic is: which room, which hour, which hand. And if the answer is a lift on the Vestry side with two women on the rota, then the answer takes about ninety minutes."

"And then they come for me."

"Then they come for you. Yes."

"Then it's me. Not her." He put his hand flat on the table. "That is the deal. That is the only deal there has ever been. If nobody is looking at her, then nothing happens, and if something happens it is because I put my hand on her, and I did not, and I am not going to be the reason a woman in a kitchen with no reading to lose ends up in a room with no window over a *diary*."

"And you will do that alone?"

"Yes."

"Lin Hao." She put her palms down, and she looked at him, and she was so tired that it showed.

"A fortnight ago you were invisible because you were frightened, and being invisible is a shield, and I want you to hear me say this once. It has never once been anything else. Invisible is not a protection. It is a *location*."

"And you have been hiding in it, and in exchange for hiding in it, every single person who ever held a door for you has been quietly paying into you, and none of them will ever know they did it, and there is no line anywhere on earth on which to write it down. The only difference between your accounting and the Wing's is that the Wing writes it down."

She stopped.

She was breathing rather hard, and it was the first time all night that he had seen her out of control, and he understood that she had been holding four thousand one hundred and nine files in her head for fourteen years and that one of them had just been her sister.

"I am not coming because you need me," she said. "I am coming because I am the only person in Ostara who can find out where a person of no reading sleeps, and that is the only thing I have ever been useful for, and if you send me back to this room tonight then in eight days a man in a uniform will come to this door instead and ask me the same question with less patience and no lamp."

He did not answer for a long time.

"You slept four hours," he said.

"I slept six. It is the first time in four years." She said it flatly. "Do not begin managing me. I have been managing myself for fourteen years and I am perfectly good at it, and I have decided."

They went down at half past four in the morning in the rain.

The Vestry line ran from Lantern Row down past the arcade and stopped at the market, and at that hour it was three people and a conductor and the two of them, and Ines sat with her file on her knees going through the Vestry premises register in the notebook, because she had requisitioned it at four in the morning from a building she had no standing in, illegally, over a telephone wire, and she had done it in eleven minutes.

"Bright, Ola," she said, under her breath. "Premises register, Vestry arcade and the two lifts, third-floor flat, two names on the lease." She looked up. "She is not a list of anything. She has no plate issue. She has a *premises* registration because the Vestry rents to people who cannot produce papers, and that is the only paper in the city with her name on it, and it says where she sleeps."

"And her job."

"Nadia is the attendant. Ola does four afternoons a week in the Vestry kitchens and she does the branch supper run on Fridays because she likes being on a tram, and she has a rota at the clinic three mornings because her sister makes her." Ines closed the notebook. "Nine hundred people a day go through that arcade, and Ola Bright has been in the middle of all of it for four months being pleasant to strangers with a pencil."

They came down into the arcade at ten to five and it was wet and it smelled of bread and wet stone and there was nobody in it at all except a man with a barrow.

At the far end, where the arcade opened onto the vestibule of the free clinic, there were benches.

They had been put out overnight — six of them, two rows, put there by somebody who had done it on purpose, which meant that somebody had done this before and had thought about where the people would sit. There were about thirty people on them and standing about them, in the cold, at five in the morning, and every one of them was waiting.

The Vestry shelter hall had been open since six the previous evening. Lin Hao could see it from the end of the arcade, because it was the room on the left with the door propped, and inside it there were people on the floor.

None of them were in a hurry.

That was the thing that went into him and stayed, walking down the length of that arcade at five in the morning: not one person in thirty was in a hurry, and not one of them had anywhere else to go, and every one of them had come anyway, on foot, in the rain, and had stood in a doorway for twelve hours because a porter had said *come back in the morning*.

And a woman in a woollen hat was sitting on the end of the front bench with a paper cup in both hands, and she had a notebook open on her knees, and she was writing in it.

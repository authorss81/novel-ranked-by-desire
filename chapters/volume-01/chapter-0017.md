# The Cab at the End of the Shift

The yards at eleven at night were rain on steel and lamplight coming off wet roofs, and the north gate had no guard on it, exactly as the cloth on his shoulder had promised. Beatrix Halm met him at the door of the number nineteen with her exercise book under her coat and said what she wanted before she said hello.

"I want a use for what I feel," she said. "Not a man. If you're here for anything else, go home."

She was direct, and tired, and angry at her own division in a way that had nothing to do with him, and she leaned in close to hear him because the yards were extremely loud and she was deaf in the right ear since the Veil and joked about it the way people joke about a roof that leaks.

"Deaf side," she said, tapping it. "You'll have to talk into the good one. Busy evenings take my head off. Forty thousand people in here, all moving, all at once, and it isn't a gift. It's a headache with a roster."

He said, "I've come for the book."

"Good. Then you'll pay for it." She looked him up and down, once, frankly, the way an operator checks a brake before she trusts it with a load. "I decide. Quickly and out loud. I want you, and I want the use more than I want you, and I'm not shy about saying so, because being shy about it wastes both our time. If that suits, come up. If it doesn't, the gate's behind you."

It suited.

He felt want and fear together, plainly and at the same moment, and the concrete want was her four years: the district's turned lines off her pages and onto somebody who could use them.

She took him up into the cab of the nineteen. It was small and warm, with the window fogged at the edges, the controls dark, a dead radio making a small ticking sound, and the smell of oil and wet wool. She shut the door behind them, and the yard noise went out like a lamp being turned down, and she put the book on the control ledge and turned round.

"And you spend them on people," she said.

"I will."

"Say it properly. Out loud. Before we start. That's the price."

"And I spend them on people," he said. "Not on myself. On people."

"Good." She nodded, satisfied, the way a woman nods when a fare is right, and put her hands on his coat. "Then we can begin."

She was nothing like Ines. Ines was a document that walked, precise and held and careful even in abandon. Beatrix was a signal, all current and heat, impatient with anything that wasted time, and she opened the scene because she had decided to and not because she had been overcome by anything.

She got his coat open and pushed it off his shoulders onto the driver's seat, and got her own jacket off and dropped it over it, and pulled him to her by the front of his shirt and kissed him hard, with her mouth open, breathing through her nose, making a low sound when he put his hands on her waist.

"Here," she said. "Hands here. You're allowed. I said so."

He got his hands under her shirt and found her ribs and the warm skin over them, and she was broader than she looked in uniform, strong from the brake lever, with a scar on her left side where something had caught her years ago on a rail. She watched his face while he touched her and did not look away.

"Good," she said. "Again. Slower. I like that."

She unbuttoned his shirt the rest of the way and put her mouth to his chest, biting lightly, then licking exactly where she had bitten, and he gasped and put a hand on the back of her head and she shook him off.

"No. Let me," she said. "I do the driving. That's the arrangement."

She got his belt open and his trousers down far enough to get a hand in, and took hold of him through his underwear, firmly, feeling the length of him come up under her palm, and made an approving noise and rubbed him through the cloth until he was hard and pushing into her hand.

"Oh, good," she said. "That's good. You're a quiet man and you're not quiet here at all."

She pushed his underwear down and took him in her hand, skin on skin, and stroked him from base to tip with a steady grip, twisting slightly at the head, watching the drops gather there. He was breathing hard with his head back against the cab window and the rain hammering the roof above them.

"Look at me," she said. "I want you looking."

He looked. She kept stroking him, faster, and with her other hand she undid her own trousers and pushed them down over her hips and out of the way, and showed him the dark hair between her legs, already wet, gleaming in the lamplight coming through the window.

"Touch me," she said. "Same as I do you. No asking."

He put his hand between her legs and found her hot and slick, and she spread her knees wider on the narrow cab floor and pushed down into his fingers. He worked her with two fingers, circling the hard knot at the top of her, and she gasped and rocked forward onto his hand.

"There. There, don't move. Stay there."

He stayed. She rode his fingers, grinding slowly, with her hand still working him, both of them breathing loud in a space meant for one person and a fare, fogging the window further. The rain came off the yards in sheets and drummed on the steel roof like somebody counting.

"More," she said. "That's it. Don't you dare be polite about it."

He worked her harder and she swore at him happily about it, and her free hand went into his hair and pulled, and he let her, and when he shifted his grip she made a noise that was not a word.

She came first, with a sharp cry she did not muffle, shaking hard, clutching his wrist to hold him exactly where she needed him, and he felt her pulse under his fingers and watched her face go open and undefended for about two seconds before she shut it again.

Then she turned round, braced both hands on the control ledge, and said, "Now. From behind. I want to feel you come inside me while the rain's on the roof."

He stepped up behind her and guided himself to her, slick from her spend, and pushed in slowly. She was tight and hot and wet, and she pushed back onto him with a groan, taking him deeper.

"Yes. All of it. Give me all of it."

He thrust, holding her hips, with the cab rocking slightly under them and her shoulder against the window, and she met every stroke and urged him louder. The window fogged completely. His hands slipped on her sweat. She reached back without looking and dug her nails into his thigh and he felt it the whole way through.

"Harder. Don't be decent about it. I told you, I do the driving, but I like it hard."

He drove harder and faster, and she cried out with each one, and he felt himself climbing, the pressure building at the base of his spine. She put a hand between her legs and worked herself while he moved in her, gasping, "I'm going again, don't stop, don't stop," and she spent a second time round him, clenching hard, and that took him over.

He spent inside her with a groan, jerking deep, holding her hips hard enough to bruise, and she leaned back against his chest and held his arms round her while he pulsed into her.

Neither of them said anything for a while. The rain went on. The dead radio ticked.

"It was you, then," she said eventually, to the fogged glass. "This afternoon. The interchange. The four hundred."

"Yes."

"I felt it in the brake." She laughed, one syllable, and it was not a happy sound and it was not an angry one. "I put a man through a wall last winter for less and I didn't even see him."

"You could have taken it out on me."

"I could have taken a great deal out on you," she said, "and I have got a shift at six and a hearing I can't afford and a book that says three hundred and eleven of you this month. So I'm going to do the thing I do."

She pulled her trousers up and did his belt for him with two fingers, the way somebody who has done it four thousand times does it.

"Which is?"

"Stop feeling sorry and go and be useful." She turned round. "That's the whole arrangement, that is. I'm not asking you for anything I can't check."

He said, "Beatrix—"

"Don't." She put two fingers against his mouth. "You'll say something now and I'll have to decide whether I believe it, and it's quarter past eleven and I have to be behind this wheel at half. Say it in writing, in the morning, or say it to somebody else and let them hear it. Out loud, in front of a person who can repeat it. That's what I want. A person who can repeat it."

Afterwards they lay on the roof of the tram in the rain, because she wanted air and because she wanted to show him the city from up there, with his coat spread over both of them and the yards spread out below in orange and black. She was warm and heavy and finished, and he had never been handed anything like what she handed him then.

She gave him the whole book. All four years of it, out loud, page by page, in the rain, with her finger going down the columns. Dates. Cars. Clusters along the Ferrier line, heavier in the last eight months. Cold minutes she had felt and cold minutes she had only inferred from a driver's face. And a column at the back she had ruled herself in a hand that got worse as the winter went on, headed TURNED, with a tick against a name and nothing written beside the name at all.

He asked her what a tick was.

"A cold I felt and did not put back," she said. "That's all a tick is. I could tell you the address of every one of them. I don't, because I've never met anybody who asked me for an address."

"And the name in the back."

"I've seen it on a staff register." She pulled the coat up over her knees. "Somewhere in your building. It's on a rota. I've never met the man."

She said the word *turn* out loud for the first time, up there on the roof, and told him what a pattern of nine did to an analyst in her own division. Nine turned lines in one season was a legible shape. Any division with a competent person and a wall map could take the shape and put a dot on it, and the dot was not a street, it was a house, and the house was not the man, it was everybody within four minutes' walk of him.

"And a good analyst," she said, "doesn't have to do it twice."

She looked down at the yards, and at the tram roof under the water, and at the city going out to the edge of what the rain would let them see.

"You've got three," she said. "I've had mine for four years and I counted them the way I count rain."

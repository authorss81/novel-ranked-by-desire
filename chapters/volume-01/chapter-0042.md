# What a Sealed File Asks

The Exchange box was a square marked on the floor of the ground-floor plate office with a rail on three sides and a stamp on a post, and on Thursday the 22nd, two days before the queue sheet went up on the wall, Lin Hao stood in it with his plate in his hand and forty people standing outside the rail listening.

He had stood there once before. The first time he had been rank five and provisional and the question had been where the movement came from, and he had answered it in front of forty people with the sentence about standing where strangers held doors for each other. This time Garrick Neve looked at him over the rail with a different sheet on the desk and a different set to his shoulders, and Lin Hao knew before the clerk opened his mouth that the question had changed.

“Lin Hao,” Neve said. He read the plate number out and the figure and the band line, with the word provisional on it the way the Board printed it. “Floor eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Tolerance three grains. Last witnessed deed, the 19th, Cordwain.”

He put the plate down on the desk.

“The weekly question under a provisional band,” Neve said, “is put by the index. This week the index asks what instrument you hold for the figure on that plate.”

Forty people went very quiet. That was the design of the box. A man read out loud and read back, and the queue listened, and what was said in the box was public by the time the man stepped out of it.

“I hold the requisition order of the 19th,” Lin Hao said. “Executed at six. Thirty-one dwellings. My name on it as subject, account carried, present by attestation of the 3rd.”

Neve wrote it down.

“The index asks what attestation you hold for the five names struck off the Fourth Branch board on the 3rd.”

“I hold my own declaration of the 23rd,” Lin Hao said. “Filed in the front of a Field file. Five names struck by declaration and not by evidence. No instrument against any of the five.”

Neve wrote that down too, and the pen made a sound on the paper that carried across the room.

“The index asks what petition you intend to bring at six.”

Lin Hao looked at him. This was the new part. The first two questions had been on the sheet. The third one had not been asked before, and Neve’s eyes said he knew it.

“A sealed file,” Lin Hao said. “Four thousand one hundred and twelve folios. Held at the Reading Office under my brother’s name.”

“What does a sealed file ask of a petitioner.”

It was not a question he had prepared for. He stood in the square with his left forearm in the sling and his plate on the desk and forty people behind the rail, and he thought about the rail at the Correction Office where he had offered the file number and been told to come back with the deposit, and about the clerk on the first floor who had entered his reading on the 3rd and said she could not invent him an instrument.

“A rank,” he said. “A held rank, not a provisional one. A deposit. And a form with the folio count on it.”

“What deposit.”

“I do not know.” He said it out loud because there was no other answer that would survive being read back. “I have not been told.”

Neve nodded and wrote that down, and then he read the whole of it back to him, question and answer, in order, the way the rule required, and Lin Hao repeated it back word for word with the queue listening.

When it was done Neve stamped the sheet and handed him the counterfoil.

“The cheaper price,” Neve said, in the lower voice he used when he was not reading. “You asked me about it on the 11th and I put it on the record. The cheaper price is standing here. The dearer one is the date at the top of your band line. Seven days now.”

“The 29th.”

“The 29th.” Neve squared the papers. “A provisional band holds you to the re-read. On the re-read the machine takes the figure on the plate and puts it against the floor, and three grains is the tolerance. Four grains down and the band lapses before the Review ever sees you.”

Lin Hao put the counterfoil in his coat pocket.

Outside the Exchange the Board was being repainted. It was Thursday and the painters were up on the frame with the white paint for the week’s moves, and his tile was still white with the black six on it and the line under it reading provisional six, struck on a requisition of property, Cordwain, 19th.

He stood in the crowd and watched them paint around it. Two thousand people looked at nine hundred names and none of them looked at his, because a man going from five to six on a requisition was ordinary in a week when thirty-one dwellings had come off a row.

Runa Sato came up beside him with her hands in her coat pockets.

“You answered the third one wrong,” she said.

“I answered it truly.”

“Those are not the same thing in that box.” She nodded at the frame. “A sealed file asks for a rank and a deposit and a form, yes. It also asks for the petitioner to be in the room on the day, with the band on the plate and not on the wall. Your band is on the wall. On the 29th the machine will look for it on the plate and it will not find it unless the figure holds.”

“What holds it.”

“Nothing you have got.” She said it without heat. “You have spent nobody since the requisition. Tessa told me. That is correct and it is also the reason the figure is going to drift. The figure moves wherever you stand, Lin Hao. You walked forty-one minutes this morning instead of riding eleven. Every door you went past put something in it and every cold you did not put out kept something in it, and the re-read measures the drift.”

He looked at her. “Then what do I do.”

“You stand in that box every Thursday until the Review and you answer truly, and you do not spend anybody, and you hope the drift is under three grains.” She took her hands out of her pockets. “And you find an instrument before the query finds you. The clerk who entered your reading on the 3rd owns the query and she cannot invent you one. A query wants an instrument and an instrument is a hearing or a deed or a finding, and you have none of those.”

They walked back across the square together without speaking.

At the Fourth Branch the corridor board had been wiped and rewritten, and the five names were still on it with the same gap at the top. Tobias Renn came out of the annexe with a box on his shoulder as they passed, and he put the box down and nodded at Runa and then at Lin Hao.

“Mr Neve read the question first this morning,” Tobias said. “I heard it from the queue.”

“Yes.”

“What instrument did you give.”

“My own declaration. It is the only one I have.”

Tobias picked the box up again. “Then I am third one down on a board because of a statement the declarant filed himself, and the declarant is standing in front of me, and there is nothing I can do about either of those things except carry boxes.” He went back up the stair.

Runa watched him go.

“He signed an attestation that was not a favour,” she said. “Off the register, in eight seconds, because he knew where the 3rd was. He is owed something and he will not take it in a speech. Remember that when you are deciding what an instrument is worth.”

She left him at the second-floor door.

He worked the basins through the middle of the day with the counterfoil drying beside the soap, and at two he went down to the plate office to ask the clerk who had entered his reading on the 3rd what a query cost the person who owned it.

She was behind the half-height screen with a stack of slips and a machine that clicked when it cooled. She looked up when he stopped, and did not pretend surprise.

“You stood in the box,” she said. “I heard it from the queue.”

“Yes.”

“A query wants an instrument,” she said. “That is the whole of it. The entry is mine because I put it in on the 3rd, and the machine took a figure with no rank behind it and made one, and now the index asks how a provisional figure got into the queue. The answer has to be a hearing or a deed or a finding. A re-read is not one. Your own declaration is not one.”

“What happens if there is none.”

“Then your entry comes off the sheet on the morning of the Review and you are not in the room.” She squared the slips. “And I answer for putting it there. That is what owning means. It does not mean I can invent you one.”

He thanked her and went back up, because there was nothing else to do in that office.

At four Tessa sent word through Ola that the rack had gone to thirteen, with the new board dated that morning, a man from the second column who had gone home looking well. He went to the Vestry at six and stood in the cold store doorway while she squared the boards, and she told him the stippling had not moved past the elbow that day, which was the first good news in a week, and he took it the way a man takes water.

“You are not spending,” she said. “That is correct. Hold it. Another cold into any of the five gets them read, and Nadia is already at three since June.”

“I know.”

“Then know it standing up.” She put the boards back on the rack. “You came here to ask whether the count has moved. It has. Faces come back whenever you stand in a crowd, and you stood in one this morning. That is geography and not intention, and it is why the re-read will drift.”

He walked back in the evening rain and stopped at Lantern Row on the way. Ines had the second of May clipboard on the table with the date at the top of it, and the folded paper with his signature beside it, and she looked up when he came in and put her pen down.

“The box,” she said.

“The box and the query and the sealed file.” He told her all three questions and all three answers, word for word, the way the rule required. “Neve asked what a sealed file asks. I said a rank and a deposit and a form. Runa says it also asks for the band on the plate and not on the wall.”

“Runa is right.” Ines turned the clipboard face down, not to hide it but to leave it alone. “A petition is brought in the room on the day with the band on the plate. Yours is on the wall. On the 29th the machine looks for it on the plate.”

She did not ask him about the date on her table and he did not offer, and the paper with his signature stayed folded between them.

He went into the washroom and took the counterfoil out of his pocket and laid it on the counter beside the soap to dry, because the rain had gone through his coat again, and he stood there with his good hand on the edge of the basin and felt the anger come up in him for the first time in three days.

The anger was not at Neve, who had read the questions fairly, and not at the queue, who had listened fairly. It was at the shape of the box itself, which required him to stand in a square every Thursday and answer truly about instruments he did not have, in front of people who had them.

Down on the first floor, in an office with a machine in it, a woman with nineteen years of entries was going to have to explain to somebody how a figure with no rank behind it had been given one. She had put it in in good faith. The machine had not asked her.

He wanted, concretely and immediately, to find one true instrument before the 29th — a paper with somebody else’s hand on it besides his own — and he had no idea where such a paper would come from.

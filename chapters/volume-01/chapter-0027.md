# The Thursday Line

The Board repaints at nine on a Thursday and by ten past nine there were two thousand people in the Reading Square doing what two thousand people do when a thing they have to look at is being repainted, which is stand in the rain and complain about the walk.

Lin Hao was in it, and he was there with eleven hundred other staff of the Fourth Branch, because the schedule sheet had a time against his name at 11.20 and a floor had been taken out of the building for the readings and the Board repaint and the weekly publication, all of which happened on the same day and had been arranged by nobody, on purpose, in a city that ran on clerks.

He was holding his plate in his hand, which everyone in the Square was doing, which was the single largest simultaneous act of public anxiety in Ostara and which nobody had ever thought to call anything.

And he found his own tile.

It took him eleven minutes to find it, because he did not know where in the ordering it had gone.

Rank five is high. He had spent four years being low down, and his eyes went to the bottom four rows out of pure habit and found nothing there, and he went up the board with a wet collar and his heart going like a bird in a bag until he got to the third row from the top and found a white tile with a black five on it and the name LIN HAO under it in the small upright print. Thirty-eighth on a list of ninety-one. In the largest type in Ostara, nine streets from a building where four thousand people knew his face.

Nothing happened.

That was the thing nobody had warned him about and nobody could have warned him about. Two thousand people looked at a board with nine hundred names on it and none of them looked at his for longer than they looked at anybody's, because a man had gone from four to five that is an entirely ordinary event in a city where people go up and down every week and there is a great deal more to look at.

A woman in front of him said to the man beside her that her sister had dropped a band and would have to sell the flat, and the man said that was not as bad as the winter, and they moved on.

He stood in the rain in front of nine hundred names and his name was on it in white and nothing happened, and it was the most frightening thing he had ever seen. Everything he had built for four years had been to make sure that nobody could read him, and a machine had read him in public, and the machine had not made a fuss, and there was no version of the rest of his life in which that could be undone.

He went and stood at the top of the Ferrier interchange steps where he could see the plate office window and the branch floor above it, because the readings were being taken inside and he had a time.

The line went along the branch's own corridor, and it had been going since seven, and by half past ten there were about forty people in it and it was moving about one name a minute and a half.

Nobody had told them anything. That was the design. A man who does not know what is going to happen to him in a room is a man who behaves in the corridor in a way that produces a clean reading, and that was not a conspiracy, it was just what happens when nobody has ever had to explain anything to anybody.

He watched the board by the second-floor door.

There was a tick column on the second sheet now, printed in red, and the column was filling in all morning in a clerk's neat hand as the readings came back and reconciled: a tick, a name, a date. Settled, and settled, and settled. Eighty-eight names in and he counted them, because he could not stop counting things, and the arithmetic he had been doing for four years had nothing to do with the machine and he knew exactly what he was doing and he did it anyway.

At 10.52 a woman called Marchetti came out of the door with her plate in her hand and stopped, and did not go back into the line, and stood against the wall, and then went back in.

At 11.06 the same thing happened with a man whose name he did not know, and this time the clerk came out after him and they stood in the corridor together and looked at the plate from both sides for about forty seconds, and the clerk said something short, and the man said something short, and neither of them raised their voice.

At 11.19, which was one minute before his own time, the same thing happened for the third time, and Lin Hao came down off the steps and went along the corridor against the flow, because he knew the face.

Emrys Vaz was standing at the wall by the filing-room door with his hands at his sides.

He was not a boy on a rota any more in the way that mattered, which is to say that four weeks ago he had been a name on a yard sheet and was now a person standing in a corridor in the Fourth Branch with a plate he had been handed that morning on a slip, because he had never held one before.

"I got one at six this morning," he said, before Lin Hao had said anything. "They gave it me at the north gate. I've never had one. They just gave it me because of the list."

"What does it say?"

"It says four hundred and forty-one." Emrys looked up at him with the completely untroubled expression of somebody who has not yet worked out that a number can be a catastrophe. "And the woman said that was low, and I said low for what, and she said low for a person. And then she asked me if I had done anything this week that somebody else had seen me do."

Lin Hao's stomach went out of him.

"And what did you say?"

"I said I carry stock up from the yard at six every morning and I've been doing it four years and nobody's ever asked me that before." The boy almost smiled. "She got a face on her. Not a bad face. Just a face. And then she went in."

The line went on. Somebody said, "Tenth floor, next."

At 11.31 they did it for Ottoline Barret, and for the third of the three it was Tobias Renn at 11.44, which was a quarter past eleven, which Lin Hao did not find funny at the time and did find funny at some point in the next two days, at a bus stop, on his own, with his hand over his mouth.

Tobias did not come out into the corridor. The clerk came out twice and went back in twice and the second time he stood at the rail for a while with his back to everybody, and then he came down the corridor to the ground floor, and went out of the front door into the rain, and stood under the awning looking at the Square where nine hundred tiles were being repainted.

Lin Hao went out to him.

He stood four feet away in the rain and he could not think of one single sentence.

"The three go down," Tobias said, without turning round. "That is what happens at four o'clock when the slips are printed. That is what 'unable to settle' means, that is what the Wing does with it, and there is nothing you can say to me about it and nothing you can say to them about it, and you have been in this building four years and you have never once asked me a question you did not already know the answer to."

"Emrys Vaz is on it too."

"Yes." Tobias turned round. "And the woman on the fourth floor who holds the door. So that is four years of being the person nobody looks at, and the arithmetic has finally worked out that it is a *liability*, and the number has come to tell them about it, and it did not ask any of us what we thought about that either." He pulled his collar up. "You are going to stand there at twelve o'clock and they are going to read your plate and it is going to say unsettled in the place where the interval goes, because it always has. And then the slips print at four and there will be three names on them."

"Four."

"Four," Tobias said. "Unless you have got something." He looked at him for a second longer than he ever had in four years. "You have not got something. You have got a plate that says the same thing it said in June. I have read it. It is on a sheet in a pigeonhole in a wall."

He went out into the rain and Lin Hao let him go, and then he stood in the awning of the Fourth Branch at twenty to twelve in the morning with two thousand people in a square in front of him and four names going into a room and no instrument in the world that would take them out.

He had thought about this for four days and he had got nowhere, because the arithmetic was the arithmetic. He could not pay them. Paying a man a figure was not a thing he could do; he could put relief into a body and it cost them a cold minute, and a cold minute does not move a tick column. He could not stop the slip printing. He could not petition anything, petitioning sealed filings was a rank-six verb and he was a rank-five man with a private office he had never once sat in.

And there was not a procedure in the city that would take a statement from a washroom attendant at twenty to twelve in the morning saying that the four names on the list were the four names he could feel in a stair and a yard and a corridor, because if he said it he would be telling ninety-one colleagues where his figure comes from, and that was the end of everything he had, and he would have to stand in front of all of them while it happened.

Runa Sato was in the corridor.

She had been in the corridor since seven with a notepad and no plate visible, doing exactly what she had told him on the 28th she would do, and she was standing at the rail now with her eyes on the floor, and when he came back in she said, without looking at him:

"You have until the slips print at four. After four o'clock those four people are referred, and a referral is not a decision anybody makes. It is a thing that has happened to them."

"What happens at four."

"At four the clerk prints. At half past four the man from the Wing collects them. At nine tomorrow morning he will have read all four files and he will have looked at them for about ninety seconds each." She closed the notepad. "I have watched it happen. It takes ninety seconds a person. It is the fastest thing the Wing does and it is the only thing they do without anybody arguing."

Lin Hao stood in the corridor of the Fourth Branch on the second floor with a plate in his hand and a line going along it and four hundred people on the floors above him doing their jobs, and he felt the dread come up in him the way it had come up every day for four weeks, and it was the same dread, exactly the same, and it had a use in it now, which was new, and which frightened him more than the other thing had.

It was going to be one sentence.

That was all it was going to be, one sentence, said out loud, in a corridor, in front of ninety-one people and a Field operative and a clerk and whatever else had walked in that day. The unsettled reading is mine. It is on this floor. It will be read out every week and you can all come and look at it.

It would not help the four. It would take the four off the list, because there would be one unsettled reading in the building instead of four, and it would be attached to a name that could not be sent to a room, because the man who said it out loud had claimed it in public and could not then pretend it belonged to somebody else.

And it would cost him the only thing he had left, which was the fact that nobody knew.

He got his plate out of his pocket and went and stood behind the man at the front of the line.

Two minutes to twelve.

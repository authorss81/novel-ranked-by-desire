# Between Floors

He had been in the Vestry for two hours by nine o'clock, doing nothing in particular, which was the first time in a month he had spent two hours doing nothing in particular and the reason was that there was nowhere else to go.

Tessa had made him eat at seven and had told him that if he was going to stand in a corridor on a Thursday for the rest of his life he was going to do it fed, and had told him that the cold store was locked until the 8th, which he had not known was a thing a person could do with a room.

Ola had come down the long stair at eight with a scarf on and her notebook under her arm and had put him on the bench outside the clinic with the whole Vestry arcade behind him going about its evening, and had said, "Right, so you told a whole corridor. Do you know how many people live in this quarter?"

"No."

"Forty-one thousand." She had said it like a woman laying down a card. "And I have been in every kitchen, every stairwell and every waiting room in it for four years, and by the end of the week you will not be able to walk to the bread stall." She had gone off up the stair and had left him with the notebook, which was open, and which he had not looked at, and which had four pages of it turned back to the 22nd of last month, which was the day he had stopped riding the tram the long way.

He was still on the bench at twenty past nine when the lift came down out of the arcade with a bang and stopped, and the doors opened, and Nadia Ferrous was standing in it with her hand on the brass handle and her uniform on and her hair up under the little hat, and she said:

"Get in."

The car went down from the arcade to the clinic level, which was four floors and about forty seconds, and it stood in the middle of it with the doors shut.

"Nadia, I've got to get down there in ten minutes and—"

"I know you have. Get in." She was not smiling. She had been shouting over a lift for eight years and she had the voice of somebody who could be heard through a tram, and at this volume in a closed steel box it was extremely close.

"Because you did it on the reading, and I did not know about the reading until about two hours ago, and by four o'clock I had told sixty-one people. And at six my sister's landlord asked me why I was telling the whole Vestry about a man at the Bureau of Merit." She tipped her head. "And I said *because he stood up in a corridor and told everybody*, and he said *that is not the same as being famous, Nadia*."

She let go of the brass handle and sat down on the little bench in the corner of the car and put her feet up on the opposite wall.

"And he is right," she said. "That is what I have been doing since I was fifteen. It is exactly what I have been doing since I was fifteen, and there is not one single person in this quarter who has ever told me to stop, because I never get anything wrong. Everything I say is true. That is my whole talent. Do you understand how much of a problem that is?"

"I think you have just made my week a great deal harder."

"I have made your week *impossible*, and I did it out of affection," Nadia said, "and I would do it again, and I want you to sit down, because there is a thing underneath the thing and I have never had to say anything to anybody this badly before and I am going to say it badly."

He sat down on the other side of the car.

"I have got a rule," she said. "Everything I feel is a floor. I can go down fast, all day, whatever the building, and I have never once in my life been able to go up."

She pulled her little hat off and held it in her lap.

"I have got a theory about it, and the theory is that I am not allowed to know anything about anyone who is going up. Every time I have ever tried I have got a headache like a tram and I have had to sit down on the floor of my own car. So I have spent four years feeling things in a lift going down." She looked round the steel box. "It is the only room in Ostara that does not hurt."

"Nadia—"

"And then a man came in off the Ferrier line in June and it was the fourth one," she said, "and it came in upward, and I have told forty-one thousand people about it and my sister says I am insane, and I am not, I am the one in this family who can feel things, and the thing I have been feeling since June is a man going up. And on Thursday this morning he went up in front of ninety-one people and I could feel it from the Vestry arcade with my hand on the brake, and I have had to sit on this bench for six hours."

The car was very quiet, and forty seconds from the clinic, and the doors were shut.

"That," she said, "is what I came to say. Badly. I am going to be living with your name now, because I have been talking about you for four months and there is no version of the next year where I stop, and I would like to be told what I am living with, and I would like to be *in* it, and I know what that sounds like at eleven o'clock in a stopped lift."

"You may be in it."

"Say more than that." She put her hat down beside her. "Because I have had two offers of a sort from men this year and I said no to both in about four seconds, and I have been perfectly happy saying no, and I am now sitting in a lift with you and I would like you to be extremely clear that I am not saying no, because if you think I am saying no then we can both go back to being sensible."

He was across the car in a second, and she was up off the bench with her hand in the back of his collar, and she was not shy and not careful and not asking him for anything at all.

"Wait — wait, hold on." She stopped, one finger up. "Two minutes. Two minutes, because I have thought about this for six hours and there is a thing I have to say first and if I do not say it first I will think about it afterwards and be annoyed."

"Go on."

"You have got four seconds of me," Nadia said, "and I am going to spend them here, and afterwards I am going to go back up. Not all the way. Up one floor, to the arcade, because that is as far as I go and everybody in the Vestry knows it and half of them make a joke about it every week, and I am not going to have it be a thing that happened to me."

She held up a finger.

"I am going to choose every single part of this, and I am going to be shouting about it in the kitchens by the weekend, and I want that on the record now, from you. So that later on, when somebody in one of your very serious offices tries to make it into something I did not choose, I have a man who heard me say it first."

"Say it again and I will write it down."

"I have not asked you for anything," she said, "I am telling you what I am doing."

And then she did not talk for a while.

She got his coat open with two fingers and dropped it on the floor of the car, and got her own jacket off and threw it over it, and sat down on his lap in her lift operator's boots with her knees spread either side of him and her hands flat on his chest, and said, "Right. There is a window. There is no window. It is a lift."

"It is a lift."

"It is a lift between the arcade and the clinic and I have had this car for eight years and I know exactly what it sounds like when it moves and I am going to be able to hear the brake the entire time and I am telling you that now because I know that is *undignified* and I don't care."

She had him inside her in under a minute and her head went back against the lift wall and she said his name twice and then stopped saying it and started making noise about something else entirely. She was fast and she was strong from eight years of hauling a brake and she did not go quiet at any point in it; she kept up a running commentary in the voice she used for announcements, which was the single funniest thing that had ever happened to Lin Hao and which he thought about at intervals for the rest of his life.

"Ooh — *there* — that is the floor bit, that is the floor bit, I knew it, I have always said the floor bit—"

"Right."

"I am going to be — oh. Oh, I am going to be extremely loud and it is going to be your problem."

He got a hand under her and she put hers on the wall and came up onto her toes, and she told him about the four-thirty stall in Cordwain and the man who put his elbow in a woman's tea and about her mother's funeral and the fact that she had told the whole quarter about her mother's funeral, and none of it had anything to do with anything and all of it was true, and that was the whole of her.

She came first with her mouth open against his jaw and her hands fisted in his shirt, and she shook from the knees down and laughed at the same time, and then she made him stay there while she did it a second time, which took longer and which he would have sat through on a tram.

Afterward she got off him, put her jacket on, and went up onto her toes and put her forehead against his and breathed for a minute.

"Right," she said. "That is done and I am not going to be weird about it. Four seconds." She held up four fingers. "I have never had four seconds of anybody and I have had twenty-three years of my life and I would like that noted."

"Noted."

"Also I have moved us one floor," Nadia said. "Up. Look." She put her hand flat on the brass. "Up one floor, from where we were. That is as far as it goes and it took everything I had and I am going to have a headache for it and I have got to be back on the car at half past nine, and I am going to be four minutes late, and the girl on the arcade car will have told everybody by the time I get up there."

"Tell them."

"I was always going to tell them," she said. "That is the entire point of me. The only thing that has changed is that now there is a thing to tell them about and it is not just a cold in a lift."

The doors went open on the clinic level.

She got out first and turned round in the doorway, in her little hat, with her boots on the sill.

"Right," she said. "One last thing and then I have to go up and be normal at people. You have got four days before anybody looks at what you did this morning, and I know because my sister's kitchen has a copy of every notice in the Vestry and she reads them at breakfast out loud. Your re-read is quarterly." She put her head on one side. "So you have got until the end of next month to make it true, and until then you are a man who said a thing in a corridor, and I have watched a lot of people say things in corridors."

"And after that?"

"After that you are a man who keeps saying a thing in a corridor," Nadia said, "and that one is worth something, and I am going to be telling everybody I know which one you are. So you'd better turn up."

She hit the brass handle and the doors went and the car went up one floor, with a sound in the machinery that she would be feeling in her teeth until she got to bed.

The doors opened on the arcade, where nine people were standing, and Lin Hao heard all of it from the landing below: somebody saying Nadia, what is it, and Nadia saying, at the top of her lungs, in a room full of strangers who knew her voice already:

"THAT is what it is. Get your coat on, I'm going to be late, and do NOT tell my sister I told you first."

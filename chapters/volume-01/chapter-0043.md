# The Longest Walk

On the 28th Lin Hao walked from the Fourth Branch to the Cordwain works and back without getting on a tram, and the walk took him past every door he had counted on the 15th and a dozen he had not, and by the time he turned for home his legs were shaking and his forearm was wet inside the sling with sweat.

He had not meant it as a test. He told himself that at the start, going out through the yard gate at seven in the morning with the stock docket trolley rattling past him. He told himself he was going to see Nell Ardery about her eleven weeks of figures, because she had asked him to come back and he had said he would.

The truth was simpler and he admitted it by the time he reached the Ferrier crossing. He was looking for a twentieth person. One face he had never stood near before, one pair of hands that had never felt a cold minute, one body that could be relieved in public, with witnesses, for four hundred grains, without marking anybody.

The crossing was full. Morning shift going one way, night shift going the other, strangers holding doors for each other four times a day the way they always had. He stood at the rail where he had stood on the 23rd of last month and practised being looked at, and nobody looked at him, because a man with a sling and a white tile was ordinary in a crowd of two hundred people going to work.

A woman with a basket went past him and he moved aside for her. A porter held a gate for a man with a crate. A girl ran for a car and missed it and laughed. Every one of those was a door, and every door put something in the figure, and he felt the weight of it the way a man feels a coat getting heavier in the rain.

He found Nell Ardery on the landing of number 14 with her figures folded in her pocket and her door standing open behind her.

“You came back,” she said.

“I said I would.”

“Then come in and sit down, because you look like you are going to fall over.”

He sat on her chair and she gave him water in a mug with a chip in it, and they went through the eleven weeks together, figure by figure, and the band had not moved in any of them, and the shortfall was still about three hundred a week against nine thousand people, and the initials were still on every return.

When they were done she folded the papers up and put them back in her pocket.

“What are you looking for,” she said. “Out there. On the crossing. I watched you from the landing.”

“A person I have never stood near before.”

“There is not one.” She said it kindly. “Not on that crossing. You walked the arc on the 15th and took twenty-two faces in one day. Every person on that arc knows your walk now. Ola told me. You stopped riding the long way on the 29th of last month and the faces stopped coming, and then you walked it once more on purpose and they came back, and now there is nowhere left.”

He looked at her. “Then what happens to the figure.”

“It keeps moving.” She tapped her pocket. “Wherever you stand. That is what you told Runa Sato on the 6th and she wrote it down. So the count stopping was always about faces and not about the figure, and you know that better than I do.”

He nodded and finished the water and gave her the mug back.

She walked him to the stair and stopped him with a hand on his good arm.

“You asked me on the 17th about the back stair door,” she said. “Chained twice and reported twice. I have been thinking about it since. The woman who chained it has not come forward and the warders have not asked again, and a door nobody asks about is a door that will be forced a third time.”

“I know.”

“Then know it for the row and not for yourself.” She let go of his arm. “You came here looking for a twentieth person and you are going home with eleven weeks of figures instead. That is not failure. That is what a man does when there is nobody left to reach.”

He thanked her and went down.

At the crossing he stopped again and forced himself to stand still for ten minutes with his hands at his sides, counting faces the way Beatrix counted cars. A driver he knew. A checker he knew. A laundress from the Vestry whose name was on the eleventh line of his list though he had never learned it. A porter from the yards who had attested a bag of brass fittings on the 20th of last month. Every one of them was one of the forty-one.

A new tram came in and strangers got off it, visitors from outside the arc, a family with luggage and a man with a survey case. For about four seconds his heart went because here were faces he had never stood near. Then he understood what doing anything about them would mean. Strangers with luggage, no plates shown, no attestation possible, no witnesses who knew the box. A deed had to be public and chosen. A cold into a stranger on a tram was how the whole thing started and it could not be how it continued.

He let the tram go.

On the way back he went past Cutler’s Row. The row was still half empty, with the furniture under the tarpaulin two streets away and the boards with numbers on them going soft in the rain, and Halvard Rees was sitting on the wall at the end of the passage with his roll of wire beside him.

Halvard looked up when he came past.

“You walked it,” Halvard said. It was not a question.

“Yes.”

“Find one.”

“No.” Lin Hao sat down on the wall beside him because his legs would not hold him up any longer. “Every face on it is one of the forty-one.”

Halvard turned the wire over in his hands. “Then you have got what I have got. Eleven B on my wire and nine years in a room and a letter and a half to show for it. The difference is that mine was done to me by a district return and yours was done by yourself on purpose on the 15th.”

“I know.”

“Do not spend one of the five.” Halvard said it without looking at him. “I have been sitting here since ten past six on the 20th thinking about the forty times, and not one of them came off me, and that is the only thing I have got, and I am telling you that the five who kept something are the five you cannot touch again. Another cold into a turned person is how you get them read, and three in a season gets them a file, and Nadia is already at three.”

Lin Hao put his head down. The wall was cold through his coat.

“I am not going to,” he said.

“Good.” Halvard stood up and picked up the wire. “Then go home and sleep. You have not slept since the 17th and it shows in your face.”

He walked the last mile home in the rain and went up to his room above the chop-house on Ferrier Street and lay on the bed with his coat on and his arm in the sling across his chest.

The room was small and clean and it held one plate, one knife, one chair, and a photograph of two men on a tram step that he had looked at every night for three years. He did not look at it now. He lay with his eyes open and did the division again, because not doing it felt like lying down on the job.

Twenty-nine deeds. Nineteen people. No twentieth. The best remaining lead already turned.

The grief came up then, sudden and physical, the way it had not since the 3rd. Not grief for himself, though there was some of that. Grief for the shape of the trap, which was that the only people he could relieve were the people relief would kill.

He got up after an hour and washed his face in the basin and went down to the chop-house and ate standing up, because sitting down felt like giving up.

The keeper asked him about the requisition and he told him the row was half empty and the wire was labelled eleven B, and the keeper shook his head and said the district return had taken three hundred a week off nine thousand people and nobody had seen it until a machinist laid eleven weeks on a table. That was the talk of the chop-house now, and Lin Hao listened to it with his bowl in his hand and understood that Cordwain knew the arithmetic even if it did not know his name.

The keeper’s wife asked him if he was on the queue and he said yes, twenty-second of twenty-two, and she nodded and gave him extra without charging him, and he thanked her and ate it, and understood that kindness in this city was always unrecorded and always went into the same place.

He went back up and took the sling off and worked the stiffness out of the forearm with hot water from the kettle, the way Tessa had shown him, and then he put the sling back on and sat at the table with the photograph face down, because looking at it would have made the division impossible.

That night he wrote a list on the back of the counterfoil from the box. Forty-one lines, five turned, all five named: Nadia, Ola, Emrys, Teodor, Tessa. He wrote the five slowly, in the order he had found them on the 20th, and then he wrote the other thirty-six as streets and doors and crossings, because he did not know half their names.

When the list was done he looked at it for a long time and then he folded it up and put it in his coat pocket.

Tessa Okonkwo would go on taking four in a day and two the next morning between six and ten at night, because that was when the shifts were, and his walking the arc had been six more hours of doors held open by strangers.

He wanted, concretely, one new face before the 29th — a stranger at a crossing he had never stood near — and the walk had proved there was not one left on any road his legs could cover.

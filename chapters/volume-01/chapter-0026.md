# Cold Store

Wenna Ost went out first and did not say goodbye and put the door back hard enough to make the lamp swing.

Teodor Mace sat on the couch with his boots in his hand and said he would wait, because he wanted to know what was going to happen to his plate, and Tessa said there was nothing more to be done for it tonight, and he said that was not an answer, and she said it was the only one she had and he was welcome to take it to the Exchange in the morning. He put his boots on, looked at Lin Hao for about a second and a half, and said nothing.

That was correct. Lin Hao would have told him it had been an accident, and it had not been an accident, and there was no sentence available that was both true and worth the air it cost. The man who had agreed to an intake test had found a number from May on a clipboard, and had the rest of his life to think about it.

Tessa waited until the outer door had shut and the yard had emptied, and then she locked the ward door behind her, which she had never done before.

"Sit down," she said. "No. Not there." She pulled the chair round from the wall and set it by the lamp instead. "There. If you are going to be sick, be sick on my boots and not on the sheet." He sat.

She had his plate and her own, and she set them on the table side by side, and she turned Teodor's disc over and read the back of it with the lamp close.

"Look at this," she said. "Not the figure. The interval."

"I can't read it in the dark."

"You cannot read it because the dark is not the problem." She brought the lamp over and put her finger on the rim of the disc. "Every plate in this city carries two numbers. One is what you have. The other is what you have per week, and the second one is what the bands are calculated from, and a man with the first number and no second number is not a person, he is an exception."

"I know what unsettled means. It's on mine."

"It is on yours and yours has said so since June and nobody has queried it because you are rank five and held and the right to be asked protects you from exactly this." She looked up. "His has not said so. His still says 402 a week, which was 402 a week at seven o'clock this evening, and he is now holding four hundred and twenty-four grains that arrived with no deed behind them and no deed in front of them, and when he presses that plate at the gate tomorrow morning the Exchange is going to run a reconciliation and it is going to find that his stock cannot account for him."

"What happens then?"

"A query." She set the disc down. "A query on a figure that cannot be reconciled is not a letter. It is a referral, and referrals go to the Wing, and the Wing does not write to you and ask whether you are well. It writes to your employer, and after that it takes about four days and a man arrives."

Teodor Mace worked at the Exchange.

That was the whole of it, and Tessa had not known it at seven o'clock when she had let him in, and she had known it at ten past, and she had gone on doing the intake test anyway because she had eight months of records that did not add up and a boy who could not sleep.

"Get his boots off," she said. "No — yours. His are already gone." She turned the lamp down and got the blanket off the couch. "This will take about four minutes and it will be very cold and I need you on your back. What I am going to do takes both hands."

She was already working when he got down, rolling her sleeves to the elbow, and he saw it then, which he had not seen when he came in: both her forearms had a grey-black stippling from the wrist to mid-forearm, and it was not a bruise, it was the colour of the inside of the cold store applied to living skin in a pattern that followed the veins.

"How long have you had that."

"Two years."

"How much of it is other people."

"Most of it." She got her hands on his wrists and turned them palm up. "This is my inventory. I can hold a body past the point it should stop. That is what I do, and the cost lands in my frame and nowhere else, and after eleven years I have run out of room, and when I run out of room there is nowhere left for it to go."

"You have never told anybody."

"I have told two people. One of them is the woman whose daughter I could not keep alive in April." She took a breath. "So. Teodor Mace has four hundred and twenty-four grains in him that the Exchange cannot account for. I am going to put the wrongness of them into myself, where nobody has to reconcile it, because nobody has ever asked me for a count and I have never offered one."

"You'll take it out of your—"

"Yes." She put her palms flat on the inside of his wrists, above the pulse, and they were freezing. "And it will feel like the cold you have been putting through people for four years, except it will not be one to four seconds and it will not be in the hand. It will be in the chest. And when it has gone you will have a woman standing in front of you who has just absorbed a balance sheet error, and you are going to ask me what it costs, because that is the sort of man you are, and the truth is that I cannot tell you. I have never once measured it."

She held on.

It was not a cold minute. It went on a long time and it came up under the sternum and sat there, and he felt the whole of his own account lean into her hands like a weight going onto a bad pier, and he could not move and could not speak and could hear the lamp cord creaking over his head.

Then it stopped, and she took her hands off and laughed once through her nose with no humour in it.

"There," she said. "Four hundred and twenty-four."

He sat up. "What did you do?"

"I moved an error out of a man into my own account, which is the only one in this city nobody checks." She was rubbing her forearms. "He will press that plate at the gate in the morning and it will read twenty-two forty and it will reconcile and he will never know that it ever did not."

She looked up at him.

"That is the best thing I have ever done for anybody," she said, "and I did it for free, because you asked me and I am a fool."

"And this is the part you are going to carry, so I will say it once, out loud, in a room with no ledger in it." She held up her forearms.

"Every time you put it into somebody, that person spends a cold minute they did not ask for, and every third or fourth one of those is a person who keeps count. And now I am one of them." She put her hands back down. "I have felt nine of yours since June, in other rooms, on other people, in the middle of the night with my hands on their chests and no idea in the world what I was feeling. I have a cold minute in me that is not mine and I can feel exactly where you put it."

"Then who was in the room."

"You are not listening." She put her hands flat on her knees. "Not one person. The cold in me is a thing I have carried for eleven years out of bodies I could not save and did not know why I could save them. I did not know it was a cold. I called it being tired." She stood and put her hand flat on the rack. "And there is somebody else in this city doing what you did in that room tonight. It is not from this building. It stopped. And I have a date."

"Whose date."

"The second of May. You saw it on the clipboard." She turned round. "I am not going to ask you who. Not tonight. Tonight you are in a cold room with a woman who has just made herself your witness by accident, and she would like that acknowledged."

He stood up and did not go round the table and he did not take the couch.

He went to where she was, at the rack, with her arm still flat across it, and put his hand on the back of her neck, cold, the grey standing at his left temple and no strength in him at all.

She turned into it rather than away from it, which he noticed, because Beatrix had led and Ines had followed and this was the third time and this was the first time a woman had simply fitted herself against a hand.

"No," she said, when he started. "Don't do it like that. If you do it like that it is the transaction and I am the price, and I have been somebody's price twice this year already."

"How, then."

"Ask me."

That was all. She said it with her hand over his on the back of her neck, and the lamp swung a little on its cord, and neither of them was tired in the way that meant what it usually meant, and there was nothing in this room that could have happened to anybody against their will.

"You have never once," she said, "in four years, asked a woman anything."

He asked her.

She came into him and put her hands flat on his chest, over the place where it happens, and made a small sound at the contact that was not pain and was not cold and which he thought about for a long time afterwards, then pushed him backwards until his shoulders were on the couch and got the blanket out from under both of them with her knee and kept it.

"There. Now. Warm room, cold woman, four hours' sleep in you and none in me, and about an hour before I check the ward round." She got her own shirt up over her head and threw it off, and the stippling on her arms was black in the lamplight, and she made him look at it, deliberately, with one eyebrow. "This is what eleven years of holding people looks like. You are allowed to look at it. You are not allowed to be gentle about it."

"You said—"

"I said I am not going to write the file." She got his belt open. "Every one of these goes in a ledger in my head. Exposure, position, response, time. I have written nine hundred and forty of them and never once written down what any of it was *for*, and tonight is the first time I have had a reason not to. I am going to lie on that couch with a man and not make a note of it, and if that is not the most unprofessional thing I have done in eleven years then I do not know what is."

"Write nothing."

"Write nothing," she said, and came down over him, and put her mouth against the place on his throat where the pulse was, and said, "and stop apologising, because I have watched you apologise twice tonight and each one cost you about four seconds."

He stopped.

It was unhurried and it was cold and neither of them hurried anything, and it was nothing like the cab and nothing like Lantern Row: no rain on a roof, no fogged glass, no book on the control ledge. There was a lamp on a cord in a room at two degrees, a blanket that smelled of the store, and a doctor who knew exactly what she was doing with her hands, because she had done it with other people's bodies for eleven years and had never once been asked to do it with her own.

He got his hands into her hair and she told him not to pull, and then told him to, and he did the second one.

She was better at this than either of the others and she was better at it because she was not thinking about anything else. When she got tight around him she put her forehead on his shoulder and worked through it in her own time, breathing in and out against his collarbone, and she said not one word at the end of it.

He went over after her and she held him down for it and then let him go.

They lay on the couch in a cold room with the blanket over them and the lamp still going.

"The bread and cheese," she said, some time later. "That is on the table. I put it there because I have never in eleven years done anything past four in the morning without food, and I am not going to start." She got up, naked, and cut it, and stood with her back to the open door of the rack eating and not looking at the eleven clipboards on it.

Then she said, quietly, to the wall: "Say the thing you have been not saying since I asked you how many."

"Nineteen," he said. "And it has not moved since the 29th, because I have stopped riding the tram the long way, and every one of them is a person I can only ever help once, and I only have nineteen, and two of them are on a wall in my building."

"Nineteen people and you have taken the source away so you cannot get any more." She turned round. "That is the most stupid and most decent thing anybody has ever done in a room like this one. I have known you three weeks and I have just decided something."

"What."

"I am going to be in it. Not as one of yours — as the person who holds it. That is not the same job and it is not a smaller one, and you do not get a vote, and you will not be able to protect me by spending nobody new on me, because I am going to be spending on them." She sat down on the couch beside him and started putting her clothes on. "So. Nineteen, four turned, two of them going into a room on Thursday, and one of them a boy who carries stock up from a yard at six. What have you done about it?"

"Nothing yet," Lin Hao said. "I have got until Thursday."

"Then you have less than you think," she said, "because Thursday is when they repaint and there will be two thousand people in the Square, and whatever you are going to do, a man who cleans the washrooms does not do it in a corridor on the second floor of a building where four thousand people know his face." She pulled her sleeve down over the black stippling. "You will have to do it where they can see you. That is what is left. There is no other room in this city left for you to do anything in."

The lamp swung on its cord.

It was twenty past midnight, the shelf had eleven clipboards on it, and nine streets away somebody was already up a ladder with a pot of white paint.

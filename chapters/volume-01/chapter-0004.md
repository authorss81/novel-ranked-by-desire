# Cold Minutes

He made the rule at ten to six on the Thursday morning, standing at the second basin with the cloth in his hand, and he made it the way he made every decision, which was alone, standing up, before anybody else was in the building.

No hands.

Not a rule about strangers. He wrote it in his head as a rule about the whole city and he knew within about a minute of writing it that he was going to break it inside forty-eight hours, and he wrote it anyway, because a rule he had broken was worth more than no rule at all. He had worked that out at sixteen.

Thursday ran.

He cleaned the second floor with his forearms against his body. He opened the door of the sixth washroom with his hip. A messenger came up the back stair at half past seven with a tube under his arm and stood in the doorway for four full seconds waiting to be let past, and Lin Hao went sideways against the wall to give him the width of the corridor and did not touch the door frame, which is what a man does when he is holding a door for someone.

At nine a clerk on the ground floor dropped a file of forty sheets down the middle of the counters and it went everywhere, and the whole queue of people watched her pick it up, and Lin Hao was behind the counter by then, and he got down on his knees and squared the sheets into a neat stack and pushed the stack across to her with the side of his hand, flat, the way you push something to somebody who does not want you near them.

She said thank you. He said nothing at all.

At nearly midday he was on the second floor with a cloth when a woman two basins along began to cry, quietly, the way people cry in a room with nowhere to go, and he stood there with the cloth and the mop and eleven seconds of a decision he was going to be ashamed of for a month.

He put the bucket down. He went back to the basin. He cleaned it twice.

The review was in a room on the second floor that had a table in it and three chairs and nothing else, and Lin Hao stood in it at ten past four on the Thursday with the wrapped plate in his hand and a junior auditor with cold hands and a bad cough asked him nine questions.

They were not the questions he had spent three weeks imagining.

They were: are you in employment elsewhere. do you hold a seal. do you use a plate other than your own. have you had any dealings with an unattributable address in the last two years. do you hold any account opened by a third party. is there anything in your life you have been unable to account for.

He said no to six of them. He said *I would not know where to look* to the seventh, which was true and which the auditor wrote down in full, and the eighth question was about his brother and it was one clause long and he answered it in four words and then stood there for the rest of the minute while the pen went across the paper.

The ninth was: do you know why your interval is blank.

And Lin Hao looked at the door and gave the answer he had been carrying since Wednesday, which was the only true thing he had said all week.

"No, sir."

The auditor nodded and stamped it, and the whole procedure took nineteen minutes, and when he came out into the corridor he stood against the wall for a moment with his eyes shut and discovered that the two days of not touching anybody had cost him the ordinary ability to stand in a corridor with people in it.

Forty names on the list, and the review was nineteen minutes, and the man with the cough did not once look up. Nine of the forty went down. That was the standing figure. Nobody had ever worked out what the other thirty-one had in common with each other and nothing about the room suggested that it was meant to be worked out.

Two auditors were talking on the landing outside and he could not get past without going through them, so he stood and let them.

"—the Vestry lift one. Four months of it now."

"She's a Turned, then."

"She's *a woman who's cold*, that's all she's—" The cough. "It's reportable if you can put a source on it. She's a lift hand. She's got no source, she's just a woman who feels it. Third time's different, though."

"Third time's different."

"They pull the file on the one it came out of." A chair moved. "That's the third one. After that it's not a complaint, it's a pattern, and a pattern gets an address."

He stood there with his heart going like a man's heart going in the dark.

Two. He had one of them on the record somewhere, or a thing that was on the record somewhere. Two more and the file came off the shelf, and the file had a name on it, and the name would be his, because he was the only man in Ostara whose account had produced the phenomenon in the first place and the only man in Ostara stupid enough to say so out loud to a clerk who put her file down.

He went down the stairs and cleaned the fourth floor and did not think about it once, on purpose, extremely well.

On the Friday he read his plate at the concourse kiosk at six in the evening.

It had gone *up* by eighty-eight grains in two days, and he had spent nothing, had touched nobody, had spent two days keeping his hands to himself like a man in a room with a contagious thing, and the machine had gone up anyway, and the interval line was still a dash, and that was the part that made him put his hand flat on the kiosk and stand there with his eyes shut.

Two days had gone up by eighty-eight. Two days of walking through a Bureau branch office and a tram and a market and a street full of people who were helping each other in doorways where nobody was standing by. Eighty-eight grains of somebody else's Tuesday, and there had been a Wednesday in there too. There was no version of his life in which he did not make that, and there was no reading in the city that would ever go down while he was walking.

He tried it anyway.

He walked from the Square to the Slough with his hands in his pockets and counted, and by the time he got to the long staircases he had counted nine separate times when a stranger had nearly gone into him and had been steadied by somebody else in the last second, every one of them in a doorway, every one of them with nobody watching, every one of them the exact size and shape of a thing he was eating three meals a day.

At the bottom of the stairs a woman at a stall gave him his change in coin and he put his hand out and stopped it, six inches short, with her hand already in his.

She looked at him. He said thank you and took the coin and went and stood in an alley for a while.

Then, because it was raining and because his own company had become a thing he could not be in, he got on the Vestry line and rode to the end of it.

The Vestry market lift went up from the arcade to the clinic floor and back down, all evening, and it was small enough for eleven people and warm in a way that the rest of the city was not, and it smelled of wet coats and floor polish. He got in at the top because the queue was shorter there and he was in it for forty seconds with a butcher's boy and two women going to the free clinic and a man with a splinted arm.

It went badly at the second floor and stopped for eleven seconds while the attendant restarted it, and everybody put a hand out to hold on, and Lin Hao got his forearm against the strap and put his hand flat on the wooden rail to stop himself from going over.

The woman running the lift had one hand on the gate and the other out in front of her steadying the man with the splinted arm, and when the car lurched the second time she took Lin Hao's wrist to stop him going into the corner, briefly, the way you take hold of a person in a moving car.

It came out of him.

It was a smaller thing than the tram. It went into her hand and stopped there and it was over in about a second, and Lin Hao took his arm back and said sorry and she laughed at him — a young, entirely unbothered, enormously cheerful noise in a small warm box.

"You're the one who came up backwards." She let go of the gate and got it open again. "Nadia. Nadia Ferrous. Third floor's this one, next time come in the right way up."

"Sorry."

"Don't be, it's the only thing that happens in here." She was still laughing. "I'm off till two and then I'm on the arcade car till nine, and I live with my sister on the Vestry side, and there's nobody at home at this hour except a person who isn't interested." She tipped her head. "Cold, though. Wasn't it. That's the third time this week I've had that, and I don't have anything anybody would take cold off me."

Lin Hao stood in the open gate of a lift two floors above the Vestry arcade.

"What kind of cold?"

"You know. In the hand." She shook it out, laughing, and did not stop laughing. "Go on. Second one's this. In we get."

He got in. He rode down with nine people and did not touch the rail, and got out into the rain and walked nine stops home in it and up the stairs to his room above the chop-house on Ferrier Street, and lay on the bed with his coat on because taking it off meant being in the room.

He lay there for two hours and did not sleep, and at some point in the third hour the notation came, the way it had come in a concourse, the way it comes, in the space behind the eyes where nobody is looking:

[Ledger. Nine lines. One turned.]

Nine.

He had known one stranger on a tram. He had touched one lift attendant for the length of a lurch.

Six of the other seven had been in his building all week and he had not looked at the face of one of them.

He got up at five and cleaned the second floor with a rule he had made the night before, which was that he was not going to spend any of it again, ever, for any reason, and he stood at the second basin and understood, quite clearly and without any self-deception at all, that he had not made that rule because it was dangerous.

He had made it because he could not stand it.

That was the whole of it. There was no principle in it. A man had stood on a tram and had four years come out of his shoulder and had said *I could sleep*, and a woman in a lift had laughed and shaken her hand out and said *that's the third time this week*, and Lin Hao had not been able to hear either of those things twice. He would rather go the rest of his life with the whole of it sitting in his chest than hear it a second time.

Which made it the most cowardly thing he had ever done, and he knew that, and he did it anyway, and that was the shape of the man who would one day be standing in a public hall being asked a question on the Board floor.

The rule lasted seven days.

It cost him four hours a night for the first week and then two, and a grey that came in at his left temple and stayed, and on the Friday he was carrying a bucket down the stairs of the fourth floor and his hand was not steady and the bucket went across the landing and put out a yard of corridor and he stood there at half past ten in the morning with the water going toward the sub-basement door and a messenger looking at him over a sealed tube.

By the Sunday he had worked out what a week of not spending was actually worth, which was that his hand was not his own and he slept like a man being woken on the hour, and that there was a floor of ninety-one people above him who were going to be re-read in a fortnight, and that the only reason he was not frightened of that was that he had not yet worked out why.

And then, in the washroom on the fourth floor, on the Saturday, pinned to the noticeboard with two drawing pins and a piece of string because somebody's pin cushion had gone home, there was a sheet of typing paper.

He read it standing up, with the water still drying on the floor behind him.

It was a notice from the Rota about the audit. It said the draw-down would open on Monday the nineteenth. It said all ninety-one members of branch staff would be re-read at short notice. It said that any member of staff whose account did not settle would be taken for a reading, and that this was not a disciplinary measure.

Below that, in a different typewriter, in a different week, and pinned at a different angle, was a second notice.

VESTRY ARCADE LIFT — REPORTS OF COLD EPISODES. RESIDENTS AND USERS ARE ASKED TO SPEAK TO THE CLERK AT INTAKE IF THEY EXPERIENCE REPEATED COLD IN HAND OR CHEST. NO MEDICAL CAUSE HAS BEEN RULED OUT. PLEASE CO-OPERATE.

There were two drawing pins in it. Somebody had taken one of the pins out and used it again somewhere else, and the sheet had been up long enough that the corners had gone soft.

Lin Hao read it four times.

It did not say which lift. There were three lifts on the Vestry side and the notice had been up since before he started, and a person who wanted to be frightened by it could not be frightened by it in any particular way, and a person who wanted to be comforted by it could not be comforted either.

He stood there with the grey at his temple and the water drying on the floor and the name of a woman in a lift he had held by the wrist for one second on Thursday night, and he understood that the city had been keeping a list longer than he had, and that his was the only entry on it that did not exist yet.

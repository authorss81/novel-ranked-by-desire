# A Fifth Figure

Seven days is enough time to become a thing people have opinions about, and by the following Thursday Lin Hao was a thing people in the Fourth Branch had opinions about.

Not a bad thing. He had been braced for a thing people had opinions about in a corridor on the 3rd and what he got was forty people who had watched him go back into the line and wait two hours for ninety others, and about a dozen who said good morning in a different voice, and one woman from the second column who put a cup of tea outside the second-floor washroom on the 9th without looking at him while she did it.

Nobody mentioned it. That was the part he had not been ready for. On the morning of the 29th a floor of four thousand people had watched a man read a printed list aloud and had said nothing, and on the morning of the 3rd, when the same man had said a true thing out loud in a corridor, they had said nothing again, and there is no arrangement of a working week in which four thousand people come at a man like that, and he went on being a man who cleans washrooms except that now the not-saying had a texture to it.

He had stood in the box at the Exchange at nine that morning, as promised, and had read his plate out loud to a public counter, and a clerk had read it back, and it had taken forty seconds, and there had been about nine people there.

Then he had gone to work.

He got down to the Reading Square at ten past ten because the painters started at nine and he wanted to see it done, and the Square was the same Square it always was on a Thursday, which is to say that two thousand people were standing in the rain looking at a wall, and nobody was making any more noise about the wall than they ever were.

The branch list had come down on the 6th. All of it, the printed sheet and the ninety-one names and the column of times, and in its place on the board by the second-floor door there was a single line in a clerk's hand: RE-REFERRED — SUBJECT DECLARED. NO FURTHER ACTION THIS QUARTER. Emrys Vaz had a rota line again, from the yard, at six, and had said nothing to anybody about it, and had gone back to carrying stock up at six in the morning like nothing in the world had happened to him, which was, Lin Hao understood, the entire point of the boy.

That was what a fortnight had bought, and it was everything he had, because a declared reading is re-read quarterly and Rhee had been exactly right about what a fortnight is for.

He found his tile in about four seconds. He knew where it was now. He had looked at it every day for seven days on his way in, standing on the far side of the tram wire, and there is a thing that happens to a man when he knows where his own name is on a board.

White tile. Black five. LIN HAO, thirty-eighth of ninety-one.

And under the name, where every tile in Ostara has two lines and nothing else, his had four lines.

The first was the district. The second was the year of issue. The third was the standing of the account, and it said CARRIED — DECLARED, in the small upright print the Reading Office uses for a category it has only just invented.

The fourth was a date: 3RD.

And the fifth figure was a number.

It was not a rank. It was on no band table in the city. It was printed under his name in the same black ink as his rank and in the same size of type, and it was 8,141, and Lin Hao stood in the Reading Square in the rain and looked at the exact figure behind his band being printed on a public tile for the first time in the history of the Board, on a Thursday, under his own name.

He worked out why inside about a minute, standing in the crowd, and the working-out was very short and it was very bad.

The Board shows a band. A band is calculated from an interval, which is the weekly rate, which is the thing a plate is actually for. Exact figures have never been on a tile because the whole design of the last twenty years is that you can compare bands and you cannot compare numbers, and a city that can only compare bands is a city that can be frightened in exactly one direction.

His tile could not carry a band. He had declared an unsettled reading in a corridor on the 3rd and there was nothing behind it to band from, because the interval was empty and had been empty since June, and so the machine had gone down the list of things it knows how to print, run out of the ones that were honest, and printed the figure itself.

The Board had published his account. Not his rank, which was a range everybody could see and nobody could argue about. The number. The actual number, in the largest type in Ostara, where two thousand people were standing in the rain looking at it.

And it was not his number. He had never earned eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains of merit in his life. Four hundred of it came off a woman with a basket at an interchange, two hundred came off a door being held in the rain by a man who wanted a smoke, and three thousand five hundred and twenty-nine of it came out of four years of a floor's pooled aggregate that had been released onto a plate on the strength of a single attestation in a witness book.

Eight thousand one hundred and forty-one grains, and nineteen people inside it.

[Ledger. Nineteen lines. Five turned. The count has not moved since the 29th.]

He stood and looked at it for a long time. Somebody bumped his shoulder in the crowd and said something apologetic and did not recognise him, and that was how it was always going to be from now on, and he had done it to himself in a corridor on the third with his coat on.

There was a second board.

It was not a tile. It was a printed sheet on a frame, put up at the left-hand end of the Board about a foot off the ground so that you had to look at the bottom of a wall to find it, which is how the Wing always did it, and there were about forty people reading it, because it was new.

ACCOUNTS UNDER CONTINUING OBSERVATION — CLASSIFICATION WING.
A reading that does not settle cannot be closed. Accounts of this class are entered on a register and held.
Register opened nine years ago. Thirty-one entries. Entries are accounts. Entries are not persons.

Lin Hao read the third line four times.

Register opened nine years ago. Thirty-one entries.

There was a column of plate numbers down the sheet in a clerk's hand, in the order they had been entered, and about half of them had a date against them and about half of them did not, and the dates ran from nine years ago to the 3rd of this month, and the last number on the list was his own.

It had no name beside it, because the register was of accounts. That was the whole of it and it was the most frightening page in Ostara. Thirty-one of something the machine could not put in a band, and the Wing had been writing the numbers down for nine years, and there were no names on the page at all, and it did not need names: a plate number is a person, and a plate number that came in off the Ferrier line in June tells a man in a coat which line to stand on. Thirty-one people. The sheet had never once said so.

Runa Sato was standing at the far end of the crowd with her hands in her coat.

She let him get to her, which took a while, and when he did she looked at the sheet and not at him.

"You have been reading about it since the 8th," she said.

"Since a quarter past nine this morning."

"You had it in a Field document on the 6th, you read it on the 8th, and you came down here on Thursday to look at it on a wall." She turned her head. "Yes. I am going to say something and then it is your week and not mine. I am allowed to be angry for about four seconds because I handed you that sheet in a corridor on the 6th and you have not looked at me once."

"I have looked at you."

"You have not looked at me once, and I have read four other files this month, and none of the four have a man on a board with a figure on it." She pulled her hand out of her coat. "Runa Sato, Field, observation only, filed on the 6th. Read by a man in an office I have never been in."

"What did he write?"

"He wrote *declared and consistent*," she said, "which is the best four words that office has ever put about a person who is not inside it, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and you have four words of paper between you and a man who comes to take readings himself."

Then she looked at the register and did not say anything for a moment.

"That is not on my sheet," she said. "I have never seen it. I have been in this division nine years and I have never seen that page, and it has been on that wall for nine years, and it went up on the same morning a Wing clerk walked past a man in a washroom and entered a plate number on a list."

She put her finger on the top line of the column without touching it.

"Nine years," she said. "Thirty-one. And you have been in this building four years, and Lin Wei went out of the public record three, and there is a woman on the Ferrier line who has been keeping a headache diary for four." She let her hand drop. "That is a five-year gap at the front of that list that nobody has ever spoken to anybody about, and I do not know what is in it, and I have been trying to work it out since the 6th on the tram."

"Five years before your book."

"Five years before anyone's book," Runa said. "The first entry on that sheet is nine years old and it has no date of entry because it predates the column, which means somebody opened this register by hand, in pencil, on a different sheet, and then got a proper form printed. The account in it is still going. It is the only one of the thirty-one that is."

The painters had knocked off at ten, the way they always did, and the Square was already emptying into the lunch hour and the wet stone and the smell of wet wool and carbolic from about four thousand people who had come to look at a wall.

Lin Hao stood under a board nine streets from a building where a corridor full of people knew his face, with his name on a white tile four rows from the top, four figures under it in the Board's own upright print, and the fifth of them the number that everybody in Ostara could now read and not one of them could band.

Nine years. Thirty-one accounts. Nineteen people.

And somewhere behind him a clerk had taken a brush and was already painting over the tile of a man named Costello, who had dropped a band, and would not be able to see it until the next Thursday.

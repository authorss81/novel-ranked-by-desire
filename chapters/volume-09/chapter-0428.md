# The Woman Who Priced The Queue

The loading bay off Cordwain had a bench under the awning and a platform scale set into the concrete at the back of it, and the scale was there because that bay took deliveries that went out on the guild road at four in the morning, and nobody in the house had ever asked why there was a scale and everybody in the house had noticed it.

Runa Sato was sitting on the bench at about eleven at night on the twenty-first of May with her coat open and her hat in her hand, and she stood up when he came round the corner, and the bench did not make a sound when she did, which was the first thing he noticed and the thing he would still have been thinking about four days later.

"First," she said, "and I am saying it before anything else happens in this bay, and I have never done it in my life for anybody and I am doing it because two women in this house got ahead of me at this and neither of the two lied to you, and I am not going to either."

"Go on."

"I am not tired, I am not on anything, I am not drunk and I am not sick, and the only thing that has gone into me today is a foreman with a docket and about nine hours of other people's week."

"I have eaten. I am exactly who I was at eleven o'clock, I am staying exactly where I am, and I will not be tired by about one."

She put the hat down on the bench. "And I have wanted you since the twenty-seventh of October, out loud, in a bay, in the plainest words this city has got, and I did it in a form with a reason box and I did not tell you for seven months and I am telling you now because I am not going to be nine minutes late to my own decision because I was careful."

## What She Says Before Anything Else Happens In The Bay

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is in the next four minutes and you are going to have to stand still for it."

She came off the bench and put her hand flat on it.

"I carry weight. That is my gift, it has been my gift since I was a junior, and I have never once in my life been able to put it down. Mass. Later, other things, and I will tell you about those in about a year and not tonight."

"What I carry, my body takes. I am heavier than I look by a stone and a half and I have been heavier by a stone and a half since I came into this book and it is going to keep going, and I have stopped discussing it, and I am discussing it now because you are going to feel it and I am not going to have you think it is a trick."

"That is the cost."

"That is the cost and I have never told anybody the figure and I have never stopped paying it."

She looked at the concrete.

"And neither of those is going to be how this happens. I want that said in the order it happened. The weight is not the mechanism. Nothing about my body is the mechanism. If you turn round at any point in the next two hours because you are worried about me, I will get my coat and go home."

"And you will not get the rest of it because you were worried about me."

"That is allowed."

"It is allowed. I am simply not doing it." She almost smiled. "And the third thing, and I am naming it because a woman told me to name things in this book or nobody would know which one we had done. Petra broke it on a terms sheet and a written promise and a boiler. Nadia broke it by giving no terms at all and telling you to work it out. Both of them broke it on the clock. Neither of them described you."

She stepped away from the bench.

"Nobody in nine volumes is going to describe you three times in words," said Runa Sato. "Not tonight."

"I am going to do this and you are going to find out what it is, and if you want it in words you are going to have to ask me for it afterwards and I will probably say no. That is the one nobody has broken and I am breaking it in a loading bay at eleven at night, and there is no sheet and no kettle and no clock in here."

## The Scale

The scale was in the concrete behind the bench, and it was a floor plate with a dial on a post at hip height, and it had been taking deliveries for eleven years and the needle had a smear on the glass from somebody's coat sleeve.

She stepped onto it without being asked, the way a person steps onto a thing they have been thinking about all week.

"That is the figure I carry," she said. "That is my weight, and I have known it since I was a junior clerk, and I have never once put it on a public scale."

"I am putting it on this one because it is the only scale in this city I know of that has no clerk attached to it and no book under it. I am putting it on it because in about four minutes I am going to hand you something that will not be thanked for, and I would like a number in this bay that is not about either of us."

She got off it. The needle came back down and stopped about a fifth of the way round, and she wrote the figure on the back of her hand with a pen she took out of her coat, which he did not have time to stop and would think about afterwards.

"I am aware of what I have done with that number," she said. "Put it in a book and it is a fact about me. Leave it it is a thing a woman said in a bay. I have left it."

Then she took her coat off and hung it on the end of the bench and came to him, and he understood in the first four seconds what a stone and a half is when it is a woman who is not pretending to be lighter, and there was nothing wrong with it at all, and it was the heaviest thing that had ever happened to him and it did not move him off the ground.

## The Bay

"You are going to want me to be quick," she said, somewhere in the middle of it, with her mouth against the side of his neck. "You have had two women in this book who were fast and one of them was fast on purpose. I am not fast."

"I have spent four years being a woman who gets to the bottom of things and I have earned the right to take my time. I am going to take about two hours and you are going to lie there and let me."

He was not going to lie there.

He had been on the end of a bench in a bay with a scale in it and a woman heavier than she looked and a figure written on the back of her hand.

Four minutes later he had both hands under her and the awning pole against his shoulder and her whole weight on him. None of it was holding him and all of it was her. He understood the sentence about the mechanism and did not think about it again until the small hours.

She took her time.

She got her own shirt open with one hand and looked down at what she had done about it and said it was competent work, in that voice, the one she uses to address a hundred people at the end of a market, and he laughed, and she put a hand flat on his chest and pushed him back onto the bench slats and told him exactly what she was going to do next in about nine words and in an order.

He got it wrong the first time on purpose, to see what happened.

What happened was that she stopped entirely and waited.

"Say it again and do it properly."

He did, and she made him say it a third time, and by the third her hand had gone round the back of his neck with her mouth on his ear, the smile had gone out of her entirely, and she made him slow down twice after that.

Then she got up onto him.

She came up onto him off the bench and came down onto him with her knees either side of his hips and her hands flat on his chest and the whole of her weight arriving at once, which she had told him about and which he had not believed.

She went down onto him slowly with her mouth open and her head back and watched his face the entire way, and she was very wet before anything had happened at all, and she said so out loud in the flat voice she uses when she reads a figure out of a column.

"There. That is the fourth time I have said something out loud tonight that I have never said to anybody, and you have now got all four, and you may keep them, and one of them is on the back of my hand."

He got a hand under her and she permitted it to stay, which was never on offer, and she rode his fingers up inside her until her mouth shut and her thighs came up against his ribs and her heels came off the slats of the bench.

"Slower," she said. "You are allowed to be fast and you are not going to be."

She made him go slower. She made him do it twice more after he had got it right, and she did it by stopping, completely, with her hips still moving about a quarter of an inch and her hand flat on his sternum, and saying nothing at all until he slowed.

"Do you know what you are lying on," she said, much later, when she was on top of him facing him and had not come yet and was clearly not going to be hurried. "Eleven years of guild freight and a bench that four men a week sit on at four in the morning. And I am on it, and I am heavier than the man who came to this bay an hour ago, and neither of us has said anything about it."

"You have not been getting lighter."

"I have not been getting lighter and I am not going to and I have stopped discussing it, and tonight I am going to tell you what it is because you are going to watch it happen."

She put her hand on his face.

"When I do something for somebody and somebody takes it off me, it goes into me. It does not go anywhere and it does not go into a book."

"It comes into my frame and it stays in my frame and my frame has to carry it and my frame is what carries it. That is the whole of the trade and I have been in it for nine years and I have never once told anybody, because the figure is in the second decimal place of a woman of my age and nobody asks for a second decimal place."

"Ask for it."

"You have just asked for it, so you are getting it, and after this I have nothing further on the subject until the twenty-eighth of May."

She went down onto him and stayed there.

"Beatrix says you have gone down to about five cold minutes a day for nine volumes. The last one was on the twenty-third of April and you gave four or five seconds to a man in a queue who had not agreed to anything."

"And it is in me now. All four of it. And it stays there, and nobody thanks me for it, and I told you in a bay on the twenty-seventh of April that there would be no thanking, and tonight I meant it more than I did then."

She came with his name in her mouth and then without it, and after that she stopped talking altogether for about a minute and a half and simply worked him, and the bay was very quiet and the whole of it came back off the concrete under them.

## The Reason Box



She sat on the bench afterwards with one leg over the other and her boots on and her shirt buttoned and the pen in her hand, and she did not drink anything and she did not smoke and she took about ninety seconds before she said anything, and he let her have them.

"Right," said Runa Sato. "Now the thing you came up sixty-one steps for."

"There is no reason to think you do not know why I came."

"I priced it on the way here and the price is a sheet of paper with my reason in it, and it is mine and not the household's."

She turned the pen over.

"Twenty-seventh of October, four years ago now. An order to watch a door, put in my own name at the foot of the application. Granted in about four minutes by a division officer who had never heard of any of you and did not ask."

"And I applied for it in the morning, before I had any idea at all what I was going to end up answering for."

"You told me that on the twenty-seventh of April and you have never invoiced it and I have never asked you to release it."

"I have not invoiced it and I am not going to and you are not going to ask me, and that arrangement is older than this conversation." She looked at the scale. "The reason box is blank. It has been blank for four years and it is going to stay blank for the rest of my life, and you know why it is blank, because I told you in this bay on the twenty-seventh and you wrote it down and you have not once since repeated it."

"I remember what you told me."

"You remember what I told you. On the twenty-eighth of May I am going to write in it."

He sat up.

"Not the household's. Not a favour and not a favour's price and not a debt."

She was looking straight at him.

"I am going to write a reason in the reason box of an instrument I applied for in my own name four years ago. The reason is going to be a sentence, and the sentence is going to say who put me in front of that door and why I said yes to him, and it is going to name you."

"I am going to write it with my own hand and I am not going to invoice it and I am not going to tell you what it says before I write it."

"Runa—"

"You do not get to talk me out of it and you were never going to be able to."

She stood up.

"A box that has been empty for four years on an order to observe a kitchen is, to anybody who comes across it in a folder, indistinguishable from a man arranging for a woman to be findable in front of his own door."

"That is what I said to you in this bay on the twenty-seventh and it is the reason the box is empty and it is the reason it is going to stop being empty. If I put a reason in that box then it stops being an arrangement and becomes a record, and a record can be read by a stranger and a stranger can check it, and I would rather be checked than be the woman who held an order for four years with nothing on it."

"That is going to cost you."

"It is going to cost me a great deal and I have not found it yet, and I told you in this bay on the twenty-seventh that it was going to, and I was right about that too."

She put the pen in her coat and did not smile.

"And There will be no thanking for it. Not tonight, not at the end of it, not in four years, and if anybody at that kitchen thanks me for this I will price them out loud at the table and they will not enjoy it."

## What She Was Going To Sign And Did Not

"I did owe you something," she said. "You came to this house in April and told eight women that a line would carry every one's name under you for four years and would not let one of you help with it."

"I priced a certificate in this bay on the twenty-seventh and decided on the twelfth of April at four in the morning to put my name at the head of a composition of that address. Then a woman at a table told me what the figure on that certificate is, and I un-decided it on the third of May in front of eight people and told a Field office I am not attending and gave no reason."

"You gave a reason here. On the seventh of May you gave eight of them."

"I gave them a reason that could not be filed and this is a different thing and you should not confuse the two." She got her hat off the bench. "The certificate is not going to happen. There is no version of the twenty-eighth of May in which I sign a figure of seven that a woman put in at a counter in nine minutes, and that has not changed since the third and it is not up for discussion tonight."

"And this is what you are giving instead."

"No," said Runa Sato. "This is what I am taking. The certificate would have been a thing I did for that address and I would have been thanked for it and it would have cost a woman something she has already paid."

"This is a thing I am doing about you, in my own name, on my own instrument, with my own sentence in it, and nobody is going to be in it but me and a clerk who will be told nothing."

She got her coat on.

"That is the difference and it is the entire difference, and I have been pricing things for nine years and it took me until about eleven o'clock tonight to price the difference between those two things properly."

## The Figure On The Back Of Her Hand

She walked out into Cordwain and stopped at the mouth of the bay with her hands in her sleeves.

"Do not follow me up those sixty-one steps," she said. "I mean that kindly and I mean it practically and there is a woman on the second floor who has a thing coming down that stair at four in the morning and I would rather that corridor was empty when it happens."

He stood in the bay with the bench behind him and the scale in the concrete and the yard light off and a woman four hundred feet away walking up an iron stair slowly, on purpose, like a person carrying something.

The figure was still on the back of her left hand. He saw it go under the light at the foot of the stairs and then the light took it round the corner and he never saw it again, and he never told anybody what it was, and it was not in any book.

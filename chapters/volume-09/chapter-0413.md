# A Queue Off The Third Court

The public post off a third court opens at eight and shuts at four, and between those two hours there is a queue in it about nine days in ten, and on the morning of the twenty-third of April he was in it for the third time in eight days and he had not come for anything.

He had come because there was nowhere else to be at half past eight, and because a man is allowed to stand in a corridor off a second landing for as long as he likes, and because since the fifteenth he had nothing to show anybody and the queue was eleven long.

There was a man at the front of it with a slip in his good hand and his other hand held the way a person holds a hand that has not been right since about the sixteenth.

## Eleven In It, And A Counter That Shuts At Four

The slip said a sum due on a loading contract and the sum was not large and the clerk at the first counter had told him on the sixteenth that a claim on work needs two people who were in a room with him on the day the work was done, and it needs them in writing, and it needs their names.

He had been getting those two people since the sixteenth. That was seven days.

The queue moved about a foot and stopped.

"You have got until four," said the woman in front of him, without turning round, to nobody. "The counter shuts at four whatever is in the queue and there are nine of us and about two of those a minute, so do the arithmetic in your own head and not out loud, love."

Nine of us. He did the arithmetic and it came to about twenty-seven minutes, and he had been in the queue since half past eight, and it was ten past nine, and the man with the hand had been in it since before him.

## The Hand He Put His Own On

He did not decide to do it.

That is the part he could not get back to afterwards, and it is the only part of the whole morning that he was able to be certain of afterwards, because everything else about it went in the other order. He was standing at a rail in a hall off a third court with his hands in his coat, and the man turned round to give him room, and their hands were not near each other, and then they were.

What came out of him went into the man and the man's face came up out of it in about a second and a half, the way a face comes up out of a thing that has been owed and has arrived early.

The relief did not make the man stronger. It made him finished. The set of the jaw went out of it. The hand on the rail stopped being held like a hand.

Lin Hao felt the cost on his own side, which was four or five seconds of a cold that came up through his palm and into his wrist, and he had felt that exact thing about nine times in as many years and had not felt it once since the thirtieth of December.

That was the first thing that was wrong with the morning, and it took him about nine seconds to work out what it was.

The man had been fed by something. Nobody in a hall off a third court is fed by the queue, and nobody standing in it had said anything to anybody, and there was no instrument in the building that could see a thing done nine days ago in a lane behind Ferrier Street by a man with a handcart who carried an old woman three floors to a clinic door because carrying her was quicker than waiting for help.

He knew the lane. It was two streets from his own front door. It had been the ninth of April and it had been about two in the morning.

## What The Man Said About It

"I am sorry," said the man.

"Nobody said anything."

"You have got a cold hand," said the man. "That is not a complaint, that is a fact, and I am saying it because I am not going to say anything else about it."

He did not say anything else about it. He put the slip on the counter when he got to it and the counter took the slip and looked at it and told him that he had come to the wrong counter for a claim on work and that the second counter on the left was where work was done, and he went to the second counter on the left and was gone for six minutes.

When he came back he was a different man. Not better. Different. There was a thing about his hand that had not been there at half past eight.

He went out past Lin Hao and stopped.

"You are in that queue every morning," he said. "I have seen you three times. Have I been rude to you."

"No."

"Because I have been rude to everybody for a week and I have not slept and I was not going to say anything to a man in a queue." He shifted the good hand. "Do you work here."

"No."

"Then you are like me and I am sorry about that as well." He went out into the street, and it was four minutes to ten, and the queue was ten long, and Lin Hao did not follow him and could not have told anybody where he had gone.

## One Hundred And Forty-Nine

He counted the book at about a quarter to eleven in the morning in the two rooms he had at Ferrier Street with the door shut.

A hundred and forty-nine lines.

It had been a hundred and forty-two on the fourteenth of March, and it rises because he keeps his round, and a round is not a decision. It is a set of streets and a set of hours and about ninety minutes a day in which he is the man who is there when nobody is looking, and it has not stopped in years and it is going to stop the day he stops.

Nine of the hundred and forty-nine were turned.

The ninth of them was him.

That was the part he could not put down. He had known about it since the fourth of January and had never once been able to make it mean anything, and a man who has felt nine of those on his own body cannot pretend to be surprised at the tenth.

The tenth was a man with a handcart who had carried an old woman three floors in the dark on the ninth of April because it was quicker than waiting, and who had been standing in that hall since before half past eight with a hand he could not close, and who had said *you have got a cold hand* and nothing else and had gone out into the street.

Nine turned lines in one season is a legible pattern. An analyst who is good at it can take the pattern and put an address on the end of it.

He had kept his count under a number for five years and had never told that number to a living soul, and on the morning of the twenty-third of April he went over it for the first time and found out what it was, which was that he had been managing two numbers for five years and one of them had been the wrong one.

The hundred and forty-nine was harmless. The hundred and forty-nine was just work.

The ten was the one with a face on it.

## What He Cannot Put Down

He sat with it until about half past eleven.

There was no way to undo it. That was not a feeling and it was not a fear.

It was the shape of the thing: the account gives back only what was given to it, and what was given to it was the ninth of April, and the ninth of April was in the man's legs and his back and the part of him that had carried an old woman three floors because carrying her was quicker, and that had gone into a hand on a rail at ten past nine this morning and it was not coming back.

The man had not agreed to anything. That was the part that sat under everything else.

Seven women had been asked. Eight had been asked in a kitchen with the price said out loud first. A woman had been asked at a Field counter and had said no in one syllable and had been entirely right. Nobody in this city had ever been asked and nobody in this city had ever been touched.

He had put his hand on a stranger in a queue because the stranger was owed something and he had it, and there is no instrument anywhere for the second half of that sentence.

A stranger was owed relief and he had touched a stranger, and the stranger had felt it, and a person who has felt it once is a person who knows it happened.

He got up at about half past eleven and went and washed his hands in cold water for longer than he needed to, and the cold did not come out of it.

## The Queue At Four

At about ten past four he went back down the iron stair and out into the street and stood on the corner, because the counter shuts at four and whatever is in the queue at four is not in the queue on the following morning.

Two women were still standing in it when the shutters came down, and one of them had a slip and the other one had come to ask about a slip.

Neither of them was him.

That was the whole of the morning.

Sixty-three days ago the twentieth of February had been laid on a table at the end of a corridor and was still on it, and a room of five could carry it, and the corridor was nine days old as a member of anything.

At ten past nine that morning he had gone over a rail in a hall off a third court and put his own hand on a stranger's hand and spent the first thing he has spent since he started keeping the round on a man who has never heard the word reservoir and never will.

The ledger has a hundred and forty-nine lines in it and ten of them have been turned and the tenth one has a face.

He knew what it was for. That was the terrible part. He knew exactly what a turned line is for and it is not a punishment and it is not a warning. It is a door somebody will open one day in an ordinary way, with an ordinary reason, by somebody who is only being careful.

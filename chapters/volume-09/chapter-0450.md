# The Name On The Black Tile

"One hundred and thirty days since the twentieth of February," he said, to a doorway. "Eight from twenty leaves eight, with thirty-one and thirty and thirty-one and thirty makes one hundred and thirty. And thirty taken from thirty leaves nothing, which is the last working day of the quarter, and there are five things on it and I am not at any of them."

He was four floors away and about a street away at six o'clock in the morning on the thirtieth of June, on his round, ninety minutes of streets and about four doors, being the man who is there when nobody is looking, and he had known since the afternoon of the twenty-eighth that he would be exactly there.

He had gone to that corner at ten to six on the strength of a decision he made in a street off a third court, and he stood on it for about three quarters of an hour instead.

At about a quarter past six he walked up to the north end of the ranks wall and the card was up and the wall was finished and there was a woman standing under it with a notebook shut on the ledge and her hands cold round the pencil.

"You were not here," said Beatrix Halm.

"No."

"You were not here at half past four either, and I looked, and I do that every morning." She took her hand off the ledge. "Say why. You are going to make me work it out and I have got about four minutes before this square fills up and I would rather have it in words."

"Because you read this wall alone," he said, "and you have read it alone for nine years, and there is a boy with a paste brush on it at half past four on the morning a card comes down, and if I had been standing at the north end when you got the chalk out then everything you did today would have been done with a man under it."

She looked at him for about three seconds and then she put the pencil in her pocket.

"Good," said Beatrix Halm.

## The Two Figures

"Read them to me."

"Two figures, as always, and I read them before it was six inches off the wall and I have written them down and I want you to hear them in my voice and not in yours, because you have said both of them out loud in this square before and I do not want to hear yours today."

"Read them."

"Eight thousand one hundred and forty-seven. Floor." She had her hand flat on the wall above the card, the way she had on the thirtieth of March. "And against premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight — "

She stopped, and read the rest of it twice more, and then she went and got her notebook off the ledge and opened it on the stone and turned it round and pushed it at him.

*Eight thousand one hundred and forty-seven. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one.*

The card itself is on the wall in the office's own hand on the office's own paper, and it reads, in figures, 8,147 across the top of it, and, in the column under that, against premises 34-6-118, 8,131.

"Sixteen under," said Beatrix Halm. "Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-seven. On the thirtieth of March it was fifteen under a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-six, and I wrote that down in this notebook in the dark at ten to six with my hands too cold to hold the pencil, and the floor has gone up one and you have not moved a grain. Out of band by thirteen."

"The tolerance is three grains and it has been three grains since I started."

## Why A Number That Has Not Moved

"A plate is not a count of people," he said. "It moves on attested deeds."

"It moves on attested deeds and there has not been one at that address since the thirtieth of December, and there has not been one since the thirtieth of December because eight people have been going about a house doing nothing that can be attested and one of them gave up an address at a counter in March and that was a bill and not a deed." She shut the notebook and put it under her arm.

"And there is something else and I have been chewing on it since about ten past six and you can have it now."

"What."

"That address is read on that card at the composition of that premises, at the hour it is read, and the composition of that premises has said seven since nine forty-one on the morning of the thirteenth of March, and the card read it at seven this morning and it did not go up by one for the eight people asleep under that roof." She knocked the wall once with the side of her hand. "It does not know about us. It has never once known about us."

"That card has been coming down at six for ninety days and it has printed eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one against that address every quarter, and the certificate of that house is made four times a year by a woman in her own division in her own hand, and neither of those two things has ever touched the other one, and in about four years somebody is going to find that out and write it down as though it had always been so."

## The Top Tile

He looked up then, because she had stopped, and the tiles go up the wall in ranks under a rail and the lowest of them is a colour and the highest of them is a different colour from all of them, and has been empty since before she started.

"There is a name on the black one," he said.

"There is a name on the black one," said Beatrix Halm, "and it is in chalk and it is in my hand, and I put it there at about ten past six this morning, and you are the first person who has seen it and you have been here six minutes."

"Why you."

"Because there is nothing to press." She did not take her hand off the wall. "Every other tile on this wall gets a name because a plate goes into a stone at a desk and a man reads the stone and the name is on the tile by the time anybody walks past."

"There is no plate for that one and there is no card and there is no floor to press into, because nine is not a band and there has never been a card with nine on it in my lifetime or my mother's."

"So it is black, and it is empty, and nothing is ever put on it, and there is no office in this city that could put a name on it if you asked them to, because there is nothing for them to read off."

She got the chalk out of her pocket and put it in his hand and did not let go of it.

"Nine years," said Beatrix Halm. "Nine years of standing under that wall in the dark with my hands cold, and every morning I looked up at the black one and there was nothing on it, and I want you to understand what I did and why. I did not do it for the city."

"I did it because I have spent nine years writing down the times of things that happened to other people and there has never been a single morning when there was anything of mine to write down."

## The Notebook

She took the chalk back and put it away and opened the notebook on the ledge and wrote, and she wrote the way she writes: quickly, in order, and with the hand that is not the one she holds the pencil in.

*Thirtieth. Six. Eight thousand one hundred and forty-seven. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one. Sixteen under.*

Then she stopped, and looked at the line, and put the pencil back on it and wrote one more thing underneath.

"Lin Hao," she said. "That is under it. There is nothing under any of the others. Seven lines in nine years with something under them and six of them were places and one of them was a person, and I have never once written a name at the bottom of a line in this notebook, because a name is not a time and I have not got a column for one."

She turned the book round and pushed it along the ledge to him, the way she had pushed it along the table in a room above a laundry nine days before.

"Read it," said Beatrix Halm, "and then give it back and do not say anything about it, because you are going to say something about it and I have watched nine years of men open their mouths in this square and I have never once wanted to hear it."

## What He Asked

"How long were you up there."

"From half past four." She took the notebook back and shut it and put it under her arm. "The boy came at half past four and he worked until the card was on and then he put the case back and went off to his office on the other side of the court and he did not say one word to me, then or at any other time."

"He does not know whose premises he read and he is not permitted to ask, and I have watched four of those boys in nine years and not one of them has ever looked up at the tiles while they work."

"And then you went up with the chalk."

"Then I stood here and read the card twice, and worked out that a floor that goes up one and a figure that does not move is the whole sentence of my life for nine years, and then I got the chalk out of my coat." She was not looking at him.

"There is a name on the black tile at the top of that wall and it will be read by every person who comes out of the Reading on this side of the square, every morning, for as long as the wall is here, and none of them will know what any of it means, and that is exactly how a plate works and exactly how a name works and I am not going to be embarrassed about it."

## What Is Still Open

He looked up at the tiles for a while. The square was filling up about thirty yards away and the lamps along it had not gone off yet although it was properly light.

"What does it do," he said. "The tile."

"Nothing," said Beatrix Halm. "You have been rank nine since the fifteenth of April and that tile is the only part of it that has ever been visible to anybody. It does not carry you into a room, it does not open a door, and it is not a certificate and there is nothing behind it."

"I read a whole record of you out loud to four strangers in a room over the counting house a week ago and I did it in public with my name and the hour on a book, and none of it mattered as much as a woman with a pencil at a wall on the morning of a card."

"It opens nothing at all."

"It opens nothing at all," said Beatrix Halm, "and you knew that in April, and you did it anyway, in a queue, with a pen, because it was the only door and you had decided you wanted to be the kind of man who goes through a door when there is one. That is not mine to judge and I am not judging it. I have wanted a thing put on a wall for nine years and you have got a tile."

"When you are standing under that black one on some morning in four years and you are wondering whether any of it was worth a girl who certified her own front door four times a year for thirty years, come and find me and I will read you the notebook."

She turned and started up the square toward the tram stop, and then stopped and came back four steps.

"I have not thanked you for not being here," said Beatrix Halm, "and I am not going to, and if you had been here I would have been at the north end for nine years and nine days and I would have put a name on that tile with a man under it and I would have known who it was for. You were not here. I was alone on it."

"That is the whole of what I wanted and it cost you a morning and it cost me nothing at all, and the ledger in your coat is not a prize and you know it."

Then she went, and the square filled up, and he stood under the ranks wall with a chalk mark on his own fingers and a corridor to be at at two o'clock in the afternoon.

## The Book In His Coat

He took the flat book out of the inside pocket of his coat, the one with a pencil elastic round it and about ninety days of a round written in one hand in a hand that was not his, and he opened it on the stone ledge and counted the lines with his thumb the way he had counted them on the ninth of May at half past ten.

One hundred and fifty-eight. He had counted one hundred and fifty-one that night at half past ten with a woman's voice still in his head about a number told twice at a corner counter, and it has gained seven lines in seven weeks, one a week, because a round writes a line when there is something on it and the round does not stop for anything.

Ten of them were turned. Nine of them had been turned since the fourth of January and the ninth of those was himself, and the tenth was a carter who works a lane off Ferrier Street and carried an old woman up three floors at two in the morning on the ninth of April because that was quicker than waiting for help, and said *you have got a cold hand*, and went out into the street.

[Ledger. One hundred and fifty-eight lines. Ten turned. None of them is him.]

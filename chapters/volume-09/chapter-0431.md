# Four Times A Year In Her Own Hand

The Vestry clinic keeps its cold store on the ground floor and its ward above it and Tessa Okonkwo's own rooms over the cold store, and on the morning of the twenty-fifth of May she had a slip of paper on the table with a ruled line on it and eleven dates ruled on that line and a twelfth ruled on that day and not filled in.

"You have been ruling dates since October," he said.

"I have been ruling dates since the nineteenth of October," said Tessa Okonkwo, "because I am a woman who rules dates and because there is nothing else to do with a hand. Those eleven are not all about you. Six of them are about a man I have been holding for two years, and I could not tell you his name, and he has never asked me for it."

She turned the slip round.

## The Last Working Day Of A Quarter

"The twelfth is the thirtieth of June," she said, "and I ruled it at about eight this morning and I have been looking at it for an hour."

"What happens on the thirtieth of June."

"Three things. Two of them I can prove and the third one I have worked out, and I have been in this building long enough to know which of those is the dangerous one."

She put the pen down.

"The first is that it is the last working day of the quarter. The second is that the Council sits on it. The third is that it is a fixed day, and it is fixed in exactly the same way for a man in a room with no window three floors under a Wing as it is going to be fixed for you, and it has been fixed that way since before either of us came here."

"Since the thirtieth of December."

"Since the thirtieth of December," said Tessa Okonkwo. "The thirtieth of December, the thirtieth of March, the thirtieth of June. A quarter each time."

She turned the slip a quarter of an inch.

"A held account gets administered on the last working day of the last month of its quarter, and the administration is done by a doctor, and it produces a medical return, and that return comes to this clinic four times a year."

"It has gone to this clinic since the account was opened."

"It will go to this clinic until the account is closed, and nobody ever asked it to. That is not a complaint about the doctor. That is how it is done and it is not done for anybody in particular."

## Two Returns In One Quarter

"Which means that on the thirtieth of June this clinic receives two returns about the same matter."

"It means that this clinic receives two returns on the thirtieth of June," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and that the two of them are about the same matter, and I want to be exact about why."

She turned the slip over and put it back.

"A clinic that receives two returns about one matter in one quarter is not a clinic with two patients. It is a clinic with one matter that has come loose from its person, and that is a thing you write a line about, and there is nowhere on the return to write it."

"Because they are both administered out of one account."

"They are both administered out of one account," she said. "One has been administered since long before you were anybody. The other begins on the thirtieth of June, because a man lodged his whole accumulated standing, in his own handwriting, on the fifteenth of April at ten minutes to nine, at a counter off a third court, in a queue, with a pen."

"A lodged citizen is held."

"A lodged citizen is held, and a held thing is administered on the same fixed day and returned to the same clinic as anything else that is held out of one account." She put her thumb on the ruled line. "And neither of the two was asked for by anybody. Neither of them ever is."

"You have known that since the fifteenth of April."

"I have known it since the fifteenth of April and I have said nothing about it to anybody, because I saw a paper in this building in the middle of March with a single box on it and I worked out what a box on a printed sheet means."

She looked up.

"The answer is a quarter, and I did not need nine volumes to get there. I have also promised a man in this city nothing beyond seeing what I see. That promise was made about a piece of paper and it has not been broken and I am not breaking it now."

## What The Return Will Have At The Head Of It

"Then what are you telling me."

"That I am going to write a line on the outside of the file," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Not on the return. On the outside of the file, on the front, in my own hand, before either of the two has been read by anybody else in this building."

"What does the line say."

"Two returns, one quarter, one matter. Dated. No reason given." She picked the pen up and put it down again without using it. "It is my clinic and my return and my administration and my line, and nobody has ever asked me to write it, and I am going to write it, and there is no thanks in it for anybody."

"Why not be thanked."

"Because a woman who writes a line on the outside of a file has told every division that ever sees it that the thing inside it is not one thing, and that is not a favour to the file. That is a warning about the file, and the file is mine."

"Do you know what I would like," she said, before he had got a word in. "I have wanted one thing for eleven years and I have never had it. I would like to be permitted to treat somebody without writing the file."

"That is the whole of it."

"That is all of it and I will not improve it. It is not a large want and it has taken me eleven years and I have never once said it out loud to anybody who could do anything about it, and I am saying it now to a man who cannot do anything about it." She shut her eyes for about a second. "I chose you on purpose and not by accident, and you may notice that I chose you."

"Noted."

"Write it down somewhere, then. In one of yours. Not in mine."

## The Tenth Line

He looked at the slip with the twelve dates on it.

"The tenth line of the one you have seen."

"The tenth line of the one from the thirtieth of December is in a different hand," said Tessa Okonkwo. "It says the man is held, three years, which is a quarter shorter than the last time. Then it says the doctor does not know what that means and is putting it in writing because she refuses to put down what she thinks it might mean."

She did not look up.

"That is a line written by somebody who has decided to be exactly correct. It is the most honest sentence in this building, and it is in a return about a man in a room with no window, and it has been in a drawer since the thirtieth of December, and nobody has asked the woman who wrote it what she meant and nobody is going to."

"She promised to say nothing else."

"She promised to say nothing else and she has kept it, and she is the only person in this city who has been able to keep a promise of that shape for five months."

She turned the slip over and put it under a book.

"On the thirtieth of June she will write another line. It will be the same hand and it will be the same kind of sentence and it will be about a room. And I will be standing in this clinic when it arrives, because I am the person who receives returns, and I am going to have to read two of them on the same morning."

"And one of them concerns a man who has never been ill in his life."

"The second is about a man who has never been ill in his life," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"The other is about a man who has been held for three years and a quarter shorter than the last, and I am going to read both of them, and I am not going to ask the woman who writes them a single question."

## What She Is Going To Do About It

"There is nothing you can do about it."

"There is one thing, and I have thought about it for about an hour, and I have decided to do it, and I am telling you the same morning."

She turned the pen over twice.

"A return goes to a clinic and a clinic is not a person. Every division that holds a part of a thing gets a copy, and the clinic holds the return and sends the return, and the part of a return that belongs to the address is the composition."

"The composition of seven."

"The composition of the address," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Which is certified four times a year at that address by the woman who keeps it, in her own division, in her own handwriting, under her own name, for as long as an account stays open."

"Yes."

"And I am going to write, on the outside of the file, in the same sentence I have already decided to write, the words *and the composition of the address*."

She said it as though she were entering a line in a book.

"Not a name. Not a division. The composition. Because in four years' time somebody will open that folder, find two returns about one matter in a single quarter, and ask what is being administered, and the answer will be on the front of the file in my hand, and it will be a number and a door."

She put the pen in the drawer and shut it.

"That is the whole of what I am permitted to say and it is more than anybody else in this city is saying. And it is going to cost me, and I know exactly what it costs, and you do not get to hear the figure."

"The cost is yours and I cannot help you with it."

"I take the damage," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That is my gift and my price, and I have been at it longer than I have been in this city, and I have a very good reason for knowing how much room I have left in myself, and the reason is that I count it."

She picked up the last cup on the desk.

"On the thirtieth of June I am going to have to read two returns in one morning and hold what is in them. I will do it. That is the whole of my evening and I have not told anybody that either."

The clinic was quiet underneath them for about a minute.

"One more thing," she said, "and then you can go and be frightened somewhere else."

"The return that is about you will be made by a doctor who has promised to say nothing else, and a doctor who has promised to say nothing else cannot be asked what she thinks of the man in the next room."

"And on the thirtieth of June she will be in and out of this building twice."

"And the second time she will be about four floors away on a corridor she has walked every working day for three years, and I do not know whether she will stop at it, and I have decided that I am not going to ask her."

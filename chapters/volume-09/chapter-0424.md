# A Recusal Only The Subject May Lift

Alma Quayle sent for him at nine in the morning on the thirteenth of May with four words on a slip, and the four words were a time and a door and no reason, which is how that building has asked him for everything for four months.

He went down sixty-one iron steps and along a sub-level and past counter-forensics, where a door was shut and a wire basket was on a desk, and she was at the end of it in a room with a glass front and no name on it, standing, which meant she had heard him coming and had decided not to sit down.

"Sit down," said Alma Quayle. "I have drafted this for about eleven days and I have read it four times and I am going to say it in about ninety seconds and then I am going to stop, and you are not to interrupt me before the ninety seconds, because in about four years I am going to be asked whether I was allowed to finish."

He sat down.

"A requisition was laid on the twentieth of February at nine minutes past ten in a room at the end of a corridor, and it was grounded on a description of premises 34-6-118 made in this building, in this wing, at about eleven in the morning on the twenty-seventh of October, over the head of the premises, by a clerk on my desk, on four words a man said in a kitchen."

She had a sheet face down in front of her and she did not turn it over. "I was the head of classification on the twenty-seventh of October. I have been the head of classification for longer than that. I signed that division's output that week the way I sign its output every week, because that is what a head does, and it is not a signature on the description and there is no place on the description for a signature."

"And on the second of December you recused yourself."

"On the second of December I recused myself and it was accepted on the fourth and it is on my own signature, and it is the only thing about the twentieth of February that I have ever been able to do anything with."

She turned round and faced him properly.

"And in four years somebody will read the fourth part of that requisition and come at me with a question. The question will be whether a woman who was recused about a sheet in December made the ground for it in October."

"There is no answer to that on any paper in this city and there never will be one. You cannot un-recuse yourself, and you cannot enter into a book a thing that happened before the recusal."

"Then what do you want."

"I want you to lift it."

## The Only Thing He Could Give

"That is not yours," said Alma Quayle. "A recusal is lifted by the subject and by nobody else, and there is no sheet anywhere in this city for lifting one, and I have looked, and I asked the chair three times between the second of December and the fifth of March and she said no in one word each time and would not see me again."

"And she is right not to see you."

"She is entirely right not to see me and I have known it since the first week of December and I went on asking her three times like a woman who had not worked it out, and there is a difference between being right and being asked."

She put her thumb on the sheet and did not move it. "Which leaves you. You are the only person alive who can say one sentence in that room that lifts me, and you have never once in four months come down this stair to use anything, and I have noticed that, and it is the only reason I am asking."

"How does it get into a book."

"You say it out loud in front of the clerk, and she writes it down, and that is all. There is no instrument. A clerk may only enter and cannot correct, so whatever sentence you use will be in her book for four years in a hand you did not choose, and it cannot be taken out, and there is no way of going back to a room and being recused again afterwards."

"And when does she write."

"Her book is written up when the room sits and the clerk enters what she can see."

Alma Quayle sat down, which was the first thing she had done since he came in.

"The room sits when it is called. It has not been called since the first of April. The next sitting that can carry anything is the thirtieth of June, the final working day of the quarter, on which the Council sits and does not sit again until the first of October. A word said to that clerk after the thirtieth of June is not written until the third."

"So the door shuts on the thirtieth of June."

"That door shut for the whole of this quarter about six weeks ago and not one person in that building has said so out loud, because not one of them wants to be the one who says it."

She picked up the sheet and put it in a drawer, and the drawer was not locked, and both of them looked at it. "Say the word now and I am in that room as a member with a standing, and I can put one thing into the minute of that carrying, and it is the only thing I have left to give that room in a year."

## The Fourth Time

"What is the thing you would put in it."

"That the description was made on the twenty-seven of October, by an officer of a division that was not recused on anything, and that the ground was in existence and in a drawer in this building for three months and two days before anybody in that room could have used it."

She put her hands flat on the desk on either side of the drawer. "That is a fact. It is the only fact I have that is worth a line and it goes in the minute or it goes nowhere, and it is the difference in four years between a stranger thinking that a woman cooked her own ground and a stranger reading a date."

He thought about it for longer than she expected him to.

"No," he said.

Alma Quayle did not move for about three seconds.

"That is the fourth time," she said, "and I am not going to pretend I did not know which four, and I want you to hear the rest of it, because the rest of it is not the reason and I have thought about the reason for eleven days."

"Go on."

"The reason is a number. You decided on the evening of the ninth of May at about ten past five that you were not going to use the only instrument you have, and you did not tell anybody, and I do not know a word of that and I have just told you that I do not, and if I am wrong about what you decided then everything below this is wrong as well and I would rather be wrong in front of you than right to somebody else."

He did not say anything.

"Your instrument is not a door," said Alma Quayle. "You cannot ask that room to do anything. You have no sheet and you will never have a sheet. What you have is that a room of five can be made to need something, and every time it is made to need something a woman pays for it out of her own future and is never told, and you worked that out on the ninth of May and you put it down." She turned her chair about nine inches.

"Lifting my recusal is using it. It is using it in the clearest possible way in the world, on the clearest possible day, in the last three weeks of a quarter in which the Council cannot sit to undo it. You would be putting a sixth seal in a room by asking a man who is the man the sheet is about. That is not a thing a subject does to a recused member. That is a thing a subject does to a room."

## What It Was Not

He said nothing at all, which was the answer, and which told her exactly as much as it told everybody else.

"That is not a confession," said Alma Quayle. "You have not told me anything. I am not asking you to tell me and I am not going to ask you a second time, and if I have it wrong then I am a head of classification who has guessed, and I have been wrong about four things in this building in thirty years."

"If you have it right."

"Then you have got a person in this city who knows it, and she sits behind glass and cannot be asked anything and is never going to be asked anything, and that is worse for you and not better, and I wanted you to have that before you said the rest of it."

He waited.

"I am not doing this because I want you out of that room."

Alma Quayle looked up.

"I want you in it," he said. "That is the whole difficulty and it has been the difficulty since the fourth of December. You are the best reader in that room. You found the phrase *at a matching composition* and you read four words back at a man at a counter in eleven minutes and you have never once been unkind to anybody in this business."

"And if you were in there on the thirtieth of June with a standing, then five people carry a sheet and one of them is a man who put the sixth there himself three weeks before a sitting that the Council cannot overrule until October, and in four years somebody reads that minute and does not need to be clever. He reads it in about four seconds."

"You would be putting a name under yourself again."

"I would be putting my own hand on the thing I swore to myself on the ninth of May that I was never going to do, and the eight women at my table would never be told, and neither would you, and that is the part I am not willing to do."

The sub-level was very quiet. On the other side of a wall a woman who had been at the same desk for eleven years initialled transmissions above her own part in four seconds and had never been asked anything.

"There is a deadline and you know what it is," he said. "Say a word to that clerk on the twenty-ninth of May or say it on the thirtieth of June and I am in that room with the sixth seal on the sheet and your line in the minute and your recusal gone, and the fact about the twenty-seventh of October is on the record for good. And I am going to let the thirtieth of June happen."

## What It Cost Her

She sat with it for about half a minute and he did not fill the silence, because there was nothing in it for him.

"That is a very expensive thing to do to somebody," said Alma Quayle.

"Yes."

"It costs me one line. It costs me a date I can put in a minute that four people will read in four years, and it costs me the standing to say it, which I have not had since the fourth of December and had begun to think I would get back." She opened the drawer again, took the sheet out, and looked at it, and put it back.

"And it costs the room, because on the thirtieth of June that carrying is done by four people who have a reason and one man who is the man it is about, and I am a recused member who hears everything and takes no part, and I have been that since December and I am good at it, and I will be good at it on the thirtieth of June as well."

"You will be good at it," he said, "and I will not be able to use it, and both of those are true and only one of them is mine."

"Do not thank me."

"I am not going to."

"I am aware. You never do."

She stood up and went to the glass front and turned the blind down about four inches, which is what she did at the end of every conversation with him. "I am going to say one more thing and it is free and it is not a favour and it is not the beginning of anything, and you may take it or leave it and it is the only sentence I have said to you in four months that I have not written down first."

He waited.

"A recused member is in the room," said Alma Quayle. "Not out of it. In it, at a chair that is not one of the nine, hearing every word of it, and taking no part in any of it, from the second of December to the thirtieth of June and afterwards."

She turned round. "So when you build whatever it is you are going to build for the last day of this quarter, count me at the table and count me as a woman who cannot be asked and cannot ask. I have been that for five months and I intend to go on being it."

## The Two Minutes He Was Not Going To Ask For

He went up sixty-one iron steps at about ten past ten with a sentence in his head that he had come down that stair to fetch and had not used, and he stood at the top of them in the yard for a while with the sun on one side of the court.

There were seventeen days left in May after the thirteenth and thirty in June after that, and the room where nine people sat could carry a requisition laid on the twentieth of February on any single day of them, and nobody outside the five of them could alter it, and he had just spent the only move he had on deciding not to spend it.

On the second sub-level, about eleven steps below where he was standing, a woman had written down four days before that his premises had a head, and had gone back to her bundles.

He was not going to go and ask her what the head was.

That was the second question he had promised not to ask, and he had promised it out loud in a kitchen on the sixth of December, and it had now been a hundred and fifty-seven days, and the only thing that had ever happened between the two of them was a piece of paper with his address on it going past her desk at four in the afternoon on the ninth of May.

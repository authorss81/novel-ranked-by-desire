# A Man Read Past At His Own Gate

He was standing where he stands every morning at about a quarter to five, under the far end of the ranks wall, on the afternoon of the fourth of April, and the two words were four days old and had been repainted once in green by a different man, which is not what the board had done to them on the afternoon of the thirtieth of March.

That was the whole of what had happened in four days. A wall in this city is repainted every week and the paint is whatever the week has given them, and on the afternoon of the thirtieth a painter had put two words up in the green this square has been using since the fifteenth of November, and eleven people crossing between the gate and the tram had stopped or turned their heads, and about nine hundred going to work had not.

On the fourth of April the same two words were in the same place, in the same green, one shade down, and nobody stopped.

He had come to read his own figure off the wall in a public square four days after two words were painted about him on it, and he got 8,131 and a floor of 8,146 and a tolerance of three, and nothing else, and the square did the rest.

## What The Wall Did Not Do

Beatrix Halm was under the north end of the same wall at about a quarter to five with the notebook open on her palm, and she had been there since about half past four because that is what she does.

"You have come down here twice in five days," she said.

"I have come down here once in five days and you have been here every morning since the third."

"That is not the same and I would like it kept straight." She shut the book. "I have been here every morning for nine years and on four of those mornings I have written a time underneath something and on the other five hundred and something I have not, and this morning is the first morning since the thirtieth of March that there has been nothing to write a time under."

She said that as a woman reporting a change in a column and not as a woman reporting a loss, and he understood that about her by the end of the first morning and had never once been able to argue with it.

"Ninety days," she said. "And then two words, and the two words have had four days and there has been no third thing. A wall in this city says one thing and stops. It has never once said three."

## Half Past Five At The Fourth Branch

He was at the staff door of the Fourth Branch at about half past five the next morning and he had been walking to that door for four years and had been looked at every single morning by a man at a counter inside it.

He had also been stepped around, for four years, by anybody holding a reading card, which is a thing that has happened to him so often that he had stopped being able to explain to people that it happens.

On the morning of the fifth of April the man at the gate read the plate, looked up at the man holding it, and then looked at the wall.

He did it in about a second and a half. He did it the way a man looks at a notice frame or a window with a list in it — flat, quick, and with no part of his attention left over for the person.

Then he said, "Go on through."

He had never said that to Lin Hao in four years. He had said nothing at all for four years.

## The Sixth Empty Line

"He has been doing it since the afternoon of the thirtieth," said Beatrix Halm.

They were on the north end at about twenty past six in the morning because she had come off the tram and he had come out of the branch, and she had the notebook out and she was not writing in it.

"Out of band is a sentence a machine can read. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one against eight thousand one hundred and forty-six, with a tolerance of three, is twelve over the line."

She looked at him.

"There is not one word in this house for a man being under his band and still being a man to be careful about, and I have been trying to find one since the afternoon of the thirtieth."

"It is not because this house has never needed one. It is because it has never happened before."

"Is it a punishment."

"No," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the part that has taken me four days and I am going to say it plainly because you will not enjoy it."

"It is not for you. He has not read you differently."

"He has read you differently because there are two numbers on the wall about you and the first of them means you are behind, and a man who is behind has his plate read again in a fortnight by somebody in an office who has never met him."

She shut the book.

"And that is all of it, and it is not going to get worse, and it is not going to get better, and in about a year somebody in that office is going to be given a district that is short and you are going to be in it."

He had spent years building the only thing he had ever wanted, which was to be looked through, and he had got it, and on the fifth of April a man at a gate he had walked past four thousand times looked at him the way that man looks at the wall, and it did not feel like anything he had wanted.

The thing he had wanted was never to be looked at.

It was to be looked at on one line only, in one respect, at a price that was his own.

## One Of The Eight Will Be Pleased

"One of the eight is going to be pleased for me on the night of the fourteenth," he said. "And seven of them are going to hear a man say out loud that he has been frightened for four months and that there is now a reason for it."

"That is a bad thing to say at that table."

"I know what it is."

"It is a bad thing to say because this house has not once in years made anybody safer by knowing that they were frightened," said Beatrix Halm. "We have all been frightened and the whole of what we have built is that the fear does not get spoken. And you are about to say it out loud, and you are going to get a kind of relief out of it that is worth nothing at all, because the wall does not go back up for being described."

She was quiet for a moment and then she said something else, in a completely different voice, and it was the reason he had come down to the north end instead of going straight to the branch.

"You want to ask me something and you have been working out how to phrase it for about four minutes." She put the notebook against her chest. "Ask it. And if it is about an address I am going to tell you I do not know what you are talking about and then we will both pretend that the last four minutes did not happen."

He had the question ready and it was not the address question.

"Can a man who is out of band lodge anything at all."

Beatrix Halm looked at him for a long moment, and then she gave him the only answer she had, and she gave it the way she gave everything, which is to say as a fact about the machinery and not as a kindness.

"A band is a figure," she said. "And a figure is a thing somebody writes on a card and hangs on a wall, and a card comes down on the last working day of a quarter and it prints a floor and it prints what is against an address, and that is the entire content of being in a band."

"And the ninth."

"There is no card." She said it the way she says everything she has checked twice. "There has never been one. Nine has no card and no floor and no interval, and that is why the top tile on that wall is black, and that is why it has been empty for as long as anybody in this square can remember, and it is not a mystery and it is not a punishment. It is a rank in this city that is not a figure."

She turned the notebook over and shut it.

"You have been out of band since the morning of the thirtieth, and you have spent four days reading that as the end of something," said Beatrix Halm. "It is not the end of something. It is the end of the *band*."

"Ask the woman who has been keeping the real books of a guild for thirty years. She has known what nine is since before you had a plate, and she has been waiting years for somebody to ask her, and you have not asked her once."

# The Days A Sheet Waits

The requisition of the twentieth of February was seventy-nine days old on the morning of the ninth of May and it had been in the keeping of the room at the end of the corridor for every one of those days with nothing whatever entered against it.

That was the thing the clerk of that room had noticed on the sixth of May, and she had noticed it because of the address.

She came out of the room at about four o'clock on the afternoon of the ninth with the book under her arm, which she had never once done in eleven years, and put it on the bench at the end of the corridor and kept her hand on it.

"Sit down."

He sat down on the bench.

"There is a sheet in the keeping book of this room that has been in it since the twentieth of February and there is no entry against it," she said.

"There has never been an entry against it. In eleven years there has never been an unexplained absence in this book, and there is one now, and it has been one for seventy-nine days, and I have known about it every single one of those days and I have not done anything about it because I did not have anything to do."

"You have one now."

"I have one now because of the address." She turned the book round and did not open it. "On the sixth of May I read a witness line into a minute. A minute is a document about a room. And that minute has an address at the head of it, which is this address, in a line of seven names. So this room now holds a document about a house, in its own keeping, in its own hand, with a date."

"And a sheet with nothing against it."

"And a sheet with nothing against it, laid against the same house, in the same quarter, sitting in the same book." She took her hand off it. "I am not going to leave that. I am not permitted to move it and I cannot carry it and I cannot decide it and I cannot receive it and I can do exactly one thing about it, which is write down that this room is holding it."

## One Line In A Book In The Keeping

She opened it at the back, where things go that are not about a person, and there was a ruled column on that page with no heading on it that he had never looked at.

"A room does not have to carry a sheet to say it is holding it. That is the cheapest thing there is. That is the whole of what I am going to do." She had the pen out and it was not the pen of the twentieth of February, which was a different pen, in a different hand, in a division four floors away. "One line. The date it was laid. The date it came in. Where it is. And that it is not carried."

She wrote it. It took her about eleven seconds and she did not read it out and she did not show him the line.

"That is now in the keeping book and it cannot be corrected by me and it cannot be struck by anybody in this city including the man it is about, because a clerk may only enter. It is the first thing anybody has ever done about that sheet. It has been in this room for seventy-nine days and the first thing anybody has ever done about it is a woman writing down that it was there."

"You did it on your own."

"This room has not been asked to do anything by one of the nine who sit in it in nineteen years, and I have not been asked to do anything either." She shut the book.

"You did not ask me. I want that on the record before you leave this corridor, because in about four months somebody is going to work out what happened here, and he is going to work out that a member of this room stood in this corridor for three weeks and said *do not carry it* about nine times, and that is not what happened, and I am not having his version."

"You think of everything."

"I think of the next person." She got the book up under her arm again. "And it goes out at four."

## One Line To Seven Desks

"A weekly return goes to seven desks," she said, "and a line of a different sort goes to the same seven, because a stream does not care what shape a thing is and there is a bag and a string and a woman four floors up who unties it without reading it."

"Seven."

"Field. Counter-forensics on the second sub-level. The Vestry. The guild. The Church of the Quiet Number. Veil-Research, and the wing the stream goes through on its way." She counted them on her fingers the way she had counted them to him five days before. "And on the second of those, a transmission comes up above a part that has been sitting in that division since before any of this began."

"Who initials a transmission."

"The officer of the division." She said it flatly and did not look at him. "That is not a courtesy. A division initials its own transmissions and a person who does not is a person whose division is not carrying its own paper, and an officer who lets that go has a division that is not hers on an ordinary morning and a bad one on a bad morning."

"That is Wren Kallo."

"That is Wren Kallo's desk and Wren Kallo's division and it is not mine to talk about and I am telling you about it because you are going to go and look at it and I would rather you knew at four o'clock than at six."

## The Second Sub-Level, Ten Past Four

He went down about twenty-five steps and along a corridor and through a door that was not locked, and Wren Kallo was at a desk with a transmission in a wire basket and a lamp on it and two other bundles behind it.

She was not surprised to see him and she did not get up.

She read the line. It was a single line and it set out when the sheet was laid, when it came into the keeping, where it was being kept, and that nothing had been done with it — and at the end of it she found the address of premises 34-6-118, because an entry that says where a thing is has to say whose house.

She read it the way she reads everything, which is once, and then she put her pen on the line above hers and initialed the transmission.

That is all it took. About four seconds.

Neither of them said one word about the address.

He had gone up that stair in December with a question about an address, been refused, and promised out loud that he would not ask a second time. He had not asked. He was not going to ask in a corridor on the second sub-level at ten past four in the afternoon on the ninth of May, and Wren Kallo was not going to volunteer anything, and that had been the arrangement for a hundred and fifty-three days and it was going to survive this.

"What is on your part," he said.

"You know what is on my part." She squared the wire basket. "A description of premises 34-6-118, made over the head of it at about eleven one morning in October. The ground is four words a man said in a kitchen. It is not mine and I have never been permitted to ask whose it is."

"You never asked."

"I never asked, and he has never asked me, and one of us is going to keep it that way until one of us stops." She put the basket down the side of the desk so it would go with the run.

"That is your whole arrangement and it has cost you nothing and it is going to cost a woman in this building something before the end of the quarter, and I am not saying that to be cruel. I am saying it because I have initials on a piece of paper underneath yours and I am the one who is going to have to remember that in about four years."

Then she went back to her bundles and did not look up again.

## What He Did Not Say In A Corridor Of Forty Feet

He went back up the steps and up twenty-five more and stood at the end of forty feet of corridor outside a room where five people sat, and a woman inside it had just entered the first line anybody has ever entered about the twentieth of February.

It was going out of the building at four o'clock on the ordinary stream to seven desks, one of which had just been initialed in four seconds by a woman who had never once been asked anything about the address of a house.

The sheet was not carried.

It was not received and it was not decided and it had not been carried on any of twenty-four days it could have been carried, and there were fifty-one days left in the quarter, and the number of seats in that room could not be changed by anybody who was not one of the five.

He stood there and worked out the only instrument he had.

You cannot ask that room for anything. There is no sheet for it in this city and there never will be one, and in nineteen years that room has never once been asked to do a thing by anybody who holds a seat in it.

But a room of five can be made to need something. A minute that carries an address makes an absence in a book into an absence that a clerk cannot sit on.

Seventy-nine days of nothing on a page becomes seventy-nine days of something on a page the moment there is a second document about the same house in the same book, and a clerk fills a space, and a clerk writes down what a clerk can see, and a clerk does not have to be asked because nobody is asking her.

He had wanted a thing from that room and could not have it and had spent twenty days being told so politely by a woman with her hands on a book.

He could get a thing from that room by making it necessary.

And it would cost a woman something she had not agreed to spend, every time, and the woman would not know she was paying until about four years later, and there would be no minute anywhere in this city saying that anybody had asked her.

He put his hands in his coat and left the corridor and went down the iron stair and did not go up it.

That is the only instrument he has, and he has decided tonight that he is not going to use it again, and there is not one person in Ostara he is going to tell that about, and on the ninth of May at about ten past five it is the only thing he has ever decided not to do.

# The Bonded Store Off Cordwain

The store off Cordwain is a bonded warehouse with a loading bay off a yard and a roller door that has not been up since the yard was dug, and at eight in the evening on the tenth of April the door was half down and about a foot of cold came in under it and went straight across the concrete and lay along the back of the room.

She came in through a side door in guild cloth with her hair wet at the ends and a tin of soap in her hand and left the tin on a crate.

"Say the thing you came down here to say," she said, "and then I am going to take this off."

## Two Things Spoken

"I am not tired, and I am not drunk, and nothing has been given to me today by anybody except a foreman with a docket, and there is nothing in my body from any of that which is going to stand up and take me anywhere."

She said it in the flat voice she reads a clause out in.

"I have been up since six and I have had a bath and I am a woman who has eaten. That is the whole of it and I am putting it in the room before anything else happens in it."

"I have been under a binding instrument for eleven years and I know exactly what it is like for a person not to be sure. You do not get to be one of them tonight."

"Right."

"Two things. There is no paper." She put the guild cloth over the back of a chair. "I am not writing anything down, and if there is a sheet in your coat I will read it and then I will not sign it, and I would like that in the air as well as the rest."

"And the second."

"The second is not about you." She had her hands on the belt and stopped.

"If a paper ever comes out of this room about this evening, it will have my name on it and it will not have yours, and you do not get to put yourself on it afterwards out of a sense of what is right."

"You will want to. Everybody who has ever been in a room with me has wanted to."

"That is not a thing you are allowed to do, and I am telling you tonight instead of in about four years, when you would have already done it."

"Understood."

"One more, and it is not a term, it is a use of the room. You are not going to tell me what you can see."

She came at the belt again and got it open.

"Not once. Not in that voice. Not halfway through. There is a woman in this city who was made to describe herself by a man in a room in December, and I have read the four words he used."

"I am telling you now, in a bay with no furniture in it, that this is not that evening, and there is nothing in here to write it on, so I am telling you it."

"I was not going to."

"I know. I am taking it anyway, because I want to be able to look back at this and see myself saying it."

Then she said the rest of it, grinning — no minute counted, nothing boiling, nothing on a hook behind her, no hour called out loud.

She had been in six of these in this city and every one of them had had a clock in it somewhere. She was going to do this one in a bay with a roller door half down and about a foot of cold coming in under it, and if he found that undignified that was his business and not hers.

## The Frame Of The Roller Door

She put her hand flat on the frame of the roller door while she talked and it was still on the iron when she finished.

That was when she told him what she had worked out.

"On the eighth of April you asked me for an attestation and I said no in one syllable and I was right and I got home about nine that night and I lay on my back and put my hand on my own sternum and waited for it," she said. "Nothing. Eleven years and nothing. It has not once come up since the fourth of May two years ago when Marguerite took it off a chained book in front of me in about four seconds."

"You have told me about that one."

"That is not the one I have been carrying." She took her hand off the frame.

"The one I am still carrying is a guild round, and I took it in about four seconds in an office I had been sent to and could not say no in, from a man who did not give his name and whose face I have never seen since."

"My hands closed on it before anything in me had voted. That is the whole of what one of these is, and it is the whole of why I cannot get out of it."

She put the heel of her hand back on the iron.

"I have been on that round ever since and I have never seen the man, had his name, or held a piece of paper with his hand on it."

"And the first thing that promise does to a body is stand you up," she said. "Not frighten you. Stand you up. It takes you out of a chair. It takes you to a door."

"It does not feel like anything at all, which is the part nobody believes. I have been telling people for eleven years that it does not feel like anything, and being told I am all right."

"I am all right. Being all right is not the same as knowing."

"And this weekend you found out something."

"I found out that I can tell the difference," she said. "A promise I agree to feels like a thing I did."

She turned round and put the heel of her hand against the flat cold iron of the frame and left it there.

"A promise I was inside when it was made feels like something that happened to me and then kept happening. I know that now because I have had both, this week, one of each, back to back."

She looked at him over her shoulder.

"I would not have been able to tell anybody the difference on the eighth. I can tell you the difference standing in a freezing bay at eight in the evening."

"And I have been under one for eleven years without knowing who made it, and I am extremely interested in the difference. That is the whole of what I came down here for and it has nothing to do with you."

"I thought about it from the eighth and I did not tell you and I was not going to."

"Then say the price."

"You are asking nine women to be evidence. That is what we are." She took her hand off the iron.

"Evidence is what people were before they were people. That is the actual cost of it and it is the only cost that is not money, and I want it said out loud in a room by a person who has been told no by the first one of us."

"I am not refusing the price. I have decided to pay it. I decided between the eighth and this morning in about nine seconds and I have not been frightened and I have not been brave."

She looked at him without moving anything else.

"And if you go back up that hill and tell anybody I was talked into it, I will not forgive you, and it will not be an act of temper."

He did not say anything at all.

"Take your coat off," she said. "You have been standing in a bay for about four minutes looking at a woman in guild cloth and it is going to be cold and we are going to be here a while."

The roller door came down another three inches on its own weight and the cold came in under it and lay along the floor between them, and neither of them shut it.

She got the guild cloth off over her shoulders and left it where it fell, and then she crouched and took her boots off, which she does before she has decided anything, and set them side by side against the crate, and she had not once looked at him while she did it.

"Right," she said. "Talk to me for about an hour and keep going when you think you have run out."

"Eleven years is a long time to go without anybody in a room, and I have had eleven years of thinking about it at four in the morning in a bay, so you will get all of it and you will not be able to pretend I am being brave."

He was not gentle, because she had told him not to be, and he was not careful either, because she had told him that as well, and she had said both of those things out loud before anything had happened, which meant that she had chosen every one of them.

She had washed and she was warm from it and she got her hands into his hair and made him come down to her height and took his mouth the way she had taken a gate off a hinge at about four o'clock that afternoon, all in one movement, and then she stopped and made him take it back, because she wanted to feel the difference between the two of them.

Her back was against the cold iron of the roller frame and she did not mind it. Her hands went down his shirt and she opened it the way she opened a docket — down the front, then out at the shoulders, then the sleeves — and she got her hand flat between his shoulder blades and pushed him down over a crate that was the right height and was not going to move.

"I am going to ask you a question that four hundred men have been asked in four hundred rooms and not one of them has ever answered me the way I want."

She put her chin up and waited for him to find her face in the dark.

"Tell me what you see. And be careful, because I have never once wanted to hear the decent version."

He told her in the dark, and not decently, and she made a sound in her throat and got her fingers into her own hair and held them there and said, "Again, and slower, and do not be kind about it."

He told her again.

"That is the one." She did not raise her voice. "Four hundred men and not one of them had the nerve, and now it is the first thing I have been told in this bay."

She got a hand into his hair.

"Come here and make me forget you said it."

She was on top of him inside about four minutes and taking her own weight and doing her own work and setting her own angle, and she told him once, in a voice that was entirely ordinary, not to be careful, because she had had careful men in this city for four years and she wanted to be able to stand up on the twentieth.

He watched her find it in about nine seconds. Her hips went back and up and then came down on him at a different angle and she made a noise through her teeth and her hair fell forward over her face and she did not fix it.

"There," she said. "That. Do not change anything about that."

He put his hands where she put them, which was on her hips, with his thumbs in the hollow underneath, and held on. She rode him harder after that and the crate went about half an inch on the concrete and she laughed into his collarbone and told him to do it again and not to apologise for the crate.

Her skin had been warm from the bath and the air off the yard was cold enough that going out of breath raised a ridge on her, and she took no notice of it at all.

Her back arched until her shoulders left the crate, and her hands came down flat on his chest and pushed once, hard, not to stop him but to feel him all the way in. Then she got her mouth down on his and made him taste the inside of her mouth while she worked.

Then she took his hands and put them flat on the crate either side of her and held them there for about a minute while she worked him with her hips and her thighs alone, and neither of them spoke in it. The yard made the noise a yard makes. The cold came in under the door and lay along the floor and did not reach either of them.

She came the first time with her fist closed around him and a sound in her that carried across the bay and back off the wall, and her thighs locked and she stopped moving entirely for about two seconds and then started again, slower, and she did not stop for it, and she was not going to stop for it either, and the bay took the sound and gave none of it back.

Then she got her mouth on him and her hand round him at the same time, her fingers wet, and worked him slowly all the way down and held there and came again with her hips moving on his and his mouth against her jaw, which she had not asked for and did not stop. She did not stop until he was finished, and then she swallowed and wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist and laughed at something.

"Sorry. It has been eleven years and a bay is a terrible place to be certain about anything."

"Move over."

"Yes," said Petra Ilyin.

She was on her back in about four seconds with her boots off and her clothes in a heap by the crate and the cold coming in over her ankles and the roller door another four inches down. She put her arms out along the concrete on either side of her, which is what a person does when the floor is cold and nothing else is.

He got into her and she made him go all the way in in one movement and then held him there and did not move at all for about four seconds. Her whole body did the thing it does when a promise is being made to it, and stopped doing it.

She looked up at him in the dark.

"There. That. Do not change anything about it."

He did not change anything about it. He put a hand under the back of her head to keep it off the concrete and she let him, which she did not have to.

She had her heels hooked behind his thighs and he could not have got out of it without her permission. She gave it four times and took it back three, and on the fourth she stopped giving it back and made a noise she had not made before.

She pulled him down onto her so that his weight was all of it, and her hands were under the back of his neck, and her knees came up and opened and she was saying something into his ear that was not words. She was very wet and very warm and she took all of him and then some and did not care in the slightest.

Afterwards she lay with an arm over her eyes and her chest going and did not count, because there was nothing in the bay with a minute on it.

## Afterwards

She was the one who broke the silence and she did it from about two inches away with her face turned into his shoulder.

"Lin Hao."

"Mm."

"I am going to ask you one question and I am not going to ask it, and I want you to hear that I decided not to, because I have decided not to about one thing in about four years and it is usually the wrong one."

"Go on."

"The question is whether you have ever had a thing done to you that you agreed to and could not get out of." She said it into his shoulder.

"Not a thing somebody did to you. A thing you agreed to, in a room, in front of people, and said yes to out loud, and then could not get out of afterwards."

She lifted her head about an inch off his shoulder.

"Because a sentence you agree to in public is a sentence you have said in front of witnesses, and witnesses are the only durable thing in this city."

The yard made its noise. The roller door came down another inch on its own weight.

He had.

Once. In a hall on Cobb Street, on a morning in an autumn he has never dated out loud, with two hundred and eleven people in a room rated at two hundred and thirty, and a certified instrument sitting on a table at the front of it.

He had agreed there to answer for a machine, and had felt one thing come out of him in public with his hand on his own side, and had then said out loud, in the sentence immediately afterwards, while his hand was still there, that it would not happen again.

It was in a transcript. It was the only piece of cover he had ever owned and he had spent five volumes being careful with it and he could not say one word about it, because a man who can carry a document in his head cannot be made to produce it and he has been extremely good at that for eight years.

He said nothing.

Petra Ilyin lay still beside him for about four seconds and then she took the arm off her eyes and looked at the side of his face in the dark.

"Right," she said, and got up, and found her clothes.

That was all. She did not ask him a second time and she did not say one word about the four seconds, and she did not ask him on the way out either, standing at the side door with her boots in her hand and the cold coming up past her ankles off the yard.

"You have got about two days to decide whether you are going to tell the seven on your table," she said. "I am going to be at that table on the eighteenth and I am going to sign a piece of paper if there is a piece of paper, and if you have not decided by then I will decide for you, and I will not do it nicely."

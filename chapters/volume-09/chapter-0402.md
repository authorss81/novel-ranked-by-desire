# Nine Signatures On One Roll

The hall at the Ninth Terrace holds two hundred and about forty people standing and there were about ninety in it at ten in the morning of the second of April, and a standing household is recognised in the open, at a rail, in front of whoever is in the room, because a roll is the cheapest instrument in this city and the whole of its price is that there is nothing private about it.

He had known that going up the walled road. He had known it since a woman at the intake desk below the walled road said out loud, in a hall with a hundred people in it, that a roll is read at every counter a member ever stands in front of. He had spent the winter being extremely careful with it, and there was no other way to do what he had come up here to do.

The desk was against the far wall with a book on it, and the book had been on that desk for twenty years.

## The Hall

Dagmar Oyelowo read the plate back to him at about four minutes to ten and made him press it into the stone herself, which she has done for every person who has ever stood in front of that counter, and the machine gave back 8,131, which is what a plate says in this city in April.

"Sit down on the step," she said. "No. You can stand there. You have been standing on steps for about four volumes and you are going to stand on this one too, and I am not going to have opinions about it in my own hall."

Then she took a roll out of a drawer under the counter. It was one sheet, printed, folded twice, and it had a name column and three others.

"Name," she said. "Plate. Under it, the bond. Under that, what you can be served at, and there is only one answer to that and it is a counter and it is the counter this is."

## The Nine

Nine of the twelve women in this household swore to one plate in this hall on the morning of the second of April, and the nine are not a count off a kitchen table.

Ines Duarte signed at Lantern Row. She came up the walled road alone at about half past seven in the morning and stood at this counter and wrote her name and her plate and did not argue about it, and she said eleven words and one of them was *fine*, and Dagmar Oyelowo wrote the time against it because a signature from a room she has never been inside is entered with the hour on it.

She does not live at Ferrier Street. She has her own plate and her own rooms and she has never once come back under that roof, and she is on this roll, and the roll is the law and quarters are not.

Tessa Okonkwo signed on her own paper from her own rooms over the cold store and brought it down herself, and Marguerite Vale signed from the Vant's smaller rooms on a sheet the Vant's real books had nothing to do with.

Six of the nine sleep at 34-6-118: Beatrix Halm, Nadia Ferrous, Runa Sato, Agnete Lindqvist, Petra Ilyin and Isolde Marchetti. A sworn member may hold her own plate and her own rooms and her name stays on the roll, and that is the whole mechanic, and it is the reason a reader who counted the women at a kitchen table would have got seven and would have been wrong.

They signed over about half an hour in an order nobody planned, and it was Petra Ilyin's hand that was the fastest on the sheet and it was Beatrix Halm's that took longest, because Beatrix Halm looked up from the book and read the whole of it twice before she signed anything.

## The Three Who Are Not Sworn

Wren Kallo came to the rail and did not put her hand on the desk.

"I am not doing this," she said, before he had said anything, and Dagmar Oyelowo wrote *declined* in a column and did not look up, because a roll takes a refusal the ordinary way and has done for forty years.

"You know why."

"I know why," said Wren Kallo. "I am a counter-forensics officer on the second sub-level and the only reason I am any use to you is that I am a woman in a division who can read a hand."

She put her hand flat on the desk and took it off again without touching the sheet.

"Swear me to a rank-nine household and I am a woman in a division who belongs to somebody. There are nine women in my own office and every one of them has somebody, and I have watched what that does to them, and it is not that it is bad."

"It is that they cannot be wrong in front of a stranger." She looked at the roll. "I would rather be useful than protected and I have been choosing that since December and I am not reviewing it at ten in the morning."

Sun Meiling did not come up the road at all. She signed nothing and sent nothing and there is nothing in this city's records that shows her refusing, which she arranged on purpose.

"I am inside Veil-Research," she said, on the second floor of a building off a third court, to a man who had come up sixty-one steps to ask her, "and I am going to be inside it for another four months at least."

She had a docket under her arm and she did not put it down.

"And the other reason is worse than the first one, and it arrived in about January, and nobody warned me. The women in this house who are sworn are the women a requisition can be served on."

"That is what the roll is. That is the whole of what it is for, and I do not want to be harder to reach than the average person in Ostara. I have spent three volumes being unreachable and it is not a gift I have got. It is a job I have done."

Dagmar Oyelowo was at her own desk and heard both of them from about nine feet away and did not turn round.

"I am not swearing either," she said, "and I am the only one of the three of you standing at a counter, so you can see my reasons without asking me for them."

She squared the book.

"Forty-one lines in this book in twenty years and every one of them went up. A roll is a list a counter reads. You take an oath and your name goes on it, and from that day a clerk in this hall reads it out to anybody who comes to this rail."

"Nine hundred people a week in this hall for twenty years and it has never once happened to me." She shut the drawer. "I am not handing it over for a man who is out of band by twelve, and that is not modesty, and I have been told it is modesty twice this year by people who have not got a word."

## The Figure In The Column

At about twenty past ten she turned the roll round and put a finger on the third column.

"Now you," she said. "This is the only part of this sheet I cannot do and I have got about four minutes' notice of it and there is no one in this building I can send and get."

"The third column."

"The third column takes the highest rank in the household on the morning the roll is taken. Not a figure. A rank."

She put her finger on the line and left it there.

"A household does not have a rank of its own. There is no such thing as the standing of a house. It is bound by the best thing in it, and the best thing in it is whoever is highest, and today that is you."

"And you are standing in front of me out of band by twelve grains, with a floor on a card that came down two days ago."

"Write what my plate says."

"If I write what your plate says, I enter a roll against a figure that is under a band. There has never been one in this hall, and I do not know what it does to the sheet afterwards, and neither do you."

Dagmar Oyelowo took the pen up.

"I am going to write the figure, and I am going to read it back to you before I put my own hand on the bottom of it. That is the only part of this I have ever got the right to."

He had not expected her to do that.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "Fifteen under a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-six. Out of band by twelve, with a tolerance of three. You are the first roll in this hall that has ever been entered against a figure below a band and I have been doing this for twenty years and I have never had to think about what it means."

"Then write it."

"I am going to write it." She did. It took about four seconds and she squared the sheet afterwards. "And nobody can correct it. That is a roll and it is a book underneath and I am the hand and I do not get a pencil back in it either."

He had put nine women's names on the cheapest instrument in Ostara and he had watched the cheapest instrument in Ostara refuse to be sensible about it, and both of those things were true and he had walked in knowing the first and had not known the second.

## Thirteen Days

He walked back down the walled road at about half past eleven and worked it out on the way and got it right the first time, which he noticed, because he had not got anything right the first time in about four months.

The roll is taken on the day it is taken. The roll was taken at ten in the morning on the second of April. The third column is the highest rank in the household, and the highest rank in the household on the morning of the second of April was a man who was fifteen under his band, and a figure of 8,131 was entered against the household's name at about twenty past ten.

On the morning of the fifteenth of April that column would take the highest rank in that household again, and by then the best thing in it would not be a figure of 8,131 at all.

Fifteen less the second is thirteen.

Nobody in this city could put it back down, and the woman who could have told him what the third column does was sixty-one steps up an iron stair off a third court and had been putting boxes in for eleven years and did not know that her own name was in the fourth block of the sheet that made the column mean anything.

And he was the only person in Ostara who knew it was coming.

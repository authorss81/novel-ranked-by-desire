# The Count She Keeps Under

The Vestry chapel keeps eleven thousand names in its consult book and Tessa Okonkwo keeps her own count in a drawer in her rooms over the cold store, and on the morning of the tenth of June she had the drawer open and he saw it because she did not shut it when he came in.

"It is one hundred and ten days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, with thirty and thirty and thirty and ten makes one hundred and ten."

"Twenty days to the thirtieth," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Thirty taken from ten leaves twenty. I have ruled dates since the nineteenth of October and I do not miss."

## What She Counts

The drawer held slips of paper with dates ruled on them, eleven now and a twelfth ruled and not filled, and under them a second stack with no dates at all, only short lines in her own hand.

"I count the room I have left in myself," she said, before he asked. "That is my gift and my price. I take the damage and I carry a working inventory of every injury I have held, and I know how much room I have left. Nobody taught me. I worked it out because a woman who holds people has to know when she is full."

"How full."

"That is the count I keep under." She put her hand on the second stack without lifting it. "I do not say it. Not to the doctor who sends me returns. Not to the man in the room with no window. Not to you. If I say it somebody will try to spare me, and sparing me is deciding for me, and I have been spared enough in eleven years."

He sat down opposite her because the rooms were small and there was nowhere to stand that was not in her way.

"On the thirtieth you read two returns in one morning."

"On the thirtieth I read two returns about one matter in one quarter, and I hold what is in them, and that is the whole of my evening." She shut the drawer halfway. "You know the cost and you do not get to hear the figure. I told you on the twenty-fifth of May and it still stands."

## Being Asked About Keeping

"I am not asking for the figure," he said. "I am asking what it costs to go on keeping it."

Tessa Okonkwo looked at him for a long time.

"It costs that I chose you on purpose," she said. "I told you to write it down somewhere. Not in mine. A woman who writes a line on the outside of a file has told every division that ever sees it that the thing inside it is not one thing. That line is going on the front of the file before either return is read. Two returns, one quarter, one matter, and the composition of the address."

"And after."

"After, I go on counting." Her voice roughened. "Do you think I have not wanted to be permitted to treat somebody without writing the file for eleven years and not once said it to anybody who could do anything about it until you. Do you think that want goes away because I said it out loud. It does not. It sits in the drawer with the rest."

She pulled the drawer fully shut.

"The keeping is the thing being asked about now," she said. "You are asking how I go on. The answer is that I go on the way I have gone on since before you came here. I rule a date. I read what arrives. I write what I am permitted to write. I do not ask the doctor a single question."

## Anger

He stood up too fast and knocked the chair back a step.

"That is not enough," he said, and the anger surprised them both. "Two returns about one matter and nowhere on either to write it, and you writing it on the front in your own hand, and nobody thanking you, and me standing here unable to do anything about any of it. It is not enough that you go on counting."

"No," said Tessa Okonkwo. "It is not enough. It was never enough. It is what there is."

She righted the chair with her foot.

"Sit down," she said, more gently. "You wanted one felt thing in this room and you have got it. Anger. Keep it. It is better than the careful way you have been standing in my doorway since March."

He sat.

"The count stays under," she said. "I am not breaking. Do not write that I break. On the thirtieth of June I will stand in this clinic when the returns arrive, because I am the person who receives them, and I will read both, and I will hold them, and in four years somebody will open that folder and find the answer on the front in my hand, and it will be a number and a door."

She opened the drawer again and took out the slip with twelve dates and put it on the table between them.

"The twelfth is the thirtieth of June," she said. "I ruled it on the twenty-fifth of May. I have been looking at it for sixteen days."

He looked at it and did not touch it.

## What Eleven Years Costs

She stood and went to the window over the cold store, where the yard below was full of carts and washing lines, and spoke with her back to him.

"Eleven years," said Tessa Okonkwo. "I came to this building wanting to treat people. What I do instead is receive returns four times a year about men in rooms with no windows, and rule dates, and count the room I have left in myself so I do not spill over. The count is the only thing that has kept me standing. A woman who does not count decides nothing. She only endures."

He did not speak.

"On the twenty-fifth of May I told you I wanted to treat somebody without writing the file," she said. "I chose you on purpose because you cannot do anything about it. If I had said it to someone who could, it would have become a request, and a request becomes a paper, and a paper becomes a reason box, and I have seen what reason boxes do to women in this city."

She turned round.

"Runa Sato is going to write one on the twenty-eighth of May with your name in it. In her own hand. Unpriced. She told you on the twenty-first and she will do it. That is her count going under too. Every woman in this city who carries anything is counting, and none of us says the figure, and you do not get to hear any of them."

"Twenty days," he said.

"Twenty days," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Go and be angry somewhere else. I have a clinic and I am going to keep it."

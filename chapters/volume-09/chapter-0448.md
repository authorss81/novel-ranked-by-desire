# The Fifth Mark That Never Came

The counting room is two floors under a sales floor and it is cold in June in a way that has nothing to do with the weather, and on the morning of the twenty-seventh of June the real book was on its board with the chain over it and Hanneke Ruis standing at the rail with her sleeves pushed up past the elbow.

"It is one hundred and twenty-seven days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, with thirty and thirty and thirty and twenty-seven makes one hundred and twenty-seven."

"Three days," said Hanneke Ruis. "Thirty taken from twenty-seven leaves three. Come down here, you are wasting my draught."

## Four Marks And A Rule

Under the glass of the plate there were four marks in one hand on a printed rule, and the dates beside them were the first of December, the first of January, the first of February and the first of March.

"Four," said Hanneke Ruis. "One hand, one rule, four dates, and about thirty years of an account at that address being called by a person with a pen on a chain round their wrist, at ten to nine on the first of the month, for as long as it waits at this rail, unpaid and unproduced, in a building with one way out of it."

"There is no fifth mark."

"There is no fifth mark and there never was going to be a fifth mark, and you have known that since April and I have said it to you twice." She tapped the glass once. "A mark is a call. A carried line is not a call."

"In April somebody drafted a quarter's sheet and everything since has been administered off that, and that is a different animal and the only people in this city who have ever confused the two are people who have never had to write either one."

"Then what goes in that book on the thirtieth."

"A line carried. Same hand. Same rule." She said it the way a woman says a thing she has said to herself for three days. "And that is the last thing I am going to explain in a cold room to a man who is not a clerk."

## The Woman Who Came Down To The Rail

Marguerite Vale came down at about half past nine with her coat open and a sheet folded in her hand that she did not unfold and did not put down.

"I want the fourth line of the quarterly corrected," she said. "I am not asking for it to be taken out. I am asking for it to be corrected, because it is wrong."

"What does wrong mean here."

"I wrote my own name in it at about nine in the morning on the twenty-third of March in my own hand, in a broker's hand with the broker's pen, because a clerk said that a person present who has no standing given is entered and I was present and I had no standing given." She turned the sheet over once and put it back the way it was. "It fills nothing. It has never filled anything."

"It is the fourth line of a quarterly return and it carries a woman who has held no seal in this room since the sixth of December and was not read aloud to anybody until the first of April, when nine people heard my name because the Council restored a man over it."

"It is in the book."

"It is in the book and it is in a bundle and it went out on the first of April with everything else, and the quarterly return goes out again on the thirtieth of June, which is the last working day of this quarter, and I have had three days to work out that a woman cannot get four words out of a book that a clerk cannot take out."

## Why Not

"A clerk cannot correct a book," said Hanneke Ruis. "A clerk may enter. That is the whole of what a clerk is."

"If it is wrong it stays wrong and the only thing anybody can do about a wrong line is enter another line near it, and the other line is a different line and the wrong one is still there underneath it, and in about four years both of them come off this plate and go into a case in a hall and somebody stands in front of it and works out which one to believe."

"So I enter a correction."

"You cannot enter a correction in that room, because that line is not read at the sitting and a member's own standing may not be altered in a room where it is not read." Hanneke Ruis took her sleeves down. "And you are not a member of that room at all."

"You are a name on a household roll at a different address in a different city and the quarterly return is not yours, and if it were yours I would still not be able to do it, because it went out in April and nothing that has gone out on a run comes back."

They stood in it for a moment. Somewhere above them, two floors up, a sales floor was doing whatever a sales floor does in the last week of June, and the sound came down the stairwell and stopped at the door, as it had stopped at the door for as long as Hanneke Ruis had been standing at that rail.

## What She Was Given Instead

"Sit down," said Hanneke Ruis.

"I do not need—"

"Sit down, there is a chair and it is the only warm thing in this room and I am going to read you your own line, and I am going to read it the way it should have been read to you on the first of April, which is in order, with the date, and all of it."

Marguerite Vale sat down on the chair.

"The twenty-third of March," said Hanneke Ruis. "About nine in the morning. At this rail. A person present, giving no standing, is entered. Fourth line. And the name entered is Marguerite Vale, in the hand of the person entering and not in the hand of the person entered, and there is nothing after it and there is no seal under it and it is not a seat."

"That is what I was afraid of."

"That is what it is," said Hanneke Ruis. "It is not a fifth mark. It is not a call. It is a line entered in a book because a woman was in a room and there was nowhere else to put her, and on the first of April a Council read it out over the top of a restoration and that was the Council's doing and not the clerk's, and no clerk in this city could have stopped it or would have tried."

## The First Time He Has Read One

"Sit up," said Hanneke Ruis. "You have been in this room twice in nine months and both times you have been looking at the glass. Come round the rail and read the line underneath."

He came round the rail. Under the four marks, on the same printed rule, in the same hand, there was a fifth line and it had no mark on it at all; it had a date and a figure and the word *carried* and nothing else.

"The first of April," said Hanneke Ruis. "Ten to nine in the morning. That is the line. That is what went in instead of a mark, and I wrote it, and I said so at the time and nobody wrote it down because nobody was going to write anything down."

"It names the address."

"It names the address and it carries the figure and it says the account is carried on the quarter's sheet from that date and administered from it." She put her thumb under it. "And I want you to look at what that line is, because you have been in this room looking at a wall of marks for ten minutes and you have not seen the only interesting thing on the plate."

"It is the only line on that plate that anybody wrote about a house."

"It is the only line on that plate that anybody wrote about a house," said Hanneke Ruis. "Thirty years of marks, every one of them a person being called. And then one line, in April, in my hand, about seven people keeping an address."

"And on the thirtieth there is going to be a second one, and it is going to be about the same house, and it is going to be in the same hand, and there is going to be nothing whatever behind it, and that is how a plate turns from a record of people into a record of houses. It does not happen on a day anybody votes for. It happens one line at a time and then it is a different book."

## The Fourth Block

She turned round to the book.

"The other thing is on the thirtieth and I would rather have it now than on the thirtieth, because on the thirtieth I write it." She put her hand flat on the rail. "The fourth block of the quarter's sheet takes a figure off a certificate of the composition of that address. The certificate for the quarter that begins on the first of July does not exist."

"A form cannot be drafted without one and nobody has drafted one and there is nobody who can draft one between now and the time the sheet goes on, and on the thirtieth of June a keeper in this building writes that premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight was kept by seven people, in her own hand, out of her own head, with no certificate behind it."

"Seven."

"Seven," said Hanneke Ruis. "And eight people sleep under that roof, and the composition has said seven since nine forty-one on the morning of the thirteenth of March, and a service made against that address in February says eight, and there is no instrument in this city that will go into that paper and change a figure to eight."

"Who signs it."

"Nobody signs it. A mark is made with a pen on a chain round a wrist and there is no wrist on it and there is no pen, and what I am going to do on the thirtieth is take a sheet and fill the fourth block in myself and get it witnessed by Solveig because a block filled out of somebody's head has to have somebody's name beside it or it is nothing at all." She turned round.

"And I am telling you three days early because you are going to hear about it afterwards and I would rather you heard it from me."

## The Only Printed Figure In The City

"Then where does the figure come from," he said. "If not from her and not from a certificate."

"It does not come from anywhere," said Hanneke Ruis. "Which is not the same answer and I want to be careful about it."

"Because there is one place in this city where that address has a figure in print on the thirtieth of June, and it is on a wall, at the head of the Reading, on a card in a different hand on different paper, put up at six in the morning by a boy with a paste brush who is not told whose premises he is reading and who does not ask."

She looked up the stairwell at the ceiling as though she could see through two floors and a sales floor to a wall she had probably not looked at in a year.

"It prints a floor and it prints the standing figure of every named premises, at the composition of that premises, at the hour it is read," said Hanneke Ruis.

"So on the thirtieth at six in the morning there will be a figure for that address on a wall in public, in the only handwriting in this city that anybody reads every morning without being paid, and I will be filling in a block at this rail at about ten to nine out of my own memory, and the two of those will not be on the same paper and neither of them is coming here."

She squared the bundle of clean sheets.

"You can see the wall and you cannot get it to me. That is the whole of it."

"Three days out and I am telling a man who can stand in a room of five what it is that I am about to do without a piece of paper to do it with, because if I do not say it out loud to somebody before the thirtieth then in about four years the only thing anybody will have is a hand on a plate in a case in a hall, with nothing behind it and nobody who was ever told."

Marguerite Vale stood up off the chair and put the folded sheet inside her coat.

"Thank you for reading it in order," she said.

"It should have been read in order on the first of April," said Hanneke Ruis, "and there was nobody in that room who had any business reading anything in any order, and I have been working in a building with nine offices above my head for twenty-nine years and I know exactly which of them would have stopped it and exactly why none of them did."

She went up the stair, and the door at the bottom of it shut with the ordinary sound of an ordinary door, and Solveig Rask came in out of the cold with the cloth over her arm and did not say anything and went straight to the board and put her hand flat on the book in the ordinary way of a woman who has stood at that board for eleven years and has never once been asked to do anything else.

The room was very cold and the chain lay across the book in a long curve and the four marks sat under the glass in one hand on a printed rule, and there was a fifth thing coming on the thirtieth that was not a mark and would not be a call and had nobody's name on it except the woman who had to write it.

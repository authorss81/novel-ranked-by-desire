# Four Minutes And A Box

The division keeps its forms on the second floor of the building off a third court, and on the morning of the twenty-ninth of May Sun Meiling had a printed sheet on her desk with a single box on it and a pen beside it that she had taken out of the drawer before she sat down.

He stood in the doorway and did not come in.

"You are early," she said, without looking up. "I do not start this until nine and it is not nine."

"I know how long it takes."

She looked up then.

"Four minutes," said Sun Meiling. "You have been told that by somebody who watched me do it once and you have carried it for two months. It took four minutes on the thirtieth of March and it will take four minutes now."

"Then I will stand here for four minutes."

She uncapped the pen.

## The Box On The Sheet

The sheet had a heading at the top and three lines of small print under it and one box in the middle of it, and the box had a line under it for a name and a date.

"The last time this came down it came down on the morning of the thirtieth of March," she said. "It came down on its own in the ordinary way with nothing else. No covering slip. No instruction. Nobody brought it and no request ever came back with it."

"Did you ask where it came from."

"I did not ask," said Sun Meiling. "A routine happens to a woman who has never once been asked whether she minded. That is what a routine is. If I ask where it comes from I make it into something else."

She put the pen to the box.

"The composition of the address," she said, writing as she spoke. "Seven. Being the number of persons keeping it. Dated the twenty-ninth of May."

She wrote the date under it and signed under that, in her own hand, under her own name, at the head of nothing but herself.

"It is done," she said. "That was the first minute. The other three are for looking at it."

## What She Knows This Time

He came in then and shut the door behind him with his shoulder, because both his hands were empty and he wanted them to stay empty.

"On the nineteenth of April you worked out what you have been the officer of since the thirtieth of November," he said.

"I worked it out from a gap in a line in my own room," said Sun Meiling. "I went down sixty-one steps to read a minute and not a file. I turned a sheet over. I have said all of that to you and I am not saying it again."

"You know you certify the composition of a house you sleep in."

"I know I certify it four times a year in my own division in my own hand under my own name. I know the account has been carried on a quarter sheet with no fifth mark. I know there has been no payment since the middle of November."

She put the cap back on the pen.

"What I do not know," she said, "is what you have not told me. There is something you have not told me since the nineteenth of April and you have carried it for forty days. I can see the shape of it in the way you stand in my doorway."

"It is ninety-eight days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Eight taken from twenty leaves eight, and eight with thirty-one and thirty and twenty-nine makes ninety-eight. The requisition has been administered weekly against the composition since the first of April."

"I know that too."

"Then the rest is the quarter that has not started."

## The Quarter That Has Not Started

Sun Meiling folded her hands on the sheet to keep it flat, the way a woman does who has had a docket folder against her spine for eleven years and knows what paper does when it is left alone.

"Say it."

"There is no certificate for the quarter that begins on the first of July," he said. "A carry-forward cannot be drafted without one. The fourth block of the sheet that goes on the first of July is a name printed off that certificate, and the name has been yours since the middle of March."

She did not move.

"On the thirtieth of June a keeper two floors under the Exchange is going to fill that block out of her own memory, in her own hand, with your name on it, and there will be no certificate behind it. She told me on the twenty-seventh, three days early, on her own and without instruction."

Sun Meiling looked down at the sheet with the wet signature on it.

"This one is good for the quarter it is certified in," she said. "That is the rule and I have said it myself. This one carries the house to the thirtieth of June."

"Yes."

"And after the thirtieth there is nothing."

"After the thirtieth there is her hand and your name."

She was quiet for a while. The building made a sound above them, a cart or a door, and went still.

"I have been certifying a house I sleep in for seven months without being asked," she said. "I knew that on the nineteenth of April. I did not know that the paper I fill in four minutes would be the paper somebody else writes from memory when there is none."

"You know it now."

"I know this one." She touched the sheet once with two fingers. "This one I did knowing it. That is the difference between March and May and you may write that down somewhere."

## Four Minutes

He looked at the clock on her wall. Three minutes had gone since she signed.

"You have one minute left," he said, and it came out rougher than he meant.

"I am going to use it," said Sun Meiling. "I am going to sit here for one minute with a certified sheet on my desk and look at it, because on the thirtieth of March I filled it in and carried on with the morning, and I am not carrying on with this morning."

He stayed in the doorway and did not speak.

After about a minute she put the sheet in the tray for the outgoing run and squared it against the edge, the way the clerk of another room squares a bundle.

"There are two days left in this month," she said. "Thirty taken from twenty-nine leaves one, and one with thirty in the next month leaves thirty-one days in the quarter. I have kept this desk for three years and I have never yet been out by a day."

She stood up.

"Go home," she said. "I have certified. You have told me one thing and kept the other, and I know which one you kept, and I am not asking for it on the twenty-ninth of May."

He went.

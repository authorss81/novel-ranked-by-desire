# A Broker's Name In The Fourth Line

The envelope came to the Fourth Branch at about a quarter to eight in the morning with a district number on it and nothing else on it, and it said the Council of the Nine would sit at ten in the morning at the room at the end of a corridor off the second landing, and it said he was served with it.

Not admitted with it.

He read it twice on the stair to the fifth floor. Served and not admitted is not a clerical accident. A man who is the subject of a live sheet is served with everything that room does, because anything that room does may turn out to be about him, and a man who is out of band by twelve grains is not admitted to anything at all, because there is no reading of a hall that puts a floor under a man who is already under one.

The first sitting since the twenty-ninth of December. Ninety-two days. He had counted them without wanting to and had found on the morning of the first of April that the count and the thing itself had stopped agreeing, which is how a man knows he has been counting.

## The End Of The Corridor

The room was the same length it had been in February, which is to say about eleven feet by nine, with a table under a window and nine chairs and a rail, and the corridor outside it was about forty feet long and had a bench at the end of it, and he stood at the bench because that is where a man stands when he is served and not admitted.

The door was open. He could hear the chair move.

At about four minutes to ten a woman came out into the corridor with a bundle of paper under her arm and looked at him the way she had looked at him on the sixth of February, which is to say without any doubt at all, and said, "You are served."

"I am served."

"You are not admitted."

"I know what I am," said Lin Hao. "I have read it about four times on a stair this morning."

The clerk turned half round in the doorway.

"Then you know the room reads it at the eleventh minute and that is all it does, and that I am not permitted to tell you what is in it, and that you are not permitted to ask."

She put the bundle down on the rail.

"I have said that sentence four hundred times and I am not going to enjoy saying it a four hundred and first. You can stand there until eleven, and there is no bench for you at the end of this corridor that anybody has ever offered."

"Then I will stand here."

## Eleven Minutes To Ten

At two minutes to ten a door at the other end of the corridor opened and Alma Quayle came out of the room and went past him without looking at him, and it is not a thing a head of classification does by accident, and he understood that she had seen him and had decided that a subject in a corridor and a recused member going in to a room are two separate items of business.

Everything she said in that room for the next hour and a half she said as a witness and not as a seal. He could hear it through the open door, the difference and the flatness of it, the way a sentence sounds when the woman saying it has written out beforehand that she is not the room.

At eleven minutes to ten a thing happened in that room that had not happened since the twelfth of February.

A standing was restored.

He knew it from the sound first. There is a particular noise a room makes when nine chairs and one chair are in it and the second one speaks, and it came up the corridor and stopped as if it had been shut off, and then the clerk read something out in the flat voice she reads a requisition out in, and it was short.

Bertram Sallow. The fourth seat. Restored by the Council, and entered in the book of the room at eleven minutes to ten on the morning of the first of April, in a hand that was not the clerk's and was not his.

The room stood at four.

"A requisition carried needs five," the clerk said, to the corridor and not to him. "That is the whole of what is different in this city today and I am not permitted to say anything else about it."

## The Price

He understood what had happened before he had finished standing there, and it took him about four seconds, and the four seconds were the most expensive four seconds of his adult life and they cost somebody else.

A restored standing is entered over the line it stands on. It has to be. There is nowhere else on that page for it to go, and the page is nine ruled lines, one for each seat, and the second seat says something about restoring somebody into the fourth.

The fourth line of the quarterly return in that room's keeping had carried *Marguerite Vale* in a guild broker's own hand since the morning of the twenty-ninth of March, with four words under it in a clerk's hand saying why it was not a seat. It filled nothing. It could not be struck by anybody, including the woman who wrote it. It is on no roll anywhere in this city.

And a clerk cannot correct a book. A clerk can only enter.

He stood on the forty feet of corridor and understood that the restoration of the fourth seat had to be written under the name already in the fourth line, because the entry belonged on the line it stood on and not on a sheet beside it, and that the line could not be read differently in that room because a member's standing may not be altered where it is not read.

So a woman who asked for nothing in this city except to be findable was read out loud to nine people on the morning of the first of April.

Her wanting is the only reason she is standing in that line. Everything else in it was done by a man who wanted a standing back.

## What He Did With It

He did not go up the sixty-one steps. He went down them, out of the building, across two courts and along to the Vant's smaller rooms with a clock in his head the whole way, and he got there at about twenty past two in the afternoon and stood on the stair with his hand on a rail that was not his.

The door was not answered.

He knocked twice, four seconds apart, and then stood there for about a minute and a half and did not knock a third time, because a woman who wanted to be findable had been found at about eleven minutes to ten that morning by nine strangers and a book, and there was a version of this evening in which he was the second knock.

On the eighteenth of March she had eaten at her own table for the first time in eighteen months. On the twenty-second she had cooked a bad plate twice. On the twenty-third of March at about nine in the morning she had gone up a stair on her own and written her own name in a line.

Somewhere in this city at that moment there was a return with her name four lines from the top of it in a hand that was not a clerk's, and it had been read out in a room, and she had not been in the room, and the reason she had been in it at all was a standing belonging to a man who had lost his on the first of February.

He had wanted the standing restored. He had gone up the road on the sixth of February and said nineteen words into a room and let it put his name at the head of a sheet, and on the twenty-third of March he had sat in a hotel corridor with a cup he had not drunk out of and let a woman go up a stair on her own.

Two days later a card came down at six with a floor on it. Four days after that a man in a corridor of forty feet had got what he asked for.

The door behind him stayed shut for the rest of the afternoon and he went down the stair again and did not go back up it.

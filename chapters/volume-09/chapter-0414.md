# The Ledger Crossing A Number

He kept his round on the twenty-fifth of April the way he had kept it every working morning since the first of April, which was ninety minutes of streets and about four doors and a woman at a laundry who had been getting up at four for eleven years and had never once been thanked for it in a form anybody could file.

The account does not stop. That is the whole of the arrangement and there has never been a second arrangement: if a man stops earning it, then in about forty days it flattens into something a machine in this city can read, and readable means scoreable, and scoreable means a file opens with his name on it and nobody else's on it anywhere in the world.

So he keeps his round. It is the only loyalty in this city that has never been tested and he does not deserve it.

He got in at about a quarter to five in the afternoon and counted the book on the table with his coat still on.

## One Hundred And Fifty

It said one hundred and fifty.

It had said one hundred and forty-nine on the morning of the twenty-third, which is two days and one night, and he had watched a man with a handcart walk out into a street because he could not think what else to do with a face.

The number had gone past a hundred and fifty in the space of an ordinary day, and it had not made any part of a sound while it did it.

[Ledger. One hundred and fifty lines. Ten turned. It is not sorry about either of them.]

He read it three times and put his hand flat on the open page, the way you put a hand flat on a door in a house where you have lived a long time.

The index at the back of a book like that one runs to about two hundred and sixty entries and this is not one of them. The book reports in people.

It has never once told him what a deed was, and it has never once told him whether a person was worth it, and on the twenty-fifth of April it told him that it had reached a number with an even number of digits and it was not sorry about that either, because it is not sorry, because it does not know what sorry is applied to.

A book that counted his acts would have been able to tell him something on the twenty-third. A book that counted his acts could have said *one* and he would have been a man who did one thing.

## Two Numbers, And He Had Been Managing The Wrong One

He sat down and did the arithmetic he had refused to do on the twenty-third, which took about nine minutes and which he had been putting off for two days because he knew what was at the end of it.

One hundred and fifty lines. Ten turned.

The hundred and fifty had been the number he had watched, and it had been a good number to watch, because it went up when he worked and it cost nothing and it was the visible proof that the account was alive. He had been managing that one for five years the way a man manages a tap he knows is running.

The ten was the other number and he had never looked at it directly in about nine days, and when he looked at it directly he found out that he did not know what he had been doing about it since the fourth of January.

He had thought he was keeping it down.

He had not been keeping it down. He had been not counting it, which is a different activity and which is the one that had been working, because a man who does not count does not have to decide anything.

A hundred and forty-nine lines went past a hundred and fifty on an ordinary day and told him nothing. Ten turned lines is the number at which the pattern stops being a pattern, and it stopped being a pattern at about ten past nine on the morning of the twenty-third of April, in a hall off a third court, on a stranger.

There was nobody to ask about it. That was the thing about it that he had to sit with.

In five volumes he had gone to nine different women with a fact in his hand and every one of them had been able to do something with it, and there was not one of them he could hand this to, because the count was the thing he had never shown anybody and the reason he had never shown it anybody was that a number you tell is a number that exists somewhere other than inside you.

## Half Past Five, Cordwain

The Field counter on the corner was busy at half past five on the way to nothing and there were about four clerks behind it and the street outside it had about ninety people going past it, which is a street with a tram on it and a market end four hundred yards away that everybody in this city knows about.

He put his hands on the wood.

"Ten."

Runa Sato did not look up for about a second and a half.

"Ten what."

"Ten turned lines. It was nine for five volumes and it has been nine since the fourth of January and it is ten as of the morning of the twenty-third, and it is not me."

"It is a carter who works that lane every night, and on the ninth of April, about two in the morning, he found an old woman on the ground and took her up three floors in his own arms, because that was quicker than going for help."

"I put my hand on his in a queue off a third court on the morning of the twenty-third. He said I had a cold hand and went out into the street."

The corner went on being a corner. Two of the clerks went on doing what they were doing. A man went past outside with a barrow.

"Once," said Runa Sato.

"Once."

"That is not turned." She picked up a bundle and put it down again. "Turned is three or four. A person who has felt it once knows it happened and a person who has felt it four times is a name in a list. You have got a man who has got a cold hand and an opinion about it."

"He knew I was in that queue. He said he had seen me three times."

"Then you have got a man who has seen you three times and a cold hand." She squared the bundle. "Stop telling me the number as though I can do something with it. I price things. I cannot price that."

"You are the only person in this city I can hand it to."

"I am the only person in this city you have decided to hand it to, and those are different sentences and you have known the difference since about the seventh of December." She looked up then. "What do you actually want."

"I want it counted by somebody who is not me."

He did not have an answer for that and it took her about four seconds to see that he did not.

## What It Costs To Be Counted

"Right," she said. "That I can price. That is a thing."

"You are not going to price it."

"I am going to price it in about four months when somebody in an office opens a book because a pattern went out of it, and at that point the price of you asking me to count it will be whatever it costs me to be the woman who had been told, and I will be the woman who had been told in a book that does not exist." She put both hands flat on the counter.

"There is no sheet for it. You do not get a receipt. There is no way to be the man who had a number and said it out loud to a person, and that is precisely why it is a price and not a favour."

"You are not writing it down."

"God forbid. If I write it down I have made a document out of a conversation at a corner counter and then it is in my file and then it is in yours, and you would be a man with a tenth turned line and a witness to the tenth turned line." She picked the bundle up.

"You are going to carry it alone and you are going to carry it in front of me, and I am going to let you, and that is the arrangement and it is not a friendship and it is not going to appear anywhere."

"Understood."

"Say the number again before you go."

"What."

"You have said it to me once on an ordinary evening on a corner with ninety people walking past it and about four clerks behind me, and there is no way to un-say it, and you know there is no way to un-say it, and you are going to go home tonight and decide that you are going to say it once more to somebody, because you cannot carry a number this size in a head that also has to work." She tipped her head. "Say it again."

"Ten."

"Good," said Runa Sato. "Now it is not an accident. Go and do your round."

He did his round.

## What He Is Carrying At Half Past Six

He counted the lines again at about half past ten and it was one hundred and fifty and one, because the round is the round and it does not stop for anything a man has said out loud at a corner.

Nine of the women in this city had been told things. Wren Kallo had been told a name she would not repeat. Sun Meiling had been told her own position by a woman reading a document in her own room. Marguerite Vale had read four words back at a counter.

And a woman who prices things had been told a number, out loud, at a corner on a street with a tram on it, at half past five on the evening of the twenty-fifth of April, and had refused to write it down, and had made him say it a second time so that it could not afterwards be called a slip of the tongue.

There is a difference between a fact that is in a book and a fact that is in two people's heads, and the difference is about nine years, and there is no instrument in this city that can move a fact out of a head and into a book or out of a head and into nothing.

He had put it in two people's heads for the first time in nine volumes.

The book said one hundred and fifty and one lines and ten turned and it was not sorry about either of them, and it had crossed a hundred and fifty without a sound, and somewhere in the city a man with a handcart who had never heard the word reservoir was going about his day with the knowledge that a stranger in a queue had put a hand on his and given him back something that had been owed since the ninth of April.

# Nine Years At The North End

The north end of the ranks wall is quiet after the painters have gone, and on the evening of the ninth of June Beatrix Halm was there with her notebook shut on the ledge and her pencil in her pocket, and he stopped about six feet away so that she could see him coming and did not have to hear him.

"It is one hundred and ten days since the twentieth of February," he said, because the date was the only thing to say first. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, with thirty and thirty and thirty and nine makes one hundred and ten."

She watched his mouth and nodded once.

"Twenty-one days to the thirtieth," she said. "Thirty taken from nine leaves twenty-one. Nine years at this wall and I know its mornings better than my own kitchen."

She turned the notebook round on the ledge so that the cover faced him.

"Come up," said Beatrix Halm. "Not here. I cannot hear a person at that kitchen table and I have not been able to for two years. I am deaf in one ear since the Veil and the city lives in the other one, and on a busy evening I cannot hear myself think. The room where your friend was weighed would be unusable to me. I live above a laundry on the second flight and it is quiet after eight. If you want me you come there."

## The Room Above The Laundry

The room above the laundry had one window over the yard and a table under it and a bed against the wall and a kettle on a stand that she did not touch, and a notebook on the table with six empty lines ruled on the open page and one filled near the top.

She shut the door with her foot and faced him in the middle of the small floor.

"I am not impaired," said Beatrix Halm, in her own mouth, before anything else happened in the room. "I am deaf in one ear and the noise lives in the other. That is the price of what I do and not a condition on anything. I feel the current of this city and I know where something is wrong before anybody is told, and it costs me the ear. Do you understand."

"I understand."

"I am not asking for care. I am saying it so that what happens next is wanted and not mistaken." She put her hand flat on the table. "I have wanted you since the thirteenth of March. Not since you stood at my wall. Since I wrote thirteenth, counter, nine forty-one under the seventh line and knew what it meant and could not tell anybody."

He stayed where he was.

"Say the rest."

"The rest is that I am the only intelligence source nobody can intercept, because there is no instrument for asking me and no paper I have ever given anybody, and I have been nine years inside the one instrument that cannot be forged, which is a woman writing down a time." She touched the notebook.

"Six empty lines and one filled. On the twenty-second of May I wrote a time on the back of a return in pencil and cannot be asked about it. Tonight the seventh line has something under it."

She opened the notebook.

*Twenty-second. Arcade hold. Nine hundred stood still. Twenty-two minutes. It is in here first.*

"This is the axis I am breaking," said Beatrix Halm. "Your others broke terms and the clock and the body described three times. Those three are spent and said on the page. Mine is a new one. I am breaking the quiet."

She looked up.

"Every scene you have had in this book was in a quiet room with the door shut and nobody talking. I cannot use a quiet room. In a quiet room the city leaves my good ear and I hear too much of you and nothing of myself."

"I need noise. I need the laundry below and the yard outside and you talking the whole time so I can place you. If you stop talking I lose you. That is not a game. That is deafness, and it is mine, and it is what makes this mine."

## Talking While Touched

He talked. He told her about the board at the end of the corridor with ten lines chalked on it and the tenth empty for four years, and about the woman who chalks it and has never been asked a question, and about the paint under it that says the Council on the thirtieth of June, while she came across the floor and put her hands on his coat and found the shape of him through it.

Her hands were cold round the cloth the way they were cold round a pencil every morning, and she kept her eyes on his mouth while he spoke, and when he stopped for breath she said, "Go on," and he went on.

She unbuttoned his coat and pushed it off his shoulders and it fell behind him, and she put her flat palms on his chest through his shirt to feel the vibration of his voice as much as to feel him.

"Your mouth moves and I feel it here," she said, her hand on his ribs. "Keep talking."

He told her about the return with nine lines and a tenth with a box, and the back of it with her own pencilled hand on it, while she pulled his shirt free and slid her hands under it to his warm back, and her fingers spread on his skin.

She kissed him then, her mouth on his mouth, open and certain, and she tasted of tea and of the evening air at the wall, and he kissed her back with his hands in her hair, and she made a small sound that she could not hear herself and laughed against his lips because she felt it in her throat.

"Again," she said. "Talk."

He said her name and the date and the wall while she unfastened his belt and he unfastened hers, and they stepped out of boots and trousers in the narrow space between the table and the bed, clumsy and quick, steadying each other with hands on hips and shoulders, and the laundry below sent up heat through the boards and the yard sent in the sound of water.

She was warm under her shirt and she pulled it off herself and stood in her skin with the notebook open on the table behind her, and he looked at her once, fully, and did not describe her again, because one looking was the whole of it and the page of this book has had enough of bodies counted three times.

"This is the second part of the axis," she said, her forehead against his. "No counted minute. No kettle after. Your others counted ninety seconds and a day and a half. I am not counting. When I come I come and you will know it because my hips will tell you, not my watch."

He lifted her and she wrapped her thighs round his waist, and he carried her the two steps to the bed and laid her down on the cover, and she pulled him down with her, her legs still round him, her heels locked at his back.

He held her hips and eased deeper, talking still, saying the words she needed to place him, and she moved under him with her hand on his hip to keep the rhythm she could feel and not hear, and the bed-frame knocked once against the wall and held.

Her inner muscles tightened round him and she came with her mouth open on his shoulder, biting to feel the pressure, wet and shaking, and she said, "Stay," and he stayed inside her, and he came after her with his face buried against her neck, spending into her with a sound he did not hold back because she wanted to feel it through his chest.

They lay joined for a while with the noise of the yard coming in, and then she shifted her hips and he slipped free, and she pulled the cover over them both with one hand and kept the other on his chest where his voice lived.

## The Seventh Line

After a while she got up bare and went to the table and picked up the pencil and bent over the notebook with her back to him, the line of work across nothing but skin, and wrote under the seventh line.

*Ninth. Above the laundry. After eight. Him.*

She turned the notebook round and pushed it along the table towards him with two fingers, the way she had pushed it along the ledge at the wall.

"Seven lines in nine years have something under them," said Beatrix Halm. "This is the seventh with something under it that is not nothing. The others were times. This one is a person."

He read it and put the notebook back the right way up for her.

"The gift arrived before anything else," she said, dressing again, straightforward, her back to him and then her front. "I felt the arcade hold go wrong four seconds before anybody stood still. That is what I do. The cost is that I am deaf in one ear and the city lives in the other. Neither was the mechanism of anything between us. You talked so I could hear you. That is all."

She sat down on the edge of the bed.

"My ending beat is mine," she said. "I am not going on the tram tomorrow to be asked. I am going to write times down at the north end every morning the way I have for nine years, and on the thirtieth of June the card comes down at six and the top tile is black, and I will write a name down for the first time in nine years and put the time against it, and this time there will be something under it."

He buttoned his coat. His hands were not steady and he did not hide it.

"Twenty-one days," he said.

"Twenty-one days," said Beatrix Halm. "Go down the stair quietly. The laundry is asleep and I want to keep it that way."

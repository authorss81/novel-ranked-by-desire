# The Minutes He Reads Himself

The clerk lets a member read the book after the run has gone, and on the evening of the fifteenth of June the corridor was empty and the bundle had gone at four, and she put two minutes on the second table under the window and left him alone with them.

"It is one hundred and fifteen days since the twentieth of February," he said to the empty corridor, because somebody had to say it once. "Eight from twenty leaves eight, with thirty-one and thirty and thirty-one and fifteen makes one hundred and fifteen."

"Fifteen days to the thirtieth," said the clerk from the doorway, and then she went away. "Thirty taken from fifteen leaves fifteen. That is the quarter."

## Alone With The Book

He sat at the second table with the two minutes in front of him and read them in his own hand, alone, the way no member has read them since the room went to five.

The first was the minute of the twentieth of February. Three seals. Laid, called, served and heard. Not carried. The premises named at the head of it four times. The ground a description made over it on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October at about eleven against the head. In the chair's own words a sentence that was true that morning and stopped being true on the thirtieth of March, and which no clerk can strike.

The second was the minute of the sixth of May. The witness line read into it twice. Seven names read twice. A gap read aloud twice. Under the fifth line a word in the clerk's hand, and under the gap the same word in the same hand, and he read both of them and did not say either out loud.

He read both twice. There was nobody in the room to be angry at. The clerk had written what the paper said and not what any person decided. The chair had written what happened and had not been asked to write a reason.

The keeper two floors under the Exchange was going to write a name from memory with no certificate behind it. The doctor was going to walk a corridor with no window to two doors. A woman above a laundry had written a person under a seventh line.

## What A Record Looks Like

He turned the first minute over. On the back of it, in pencil, in a hand the clerk knows and will not name, there was nothing, because the back of a minute is not part of it and nobody had written there.

## Fifteen Days

He put his hand flat on the table the way Beatrix Halm puts hers, to feel the talk that was not there, and he thought about Sun Meiling at the end of a kitchen table with a folder, and about Tessa Okonkwo ruling a twelfth date, and about Hanneke Ruis standing at a rail with a cup she had not touched, and about Runa Sato writing a reason into a box on the twenty-eighth of May with his name in it, in her own hand, unpriced.

The reason box had been written twelve days ago. It was in a drawer in a loading bay off Cordwain. It named him. Nobody had read it aloud. Nobody would strike it.

## What Remains Unwritten

He opened the first minute again and ran his finger down the four namings of the premises at its head, and then down the ground beneath them, the description made over it on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October at about eleven against the head, on four words said in a kitchen, and he read them again here and did not say them out loud.

A division that does not sit in the Wing and keeps no list of addresses cannot reach a woman on a list and can only reach the building she is in. That has been true since the first of January. The copy in the division of classification has been there for eighty-four days. Neither he nor the officer of counter-forensics has said one word about the address, and that arrangement has held for a hundred days and more.

He closed the two minutes and squared them against the edge of the table.

The clerk came back.

"Did you find what you came for."

"I found what a record looks like from the inside when there is nobody in it to be angry at," he said. "It looks like people doing exactly what they said they would do. A clerk entering. A chair refusing. A keeper drafting. A doctor administering. A woman certifying a house she sleeps in four times a year in her own hand."

"And the fifth."

"The fifth stands up when it is called." He stood. "Fifteen days. The Council sits on the thirtieth. The last working day of the quarter. Two returns about one matter at one clinic. A draft carry-forward. Your book written up when the room sits."

She took the minutes and put them back in the keeping.

"Then go home," said the clerk. "You have read them yourself. In four years a stranger with a folder will read them after you, and he will want to know who else has seen the service, and who wrote on the back of a return, and whose name is in a reason box, and what the fourth block of a sheet stands on."

He went out. The corridor was dark behind him and the court was dark ahead, and fifteen days stood between him and the day with five things on it, and every one of the five was already written somewhere in a book he could not correct.

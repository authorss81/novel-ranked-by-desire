# The Man On The Third Floor

He went up sixty-one iron steps at about eleven in the morning on the seventeenth of May with one question, and he had worked out on the way that he was going to ask it in a doorway and be answered in about four minutes, and it took a little over eleven.

The third floor of that building is one corridor with a door at each end of it and eleven desks, and ten of them had somebody at them and one of them had a man at it who had been at that desk for nine years and who had a bundle on his hip that he was not going to put down for anybody.

"Sit down," said Perrin Sowe, before Lin Hao had got to the end of the corridor.

"I have one question."

"Then you will ask it in the corridor, because you cannot ask me anything at that desk and neither can anybody else, and I did that to myself three years ago in writing, and I would like you to understand the shape of it before you ask it."

He did not look up. "A man who has asked in writing to be left off the distribution of anything further upon file One is a man who cannot be handed a thing at his own desk. Handing at the desk is distributing. That is the whole of it and there is no room in this building to argue with it and I have argued with it in three directions in three years and I lost all three."

"Then I will ask it out here."

"You will ask it out here," said Perrin Sowe, "and I will answer it out here, and it will take about four minutes, and then I will go back to my desk and we will both of us have done something we have not done in three years and neither of us will enjoy it."

## Four Minutes In A Corridor

He put the bundle on the sill and came out of the door and stood about four feet away, which is not a friendly distance and is the distance of a man who has decided to be exact.

"What comes out of that door."

"Nothing," said Perrin Sowe. "For three years. There has been no production off that door since the morning I wrote the letter, and the reason is in my coat and I have never taken it out of my coat, and I am not going to take it out for you."

"You asked to be left off the distribution of anything further upon file One."

"That is the letter."

He said it exactly. "Not off the distribution of everything. Off what is further upon one particular file, and nothing else. That is narrower than it sounds, and it is the only wording I could get past a post office and a man who had never heard of me."

"I have never regretted the wording and I have regretted the rest of it daily for three years, and both of those are true, and I have never been able to get anybody to hold both of them at once."

"And the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I asked the question on the third of February of the year before last, in this corridor, and I asked whether what came up that stair went anywhere else, and I was told by a man who has since stopped doing this work that it did not, and I believed him, and I believed him because it suited me, and I have thought about that for three years and I would not do it again." He shifted his weight. "Ask the next one."

## The End Of The Machine

"Can I have the name of the person who asked for the description of premises 34-6-118."

"No," said Perrin Sowe.

"You did not have to come out of the door for that one."

"No. But I did not have to come out of the door for any of them, and I have come out of that door four times in three years, and the last time was the fourth of February. Three of the four were you."

He said it without any weight in it at all, which was worse.

"I keep no names. That is neither modesty nor loyalty. Anything a floor files under a box has to be able to answer one question about itself, and the question is who it asked, and the answer is never the office that wants to know."

"The answer is a man at a desk on a floor. The department puts the box against him and files what he says. That man is me."

"I am the far end of the machine. I take names in and I keep none of them, and in nine years at that desk I have understood about four people who gave me one and not one of them has ever known it."

"You understood one."

"I understood one in about four seconds on the fourth of February, and I have not gone back up that corridor to say so, and I am not going to."

He looked at the bundle on the sill through the open door.

"Six letters of a woman's name went into a box record. It is filed and it is not wrong, and it is the only answer I have ever given that was not somebody's whole name. I have never been told whose it is and I have never asked, because asking is a distribution and I asked not to be distributed."

"You have worked it out."

"I have worked out that the six letters came from the floor below me and that the woman on the floor below me has said no to two things in three years and has given me six letters instead of a name, and I have worked out that she chose six letters because six letters is a box record and a box record cannot be read back to a person by anybody who has not got the file."

He went back inside and stood behind his chair. "I have not gone down that corridor. There are eleven days in it and I have not used one of them."

## What He Wanted Instead

Lin Hao stood in the corridor for a while.

"There is a document I cannot get a copy of," he said. "A production gets read out in the room that produces it and it lives there afterwards. I heard mine read out in a corridor forty feet from here on the fifteenth of May, and it stays where it was read. Is there anything else."

"There is one thing and I am going to tell you about it and then I am going to go back to my desk, because if I do not go back to my desk within four minutes then somebody else comes and looks at mine."

"Go on."

"A request of that kind can only be withdrawn by whoever it was addressed to, and mine went to a post. A post lasts about nine years and after that it is a drawer in a building that has been sold on."

He said it as though he were reading a date off a shelf.

"Three years gone. Six left, and then it stops being mine to withdraw at all. No office in this city can do it for me, I have looked for one, there is no sheet for it, and if I asked you for one thing it would be that and you could not do it either. That is my entire position."

"You are going to ask me for something."

"I am going to ask you for a date," said Perrin Sowe.

## The Date

"There is nothing in this city I can give you a date for."

"I am aware of that and I am asking anyway, and I have decided to ask, which is not a thing I have done in three years, so you may put the asking next to the walking out of that door and make a bundle of the two."

He came back to the doorway. "The second floor. Somebody comes down that corridor and asks a woman for a name for a box record and she says no and gives him six letters instead, and in three years he has not gone back, and I would like to know whether that floor is still going to be there when somebody needs a name on it."

"That is not a date."

"It is the only question I have," said Perrin Sowe. "I have had it for three years and I have never put it in words in front of anybody, because in front of anybody else it is a man asking after a woman who is not his business, and in front of you it is a man asking after a floor of a building he works in, which is a duller thing and is therefore askable."

"There is no instrument I can use on it," said Lin Hao. "You know that, because you have spent three years being the far end of an instrument. Nothing in this city lets a man find out where a person works."

"I know what it does not let him do. I am asking you what it lets him do and you do not know either, and I have watched you not know things in this corridor four times now and it is the only thing about the four of them that I have any use for."

That was the end of it. He went back behind his desk and picked up the bundle and put it on his hip, and the conversation was over, and he had been in the corridor for about eleven minutes and had answered one question fully and one question not at all, and the not at all had cost him nothing and the one he had answered fully had cost him the only fact he has.

## Sixty-One Steps Down

He went down one floor at about twenty past eleven and stood in the corridor outside the second floor with his hands in his coat.

On the sixth of December he had asked her one question about an address and had been given a standing answer, and he had said out loud that he would not ask it again. That was a hundred and sixty-one days ago. Since then the only thing that had passed her desk about him was one sheet of paper on the afternoon of the ninth of May, and it had been initialled above her own part in about four seconds.

The door was shut and there was a light under it. On the far side of that door a woman had put her name at the front of three separate records inside nineteen days. She had spoken about one of them to him once, on the back of a docket. The other two she had done at her own desk in daylight and let the paper find him instead.

There was nothing whatever in the world he could do about any of it.

He did not knock.

He stood there for about a minute and a half and then he went back up and out through the ground floor and into the court.

Behind him, on the third floor of a building that has never been named on any paper, a man who had asked in writing three years ago to be left off the distribution of anything further upon file One came out of his door with a bundle on his hip, walked the length of his own corridor to the far end of it, and stood at the rail above the stairwell.

He looked down at a floor he had not gone back to since the fourth of February.

Neither of them distributed anything.

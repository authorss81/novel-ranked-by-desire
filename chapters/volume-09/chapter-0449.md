# The Boy With The Paste Brush

He went to the public post off a third court at about twenty to four on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of June because there was nothing else in the city to go and do about it, and because the counter shuts at four whatever is in it, and there were four people in the queue and one of them was a man who had been in this queue on the fifteenth of April and had taken about ninety seconds.

"It is one hundred and twenty-eight days since the twentieth of February," he said, in the street outside, to the shut shutter. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and twenty-eight makes one hundred and twenty-eight."

He knew that boy. There was a card up on a wall at the head of the Reading at six on the morning of the thirtieth of March, and it had gone up in the way a card goes up, and the hand that spread the paste on it was the hand that took the sheet on the fifteenth of April.

That was the whole of the boy. There was no name and there had never been one in this account of him.

## The Queue Of Four

He stood at the back of it. The four in front of him were ordinary and none of them was in a hurry in the way that people are in a hurry at a post, and he had not been in this room since the fifteenth of April and the last time he had been in it he had handed a sheet of plain paper to a person behind a counter with a pen in it.

The boy did the four and then looked up at him.

"Morning," said the boy.

"Morning."

"Post or lodgement."

"Lodgement," he said, "and then something else, if you have got a minute, which I know you have not."

The boy put his chin on the side of his head for about a second.

"What sort of lodgement."

## The Ordinary Way

He told him, in about nine words, and the boy listened to all nine of them, and then the boy went along the corridor and found the sheet that says what a lodgement in the ordinary way is, which is not much, and read it, and came back.

"There is a sheet for that," said the boy, "and you do not have to have one, and the thing you write goes on the hand and it goes up the outside stair, and whoever is on the stair takes it."

"And nobody writes anything."

"Nobody writes anything," said the boy. "That is the whole of it. It goes up and it is gone and there is no receipt and no copy and no way to ask after it, and if you lose it you have lost it, and that is the same for everybody who comes in here with that sort of sheet."

He said it the way a boy says a thing he has said ten thousand times, which is to say without any weight at all, and then he said, "You have had one before," and went back to his counter.

## The Brush

That was the end of the lodgement. The other thing took another eleven minutes and it came about because of a jar.

There was a jar on the shelf under the counter with the lid off it and a brush standing in it, and the brush was about nine inches long with a flat bristle head, and the handle had been worn pale in two places by about fifteen years of a hand in one grip.

"What is that for," he said.

The boy looked at the jar the way a man looks at a thing he is asked about twice a week.

"Paste," he said. "That is paste. It is wheat and it goes off if you leave it, so it gets washed out every night and it gets made again in the morning, and the brush is in it because the brush is the only thing you can spread it with."

"Where does it get used."

"On walls." The boy wiped his hands down the front of his shirt without appearing to notice. "Not this office. This office does counters. That is a different office, on the other side of the court, and they come and get the jar and the brush off this shelf in the morning and they take it out in a case."

"How often."

"Four times a year," said the boy. "Last time was the thirtieth of March. Next one is the thirtieth of June."

## Half Past Four On A Wall

He was aware that he had stopped being a man in a queue.

"Where do they put it up."

"At the head of the Reading. There is a wall of tiles there and the card goes on the wall under the tiles, and they have to be at it at half past four because the card comes down at six and it takes a while to get it up flat." He wiped the counter with the cloth that is under the counter for wiping the counter.

"You have to be there early because you cannot paste onto a wall that is wet with the night and you cannot paste onto a wall that other people are standing in front of."

"You are the boy who does it."

"There is a list," said the boy. "There is a list of the numbers on a card in the case and you read them off the card so that you do not paste a four in where it should be a one. I have done it four times. You go at half past four, you put the card up, you read the numbers so you know which is which, and nobody tells you anything at all."

"Nobody tells you whose premises you are reading."

"Nobody tells you anything," said the boy. "You are not told and you are not supposed to ask, and that is not because anybody thinks you would do something with it. It is because there is nobody to tell. Whoever sets the card has the numbers and they go home."

"The wall has got the numbers on it for about a quarter and then it has got the next ones on it, and nobody in this building has ever looked at that wall and could not read it, and nobody who could read it has ever asked me what it said."

## The Outside Stair

"That is the one I asked about in April," he said. "The one that went up. Where does it go."

"To the room at the end of the corridor."

"Not through this building."

"It goes out of the door and across the court and up the outside stair of the hall and along a landing and into that room, and whoever is on the stair takes it off you and says nothing and goes on." The boy turned the cloth over. "I have been on that stair twice. They come down for it at the hour and you give it over and they take it in and that is the whole of the transaction."

"And this office keeps nothing."

"This office keeps nothing about anything, ever," said the boy, "and that is not a rule anybody made. It is just that there is nothing to keep. A lodgement in the ordinary way is not an event. It is not a receipt and it is not a payment and it does not change what is in anybody's book, and there is no list of them because no one has ever drawn a sheet for one." He looked at the shelf where the jar was.

"If you wanted to know whether you had done it, you would have to go and ask whoever has the thing, and they would tell you it is there, and there would be nothing at all in this building you could hold on to."

"So the only piece of it that exists anywhere is four minutes from this counter."

"It is four minutes and about two streets and it is in a book with about nine other things in it, and whoever wrote it has never met you and cannot be asked about it by anybody who has not already asked her." The boy shrugged one shoulder. "That is how it is supposed to work."

## Four People In A Queue

He stood there a moment and understood that he had gone twenty-odd streets to hear four sentences about a jar.

What he had actually come for, and had not said out loud, was that on the fifteenth of April he had stood at this counter and handed a sheet of plain paper to this boy and gone out into the street with nothing in his hands, and that for seventy-three days it had been the only act of his life with no witness to it that he could not produce anybody for.

Nobody knows about it except him and a clerk in a room and, since the ninth of May, seven desks and a woman in an office who read a line in a bundle three days later.

And a boy at a counter who was not told what he was holding, did not ask, and has not thought about it once since April because there was nothing in his hands to think with, and that is not a failure of the system or a triumph either. It is the ordinary way.

## The Fourth Of Four

The shutter went at ten minutes to four, the way it always went, and the four people who had been in front of him went out through it, and the boy put a board across and then came back and pulled the board straight again so that it was square in the frame, and did it the way he did it, without appearing to have a thought about it.

"Tomorrow is the thirtieth," said the boy, from the other side of the counter.

"Yes."

"You will be somewhere."

"I do not know yet," he said.

The boy nodded and put the jar lid back on the shelf and gave him the nod that a public post gives a man who has finished being in front of it.

## Two Days

He got out under the shutter with about twenty seconds to spare, and stood in the street off a third court in the light that this city has at about ten minutes to four on the last working day of a quarter, and worked out the thing he had gone down there to go and look at, and found it was simpler and worse than he had expected.

There is a woman who stands under the north end of that wall every morning of her life.

She has a notebook and her hands are cold round the pencil and she has done it for nine years and not one person has ever asked her a question about it, and on the thirtieth at six a card will come down on that wall and she will read it, and she has said out loud, at a kitchen table, that she will write a name down and put the time against it.

There is a tile at the top of that wall that has nothing on it and has had nothing on it for twenty years and is a different colour from every tile under it, and on the thirtieth there is a boy with a paste brush and a list of numbers at half past four and the only person in this city who will look at what he has put up is a woman who reads walls in the dark and has done it every morning for nine years.

He stood in the street and understood that he was going to go and stand there.

Not at six. At half past four, on the thirtieth morning, in the dark, at the north end, where the boy would be working and where a woman would arrive to find a man standing there in the way a man stands when he has come to see what a wall says.

He worked out in about ninety seconds, on a street off a third court, on the twenty-eighth of June, that he was not going to do it, and that this was the first decision he had made in ninety days that cost him something instead of saving somebody else something.

She reads that wall alone. She has read it alone for nine years and she has never once had a man standing at the north end when she came, and the only reason she has ever wanted to write a name on the black tile at the top of it is that in nine years not one person has.

If he were standing there when she picked the chalk up, then whatever she wrote on the thirtieth morning would have been written with a man under it, and in about four years there would be a stranger with a folder and a wall and a tile with a name on it, and no way on earth to tell anybody whether the woman who wrote it did it for herself or because she was not alone.

He would be in the room at the end of the corridor at about two in the afternoon on the thirtieth, answering a requisition with a seal he had not asked for, in front of a clerk and four people including a man in the fourth seat and a woman in a chair that is not a member's.

He would be about a mile and four floors away at six in the morning, on his round, ninety minutes of streets and about four doors, being the man who is there when nobody is looking.

There is a version of this in which he stands at the north end at half past four and watches his own name go onto a black tile with a paste brush on it, and he gave that version about ninety seconds in the street off a third court, and then he walked home and did not take it with him.

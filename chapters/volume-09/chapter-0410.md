# Seven Of The Eight

The sheet was one piece of plain paper with a line ruled across it and a heading at the top, and it had come down the chain on the fifteenth with the return on it, and it said that a consignment is witnessed on the day it is lodged and at the address of the holding.

The holding was kept at premises 34-6-118.

So the sheet came back to a kitchen.

"There is no deadline printed on it," said Beatrix Halm, at about eight o'clock on the evening of the eighteenth of April, with the paper flat on the table. "I have looked for one for four minutes. It does not say what happens if it is not witnessed, and it does not say by when, and it does not say whether a person may be on it twice or not at all."

"It is witnessed on the day it is lodged."

"The fifteenth was three days ago," said Beatrix Halm. "So that has gone, or it has not, and there is nothing on this sheet that tells me which, and I have been in this city for nine years and I have never once been able to make a rule out of a thing that is not printed."

Seven of them signed before half past eight.

## Seven Hands

There was no order and no ceremony. Agnete Lindqvist signed first because she was standing nearest. Runa Sato signed second and made the pen go back exactly the way it had been, which she does. Nadia Ferrous signed third and read it first, all of it, twice, the way she reads everything, and then said she was sorry and signed.

Wren Kallo signed and looked at Lin Hao while she did it and did not say one word about it.

Isolde Marchetti signed, put the pen down, and said one thing out loud to nobody in particular: that she would be signing it for herself and not for him, and would thank him not to read it the other way.

Beatrix Halm signed and put the pen down at a right angle to the edge of the sheet.

And Petra Ilyin came in at about ten past nine, two days late, in guild cloth, and signed without reading it, and said she had told him in a bay that she would be there on the eighteenth and she had not said she would be early.

That was seven.

## Two Fingers Wide

Sun Meiling had been at the table since about half past seven with a second cup that she had not drunk from, and she had watched all seven of them sign, and she had not put her hand near the paper.

"There is no rule that says eight," she said.

"There is not."

"A witness line is a list of the people who were in a room on a date. Seven of us were in this room on the eighteenth. I was in this room on the eighteenth." She looked at the line and at the space in it. "That space is two fingers wide and it is mine, and it is the fourth gap of this kind in this house and the first one I have put there myself."

"Say why it is there."

And she said the reason in one sentence, the way she says everything that matters, without raising her voice and without any warning at all.

"A witness line naming me on a consignment of that holding makes me an officer of that holding, and an officer of a holding is a person the room can serve."

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds.

"You are right," said Lin Hao.

"I have been inside Veil-Research for three years and under a permanent bar since the first of September, and I have spent every one of those years making myself expensive to reach."

"I have done it on purpose. I have never once used either of them as anything except a wall, and I have used them very well."

She laid her palms down on the cloth, one to each side of the blank, so that the gap was framed by her own hands.

"And I am not going to be the reason there is a name from this address in a book at the end of a corridor that a room of five can serve anything on."

"That is the whole of it and it is not a vote and it is not a favour to you. It is a woman declining to be reachable, in a room where she is reachable, in front of seven people who have just made themselves reachable."

## What He Had Done

"Sun—"

"Lin Hao." She said his name the way she had said it on the second of April at a rail on a walled road, which is to say as a person correcting a document. "You did not ask me to be in that room."

"No."

"You did not ask me to be in this room either. I was in it, because I eat at that table, and you put a sheet on it and asked eight people and I was one of the eight."

"Yes."

"That is not a complaint and it is not a wound, and I would like it understood that I have had the same answer out of two rooms now."

She straightened the cup she had not drunk from.

"It is arithmetic, and I worked it out on the evening of the seventeenth in about four minutes."

"A line is a list of the people who were in a room on a date. There were eight of us at this table on the evening of the fourteenth, and seven of us signed on the eighteenth, and the space is mine."

"And the space is the reason."

"Then what is the space for."

She did not answer him. She picked up her cup, found that it was cold, and put it down again.

## The Two Facts In One Room

Runa Sato was washing up and said it to the sink, not to the room.

"The line goes down the chain on the ordinary stream at four on the ordinary days, and it came down from a public post off a third court, which is the building with the iron stair in it."

Nobody looked up.

"And the woman who certified the composition of that premises on the thirtieth of November works on the second floor of that building," said Runa Sato, "and has done since before any of us knew there was a holding, and the man on the third floor has been there nine years and came down her corridor once, for a box, on the fourth of February."

She shut the tap.

"I am not joining them up. I am putting them in the same room because there is no other room, and because Runa Sato says the arithmetic out loud and does not get to choose what somebody else does with it."

The kitchen went on for about a minute. Something was boiling in a pot at the back and nobody went and looked at it.

## What A Gap Says

At about half past nine Sun Meiling stood up and did not leave.

She put her chair back under the table, which she does, and then she stood with her hand on the back of it and looked at the sheet with seven names on it and a space two fingers wide in the middle where she should have been.

"You are going to think about the gap," she said. "You are going to think about it on the way up sixty-one steps and you are going to decide it is a hole in a document, and it is not. It is not a hole. Nobody at that address was ever on this line."

"Then what is it."

She looked at him across a kitchen with seven signed names on a sheet between them.

"A gap in a line tells the person who reads it exactly one thing," said Sun Meiling, "and the thing is that the person who put it there decided to."

She did not explain it. She did not soften it and she did not look away while she said it, and she did not stay for another four minutes to see what he did with it.

She went down the stair at about twenty to ten, and the stair did not make a sound under her, and the sheet stayed on the table with seven names and a space in it.

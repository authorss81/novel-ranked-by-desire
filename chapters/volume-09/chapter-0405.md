# The Promise She Cannot Remember

The doorway off Cordwain is nine feet of iron between a loading bay and a street and it is not a place anybody stands in on purpose. Petra Ilyin came out of it at about half past four in the afternoon of the eighth of April in guild cloth with her sleeves pushed up and a docket under one arm, and she had been at a job since about six in the morning, and she was going to be at another one at seven in the morning.

She saw him from about nine feet and did not change her pace and did not stop.

"You have come to the wrong doorway," she said. "This is a doorway. There is a bay on the other side of it and nine men in the bay and none of them are interested in you."

"I know. I waited for you anyway."

"You waited about eleven minutes and two men went past you carrying a coil." She put the docket down on the sill. "Say what you came to say, and then I am going to eat, because I have been on my feet since six and I am about half past four in the afternoon and that is the whole of what I have."

## The Ask

"I need an attestation," said Lin Hao.

Petra Ilyin looked at him for about two seconds.

"No," she said.

He had a second thing ready and it was about the price and about there being a different way to do it, and he got about four words into it.

"Stop," she said. "You have not asked me what it costs yet. You have asked me and I have said no in one syllable and you are already building. I will tell you why I said no and then you are going to find it extremely difficult to argue with, and I am telling you that in advance so that neither of us wastes the afternoon."

"Tell me."

"I am going to tell you what I am and not what happened to me, because what happened to me took eleven years and you have about twenty minutes and I owe you neither."

She took her sleeves down.

"My gift is a sworn promise. That is the whole of it and there is nothing else about me you would call magic."

"When I give a promise, it binds my body. Not my word. My body."

"I can eat a meal afterwards. I can walk out of a room. I cannot, while the promise stands, do the thing I did not promise, and I do not remember deciding not to do it."

"Nothing about it frightens me. That is the part nobody ever believes."

She rolled her shoulder.

"It feels like setting a cup down on a table you have already decided about."

"And you are under one."

"I have been under one for eleven years." She said it as a clerk says the time.

"I have no instrument. I have never seen it. I have asked the guild twice and both times they told me an oath of holding is discharged by the person who holds it."

"The person who holds it is a man I have never spoken to. I have been trying to find him since I was about three years into the thing, and there is nothing anywhere in this city with his face on it."

## The Arithmetic

"So I cannot give you one."

"Petra—"

"I have not finished and I have not raised my voice, and if you interrupt me again I will go inside and you can talk to the iron."

She waited until he had stopped.

"A promise binds my body. It is still binding. It is binding right now, standing in this doorway, and you can test it, and I would rather you did not."

"And I cannot put a second one on top of the first. That is not a rule anybody made. That is what a binding instrument is. It is one at a time, and mine has not been discharged, and the only thing in this city that can discharge it is a man I have spent eleven years failing to find, who was in a room I was not in at eleven o'clock in the morning and has never been in the same street as me since."

She picked the docket up off the sill.

"That is the price. It is not fear and I want that written down in whatever you are keeping, because you have gone eight volumes without asking anybody for one single thing and this is the first time you have asked and you are going to go home and tell them a woman said no, and the true sentence is that a woman said *one at a time* and has been saying it in her own mouth for eleven years."

He did not argue. It was the first time in nine volumes that he had been told no by somebody who wanted to say yes, and the not-arguing took about four seconds and cost him more than anything he had said that week.

"Right," he said. "Thank you."

"Do not thank me, it is arithmetic." She was already turning. "And do not come back on the sixteenth. I am at the Exchange all day on the sixteenth and there is nothing in that building about you that I have not already said on a stair."

## Two Instruments

She got two paces.

"Wait," he said. "You signed a roll on the second of April."

"I signed a roll on the second of April." She turned back round and there was something almost like amusement in it.

"I want to be very clear with you about that, because it is going to look like an inconsistency on about the fifteenth and I am not going to be having it twice."

"A roll is a roll. I have signed a roll."

"It is a list, and I am one of the names on a list, and there is a column on that sheet with a figure in it, and the figure is fifteen under a band, and none of it asks me anything."

"I could have refused it. I thought about it on the stair and I signed it, because a roll is a list of people who live somewhere and I live there, and I am not going to be the one who stands in a hall and makes a thing out of that."

"And this is not that."

"The attestation is not that. The attestation is a promise, in your form, in your building, about a holding, with my name on it for four years." She put her hand flat on the iron of the door frame and left it there. "Those are two instruments and one of them is a list and one of them is a chain, and I have signed a list and I am not putting on a chain this year or any year while the other one is on."

"Sun Meiling and Wren Kallo have not been sworn at all."

"Then they are having a better April than I am." She took her hand off the frame. "I will come and find you. I am not going to let you pick the day, because you will pick a day that is convenient and then it will be a thing you did to me. I will come and find you and it will be within a week and I will not write to you first."

## The Queue, Priced Out Loud

Runa Sato was at the Field counter on the corner at about twenty past five that evening and she was enjoying herself, which at a Field counter in April is a thing you can see from across the room.

"Two hundred and eleven," she said, before he had got to the rail. "That is the queue as it stands this afternoon, and I have just been asked to price it by a man in a bad coat who does not work here."

"Here it is. You are going to stand there and take it like a plate."

"Two hundred and eleven people want to give a paper saying they were in a room on a date. Sixty-one of them have done it before, which in this city means they have done it once and cannot get it back."

"Nine of the sixty-one are at a division that serves papers at an address, so nine of them get served every time anybody writes their name anywhere."

"Six of the eight at your table hold a post that a division can serve a paper at. Field, counter-forensics, the Ferrier line, the guild, the Church, and Veil-Research."

"Veil-Research is the second floor of a building off a third court, and the woman on that floor is not on your roll and cannot be, and there is a man two floors above her who has been there nine years and has not come down that corridor since the fourth of February."

She put the pen down.

"That is the price of your eight, and I have given it to you in the wrong order, and I am giving it to you again because you did not write it down the first time."

"The two who do not hold a post."

"Two of the eight are not in a division at all. One of them is a lift between two floors on a shaft on Ferrier Street and the other one sings in a room in Bellhouse four nights a week." She squared the sheet.

"And neither of those two is the woman who swore your roll, because that woman is not one of the eight. She runs a counter on a walled road, she read nine names out loud off one sheet on the second, and she has had her finger on that roll for about half an hour a day ever since."

He had not thought of it that way and he had been thinking about it for six days.

"And the standing order," he said.

Runa Sato looked at him.

"No," she said. "You do not get to ask me about that, and you do not get to use it, and you have never invoiced it, and you are not going to."

"I wrote a standing order on the thirtieth of October and it is mine, and the fact that it exists is the reason I am standing at this counter and not at a desk."

She looked at him for about a second and a half.

"If you ever put it next to a price in any conversation we ever have, I will take myself off your table entirely, and you will not be able to stop me. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Good." She picked the pen up. "Now go and ask the six that are not already hers."

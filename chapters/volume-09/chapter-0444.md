# The Cheapest Thing In The Room

The room where nine people sit has nine chairs that count and one that does not, and the one that does not has been standing empty at the table since the fourth of December, and on the afternoon of the nineteenth of June Lin Hao spent most of an hour being told what it cost.

"It is one hundred and nineteen days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and nineteen makes one hundred and nineteen."

"Eleven days to the thirtieth," said the clerk. "Thirty taken from nineteen leaves eleven. Sit down. You have been standing in that corridor for four days and it is going to take more than standing."

## Nine Chairs And One

He counted them from the doorway, which he had done before, and got nine and one again.

"The nine count and the ten does not."

"The ten does not count for anything at all," said the clerk. "It is not a seat. It is a chair that a woman is allowed to sit in at that table while the room does its business, and it goes in no book, and she takes no part in anything said at that table, and she cannot be produced against one word of it."

"Alma Quayle has sat in it since the fourth of December and has heard every minute of this quarter and has sealed nothing and entered nothing and will never be a member of anything again."

"Then what is it for."

"It is for a person who has to be in the room and cannot be in the room," said the clerk, "and it is the cheapest thing in this building and it has been the cheapest thing in this building since December, and I will tell you what it costs, because you have been standing in my corridor for four days working out whether you can afford something and this is the one thing you cannot afford and do not need to."

"It costs the room nothing."

"It costs the room nothing whatever," she said. "It is a chair."

## The Woman Who Came Down The Corridor

Sun Meiling came down the corridor at about half past one on the nineteenth of June with a docket under her arm and her coat still on, because she had been up two floors of her own building that morning certifying the composition of a set of premises that had nothing to do with any of this, and had come to this hall on ordinary division business at about one, and had been told by a woman at a rail that the fifth was in there.

She had a folder as well as the docket and she put the docket under her arm and kept the folder in both hands.

"They will not let me stand," she said, from the doorway. "I have been told to sit down twice and I am not on the roll and I am not a member of anything and I would like to hear what is being answered."

The clerk came out from behind the second table and stood in the corridor and looked at the chair at the end of the table for some seconds.

"You may sit there," she said. "You do not have to and nobody has asked you to and nobody will write down that you were here. If you sit there you will be in the room when the subject answers a requisition that names the premises four times and goes out at four the same afternoon."

"That minute names a composition and a composition is administered against the persons keeping the address, and the person certifying that composition is you, and you are not on the roll and you cannot be sworn and there is nothing in this city that would put a wall between you and that minute."

Sun Meiling stood in the doorway and did not move for about four seconds.

"That is exactly what it is for," she said.

## Alma Quayle

She was already in it. Alma Quayle had been at that table's end since the fourth of December with a bundle of her own division's paper on her knees and a face that nobody in this building had been able to read in ninety days, and she did not get up when the other woman came down the corridor, and she did not look at her either.

"Two," said Alma Quayle, without moving her head.

The clerk shut the door.

"Two women in one chair that counts for nothing, and the two of them are the only two people in this building who cannot be produced against a standing and are being produced against something anyway," said the clerk. "Now go and stand at your table like a man who has been asked a question and not like a man watching a thing happen to two people he put there, or you will spoil it for them."

He went and stood at his table with his hand on the back of the second chair on the left, which was four inches lower than the other eight, and he did it for about an hour and a quarter, and he said nothing at all.

## What He Was Not Allowed To Do

Twice in that hour and a quarter he got as far as opening his mouth, and twice he shut it again, and the clerk saw both of them from the second table and let them stand.

He wanted to say thank you. He wanted to say it in the ordinary way to a woman in a coat at the end of a table, and the ordinary way would have turned an afternoon into a thing he had arranged, and he understood somewhere around the fortieth minute that this was the whole of what the chair was: not a place for a person who is owed something, but a place for a person who has come anyway and would rather be owed nothing.

The woman at the end of the table looked at him once, at about twenty to three, and put two fingers flat on the oak, and that was the entire conversation.

## What The Expensive One Costs

At about ten past two he asked the clerk what a minute of the carrying actually is, and she told him, because he had been circling it for four days and she was tired of watching a man circle.

"Five seals. One name of the subject under the fifth line, four other seals above it, the premises named four times in the body, the ground described in the body, the composition stated with the hour it was read, and the date. That is the whole of the instrument and it takes a clerk about eleven minutes to make and it costs the room nothing. That is not the expensive part."

"What is the expensive part."

"At four o'clock," said the clerk, "it goes out on the ordinary run to seven desks, and it goes out whole, and there is no way in this city to put it back in a drawer once it has gone to the first one. Six of those seven have a woman from your address at them and you have never once met any of them and two of them you have never even been told the names of. A minute does not have to be read."

"It has to be there, in a bundle, on a working day, at four, in the ordinary way, and a woman in a division who has never heard of you will look at a sheet with your address on it four times and she will put it in a pile."

"And the third desk is not one of those six."

"The third desk is the wing off the third court," said the clerk. "Six doors, a glazed counter, a number on a door that is on no register of the wing, and a woman who has been at that desk for nine years and who had never been told the room at the end of this corridor stood at three. Nothing is ever addressed to it. Everything addressed to the sixth goes up, past and through."

"If your address goes on a run, it goes through that corridor on somebody's way to somewhere else, and in four years there is a stranger with a folder and he has heard that a wing nobody can name exists, and this is the first minute in twenty years with your premises on it four times."

"And there is nothing I can do about the third desk."

"There is nothing anybody can do about the third desk," said the clerk. "That is the part of your life you have spent to other people's figures and it does not come with an instrument. It comes with a stamp you put on yourself in a queue."

## The Woman Who Has Been Sitting Here Since December

He looked at the chair, which was empty again by then, and at the woman who had been in it.

"Alma Quayle has been in that chair since the fourth of December."

"Since the fourth of December."

"Ninety days in a chair that counts for nothing, in a room where nothing has happened, and she told me on the thirteenth of May to count her at the table and she told me in her own words that a word after the thirtieth is not written until the first of July, and she has been right about everything she has said to me since December." He put his hands flat on the back of the second chair on the left.

"I have never once asked her what it is like to sit there."

"No," said the clerk. "And there is a reason for that which is not her being difficult, and I am going to give it to you once and then it goes. A chair that counts for nothing is the safest object in this building. A woman in it hears everything and is named in nothing. Nothing that happens at that table is hers, and nothing can be taken off her, because she never put her hand on anything."

"In ninety days that chair has cost this room nothing, and in ninety days it has been the only place in this building where a person could sit for free and be left alone."

"And you never told her."

"I never told her because she has not asked me and she has not asked you and she has not asked anybody, and a woman who has not asked is a woman who has decided what she is sitting in, and if I had come down here and explained it to her on the fourth of December she would have had to decide whether to accept it, and she has got a division of her own to sit in."

The clerk turned a bundle over and squared it against the table. "That is the other thing about that chair, and you have had it in your hands all afternoon and you have not looked at it."

"It is not safe because it is cheap. It is safe because nobody has ever been made to want it."

## What It Cost And Who Paid

At three o'clock the clerk opened the door and the two of them got up, and Alma Quayle went out first with her bundle, and did not speak in the corridor, and has not spoken in it since.

"The room paid nothing," said the clerk, when the door was shut again. "Say that out loud to somebody once so that it is in the air in this corridor and not only in your head."

"Somebody paid," he said.

"Somebody paid," said the clerk. "You did not. I want to be exact about that, because you will spend the next eleven days telling yourself you put her in that chair. You did not put her in that chair."

"You have never in your life put anybody in a chair, and there is no instrument in this city by which you could, and that is the one thing you have always been able to rely on and it is the reason I have let you stand in my corridor for four days." She squared the printed sheet that was still on the second table.

"She came down that corridor on her own feet at half past one with a docket under her arm, and she sat down because a chair was empty, and if I had said no she would have gone back up two floors and heard all of it in a docket instead."

"And you said yes."

"I said yes because a chair was empty and because I have been in this room for nine weeks with a requisition in my keeping that nobody will carry and I would have gone to the wall myself on the thirtieth of June if I could have got out of this building before four." She put the sheet in the drawer. "Do not thank her. Not tonight and not at the end of it."

"A woman who sits in a chair that counts for nothing so that she can hear what is being done about her own front door is not doing you a favour and she would find the favour unbearable."

## Eleven Days

He went out into the court at ten past three and the light was the colour it was in this city in the last week of June and there were eleven days left in the quarter.

He had spent four days learning what standing up was made of and one afternoon learning what it was made of when the standing up was not the point, and he had come out of the corridor with nothing in his hands and something behind him that he was not going to be able to put down or hand over or thank anybody for, and the thing behind him was that a woman had chosen to be in the room.

"She will be there on the thirtieth," he said.

The clerk was in the doorway with the drawer shut.

"No," she said. "She is not on the roll and nobody may ask her and there is no sheet."

"And that is the part you should take upstairs with you, because it is the only thing you can do with it: on the thirtieth of June, at about two in the afternoon, a man is going to stand up in a room and answer a requisition out loud."

"There is no way on earth to keep the woman who certifies that composition four times a year in her own hand out of the corridor while he does it, and he is going to have to do it with her in the corridor, and he will not know whether she came."

Then she shut the door, and the corridor was forty feet long and empty, and there was a bench at the end of it that nobody had ever offered him.

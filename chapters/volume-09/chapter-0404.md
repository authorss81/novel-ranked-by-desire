# The Entry She Never Closed

She had the table out when he came up, which was new, and there were two plates on it, and one of them had something on it that had been covered with a second plate for about four hours in case either of them lost their nerve.

"Sit," said Marguerite Vale. "And do not tell me about the first of April. I have known since the morning of the second and I ate a very good lunch on the second and I intend to have this conversation about something else."

"You have known since the second."

"A man in the second sub-level owes me a box." She took the plate off.

"A standing was restored in a room on the morning of the first at about ten minutes to ten, and the line it was restored over is the line I wrote my name in on the twenty-ninth of March. It was read out. There were nine people in that room and one of them was a man who has no standing in it."

She sat down.

"I wanted to be findable. I have wanted it since before the Vant. I have been findable for four days and it has cost me nothing at all, and I am not going to be dramatic about it in front of a plate."

Ink sat in the cuticles of her hands in a way that a broker's hands sit in it. He looked at them and did not say the thing he had come upstairs to say, which was that she had been read aloud to nine strangers in a building she has never been inside in her life because he had asked for a standing back.

"Say the other thing," said Marguerite Vale.

"What is rank nine."

## What A Consignment Is

She put her fork down, which she did about four times a year.

"Right," she said. "That is a question I have been waiting five volumes for and I want it in the right order and I am going to take about nine minutes and you are not going to get a look in."

She took a folded sheet out of the side of the table and squared it and did not open it.

"First. What you have been doing since September. You have been asking me for prices and I have been pricing things and you have been assuming every time that the thing being priced was the reservoir, and it has not been, not once, and I have not corrected you because correcting you would have been free and I do not give anything away that I can hold over a person."

"Then what is being priced."

"A rank." She turned the sheet round. "The Ninth Seal is not a band. That is the whole of it and everything else follows and I am going to say it once. A band is a figure. A figure is read off a card. The card comes down on the last working day of a quarter and prints a floor and prints what is against an address. That is the entire mechanism by which this city has ever told anybody what they are."

"And the ninth."

"There is no card." She said it flatly. "There has never been one. Nine has no floor and no interval and no figure on any sheet in this city, and that is why the top tile on that wall is black, and it has been black and empty for as long as anybody alive can remember, and there has never been a reason put on it and there is not one to put."

He had been at that wall every morning of a nine-year woman's life and had never once asked why the top tile was black.

"So how does anybody get to it."

"You lodge." Marguerite Vale said the word the way a clerk says *fourpence*.

"That is the whole verb, and there is nothing else in this city that means what it means, and I want you to hear it in my mouth once."

"You take your whole accumulated standing — every figure you have ever held, everything anybody has ever put under your name, all of it, the lot — and you lodge it. One movement. Your own hand. At a public post, in the ordinary way."

"The way a woman gives up an address at a counter. With no instrument and no witness and no holder and nobody standing behind you."

Her hands were flat on the table.

"And it cannot be partial. You cannot top it up in June. You cannot draw against it, not one grain, not for a day, not for a doctor. And you cannot be given it back. There is no paper in this city that puts a consignment back into a person and there never has been, and I have looked, and I have had a chained book open on my knee about it for eleven years."

## What It Does To A Person

"And then you are held," she said.

That word. He had two rooms in his life and a man in one of them.

"It is not a cell. Do not make it a cell in your head. It will make you do stupid things." She said it fast.

"In law it is a form and a room and an administration every quarter, and a medical return to a clinic every quarter, like the one a man in a room with no window on the third sub-level gets."

"And standing that is not yours to spend while it stands. That is all. You keep your plate and your name and your trade, and you can be walked up a hill and arrested in the ordinary way."

She put two fingers on the table.

"You cannot spend."

He put his hands flat on the table too.

"And the Council holds it," said Marguerite Vale. "All nine of them, as a standing thing, not as a favour and not as a job. Which means that when you are lodged, five people in a room at the end of a corridor can answer for you, and answer *about* you, and serve anything they like on you, and take nothing off you to do it, because they already have the only thing there is to take."

"That is the point of it."

"That is the point of all of it and I am glad somebody finally said it in a room." She sat back.

"Five people in a room can answer for a man who has handed them everything and has nothing left to refuse them with. That is why nobody has ever wanted it."

"Not a cap. There is no cap anywhere, take my word and check it."

"There has never been a tenth because in this city for twenty years nobody has been willing to hand their whole life to nine strangers."

## The Only Thing That Can Be Given

He understood the whole of it there and then, and it took about four seconds, and one of them was the wrong way up.

He could not climb on merit. He had been at the floor of his band on eight hands and four sheets of plain paper for ninety days and everything he had ever done with his own life had scored nothing at all, because deeds without instruments do not score in a city made out of figures.

And he could not climb on what was in him, because not one grain of it was scoreable, and that had been true since Chapter two and it was the engine of everything and there was no reading of it that made it a figure.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one," said Marguerite Vale. "That is what you are. Fifteen under a floor you have not got to yet. There is nothing in this city you can climb on. Not the reservoir, not your own hands, not a thing anybody has ever given you for nothing."

"No."

"No." She turned the folded sheet over and did not open it. "The only thing in this whole business that can be given away is a figure that other people have already put under you. And every one of those names is now a document somebody can produce. That is the trade and there is no other version of it, and I have been able to say that sentence since about Chapter two hundred and I have been waiting for you to get near enough to hear it."

She took the plates off the table.

"So there is one thing left to do with those eight women," she said, "and it is not climbing, and it is not safety, and it is the reason I have been waiting five volumes."

## She Will Not Put Her Name Under It

"No," said Marguerite Vale.

She said it before he had asked, and she said it without any weight in it at all, the way she said prices.

"I am not one of the eight. I know that better than anybody in this house."

"I am a guild broker, and there is no instrument in this city that will enter my standing anywhere. That is the reason I wrote my name in a line, and the reason nothing happened when it was read out."

She squared the sheet she had never opened.

"And I am not going to put my name under a consignment of a holding I am not answerable for. That is not modesty, and I have been told it is modesty twice this month by people who have not got a word."

"Then tell me the price and I will work on it."

"You cannot work on it. That is the thing you have been getting wrong since January." She looked at him.

"Ask the woman at that table whose gift is a binding instrument. Ask Petra Ilyin what a promise is, because a consignment is witnessed and an attestation is a promise, and the two of them are not the same instrument."

"She has been under one for eleven years and she cannot remember who put her under it, and she will tell you the price in about four sentences, and then she will not be able to tell you a price at all."

She pushed the plate towards him.

"Eat that. And then go and ask her, and when she says no — and she is going to say no, and she will be right, and it will have nothing to do with you — do not come back up here and tell me she was frightened. She will not have been frightened. She will have done arithmetic."

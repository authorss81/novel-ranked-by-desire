# The Singer Who Cannot Sing Here

The back corridor of the Bellhouse is nine worn steps up a side stair from the yard and it has four doors in it and a fire door at the end with a bar on it that has been painted so many times the wall has bowed out about half an inch.

It is the only corridor in Ostara Isolde Marchetti goes into on purpose and she had known that for about twenty years.

"You cannot sing in a corridor," she said. "I am going to say that twice and then you are going to tell me why, and if you get it right I will not make you sit on a step."

"Because you cannot choose."

"Because a corridor is not a room. A room is a room. You can tell where a room starts and you can shut the door of it and you have been in it four hours and you know the ceiling. A corridor is a road between rooms." She put her back against the wall opposite the first door. "You cannot sing in a road, because a road goes somewhere."

She had her coat over her arm and nothing on under it but a work blouse with the sleeves turned, and she had come out of the Bellhouse at about twenty past three in the afternoon and the awning three feet below the window at the end of the corridor had a bird on it that she did not look at.

## The Only Place In This City Where She Can Think

"Do you know what the rule is," she said. "Not the gift. The rule I have to live inside."

"That a room agrees with you."

"A room agrees with me about four seconds after I start and it agrees before it has thought about it, and two people in that room who have known each other for twenty years will find that they agree about something neither of them has ever raised, and by the end of the week neither of them will have any idea that it happened." She said it fast and flatly, the way a person recites something they have had to recite to a doctor.

"And I have never once worked out how to switch it off and I have stopped trying, and I did test it nine times and then an empty church and then my own kitchen at four in the morning, and it happened in all eleven, and I have not tried since."

"So why the corridor."

"Because I do not want it to happen and because I want to think. Both. In that order." She tipped her head at the wall she was leaning on.

"It has not happened in this corridor in about six years. It is the only place left. There is a fire door at the end and four doors and two of those four are store rooms and one is the bar and one is the box office and there is nobody behind any of them at half past three in the afternoon on a day I am not singing."

## Sums

Then she started doing sums out loud, which is what she does, and which is the reason she told seven other women which pair of hands were behind two documents made inside a month, in a kitchen, in January.

"Four doors, two store rooms, a bar, a box office. Nine steps. Eleven bulbs in a mirror upstairs and eight of them lit because three of them have been out since the spring and the man who changes them comes in the morning and not at night."

"Two hundred and eleven people past the Vestry arcade doors in a day. Nine hundred seats in that house and about four hundred in them and about two hundred on the Row in the wet. Twenty pence a verse at a box office that keeps no names, which means that in nine years not one person can be traced by what they bought off me, and I want that on a wall somewhere."

"That is arithmetic and none of it is an opinion."

"Nobody agrees with arithmetic. That is the whole of it." She pushed off the wall. "You can check arithmetic. You cannot agree with it. Nine women have been sitting at a kitchen table since the fourteenth of April and not one of them has ever once agreed with me about anything, and that is not because they are unkind. It is because it is a table and I only ever do sums at it."

## Nine Seconds

"So a table agrees with nothing," she said, "and a corridor agrees with nothing, and those are the two places I can think in, and I have about one of them and half of the other, and there is a third one which is a room with nine hundred people in it four nights a week, and that one agrees with everything I have ever said."

She stopped walking.

"I have had nine seconds in my mouth since the third of January."

He did not say anything.

"It is nine seconds long. It is not a song anybody else has written and it is on a nursery-count tune that every woman over about forty in this city has in her, and it does one thing." She put her hand flat on the fire door's bar, which was not painted, only the wall around it was.

"On the third of January I said a sentence out loud in that kitchen in front of seven people, and the sentence was that you were the reason I was frightened, and that you had done nothing to cause it, and I worked out inside about four seconds of putting my cup down that there were nine seconds of tune in this city that would end that sentence properly."

"And it has been in your mouth since the third of January."

"It has been in my mouth since the third of January. It is not that one, by the way, the one that went out in October. That one went out properly and I have never once regretted it and it is not this and it is not the same nine seconds and I am not confusing the two of them in front of you."

"What did you do with the nine seconds."

"I set them for the first of April." She turned round. "I said to myself in January: the first of April, because the quarter turns, and it is the first day of anything. And then the first of April came round and there were nine women at a table and a sheet on it and a man asking for a thing that could not be given back, and it was not the first of April any more. It was a different day with a different thing on it."

"So it is waiting for another date."

"It is waiting for me to stop moving it." She took her hand off the bar. "That is the truth and it took me four months to get to it. I have not been waiting for a day. I have been putting the day off every time the day got here, and there is no version of that where I am a woman being patient."

## Nine Seconds In A Corridor

"And I have been standing in this corridor since the second of January trying to find a way to do it that lands on nobody," she said, "and there is no way to do it without hurting anybody, and the only thing that stops it being the worst version is that I am not going to sing it to a room of nine hundred."

He said, "Nadia."

"Nadia has a table." She said the name without any weight at all.

"Nadia has got a kitchen table and eleven people through that door in a week and a mother-in-law's brother who cannot hold his tea, and if I sing nine seconds in that kitchen every woman in it agrees with all of it and none of them will remember it by the next morning."

"Nadia Ferrous will not be the person who stops it, because Nadia Ferrous cannot stop agreeing with a room and it is not a thing she does. A table does not do it and a corridor does not do it and the only place it does is a room."

"So here."

"Here." She was already looking at the two shut store-room doors. "Two of these are stores and they are locked and the bar is on the ground floor at this hour and the box office has got a woman in it who has been counting my house since the autumn and who is called Ada and who would take the whole thing as a favour and would be ruined by it inside a week."

"You have counted them."

"I have counted them every week for about six years and I have never once used the count for anything and I would like to say that I have never used it for anything."

She sang it.

## It Went Down The Corridor

He had heard her four times in this city and had never once been in a room while she did it, and there was no other way to describe what it was like than to say that she was not performing and the corridor was not a room and both of those were true and neither of them helped.

Nine seconds.

It was on a nursery-count tune and it was about nine seconds long and it did exactly one thing, and what it did was end the sentence she had started in a kitchen in January, and it did it so completely that the corridor it was standing in was not the same corridor afterwards.

Then the bar went quiet and the bird on the awning came back.

Nothing.

## What Came Back Up The Stair

She stood with her hand flat on the bar and her head slightly down and she was breathing harder than she had been four seconds before, which was the other half of the gift and the half that nobody in Ostara had ever once thought to ask her about, because it is exhausting and it is local and it is impossible to turn off.

"No," she said. "No, nothing. Nothing came back up the stair. Nothing in this corridor. Not the stores, not the bar, not the box office, not the front, not nine hundred seats, not the Row."

"Is that the good version or the bad version."

"I do not know." She said it without any performance in it at all. "That is the honest answer and I have given you about nine of them in four months and every one of them has been about a thing I could not control and this is the first one about a thing I did."

He understood then, and it took about four seconds, and he felt it go through him the way a cold goes through a hand.

Somewhere in a building within about two hundred yards of a fire door in Bellhouse Row, in a room with the door shut and people in it, a room had agreed with nine seconds that it had not been in.

"It was local," said Isolde Marchetti. "It has always been local. That is the entire shape of it and I have built about nineteen years on the word local and I have just found out that it is not a distance. It is a fence, and I put it where the walls are, and nobody has ever shown me where the walls are, and it turns out they are not the building."

"Who is in a room at half past three on the twenty-ninth of April that could have heard it."

"I do not know. I have got four hundred people through that house in a week and about two thousand through the front of it in a month and there is no door in Bellhouse Row that I can tell you is shut." She took her hand off the bar. "That is what I have got instead of a ninth door I could count."

She went down the nine steps with her coat over her arm and stopped at the bottom of them, in the yard, where the bird was still on the awning and the room was a room and there were eleven people at the door.

"Lin Hao."

"Yes."

"Four months in my mouth and no idea at all who is holding it now, and that is the first time in nineteen years I have put anything into a room that I could not find again in a fortnight." She put her coat on.

"I am not sorry. Do not ask me if I am sorry, because I am going to be extremely unpleasant about it for about a week and then I am going to want to do it again."

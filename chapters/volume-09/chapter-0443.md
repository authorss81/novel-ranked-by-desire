# The Requisition In His Own Answer

The clerk of the room at the end of the corridor had a printed sheet out on the second table under the window on the morning of the seventeenth of June, and it was the first time in ninety days she had put one out for him, and he stood at the end of it for a while before he understood what it was.

"It is one hundred and seventeen days since the twentieth of February," he said, because the date had to be said somewhere. "Eight from twenty leaves eight, with thirty-one and thirty and thirty-one and seventeen makes one hundred and seventeen."

"Thirteen days to the thirtieth," said the clerk. "Thirty taken from seventeen leaves thirteen. Sit down, I have had this out since eight."

He sat down. She turned the sheet so that it faced him, and it was not a form for a requisition and it was not a form for an answer either; it was a form for the minute, which is a different animal again, and she had laid it out flat with four ruled lines at the foot of it for four seals.

## The Form With No Box In It

The head of the sheet carried the premises, then the holding, then the ground, then the subject, and after the subject there was nothing at all.

"There is no box," he said.

"There is no box," said the clerk. "I have looked at it for four minutes this morning and there is no box on it for what the subject says, and there is not going to be one, and here is why. A minute of the carrying carries five seals. Four ruled lines for four of them, and the fifth line is the subject's and it goes in under his own hand."

"There is no box for his words because a clerk may only enter and cannot correct, and if a man wrote his own words on this sheet I would have to enter them exactly as written and I could not do one single thing about them afterwards, and no clerk in this city will be the woman who put a man's own words into a book."

"Then what goes in it."

"Whose holding, whose ground, and the answer," said the clerk. "Three lines and five seals and the seals are the part that costs."

## A Minute That Says His

He turned the sheet around again and looked at the three lines with nothing ruled under them.

"If it says the ground is mine, it is a sentence that can be read against anybody."

"It is," she said. "A minute that says the ground belongs to a man says it about a piece of paper that was made against his front door on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October at about eleven by a clerk called Imrie on four words he said in a kitchen."

"In four years there will be a stranger in a chair with a folder and he will read that line and he will want to know what paper the man was talking about, because a man who says the ground is his is a man claiming an advantage and a man claiming an advantage has to be asked where it came from."

"And the answer to that is not on this sheet."

"The answer to that is what a subject says with his mouth in a room, once, and then it is written down by a clerk and goes out at four with everything else." She put her thumb on the blank space under the word *ground*.

"If you write it on this, it is your handwriting in a minute and it is the same thing and it is worse, because a clerk's hand is a clerk's business and a man's own words in a minute is a man who wanted to be asked."

## The Only Way To Identify It

He did not answer for a while. The court at the end of the corridor had the sound of it in it, carts and one bell, and he had been in and out of this building more times since the fifteenth of April than in the nine months before it.

"There is no other way to name it," he said at last. "Is there."

"No," said the clerk.

"A description of those premises, made against the head of those premises, on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October at about eleven, by a clerk called Imrie, on four words said by me in a kitchen on the same morning. That is what it is."

"And if I say that in that room on the thirtieth of June then a stranger in four years knows there were four words and that they were mine and that they were said out loud in a building with a clerk in it. And if I do not say it, then the ground is a description and a description does not have an author and a description cannot be answered for."

"That is correct and it is the whole of the difficulty and I have had it in front of me since the start of the week."

"You told me on the fifteenth not to say them in this corridor."

"I told you on the fifteenth not to say them in this corridor," said the clerk. "I said it because a corridor is where people stand about and because I did not want to be the woman who had it on her floor. I said it for my own floor and not for the city and I am not going to pretend otherwise to you now that you have put it in front of me like that."

## He Said Them

He stood up because it was easier than sitting.

"Because it is cheaper," he said.

The corridor went on being a corridor. Somebody went along it with a tray.

The clerk took the pen out of her hand and put it down, and then picked it up again, and wrote on the back of a slip in the drawer and not on the printed sheet, and she wrote slowly, and she did not ask him to repeat it, and when she had finished she signed it and turned it round and pushed it along the table towards him the way she pushed things along the table, without ceremony.

He had said them.

They were in a book now.

Nobody could take them out.

"That is the second book," she said. "The first is the description, in her hand, made on the twenty-seventh of October. This is the second, in mine, made this morning on a slip in a drawer in a room with four seals on the sheet behind it. Mrs Imrie may not be asked a single question about that entry, ever, by anybody, including you, including anybody who comes after you with a folder."

"And now there are four words of yours on a line in a second book and they are not hers, and in four years a stranger who has read both will know that the woman who wrote the description was transcribing a man in a kitchen, and there is no version of this in which she is only writing down the weather."

## What It Cost Him To Ask

"You were told on the thirteenth of May that a woman in that building had worked out what I decided in May," he said. "I neither confirmed it nor denied it."

"You did not, and it has held, and it holds today." She put the slip in the drawer with the one from the ninth of May. "What you have done this morning is not what you swore on the ninth of May that you would never do. You did not make this room need anything."

"This room was going to need an answer on the thirtieth whether you came or not, and I was going to enter a line with nothing under it, and you have brought me something to enter. That is a different act and I will not have it confused with the other one, and neither will you, because the day you let yourself think those two are the same thing is the day you start doing the other one on purpose."

"Then what have I done."

"You have made yourself legible," said the clerk. "That is all. In ninety days you have done one thing that cannot be undone and you did it with a pen in a queue in about ninety seconds and it cost eight women their names, and this morning you have done the second one and it cost a clerk who did not ask a woman she is forbidden to ask anything. Both of them are yours now."

"Neither of them can be given back and neither of them is the same as the other."

## What Standing Up Is Made Of

He had been told that he would stand. He had not been told what standing was made of, and it turned out to be about eleven inches of oak and a habit.

"The fifth seat," said the clerk, "is the second chair on the left as you come in, and it is four inches lower than the other eight because it was made for a man who was not a member and has never been repaired. You will stand on the inside of the table with your hand on the back of it. That is the shape of it."

"You do not step forward and you do not raise your voice and you do not address the chair; you answer into the room, and the room will take it."

"How long do I stand there."

"As long as it takes to say what you have come to say. There is no time against it and nobody will count you, and if you stand there for nine minutes the room will sit through nine minutes, because a member who has been called is not a member who has finished." She moved the printed sheet about half an inch. "You will have a seal in your hand."

"It is not given you at the door, it comes off the tray with the other four when your name is read, and you put it in front of you on the table and you do not use it until the minute is made, and when it is made you put it down beside the minute and that is the fifth, and it is yours and you did not choose it and you cannot give it back."

"Do they take it off me afterwards."

"No," said the clerk. "A member who lodges a consignment holds his seal for as long as the consignment stands and the consignment is entered in the keeping book on the day it was lodged and is not entered out on any day. You will carry that piece of brass out of that room at four o'clock in the afternoon of the thirtieth of June and you will carry it into every room in this city for the rest of your life."

## The Words He Will Use

He said them out then, standing at the table with his hand on the back of a chair that was not there, and the clerk stopped him in the first sentence.

"That is what you have written in your own hand about nine hundred times," she said, "and it is a man being correct. Try the other way. Say it as though you are a man who has been asked a question by nine people and is going to give an answer that costs him something, because that is the man who is going to be standing there."

He stood there and got it wrong twice. The second time it came out of him short and plain, with no nouns in it at all, and the clerk wrote the date on the corner of a second slip and let it stand without writing the words down, because a slip in a drawer is not a minute and a rehearsal of a sentence that has not been given is nobody's business.

"That is the answer," she said. "It is thirty-one words and not one of them is a noun you could read to somebody in four years without hearing your own voice in the middle of it. You will say it at about two o'clock in the afternoon on the thirtieth of June, if the room gets to it, and four people will be in that room and one of them will not be able to hear a word of it."

He looked up.

"Wren Kallo is not on that roll," said the clerk. "I am not going to discuss it and neither are you."

## Thirteen Days

He stood at the table with his hands flat on it, the way he had stood at a table in a kitchen on the fourteenth of April with eight women in it.

"Does a slip go out at four," he said. "Like everything else from this room."

"A slip does not go out at four. A slip is not on the run. A slip is a piece of paper in a drawer in a room, and a clerk may keep it, and I may keep it, and in four years it may be in a bundle and it may be read by somebody who was told to read the bundle."

"And a minute goes out at four."

"A minute of the carrying carries five seals, one of them yours, and it names those premises four times, and it goes out at four to seven desks, and six of those seven have a woman from your address at them." She put both hands flat on the printed sheet, which was the only time in ninety days he had seen her do that. "Your four words will be sitting underneath all four of the namings. Not in her hand. In yours."

"Out in the open at four o'clock in the afternoon of the thirtieth of June."

The court was loud for a second and then it was not.

"Thirteen days," he said.

"Thirteen days," said the clerk, "and it is safe in that drawer until the room sits, and it stops being safe at the moment the minute is made, and I am not going to be able to help you with any of it, and you did not ask me to."

He straightened the sheet on the table so that its corners were square, because it was the only thing in the room he had ever been allowed to touch.

"Then I will answer for myself," he said, "on the thirtieth, and I will not make this room need one word of it."

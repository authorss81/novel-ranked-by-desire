# The Fourth Seat Fills And Empties

Hanneke Ruis was at the rail outside the room at the end of the corridor at about ten past nine on the morning of the twenty-fifth of June with Solveig Rask and an armful of a book on a chain, and the book had to be laid out on a table in that room on the thirtieth because the quarterly return is drafted from the real book and there is no other table in this quarter long enough for it.

"It is one hundred and twenty-five days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and twenty-five makes one hundred and twenty-five."

"Five days," said Hanneke Ruis. "Thirty taken from twenty-five leaves five. And do not stand in the doorway of that room with your hands behind you, you look like a man waiting for a bus."

## A Keeper Cannot Sit In A Member's Chair

She had the chain in both hands and Solveig Rask had a cloth and a bundle of clean sheets and there was nowhere in the corridor to put any of it.

"Solveig carries it up on the thirtieth at about half past eight and it goes on the table and it stays on the table until the room seals, because the quarterly is drafted off the real book and not off anything anybody has said it was," said Hanneke Ruis. "I do the drafting. She carries."

"Neither of us can sit down when it is on the table, because a keeper is not a member of that room and a member's chair is a member's chair, and I have been told so twice by a clerk who was extremely polite about it."

"There is a chair at that table that is not a member's chair."

"There is a chair at that table that is not a member's chair," said Hanneke Ruis, "and it is Alma Quayle's, and she sits in it, and I have not asked her about it and neither have you."

## The Clerk Sent For A Man

The clerk came out of the room at about a quarter to ten and asked whether anybody knew where Bertram Sallow was, which she had asked twice before in that corridor and got no answer, and Hanneke Ruis said that the clerk had better write to him, and the clerk said no.

"The list for the sitting is made out of this room on the thirtieth," she said, "and every standing in it is read out once, in order, and a member whose standing is read may attend and answer one thing about it and may not answer a second thing. His has been read aloud in that room five times since the first of April and he has not been in it once. I am going to send for him."

"You are going to send for a member on the twenty-fifth."

"I am going to send for a member with a runner at noon and he will be here tomorrow morning at the earliest, and if he does not come I will read his standing on the thirtieth without him, which is what happens anyway."

Hanneke Ruis said, "Then he will come," in a way that meant she had understood the whole of the instruction inside about four seconds, and went back to her book.

## He Came At Nine

He came at nine in the morning on the twenty-sixth and he came early, which the clerk noted and did not comment on, and he was wearing a coat that had been bought for something and not for coming down a corridor at nine in the morning, and he took it off over the chair at the end of the table and hung it on the back of it.

Bertram Sallow had not been inside that room since the sixth of December.

He stood in the doorway for about four seconds, looking at nine chairs and a table and a window, and then he came and sat down in the fourth seat, which is on the right of the chair that is not a member's, and which had carried his name in the weekly return since the seventh of April, when a clerk wrote it there because a clerk may only enter.

The room stood at five before he came into it and the room stood at five with him in it.

## What He Said He Had Been Doing

"You were re-read at the valuation of the first of February," the clerk said, from behind the second table. "That is on the register. A man with no standing cannot hold a seal, and after the second of February you had no standing, and you did not come, and it was not written down anywhere, because nothing required it to be."

"I did not come because I did not have a seal to come with."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is the answer to it," said Bertram Sallow. "Five months. I sat outside this door twice and both times a clerk came out and told me there was no business and I went away, and I have been a man who is not in a room for five months and it turns out that is not a thing a man can be once a month in a city this size."

"I went to the hall twice a day for five months and nobody said anything to me, and I want you to understand that being left alone was the entire punishment and it worked."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"And on the first of April you were restored by a vote of a Council that had not sat since the twenty-ninth of December," said the clerk, "and it was entered over the line you stand on, and it has been read aloud five times since, and you did not come to any of them."

"Yes."

## The Item Of Business

She worked through the preparation for the sitting for ninety minutes with him in the chair, which is the ordinary way that room is made ready, and at about half past ten she came to the requisition on the list and read it into the room the way it will be read on the thirtieth, in order, with the date first.

Bertram Sallow listened with his head slightly down.

Then he said, "The fourth part."

"There is no part of that you may ask me."

"There are four parts and you have read three of them," said Bertram Sallow. "I have sat in this chair for ninety minutes and you have read a date and a holding and a ground and a subject and you stopped before you got to the fourth, and I am not asking you a question about a name and I am not asking you a question about a space in a witness line, and I am asking you what the fourth part is."

The clerk looked at him for about two seconds.

"The fourth part is the premises," she said. "Thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight. It is a description made against the head of those premises on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October at about eleven, and it stands as the ground of the sheet, and it is not a person and it is not a name and there is nothing in it that is anybody's business but the subject's."

"That is the first time I have been told," said Bertram Sallow.

"Yes."

## The Fact Left The Room

He sat with it for about half an hour and did not say anything else, and at about ten past eleven he stood up and put his coat on and went out, and the room stood at five with him gone, exactly as it had stood at five an hour and a half before.

That is the whole of it and it is the thing the clerk wrote nothing down about: a seat can fill and empty nine times inside a morning and the number in the corner of a weekly return does not move at all, because a standing is not a body in a chair, it is a line in a book, and the line was written on the seventh of April by a clerk who may only enter and who has not been near it since.

What changed was that five people in a room now know that a sixth person outside the room knows what the fourth part is, and that a man who lost his standing in February and got it back by a vote in April has spent five months not coming because a city that says nothing to you is the punishment, and has now come, and has gone back out.

"He will be back on the thirtieth," said the clerk.

"How do you know."

"Because he came at nine instead of at ten and he took his coat off," said the clerk. "A man who has not sat in a chair for five months hangs his coat on the chair. A man who has come to do business keeps it on."

## In The Corridor Afterwards

He caught Sallow on the stair, which was the only way out of that building and was also the way up to the second and third floors, and the man stood at a landing in his good coat with his hands in his pockets looking as though he had come out of a room and was about to find out whether it had happened.

"Four of us in October," said Bertram Sallow. "Four of us in February, and I was not one of them, and the fifth was recused and had been recused since December. I want you to know that I know what the room does. I sat in it for nine years."

"It keeps four lines and it reads them and it seals and it sends, and in nine years I never once saw it refuse anything, and I would have told you that in October with my hand on the table."

"And now."

"Now there are five." He did not turn round. "There were four on the first of December and there were four on the first of February and the difference between them was me, and in February I was not one of them, and in April I was one of them again because a Council that had not sat for three months sat for eleven minutes."

"And now there are five and I am one of the five, and I would like it said somewhere, by anybody, in any building, that I do not know what the fifth is for."

He got to the landing above and stopped.

"I have been a rank for three months and a nobody for the two months before that and a rank for nine years before that," said Bertram Sallow.

"Not one person in this city has ever asked me which of those three I would rather be, and the answer is that a man who has been a nobody in a city of this size would rather go on being one than be a thing that is read aloud."

"And I am saying it out loud in a stairwell and not to a clerk, because I have worked that out about this city in five months of being stepped around by anybody holding a card."

Then he went up, because the second floor on that stair was Sun Meiling's and the third was Perrin Sowe's and neither of them had anything to do with him at all.

He stood on the landing and thought that a man had just told him, in a stairwell, on the twenty-sixth of June, that he would rather be a nobody than a rank, and that he had said it to the one person in the city who could have made that true by doing nothing at all, and that this was the second time in nine years somebody had handed him the truth about himself in a passage and walked off up the stairs.

## A Minute Is Not A Certificate

Hanneke Ruis was still on the corridor with the book in her arms and Solveig Rask had gone down to the rail with the cloth, and he stood with her for about twenty minutes because there was nowhere else to stand.

"The fourth block of that sheet takes a figure off a certificate of the composition of the address," said Hanneke Ruis. "The certificate is made four times a year and the quarter that begins on the first of July has not got one, because a form cannot be drafted without one and no one has drafted one, and there are five days of this quarter left."

"Then what goes in the block."

"A name, in my hand, out of my own memory, with nothing behind it." She shifted the book. "That is not a small thing and I am not going to be dramatic about it in a corridor. It means that on the thirtieth of June a keeper in this city writes down that premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight was kept by seven people, in her own hand, and the certificate that says it does not exist."

"In about four years the chain comes off that book, and the plate is scraped off its backing, and a new plate is cut with whatever is on the old one and no more, and it goes up in a case in a hall where nobody stands still, and after that there will be no way anybody has of telling which of them was the one that mattered."

"And the minute upstairs does not help."

"The minute upstairs does not help at all," said Hanneke Ruis. "A minute says that a man answered a requisition in a room on the thirtieth of June. A certificate says who was living in a house. They are not the same animal and it is not a confusion anybody makes, and I have been three days working out whether a minute can stand in for one and it cannot, and that is not a question I can put to a room."

She looked down the corridor at the door and then at the man in it.

"You go and be somewhere else until the thirtieth," said Hanneke Ruis. "There are five things on that day and you are the only one of them that can be got out of by standing somewhere else, and every hour you spend in this building I am one hour closer to writing a name on a piece of paper that nobody certified."

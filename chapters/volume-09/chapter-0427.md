# The Consult She Takes To A Board

The board of the Church of the Quiet Number sits on the first of the month, and the book a consult goes into closes on the day before it, and the first of June was twelve days away on the morning of the nineteenth of May.

Agnete Lindqvist had been standing outside her own door since about eight with a sheet of paper in her hand, and the reason she was standing outside it was that she was about to stop being a woman who stays in rooms.

"Eleven seconds," she said. "And I want you to stand there and let me get it right, because I have written this out four times and thrown three of them away."

He stood in the corridor.

"We are a body with no ranks, no reading and no register, and that has been the whole of the doctrine for twenty years and it is not going to be improved this month."

She looked at the sheet.

"A thing done and not counted is still done. A city cannot say the person who did it was worth nothing if it cannot say who it was. And we take about four hundred returns a quarter, and we have never asked for one of them, and we do not know what they are."

"You know what six of them are."

"I know what six of them are," said Agnete Lindqvist. "One is a woman who sleeps at my address and has been under a binding instrument for eleven years and cannot remember who put it there. One is a division on a sub-level that has just had a figure about a room put into it."

"And the sixth."

"And the sixth is us, and that has been true since the first of January, and it stopped being a figure on the ninth of May."

## Twenty Years Of Being One Of Six

"Tell me the twenty years."

"For twenty years the ordinary stream out of that room has gone to six desks and through a wing off a third court that nobody has ever named, and we have been one of the six the whole of the time."

She folded the sheet once.

"The reason we are one of the six is that a Church is a body that other offices keep returns about. That is all it has ever been. In twenty years not one person has ever asked us to say so out loud."

"And since the first of April."

"Since the first of April that room has stood up from three to four to five inside fourteen days. On the fifteenth of April a figure about it went out on the ordinary stream for the first time in twenty years, it went out carrying five, and it came to us."

She put a hand flat on the door.

"We keep no register, and nobody here ever asked for the return, and it has been sitting in a pigeonhole under the stair for four weeks while I have not known what to do with it."

"You have known what to do with it."

"I have known what to do with it since the ninth of May," said Agnete Lindqvist. "What I have to do with it is take a consult to a board."

She held the sheet up against the door.

"The consult goes in on the twenty-ninth of May at the latest. The book closes on the day before the board sits and the board sits on the first of the month. If it is not in by then it waits a quarter, and a quarter from now is the first of October, and by then this will have stopped being news in a way that makes it worse."

## Why A Board Is Not A Room

"You will get it in here," she said. "All of you. Eleven people, because eleven is what the board is, and it has been eleven for twenty years, and we do not put a man in it."

"It is your board."

"It is a board of this Church and it is made up of nine of the eleven thousand people in it. I do not choose it and I cannot unseat anybody on it, and I have never once in twenty years asked it anything at all."

She opened the door.

"That is because a body that has never been asked anything is the only kind of body in this city that cannot be made to do a thing. And there is one more thing about the room you are about to stand in, and I am telling you now because Petra gave you terms in a bay and Nadia gave you nothing at all in a car, and neither of those women lied to you, and I am not going to."

"In this room nobody can be compelled to testify."

"That is my gift and it has been my gift for eleven years and I have never once used it on anybody," said Agnete Lindqvist. "It is not a wall and it is not a charm. While I am in a room, no person in that room can be made to say a thing. Not by an officer, not by a board, not by a court, not by me."

She stood aside.

"So whatever eleven people say in there is said because they came to say it. When I ask this room a question I do not get the answer I wanted. I get the answer the room has."

## What She Asked

The eleven came in at about eleven o'clock and sat on eleven chairs that had been in that room for eleven years, and not one of them looked at the sheet.

"What I am asking is narrow and I want it narrow," she said. "I am not asking whether a room that can carry a paper ought to carry it. I have no standing to ask that and I would be refused in about four seconds and I would deserve to be."

"What are you asking."

"Whether this Church wishes to be counted as one of the places a room's paper goes to."

The room made no sound, which is what a room in which nobody can be compelled to testify sounds like when eleven people are all deciding at once.

"Answer it properly. This board can say yes or it can say no and it cannot be made to say either. If it says no then nothing goes in that book and I will carry the pigeonhole myself for another quarter and that is the end of it."

She let that sit.

"But if it says yes, there is a line in a book in this building saying that the Church of the Quiet Number has been asked, and has answered, and what it answered. The answer will be read out on the first of June in front of eleven people. There is no register of addresses in this Church, and there is no body in this city that can take a line out of a minute once it is in it."

"That is a lot for a pigeonhole."

"It is a very small thing for a pigeonhole," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I want you to sit down and let me finish. Because after they have answered, I am going to write a line in this Church's own book about the answer, and I am going to write the address of the premises in it, on my own account, and not on the household's and not on yours."

She put the sheet down.

"And the reason I am going to do that is that if I do not write it, then in four years somebody opens a file and finds that the Church has been receiving the returns of a room for twenty years and has never once been asked whether it wanted to. The answer to that will be that nobody thought of it, and there will be a line in this book with the address of my own house on it and nobody will have said yes to anything."

Eleven people said yes.

It took about ninety seconds, and there was no vote and no show of hands and no minutes taken by anybody, because a body that has never been asked anything does not know how to be careful in the way a body that has been asked a great deal is careful.

## What It Cost

"You have not been outside this room in eleven years."

"No."

"And you are going to have to be outside it four times between now and the first of June."

"I have been out of that door twice in eleven years and both times I was carried."

She put the sheet down on the table.

"That is what it costs. Not the line. The line is cheap and it is correct and it will say what it says. The cost is that I asked eleven people a question in a room where I could not make them answer it, and they answered it, and I will not know for the rest of my life whether they would have said yes in a room where I could have pushed."

"That is a strange thing to be frightened of."

"It is the only thing in eleven years I have been frightened of," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and you did not expect to hear that from a woman who is about to walk into her own kitchen and say a thing out loud that four people already know."

## What She Said In The Kitchen

She said it at about half past five that evening with her coat still on, standing up, the way she had stood in the corridor for about an hour and twenty minutes.

"The Church has been asked a question and it has answered it and it is going in a book on the first of June with the address on it. I put it. Not the household and not him. Me, on my own account, because I am the chaplain of a body of eleven thousand people and I did not ask them first."

That was the whole of it. She did not explain the room. She did not mention that no person in it could have been made to answer her, and she was never going to mention it, and there was one woman at that table who would have understood it inside four seconds and had said nothing at all about it since January.

"You should have asked us."

"I did not, and that is correct," said Agnete Lindqvist. "If you want the reason: a house of eight women cannot make a board of eleven thousand people do a thing. A house of eight women can only make a room of five do a thing, and I have spent four days watching a man work out that the second one is the only one there is and put it down."

She sat down at last.

"There is a date on it. The twenty-ninth of May. If it is not in the book by then it waits a quarter, and a quarter is the first of October, and the Council sits on the thirtieth of June and does not sit again until then."

She squared her hands on the table.

"So there is a date, and nobody in this city has ever had a date about a thing like this before, and every one of you knows exactly what it is going to cost to make it. I have not asked you for a penny of it and I am not going to."

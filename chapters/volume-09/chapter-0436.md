# A Correction She Has Not Filed

The clerk of the room at the end of the corridor keeps a drawer of slips she has written and not filed, and on the morning of the fifth of June she had one of them on the second table under the window with her hand on it, and he saw it because she did not move her hand away.

"It is one hundred and six days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, with thirty and thirty and thirty and five makes one hundred and six."

"It is also twenty-five days to the thirtieth," said the clerk. "Thirty taken from five leaves twenty-five. I have kept this book for eleven years and I count it the same way every morning."

## The Slip

The slip was in her own hand and it was correct. It said the entry of the ninth of May was entered on the ninth of May, and that the sheet of the twentieth of February is in the keeping of the room and is not carried, and it gave the address at the end of it, and it was dated the fifth of June.

"It does not correct anything," said the clerk. "A clerk may only enter and cannot correct. This is an entry. It says the line of the ninth is where it is and that I put it there on my own, without instruction."

"Then file it."

"I have not filed it." She kept her hand on it. "If I file it there are two lines in the keeping book about one sheet, eleven hundred feet from two words in two other books that nobody can read, and in four years somebody will ask why the second line was needed."

"Was it needed."

"It is needed the way a second lock is needed." The clerk looked out of the window. "The minute of the twentieth of February carries a sentence that stopped being true on the thirtieth of March. I read it out on the fifteenth of May. Three seals. Laid, called, served and heard. Not carried. Nobody can strike it. A second entry does not touch it either, and that is why I wrote it."

## Whose Future Pays

He sat down on the bench at the end of the corridor, which she had once sat down on herself, and did not touch the table.

"If you file it you pay for it," he said.

"A room that is made to need something is paid for by a woman out of her own future," said the clerk. "You worked that out on the ninth of May at about ten past five and you decided never to use it. I have watched you keep it for twenty-seven days. This slip is mine. You did not ask for it and you are not going to be told I paid."

"Then do not file it."

"I am not filing it." She put the slip back in the drawer and shut it. "That is the whole of this chapter in this building. A correction that exists and is correct and has not been filed, and the cost of filing it is somebody else's besides mine, because a bundle that goes into the keeping on the thirtieth of this month would carry it to seven desks at four."

She squared the bundle on the table under the window.

"Somebody going through the keeping in four years will want to know why a book has one line about a sheet in May and nothing else. The answer will be that one line was enough. If I file this there will be two lines and he will ask why two were needed, and the answer will be that a woman in this corridor was frightened."

"I am frightened," he said.

"You are not the woman in this corridor." The clerk stood up. "Go and be frightened somewhere else. I have a book and I am going to write up what the room does when it sits, and the room does not sit until the thirtieth."

## What Stays In The Drawer

He went to the door and stopped.

"Will you file it after the thirtieth."

"Anything you say to me after the thirtieth of June goes into no book until the first of July." She did not turn round. "That is not my rule. That is the rule of the floor and the rule of the book. Ask me on the first."

## The Bundle That Goes At Four

The clerk squared the bundle again, though it was already square, and he recognised the gesture because Hanneke Ruis does it at a rail two floors under the Exchange and Beatrix Halm does it with a notebook on a ledge.

"At four this goes out on the ordinary stream to seven desks," she said. "Nine ruled lines and a tenth with a box. Five standings in it. The seventh has stood at five since the fifteenth of April. It will come back in the morning with nothing on it or with something on the back of it in pencil, and either way it goes into the keeping at the end of the month."

"And the slip stays in the drawer."

"The slip stays in the drawer." She rested her hand on the drawer front. "A correction that exists and is correct and has not been filed is not a secret. It is a thing I have written and not sent. In four years, if I am still at this table, I will be able to say I wrote it on the fifth of June and held it, and the book will show I held it, because the drawer is mine and the date is on it."

He looked at the window. A runner was crossing the court below with a sheet up an outside stair.

"You are angry," said the clerk. It was not a question.

"I am tired of watching women pay for a room I put at five."

"Then be tired." She said it without cruelty. "You lodged at ten minutes to nine on the fifteenth of April with a pen, in a queue, with no witness. The boy at the counter did not ask what he was holding. The fifth went into the book at about ten past nine. A requisition laid on the twentieth of February became capable that morning and has not been carried on any day since. Every paper since has been somebody else writing down what that morning made true."

She opened the drawer an inch and shut it again.

"Go. I have a book and the room does not sit until the thirtieth, and I am going to write up what it does when it sits."

He went out. The slip stayed in the drawer, correct and unfiled, and the keeping book kept its single line of the ninth of May, and the minute kept its sentence that had been untrue since the thirtieth of March, and none of the three could be struck by anybody including the man they were about.

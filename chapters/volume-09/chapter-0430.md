# The Calendar Is The Only Door

There is a board at the end of the corridor off the second landing with the day's runs chalked on it in a hand that goes up at the end of every line, and at about ten past ten on the morning of the twenty-fourth of May a woman was at it with a box of chalk.

She does it the way she has done it every morning for four years, which is to say quickly, and in order, and without stopping to read anything she had written the day before.

Hester Vaile had been at that board for four years and had never once been asked a question about it by anybody, and she told him that, and she told it before he had got his coat off, because she had noticed him on four other mornings and had worked out what he was.

"You are not here about the runs."

"I am here about the bottom of it."

"Then you want to stop looking at the bottom of it," said Hester Vaile, "because everybody who comes down this corridor ends up looking at the bottom of it and I chalk over it every morning and it comes back in the afternoon and I have stopped thinking about it."

## Ten Lines On A Board

The board was about five feet by three and it had ten lines ruled across it in paint, and Hester Vaile chalked nine of them every morning with what was on the run, and the ninth one had four things on it and the seventh had one.

The tenth line had nothing on it.

"It is the same board the whole time?" he said.

"It is the same board and the same paint and it was painted before I came, and the bottom line has been empty for four years and I chalk nine lines and I have never once put anything on the tenth."

"What is it for."

"Nobody has ever told me what it is for," said Hester Vaile. "I asked, when I started. I was told it was for the day the room sits. And the room has not sat since I came, so I have never had to find out, and every morning I chalk nine lines and leave the tenth and every morning the afternoon looks the same."

He looked at the ninth line and then at the tenth.

"There is no box on it," he said.

"There is a box on the end of it," said Hester Vaile. "Same as the others. The box is where the number goes, or the time, or nothing, and on the ninth one it is four and on the sixth it is one and on the fifth it is nothing, and on the tenth one it is nothing every morning."

## The Quarter Ahead In Paint

Under the ten lines, and under a strip of board about two inches deep, there was a line of paint that was not part of the board's working and never had been.

It was not chalk. It was paint, and it was a different hand from the ruling, and it was small and level and it had a date at the end of it.

*The Council. The thirtieth of June.*

"There," said Hester Vaile.

He had seen it before. He had walked past the bottom of that board eleven times in seven weeks, on the fourth of May and the sixth and the ninth and on a great many ordinary mornings, and he had read the top nine lines every time and had never once got his eyes to the bottom, and neither had anybody else, and he understood within about four seconds exactly why, and it was not because he was slow.

"Paint," he said.

"Paint, and it is repainted every quarter by a man from the works who does it on the first day of it and does it in about nine minutes, and I have watched him do it three times and he does not touch it while I am standing here, and I have never once asked him why, because I have worked out that the answer is that it is painted on the first and there is nothing to ask after that."

"It has not moved since the first of April."

"It has not moved since the first of April because it does not move. It goes on the first day of a quarter and it stays there for the whole of the quarter and it comes off when the next one goes on over the top of it."

She put the chalk back in the box.

"That is a quarter ahead. That is what a Council sitting is. You do not call one when you want one. You enter it on a calendar a quarter before it and then you turn up."

## The Only Door In The City

"So that is the door."

"That is the only door," said Hester Vaile. "Four years at this board, and I am a woman who chalks, and I will tell you exactly what I know about it. The room at the end of this corridor can do about eleven things and every one of them happens inside the room. The Council can do about forty and every one of them happens in a room with nine chairs in it and a window."

"And you have read the minute."

"I have read the minute," said Hester Vaile, "and I will tell you the thing in it that put me in front of the plate office three years ago, and then I am going to chalk and you can stand there or go away."

She took the chalk out again and turned it over in her fingers without marking anything.

"About nine weeks ago, when that room went from three to four, a man read through a list of decisions and found the room had gone up one seat in about three days, and found nothing in the list that said why."

She put the chalk down.

"And it did not say why because there was no why in it. There was a standing restored and a date, and a restored standing gets entered over the line it stands on. That is what a minute is for. It is a list of what happened and it is written by a person who is not asked to write a reason."

"You are telling me the Council reads a list."

"The Council reads a list of decisions and a list of standings and it does not read anything else, and it cannot read anything else, because the face of a Council paper gives a reason nowhere to go and has always given it nowhere to go."

She shut the chalk box.

"So if you want a Council to do something in this quarter, the only thing you can do about it is make sure the list says the thing you want it to say. And the list is made in this building, out of the room at the end of this corridor, by nine people, and one of them is you."

He did not answer for about half a minute.

"That is what I have been doing since the fifteenth of April," he said.

"You have."

"Not on purpose. On the fifteenth of April I stood at a public post off a third court with a pen and I made that room capable of carrying a sheet, and everything since has been the list filling itself up."

"Yes," said Hester Vaile. "That is what a quarter is for."

## The Tenth Line

They stood there for a while. Down the corridor, about eleven steps away, the second sub-level had a door shut behind it and a wire basket on a desk under it, and further along there was a room with nine chairs in it and eight of them had nothing in front of them.

"There is a return that goes out of that room at four every week," he said. "Nine ruled lines, and then a tenth line carrying a box at the end of it."

"I know that return. It comes down past me at four and I put it on the run to the seventh."

"And the seventh of those nine lines is standing at five, and it has been since the fifteenth of April, and it is the first time in twenty years that any figure about that room has gone anywhere, and this morning it came back up the chain with something written on the back of it in pencil."

Hester Vaile put the chalk box down on the ledge under the board.

"Then somebody has answered it," she said.

"Somebody has written on it. Not answered it. There is nowhere on that paper to answer it and she wrote on the back because the back of it is not part of it."

"That is a thing I have never had to think about," said Hester Vaile. "Four years of chalking nine lines every morning of my life, and I should never have thought of it in a hundred years."

"A woman on a tram worked it out in four seconds on the twenty-second of May and sent it back up the chain, and it goes into the keeping at the end of this month, and nothing in this city can put a line back down once it is in."

She picked the box up again.

"It is the same shape as the board," she said. "Ten lines and a tenth carrying a box, nine of them filled every morning and the tenth empty, and neither the tenth line on the board nor the tenth line on that return has anything in it, because nobody has ever had a reason to put anything in either of them and there is nowhere to put it."

"Yes."

"On my first morning I asked what the tenth line was for and I was told it was for the day the room sits," said Hester Vaile. "And it has never sat since I came. And the Council is painted along the bottom of this board for the thirtieth of June, which is the last working day of the quarter and is thirty-six days from now, and it is the last day anybody in this city can change that room."

She turned back to the board and put the chalk on the first line.

"Thirty-six days," she said. "And a quarter of that is already gone and it has not gone on anybody, which is the part you are not going to be able to put in a list."

## Thirty-Six Days And One Date

He went down sixty-one steps and out into the court and stood in it.

The requisition was ninety-four days old. It had been capable of being carried for thirty-nine days and had not been carried on one of them, and there were thirty-six days left in the quarter.

On every single one of those thirty-six days a room of five could carry it. On every single one of those thirty-six days a Council could restore a standing, take one away, or lift a recusal, if a Council were sitting. It was not going to sit until the thirtieth of June, which was the last of the thirty-six.

The calendar was the only door in the city and it was painted on a board by a man from the works who did it in nine minutes, and it opened on a list.

And the list was made out of a room at the end of a corridor, and that room had stood at five since the fifteenth of April.

A woman had said on the seventh of May, at a kitchen table, in front of eight people, that in about nine weeks somebody who is not cruel and is simply working would go through a list and read that a room stood at five for fifty-three days holding a sheet that was never carried — and would read that as a decision.

He stood in the court with the sun on one side of it and worked out that the door and the thing coming through it were the same object, and that there was no other door, and that a man who wanted the Council to do anything on the thirtieth of June could only do it by making sure the list said it.

He had sworn on the ninth of May, in a corridor, at about ten past five, that he was never going to use the only thing he had.

It turned out to have two ends.

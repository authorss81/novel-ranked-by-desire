# Nine Women And A Witness Line

There were eight women at that table on the evening of the third of May and a man, and Beatrix Halm could not hear a single one of them.

That was the ordinary condition of that kitchen on an ordinary evening and it had been the condition of it for about two years, and she had got very good at it, and it meant that she talked with her hands and watched four mouths at a time and did not attempt to follow anybody who was not in front of her.

What she could do instead was feel it. Forty thousand people moving on the Ferrier line and everything else on the wires out of the arcade, and a tram stopped anywhere in this quarter put a change through her like a hand laid flat on the back of her neck, and a kitchen at eight in the evening on Ferrier Street was about nine hundred moving movements a minute with a stove in it.

She put her hand flat on the table at about half past eight and waited for the quiet, and the quiet did not come, and at about twenty past nine she said it anyway.

## Thirteen Days Of Not Saying It

"There is something wrong with a corridor in this city," said Beatrix Halm, "and it has been wrong since about four o'clock on the afternoon of the twentieth of April, and I have been not saying it for thirteen days, and I am saying it now because it has got to the stage where not saying it is a thing I am doing on purpose."

The kitchen did not stop.

"A sheet went down off this address at four on the afternoon of the twentieth with seven names on it and a space in the middle of it." She was looking at the table and not at anybody.

"It went down the ordinary chain in the ordinary way and it has not come back, and nothing has come up that corridor in thirteen days, and in this house everything that goes down a chain comes back up it inside four days, and I have watched this house for nine years and I am telling you that this one has not come back."

"What does not coming back mean," said Runa Sato.

"I do not know what it means. I know what it is." Beatrix turned her hand over on the table and put it back the other way up.

"I know how the lines go and I know what a line weighs and I know that is the only sentence I have ever been able to say that was true. And that is not a return and that is not a correction and there is not one thing a sheet off a house does in this city thirteen times out of fifteen that is that."

She did not open the notebook, and she had not been asked to and was not going to be.

## What Two Documents Make Between Them

Runa Sato had a sheet of paper out by then and had ruled it herself and had not written anything on it, which was her way of saying she was listening harder than anybody else at the table.

"Two documents," she said. "Say them out loud and do not add anything to them."

"One roll," said Beatrix Halm. "Nine names, taken at a hall on a walled road on the second of April, with a figure in a column that is fifteen under a band, and nobody can correct it and nobody can take it back."

"And one line."

"One line. Seven names and a space, made at this table on the evening of the eighteenth, about a holding kept at this address, and it went down the chain on the twentieth."

"Right." Runa Sato wrote two lines on her ruled sheet and did not put anything next to either of them. "Now here is the part nobody in this room has said out loud. I have been sitting on it since about eleven o'clock while I was washing up. You are going to get it once from me and then I am going to leave this table."

"Nadia," said Wren Kallo.

"It is all of us." She put the pen down. "A roll binds a household by the highest rank in it and the binding went into that column on the fifteenth of April and there is no way in this city to put it back down."

"A consignment is witnessed at the address of the holding. A witness line names the people who were in a room on a date. A woman named on a line about a holding kept at an address is an officer of that holding, and an officer of a holding is a person that room can serve anything on."

"It has been on the table since the eighteenth," said Sun Meiling, without looking up.

"Yes," said Runa Sato. "It has. And the person who put that sentence on this table on the eighteenth of April was doing it to keep herself out of something, and she was entirely right, and six of us heard it and nodded."

She put her finger on the sheet.

"So the seven of us who signed are seven officers of the holding. Not the eight. Seven. And the space in the middle of that line is the only thing in this city standing between any of us and a room of five serving a paper on us, and it is hers, and she put it there herself, and we put her there."

The kitchen was very loud for about nine seconds. Somebody put a spoon down too hard.

"Seven," said Isolde Marchetti.

## Isolde Does The Other Sum

She had not said anything for about a minute and a half and she said the one word from the end of the table.

"Seven what."

"Seven is the composition of this address and seven is the number of names on that line, and they are the same figure and they have nothing whatever to do with each other, and I have been sitting here for about twenty minutes not saying so because it looked like a coincidence and it is not a coincidence." She stood up and did not leave the table, which she does.

"One seven is a building. One seven is a room on a date. They read the same and they have never once been the same thing, and if they ever were then whoever reads them could make one out of the other, and that is the single most load-bearing thing in this house and nobody is going to be told about it by me."

"Agnete."

"They have always been two documents." Agnete Lindqvist had not moved. "Thirty-five days ago Nadia put a number in at a counter and for thirty-five days the two of them have read the same, and that was a good day, and nobody said at the table on the thirteenth of March that by agreeing in a figure they had welded the two documents together."

"And nobody has said it since, and I have been in eleven thousand services about what people do to documents they did not mean to do to each other."

"Nadia," said Isolde Marchetti.

## Nadia Ferrous Prices It Out Loud

"I have been doing sums since half past eight," said Nadia Ferrous.

She put her hands flat on the table, which she does, and there was a moment when everybody at that table was quiet, and then she went on at the speed she goes on at.

"The composition of this address went from eight to seven on the morning of the thirteenth of March at about nine forty-one, at a counter on the ground floor of the Vestry, in about nine minutes, on my own account, because I am not the head of this household and I wanted my name off every paper with this address at the head of it. That is what I did it for. That is the whole of the reason and there was not one other reason in it."

"Nadia—"

"I have not done it out loud at this table before and I have done it in a stopped lift on the first of May to a man who asked me to, and he understood about half of it, and I have thought about it every day since." She did not look at him.

"And there is nobody at this table who does not know that on the twenty-eighth of this month Runa is going to go and sign her name at the head of a composition of this address, in her own hand, with nothing above her name, in front of a clerk."

Runa Sato did not move.

"So here is the bill," said Nadia Ferrous, "and it is nine seconds and then somebody is going to write it down and I would rather it was said than written. From the first of May: the paper says seven and eight of us sleep under this roof. That is the bill. That is all of it."

"Not the address, which I get back in four years. The count. For the rest of my life, in every room in this city where a figure about this house exists, I am the reason it is one short, and nobody will ever correct it, and nobody will ever ask me about it, and I will not want to be asked it either."

She sat down.

"That is me being reasonable," she said, "and I told you in a lift I would not be, so I am going to stop now."

"No," said Runa Sato.

The kitchen went on being a kitchen.

## Runa Sato Does Not Go On The Twenty-Eighth

"You gave that a date in a loading bay on the evening of the twenty-seventh and I have not mentioned it once and I am not going to now, because you told me not to and because a woman who prices things does not take a thing back over a table."

"You are not going on the twenty-eighth."

"I am not going on the twenty-eighth." Runa Sato put her pen down and squared her sheet. "And I want to say the reason in the right order, and I want to say it once, and then I am going to leave this house and go home."

"Go on."

"One. I decided on the twelfth of April, in this room, at four in the morning, that I would certify that composition. I have said why three times now and I am not going to say it a fourth." She looked up.

"Two. I priced it on the twenty-seventh in a bay and I did not invoice it, and the price was not a number, it was a woman in a lift, and she has just told me what it is in front of eight people."

"That is not a reason to stop."

"It is the only reason that has ever existed in this house." Runa Sato picked her sheet up.

"If I put my name at the head of that composition on the twenty-eighth then for the life of that account there is a document in a division with Nadia Ferrous in the fourth block of it in her own words, dated the thirteenth of March at nine forty-one, and my name at the head of it, and the two of us never spoke about it."

"In two years a stranger in an office who is not cruel is going to read it and he is going to be entirely correct about what it means, and neither of us will be in the room."

She stood up.

"Tell the Field office I am not attending the twenty-eighth," she said, "and give them no reason, because a reason is a thing that gets filed, and I have spent six months watching one address with a blank box and I am not opening a second one."

"Runa—"

"I am not doing this for you and I am not doing it for her and I am not doing it because a woman asked me to in a kitchen at ten o'clock at night." She put her coat on at the table, which she does not.

"I am doing it because I would have had to sign a figure that is a bill and I am not going to be the woman who signed it. That is my price, and I have had it for three days, and it was in a drawer in a bay, and it has been said out loud."

Nobody moved for about a minute.

"There is nothing on this table," said Wren Kallo. "I have been looking at it since half past seven. There has not been a sheet of paper on this table since the twentieth of April and there were seven names on it and a space in it and a gap two fingers wide, and it went down the ordinary chain at four o'clock on the afternoon of the twentieth, and it has been gone for thirteen days."

"It is in a book," said Sun Meiling. "At the end of a corridor. In the keeping of a room that stands at five, and the fifth is him."

## What Is Not On The Table

"Yes."

"Then it is exactly where it is supposed to be and it is going to stay exactly where it is, and nobody in this house can do one single thing about it, and that is the arrangement and it was agreed to on the fourteenth of April in this room by eight people and one gap." Sun Meiling had not moved her hands. "What I have never worked out is who reads it."

Nobody answered her.

The kettle had gone off about ten minutes ago and the thing in the pot at the back had been going since and nobody had gone and looked at it, and outside there was a street in this city with a tram on it and a corridor off a second landing about eleven minutes away with forty feet of it and a bench at the end of it.

Beatrix Halm took the notebook out of her coat pocket at about a quarter past ten and did not open it on the table.

She opened it facing away from everybody, and wrote a second line in it under a line that said *thirteenth, counter, nine forty-one*, and the second line said *third of May, table, seven*, and she put the time against it, and she closed it, and that was the first time in nine years that she had written two lines in one sitting and there was something under both of them.

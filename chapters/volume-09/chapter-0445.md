# The Clause Under The Minute

He had three days to write a clause and he wrote thirty-one words for it in a notebook at Ferrier Street on the evening of the nineteenth, and on the morning of the twenty-first of June he took them to the room at the end of the corridor and put them on the second table under the window face up.

"It is one hundred and twenty-one days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and twenty-one makes one hundred and twenty-one."

"Nine days," said the clerk. "Thirty taken from twenty-one leaves nine. And that is a clause and not a sheet, so put it somewhere else before I touch it."

## What A Minute Is Going To Say

The clerk read the thirty-one words twice, which was one more than she needed and one less than she gave herself.

"A minute of the carrying names those premises four times," she said, "and it goes out at four to seven desks, and in about two years a person who has never met you opens a bundle because a clerk in a division of classification is bored on an ordinary day, and there are your address four times on one sheet and nothing else on the sheet."

"And so?"

"And so what you have written is a sentence saying that a naming of premises in a minute is a naming of the holding and not a naming of any person keeping it." She put it down. "That is the oldest clause in this kind of instrument and it has been in the book since before I came here and nobody has ever read it."

"Then it is worth having."

"It is worth having and it is worth exactly nothing," said the clerk. "It tells a person reading the bundle in two years that the address is a holding and not eight women."

"It does not tell them one single thing about what happens to a woman who is named on the witness line of the same requisition, and there is a witness line on it with seven names in it and a space two fingers wide, and your clause does not touch it, and it was never going to."

## What He Actually Wanted

"I know," he said. "That is the other one and it is longer."

"Say it and do not read it to me off the paper."

He took about half a minute to get it right, and when he got it right it was not in his own words, and she let it stand anyway.

"Where a minute names premises, no person keeping an address so named may be produced by that minute otherwise than in the presence of the subject, and the subject may not be absent from the making of it."

The clerk put her pen down.

"Say the second half again."

"The subject may not be absent from the making of it."

"That is not a clause about the bundle in two years," said the clerk. "That is a clause about the thirtieth of June. You have written a sentence that puts you in the room when the instrument is made, and a man who is in the room when the instrument is made is a man who owns it, and a man who owns a minute in a room of five owns every word that any of the five say into it for ever afterwards."

"That is the price."

"That is not a price, that is a purchase," said the clerk. "You are not paying anything. You are taking. In about nine days you are going to buy a minute of a room of five with the eleven words you have just said twice, and there is no money in it and there is nothing to be refused, and you have written it out in a notebook at home and brought it in on a working morning like a man bringing in a bill."

## Runa Sato Came Up The Corridor

She was at the second table at about half past ten with a bundle under her arm and the look she gets when a division has written on a paper and sent it back up a chain.

"Say the rest of it," said Runa Sato, "because I have been standing here for four minutes listening to one half."

The clerk looked at him. He looked at the sheet.

"Read it to her," said the clerk.

"It is a clause under the minute of the thirtieth. Where a minute names premises, no person keeping an address so named may be produced by that minute otherwise than in the presence of the subject." Runa Sato did not move. "And it does not reach the ninth of May."

"No."

"Then it is half a clause and I will tell you what the other half is worth." She put the bundle down on the table and did not open it. "On the ninth of May at four o'clock a clerk in this room entered one line in that book on her own initiative, unasked, saying that the sheet of the twentieth of February was in the keeping of this room and was not carried. It went out on the ordinary run. Seven desks."

"I read it in a bundle three days later. So did a woman in a division that has never heard of this room, and so did a woman at a counter in a lane, and so did a woman in a church of eleven thousand people, and one of those seven read it in a building I have worked out of for eleven years and put it in a pile."

"The clause does not reach it."

"A clause is entered under a minute at the making of the minute," said the clerk.

"Then it does not reach it and it cannot," said Runa Sato, "and you know that, and you brought me a half clause on the twenty-first of June so that I would find out at the end of it that there was no way to get the ninth of May back. It cannot be reached. It went out."

"It cannot be reached," said the clerk, "and neither can the sentence in the minute of the twentieth of February that was true that morning and stopped being true on the thirtieth of March, and I have been asked about that one twice this quarter by people who are not you and I have said the same thing both times and I am not going to be more sympathetic the third time."

## The Price Of The Other Half

"Then take the half that works," said Runa Sato, "and make it work properly. If he is going to buy the room, buy all of it."

The clerk sat down, which she almost never did.

"Once that clause is under the minute on the thirtieth of June, no person keeping that address may be produced by anything that room does, ever, except with him standing in the corridor outside the door." She turned to face him. "Including you. Including the six who sleep under that roof and are sworn. Including the one who is sworn and sleeps on Lantern Row with her own plate. Including the two who sleep under that roof and cannot be sworn."

"Including the officer of the composition, who will be sitting in a chair in this corridor at two o'clock on the afternoon of the thirtieth whether anybody asked her or not."

"Yes," he said.

"You have understood that I have just listed nine women and that you have just made yourself the address that every one of them has to be fetched through."

"I have understood it."

"You have not," said Runa Sato. "You have understood that you will be able to help them. What you have done is that there is now no office in this city that can come at any of us on a matter to do with that address without walking past you first, and you did it in a notebook, and it did not cost you a grain, and it is the first thing in four years that you have got for nothing." She picked her bundle up.

"I am not going to thank you for it and neither is she and you should count on spending the next forty years being described as a man with a grudge on a corridor."

"You are welcome to say it to me like that."

"I am saying it like that," said Runa Sato, "because I price things and I am telling you what this cost, and the price is that from the thirtieth of June you are not a man in a building."

"You are a doorway, and every woman who keeps that address is behind it, and a doorway cannot be moved and cannot be closed and cannot be got out of the way, and if there is ever a thing you want done at that address you will find out about it eleven years late from a stranger at a counter."

## The Order Of A Sitting Day

She let him stand there and then, apparently as an afterthought, told him what a sitting day is, because he had never seen one and had been planning one in his head for a week.

"The list is made in this building out of this room, and it is made on the thirtieth, and it is made the way a list is made, which is that it is made on the day and nobody asks anybody whether they are on it."

"Then the room sits, and the chair takes the first item, and every standing present is read out once, by name, and a member whose standing is read may stand up and say one thing about it and may not say anything else. Then the requisitions on the list are called, in the order they are written, and one of them has been written since the twentieth of February."

"Then the room seals, one seal at a time, and the seals go down at four, and I write my book up after the seals and before anybody leaves the building."

"Where does a clause go."

"Under the minute, at the making of it, after the last seal and before it goes down. Not into the body. Never into the body, because a clause in the body is a term and a term is a thing the parties agree and there are no parties in this room, there is a room and a man and a clerk." She turned the key over once in the lock and did not take it out. "And the room will not read it."

"That is the part you should have asked me about and you did not. When the minute is made, everybody in that room will know what you have bought, because I will have read it as I enter it and I read everything I enter. It will not be in the book. It will be read by whoever opens that minute in four years and by nobody else, ever, including the five of us."

"Then how does it work tomorrow."

"It does not work tomorrow," said the clerk. "It works in four years, in one room, with one stranger in it, and that is the entire mechanism, and it is the cheapest thing in this building, and it is not free, and I want you to have understood both halves of that sentence before you go down the corridor."

## Nine Days

The clerk took the slip at four o'clock and read the thirty-one words he had brought in from Ferrier Street and the eleven words he had said aloud twice with a pencil beside them, and then wrote out on the back of a third slip, in her own hand, one sentence, and signed it, and did not read it back to either of them.

"That is the clause," she said. "It is free. It goes under the minute when the minute is made and not before, and it cannot be entered now because there is no minute, and it cannot reach the ninth of May, and it cannot reach the twentieth of February, and both of those are in books that are written up and a clerk may only enter and cannot correct." She put the third slip in the drawer with the other two.

"I will enter what you have given me and not one word more, and if you come back here on the twenty-ninth with something better I will enter that and this will still be in the drawer, and both of them will be read on the thirtieth by everybody who opens that minute."

"Do you want it," said Runa Sato, from the corridor. "Say it out loud before I go down the stairs, because you have been writing in a notebook for three days and I would like to hear whether you meant it."

He looked at the drawer.

"I do not want to be a man in a building," he said. "I want the four words I said in that corridor last week to be the only thing that room knows about me that somebody else put there."

"I have not been able to do that for ninety days and I am not going to be able to do it after the thirtieth, and I would rather have done it on the twenty-first of June on my own two feet than have had it happen to me at a sitting with a seal in my hand."

Runa Sato looked at him for slightly too long.

"That is the first sentence you have said in this corridor that was not about what somebody else can do," she said, and went down it.

The clerk shut the drawer and turned the key in it, which she had never done in ninety days, and stood there with her hand on the key while he put his coat on.

"Nine days," she said. "And there are three slips in that drawer and none of them is a minute, and on the thirtieth of June all three of them turn into one, and you have just spent the isolation you have spent four years building and it took the eleven words of the second sentence and it cost me a pencil."

She let him out at the end of the corridor into a court with nine days in it and the paint at the bottom of a board at the end of a stair saying the Council on the thirtieth of June, and behind him, in a drawer in a room where nine people sat, three pieces of paper that could not be taken back had been lying under a lock that had never been locked before.

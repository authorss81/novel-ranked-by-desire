# The Room In Which He Is Read

The reading room is on the first floor over the counting house and it has a table, four chairs, a window with a blind half down, a rail along the far side for people who are not reading, and a day's book on a stand at the door where the applications go in with the hour and the name of the person who wants it. The door is open. It is open all day.

That is the rule and it is the only rule anybody enforces.

He said the date to a clerk on the stair at a quarter to nine, while she was still walking. "One hundred and twenty-three days since the twentieth of February. Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and twenty-three makes one hundred and twenty-three. And seven days to the thirtieth, because thirty taken from twenty-three leaves seven."

She said the seven back to him without breaking stride and went on up, and that was the only person in this city who had said a number to him that morning and gone away with it.

Ines Duarte put an application in the book's stand at twenty past six on the morning of the twenty-third of June and asked for the third sitting of the day, and the clerk wrote the hour and the applicant's name and the subject's name, and wrote *public* against it, and did not ask why.

"There is a rail," said Ines Duarte, "and there will be people at it."

"There will be people at it. It is the third sitting. On a public application about an address in this city you get a rail."

## Her Terms, In Her Own Mouth

She came up the outside stair at five minutes to nine and stood in the middle of the floor with a coat over her arm and no folder and nothing in her hands at all.

"I am not impaired and I am not tired and I am not in a trance and nothing in this room has any hold on me," said Ines Duarte. "I say that first because I have done this before in a different building and I say it before anything else happens."

"What I am doing this morning is a decision and it costs me, and I will tell you the price inside about four hours so that you cannot say afterwards that you did not know what it was."

"What is the price."

"About an hour of a particular twenty-odd days," said Ines Duarte, "which I will not be able to name later, and which I have known the price of for eleven years and have never once been asked for it by a man, and you are not going to get it for nothing and I am not going to hold it over you either."

"I am going to read a record aloud for about four hours in a room with the door open, and at the end of it I am going to be a woman who did that, and that is the whole of what changes."

"Say the other thing."

"The other thing is that a record in this city is read at a counter, or over a shoulder, or by the person it is about in a locked room to one other person, and every one of those three is a favour and two of them are a lie about what a favour is." She put her coat over the back of a chair. "This is not a favour."

"There is a rail and there is a day's book with my name and the hour on it and there is a woman from the hall who has not seen me before. I am not reading you to you in my rooms and you are not coming up Lantern Row tonight, and I want the difference on the record, because the difference is the only interesting thing I have to offer anybody."

## What The Room Does And Does Not Do

She read the rules off the card on the wall by the blind, which everybody who applies to use that room does, and she read them as a woman confirming an appointment rather than as a woman making a speech.

In order. The date first, always, and the hour with it when the record carries one. Nothing skipped, not a name and not a figure and not a box with nothing in it. A person who asks for a reading may stop it. A subject of a reading may attend it and may not stop it. The room does not hurry anybody and does not keep anybody past the hour the applicant asked for.

"I am the applicant," she said. "I cannot stop it either once it is open, which is why I have written out what I am going to read and left the last hour out."

"You have left an hour out of a four-hour application."

"I have left an hour out of it and I have not marked the hour in the day's book, because the rule says nothing is skipped and I am not going to make an exception for myself in a book with a clerk's hand in it." She sat down at the table and squared the chair opposite her. "Sit there. You are the subject and you are entitled to look at everything and you may not stop one word of it."

"You have had three months of people deciding what you are in rooms where you were not present, and this morning you are going to be present for all of it."

## The Fifteenth Of April

She began at the beginning and she did not hurry, and the woman from the hall came in at about a quarter past nine and stood at the rail with her hands on it, and by half past there were four of them, and nobody came in late and nobody left.

"The twenty-seventh of October. About eleven in the morning." Her voice in a small hard room was a plain thing and did not need to be anything else. "A description of the premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight, made against the head of those premises, by a clerk of the division of classification. Four words. Spoken by the subject in a kitchen on that same morning and written down at the head of his own address."

He did not move.

"The thirteenth of March. Nine forty-one in the morning. At a municipal counter, a woman gave up an address, and the composition of those premises became seven, and the paper says seven, and it has said seven every quarter since."

"The seventeenth of March. The ninth minute. The requisition of the twentieth of February was read at a room where nine people sit and it stood at three that morning, so it was administered and not carried."

"The first of April. Eleven minutes to ten in the morning, at a sitting that had not been held since the twenty-ninth of December, one standing was restored. The room went to four."

Then she stopped, and turned a page of nothing, because there was nothing in her hands to turn.

"The fifteenth of April. Ten minutes to nine in the morning."

She did not look at him.

"At a public post off a third court, in the ordinary way, one sheet of plain paper, in his own hand, in about ninety seconds, with a pen. No instrument. No permission. No witness. No holder. No receipt and no copy."

"The sheet lodged the whole of the subject's accumulated standing and it was entered in the keeping book of the room that holds this requisition on the day it was lodged, and no paper in this city can take it back out of that book on any day, and none was ever drawn."

She put both hands flat on the table.

"That is the alteration," said Ines Duarte.

## What She Reads In A Man

"There is exactly one place in that record where anything has been changed, and I have held it in my head since the sixth of May and I know the taste of it the way you know the taste of a mint that should not be in a cup of tea." She turned to face him properly. "And nobody did it to you."

"Not the office that keeps the plate, not the clerk in the corridor, not the woman who read the service in February, not the eight women who put their names under yours and thought they were doing one thing."

"You did it, in April, at a counter, with a pen, in ninety seconds, and you did it because you wanted to be legible, and no force on earth made you, and every other line in that record is a person doing exactly what the paper told them to do."

"I wanted to be the fifth."

"You wanted the standing to answer a requisition nobody else could answer, and you went and got it, and the getting is the whole of it." She turned to the woman at the rail and then back.

"I want to say it out loud in a room where four people who have never met him are standing, because in about four years there will be a stranger with a folder and he will read this and he will do what everybody in this city has done since the fifteenth of March, which is read you wrong."

"He will read a man with a secret in him, or a man with a machine in him, or a man who has cheated eight women, and he will be wrong in the ordinary way, because the record does not support any of those three and he will not have me in it."

She leaned in.

"You are not a man with a secret. You are a man with a document, and the document says everything, and that is what the ninth is. It is not a rank. It is the standing to be read."

"Nine is the only rank in this city that has no card and no floor and no interval, and the reason there is no card is that there is nothing about it to put on a card, and the reason there is nothing about it to put on a card is that the whole of it is that somebody handed it over in a queue. You did not climb. There was nothing to climb."

"You filled in a form that is not a form, in the ordinary way, at a public counter, and that is why it cannot be struck, and that is why you cannot be the only person it can be used against."

She sat back.

"Now that is done," she said. "And that is the last time anybody in this city gets to do that to you by accident, and I have booked it publicly so that it is on the day's book with my name and the hour on it, and so that if it is done again by anybody, there will be one entry in this city that says a woman did it first and on purpose and named herself."

## Reading While

"The twenty-seventh of May," she said. "A composition certified again, in the officer's own hand, under her own name, at the head of it, in her own division, knowing what it made her."

He had both hands flat on the table and could not get them to do anything else.

"The twenty-ninth of May. The same, again, and this time knowing that there is no certificate for the quarter beginning the first of July, which nobody has drafted, because a form cannot be drafted without one."

She read on and he understood somewhere in the middle of it that he was not being read at any more, and that was when it started.

"Read the twentieth of February again."

She had her hand flat on the wood. "Once."

"I have heard it twice in a clerk's voice and once in your voice over a stair and I have never heard it in the voice of a woman who thinks it is the most interesting sentence of the quarter."

"The twentieth of February," said Ines Duarte. "Three seals. Laid, called, served and heard. Not carried. The premises named at the head of it four times. The ground described in the fourth part, being a description made against the head of those premises at about eleven on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October."

"And a sentence in the chair's own hand, which was true that morning and which stopped being true on the thirtieth of March and which no clerk in this city is able to strike, because a clerk may only enter."

"Again."

"No," said Ines Duarte. "It is read once, in order, and a thing that is read twice in a room like this is a thing somebody has asked for twice, and I do not do things twice for a man who wants to hear them."

## The Nineteenth Of June, In Her Voice

"What have you got left before the sixteenth of May."

"There is not one thing on the list for the nineteenth of June," she said, "and I am going to read you the blank, because the blank is in the record and the rule says nothing is skipped, not a name and not a figure and not a box with nothing in it."

She read a blank date out loud in a small hard room with four people at a rail, and he laughed, once, and she did not look up and she went on.

Then she read the ninth of May out loud again and he came before she had finished the sentence, with her fingers in his hair and her mouth against the top of his head, because she had not come round the table at any point at all and neither of them had noticed her do it.

## She Does Not Stop Reading

"That is the first," she said. "I am not counting anything and I am not going to."

She read the first of June into the empty part of the room while she got his coat off his shoulders and it went down the back of the chair, about a woman being told the rest of something in a kitchen by a man who had decided not to protect her with silence, and she did not slow down for the buttons and neither did he.

Her ink was at the cuticles of the first two fingers of her right hand where soap had never lifted it in eleven years.

"The officer of the composition is the only woman in this household who cannot be sworn and cannot be protected by the roll and did not know until the eleventh of April that she was an officer of anything."

"And she certified again on the twenty-ninth of May knowing the July position, and you told her the rest on the first of June in a kitchen with eight people in it, and she neither thanked you nor forgave you."

"I am reading that sentence out loud because it is the only sentence in this record that anybody in it has ever been thanked for."

He had his hands on her hips. Her shirt was still on and her mouth was a foot away from his and she was reading the way she did everything, in order, at a rate.

She got the collar of it open one-handed with two fingers, the way a woman opens something she is going to have off in a minute, and she read the whole time.

"Hands on the table," said Ines Duarte. "I am reading. Look at me and not at the window."

He looked at her. Her eyes were not on anything at all, because she had held this record since the sixth of May and had not needed a piece of paper since the seventh.

## The Table

She got her back against the edge of the table and pulled him in by the hip and went on reading with her thighs against the front of his and her mouth against his jaw, and the date she was on went into the side of his neck while she shifted her weight and pushed her hips forward and took him inside her without breaking the sentence in half.

He got both hands into her hair and she had to stop.

"One second," she said, and went on reading with her fingers closed in his, her forehead against his, and the room went white at the edges for him and came back with her voice still going in it.

The bar of light along the bottom of the blind behind her moved about an inch over four hours and neither of them knew what time it was for most of that. She said the hour out loud before she did it and then did not say anything at all for some time afterwards, and what was left of her voice in the room was the sound the two of them made on the boards and nothing that anybody could have written down.

## The Boards By The Rail

She took him off and turned him round and put him back against the end of the table with her hands flat on the wood on either side of him, and read the ninth of May at him for the second time that morning at the pace of a woman who has read four hundred bodies in four hundred documents and has never had one in front of her.

"You are warm," she said, in the middle of a figure, "and I have been reading at you for four hours and I would like it on the record that I can still read and that is not an accident, and that about eleven years ago I could not hold a single sheet for a day and now I can hold nine months of a man's life and do this at the same time."

He came with her mouth open against his shoulder and her hand flat at the back of his head, and she held him there and read the rest of the line with her voice going through her own chest into his face.

The clerk came in at about twelve and wrote in the day's book that the reading was in progress and went out again and neither of them stopped, and the woman from the hall who had stood at the rail since a quarter past nine watched a man and an auditor finish a morning's work on the boards of a room over a counting house and did not say one word about it then or afterwards.

Ines Duarte got up with her hand in his hair and read the last line of the nineteenth of June, which was nothing, and then stood over him while he came, reading the hour out loud and nothing else, and her mouth was open on the number and the grey at her left temple had gone darker with the heat of her and the ink on her right hand was wet where she had put her palm flat on the table.

She wiped that hand on her skirt without stopping, and kept reading, and there was nothing left on the list.

## The Only Time She Stopped

She stopped once in four hours, at about eleven, and it was not for anything he had done.

"You have lost the thread," she said.

"I have not lost it."

"You have lost it, and you have been holding my hip with your hand flat for about four minutes, which is what you did at a table in a kitchen on the fourteenth of April and again in a corridor in May." She was naked to the waist with her back against the end of the table and her thigh hard along the outside of his and her mouth an inch from his, and she was not reading.

"So. Where are we."

"The ninth of May."

"The ninth of May," said Ines Duarte. "A line entered in a keeping book on a clerk's own initiative, unasked."

"That is the date and the hour is four in the afternoon and the run goes out at four and I am giving you those first every time, because you have been standing in a room since the fifteenth of April being handed one enormous fact at a time and you have started carrying it in the place where you used to carry a figure off a plate."

"That is what an auditor does."

"That is what I do," she said. "And you have not been able to do it since you were nineteen and you have spent nine years finding women who will do it for you at a kitchen table, and this morning I have been doing it at you for four hours and you have not once told me to stop, and I am going to be honest with you about what that tells me."

"Tell me."

"It tells me that a man who has been carrying a whole city on his own since he was nineteen has stopped, and that he has stopped because a woman with a date in her mouth told him where they were, and that he is not going to be able to go back to the other thing afterwards." She put her hand round the back of his neck. "I am not frightened of telling you that because I am the one who read you."

"And I want you to hear it in my voice, at the correct hour, in the correct order, before we do anything else, because a thing said badly and out of order is the only thing in this city that nobody can take back."

Then she read the ninth of May out loud again from the beginning, and her hand went down between them, and he was wet inside her before she had finished the run, and she said the seven desks out loud with her mouth against his jaw.

## The Hour She Does Not Read

At about one o'clock she stopped, in the middle of the sixteenth of May, and sat back on the edge of the table with her hair stuck to her jaw.

"That is where I stop," said Ines Duarte.

"You said four hours."

"I said about four hours and I have read for four hours and I am going to sit here for a minute, and the room does not hurry anybody, and the hour is not in the day's book because I did not mark it." She put her hand flat on the wood and looked at it.

"There is one hour of that record that I am not reading out loud this morning and I am not going to tell you what is in it and neither is anybody else, and you will not get it from me in four years either."

"How much is in it."

"About an hour," said Ines Duarte. "That is the price and I told you the shape of it this morning and not the figure."

"I have been losing my twenties behind other people's paperwork for eleven years and this morning I chose to hold one more hour of it on purpose, in a room with the door open, with my name and the hour on a book in a stand in the corridor, and there is a woman at the ninth terrace desk who has been doing the same arithmetic about the same thing for twenty years and never once had anybody ask her what it costs her." She got her breath.

"And I want to say the other half of it, because I do not want it going round that I made a sacrifice this morning."

"What is the other half."

"The other half is that I wanted to," said Ines Duarte. "It was not a tax and it was not a price and nobody took anything."

"I sat down in a room with a rail and a day's book and a man who cannot be struck and I read him out loud in front of four strangers, and it is the best work I have ever done and I would like it recorded that it was also the best thing I have ever wanted to do, and that I would do it again in about four years for the hour I have just paid."

## What The Rail Cost

The four people at the rail had gone by about half past one and the clerk had come in twice to write down that the reading had finished early, and the day's book in the stand in the corridor said a third sitting of the day, applied for by I. Duarte, subject L. Hao, public, commenced at nine o'clock and concluded at ten minutes to two.

Ines Duarte washed at the basin in the corner with cold water and stood afterwards with her back to the room for a moment longer than a woman who has just washed at a basin usually stands.

"There," she said. "That is what a reading costs. Not the hour. The book in the stand."

"There is a room in this city with an entry in it that says an auditor asked for a public reading of a man's own record and that man was in the room and could not stop it, and I could have done it on Lantern Row in four minutes with the door shut and it would have cost a quarter of what it has cost me and it would have been worth nothing to anybody except you."

"A stranger in four years."

"A stranger in four years, an auditor in a division, a woman with a reason, and anybody who has ever wondered why nine is a rank with no card on it." She put her coat on. "You will not be read wrong again by accident."

"You will be read wrong on purpose, by somebody with a file, and I have made that the more expensive of the two and I have done it in public and it is on a book with my name on it."

She got to the door and stopped in it.

"I am going to go down the outside stair and I am going to go home and I am not going to be at Ferrier Street tonight," said Ines Duarte.

"Nine sworn women have been put behind a clause you wrote in a notebook, and one of them who cannot be sworn is going to be in a corridor at two o'clock on the thirtieth of June, and I am the ninth, and I am not going to stand at the table and be counted."

"You will do it in front of people and I will be one of the people and that is the arrangement, and there is nothing to thank and nothing to price and I am not going to be thanked for this morning by anybody including you."

She went down the outside stair and he stood in a reading room over a counting house with the blind half down and four chairs at a table and a rail along the far side, and the day's book in the stand in the corridor said what she had done to him in public and on purpose and with her own name on it, and there were seven days left in the quarter.

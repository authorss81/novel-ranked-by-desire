# The Chair Who Will Not Meet

The chair keeps a room on the first floor of the building at the end of the corridor, and on the morning of the third of June the clerk of that room told him at the counter that she would not see him, and said it the way a clerk says a thing she has said before.

"She is not refusing the room," said the clerk. "She is refusing you. There is a difference and you know the difference."

"It is one hundred and three days since the twentieth of February," he said. "Ten from twenty leaves ten, ten with thirty and thirty and thirty and three makes one hundred and three. I am not asking for a sitting. I know the Council does not sit again until the thirtieth."

"Then you know more than most of the people who come to this counter."

## At A Chair That Is Not One Of Nine

He waited on the bench at the end of the forty-foot corridor because there was nowhere else to wait, and at about ten the door at the end opened and Alma Quayle came out with a folder under her arm and stopped when she saw him.

"You were told," she said.

"I was told she will not meet me."

"She chairs the room and she may refuse any person." Alma Quayle sat down on the bench beside him, which nobody had ever done. "It is not the sitting of the thirtieth. The thirtieth is entered a quarter ahead and cannot be moved. This is a chair deciding she has nothing to say to a member before it."

"Is she right."

"She is right the way a door is right." Alma Quayle put the folder on her knees. "You cannot ask this room for anything. You worked that out on the ninth of May and you decided never to make it need something. Coming here to ask the chair to meet you is asking."

He did not answer.

## What Counting Means

"I told you on the thirteenth of May to count me," said Alma Quayle. "A recused member is in the room at a chair that is not one of the nine, hearing everything and taking no part. That has been true since the fourth of December. I am still recused and I will still be recused on the thirtieth."

"I counted you."

"Then count this too." She opened the folder and showed him a slip with nine lines on it, five of them carrying a standing. "The room stands at five and the fifth is you. The chair knows it. The chair also knows that a requisition capable since the fifteenth of April has not been carried on any day, and that in about nine weeks somebody who is only being careful will read that as a decision."

"She has read the minute."

"She wrote the sentence in it that died on the thirtieth of March." Alma Quayle shut the folder. "Nothing has been taken off any person. It was true on the twentieth of February. The room went to four on the first of April and to five on the fifteenth. A minute does not expire. It is only untrue, and a clerk cannot correct it."

## The Refusal

The door at the end opened again and the clerk looked out.

"She says you may put one thing in writing," said the clerk. "One page. No question on it. It will be entered as received and not answered."

"Tell her I have nothing to put in writing."

"Tell her yourself by not writing it." The clerk went back inside.

Alma Quayle stood up.

"You refused me on the thirteenth," she said. "A sentence out loud in front of a clerk. The only thing in this city you can give anybody. You were right to refuse and I am not asking again. A word said to that clerk after the thirtieth of June is not written until the first of July, and we are on the third."

"I know."

"Then this is what a chair refusing looks like from the bench." She picked up the folder. "It looks like nothing. Nobody comes out. Nothing is entered. The number of seats does not change. The thirtieth stays where it is."

She walked back toward the door and stopped.

"Do not make this room need something before the thirtieth," she said. "You swore it on the ninth of May. A woman guessed it on the thirteenth and you neither confirmed nor denied it. Keep it that way. The calendar is the only door and it is painted on a board by a man from the works."

## What The Bench Costs

He stayed on the bench after Alma Quayle stood, because getting up too soon would have looked like an answer and he had none.

"You told me to count you," he said to her back. "I have counted you every day since the thirteenth of May. A recused member hearing everything and taking no part. That is not nothing. In about four years a stranger with a folder will read a minute of a carrying and find five seals where the February minute had three, and he will ask who was in the room when it was made."

Alma Quayle stopped with her hand on the door.

"And the answer will be nine chairs and one woman at a chair that is not one of the nine," she said. "Hearing everything. Taking no part. That is what recusal means in this building. It does not mean absence. It means presence without a seal."

"Does the chair count you."

"The chair knows I am there." Her voice was level. "The chair refused you this morning and she did not refuse me. I go in. I sit at the side. I hear the list being made. I take no part in it, and no clerk writes my name, and in four years nobody will be able to say I decided anything."

She turned then, fully, with the folder against her side.

"Do you understand what you refused me," she said. "One line in that minute. That the description was made by an officer of a division that was not recused on anything, three months and two days before anybody could have used it. Without that line the ground of the requisition is a description by a clerk called Imrie on four words said in a kitchen. With it, the ground is clean."

"I know."

"You refused it to keep your oath of the ninth of May." She nodded once, as though closing a book. "Lifting me in the last weeks of a quarter in which no Council sits would have been making the room need something, and a woman would have paid for it out of her own future."

"You chose my standing over the ground of your own sheet. That is the most expensive refusal you have made in this city, and you made it to protect me, and I am telling you so because nobody else will."

She went in. The corridor was empty. He sat on the bench for a while with his hands on his knees, and then he got up and went out into the court, where the sun was on one side of it and a runner was taking a sheet up an outside stair.

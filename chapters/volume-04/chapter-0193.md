# One More Refusal

The third sheet came up on the wire at the dispensary hatch on the thirteenth and it had been printed on the eleventh and it was one side and it had no name on it and it fitted eleven establishments in the quarter the same way the second one did.

Tessa Okonkwo read it at the counter at ten to eight with the queue behind her, the way she read the second one, standing up, in about eleven seconds.

An establishment in connection with the file of a member of the Bureau is required to furnish the register of attendance at that establishment for any day on which that member attended, and the register is to be produced to a person holding a warrant within two hours of the requirement taking effect.

"Not the day book," she said.

"The day book is the register," said the man. "It is one book. It has the hours on the left and the count on the right and it is kept in this building and it is the only register of attendance that exists."

It had taken effect at eight that morning. He had come in at about ten to eight, which was a quarter of an hour after it took effect, and he had a warrant, and the whole of the queue behind her could see the fold of it.

Tessa Okonkwo set the sheet down square on the counter with her hand flat on it.

"You will have it in two hours," she said. "It is in the back room and I will fetch it myself and I will stand here while you read it, and you will read it here, and you will not carry it out of this building."

The queue watched.

The register is nine years of it in a hand that gets smaller in the winter. It is a hard book with a canvas spine and it opens flat, and there is no name on any page of it, and there are three columns: the hour, the minute, and the number of people the person at the counter spoke to that day.

There are no names because there are no names. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it for nine years and about two hundred and three hundred people a day have trusted it.

She had brought it out at four minutes to ten and put it on the counter and he had read the line and gone at about a quarter past ten, and she had opened the hatch again at ten past and had served about two hundred and forty-one people through it before the room emptied at four.

"Turn it up," she said. "It is not in date order, it is in the order the days happened, and you want the seventh of April, and it is about a third of the way back from the end for this month."

He turned it up himself.

There were four lines for the seventh of April. The count column said two hundred and forty-one. The hours ran from eight to four, and at about four in the afternoon there was a line in the hand that was smaller and colder than the rest, and it read:

FOUR P.M. HATCH CLOSED. MEMBER PRESENT AT THE HATCH AT CLOSING. NO ATTENDANCE.

He read it twice.

"That is not an attendance."

"That is an attendance," said Tessa Okonkwo. "He was here at four o'clock in the afternoon and he stood at this counter and I was here and the hatch was shut behind me and we were the only two people in the room. It is the only line in nine years of that book that has a member's word in it and it is in my hand and I wrote it and I would write it again."

"Then he attended at five to midnight on the seventh," the man said. "The chart says so."

"The chart is mine," she said, "and the chart says he was treated at five to midnight in a washroom on the sixth floor of his own place of work and that he was not a patient of mine, and both of those are in your file already and you do not need a register to tell you about a washroom in a Bureau building at midnight."

She put her hand flat on the open page.

"So the answer to your question is that there is nothing in that book for you and there is nothing in it that is worth the two hours of a queue standing behind me, and I have answered it, and you may take the book or you may leave it on this counter."

He did not take it.

He stood there for about a minute and a half, and he looked at the book, and then he looked up the queue, and the woman who has been coming to that counter every morning since before the war with the fever was four places back in it with her bag on her arm.

"That one," he said. "She comes at a quarter to eight. She has come at a quarter to eight for nine years, she has not missed one, and there is no name in your book because you do not take names, and it is on my sheet now."

The room did not move.

"You have been in this queue once a week for nine years," he said, "and you have stood on the outside of this counter every day for nine years, and you have never once given this building your name, and that has been your protection, and you are right about it, and it is over at about four minutes past eight this morning because a man in an establishment section has written the hours on a piece of paper."

"That is not the requirement."

"That is the requirement," the man said. "The requirement is the register. The register is the hours. The hours are a person who comes at the same minute every day for nine years in a building with one door, and that is the whole of what I came for, and I did not know it when I came."

He put the book down on the counter, squared it, and did not close it.

"You can keep the book," he said. "I have what I came for. I will enter the hours and the day count and the establishment and the member and the hour of the requirement, and a person who attends an establishment at a stated hour over a stated period is a person who can be entered, and you did that yourself, on the eighth, in four minutes, at this counter."

"I did that myself," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"You put your name and your hours on a card and said a person with a warrant could ask you anything he could ask a building. That card is on this counter and it is the reason I know the hours, and I am not going to pretend that I got them anywhere else."

He wrote for about four minutes at the end of the counter and then he went out into the street.

Nobody stopped the woman. The woman had a bag on her arm and she came to the counter at a quarter to eight the next morning as she had every morning for nine years, and she was not asked for her name, and Tessa Okonkwo did not look at her while the queue moved.

At four in the afternoon, at the bench outside with the brass plate on the end of it, she had the register on her knees with the canvas spine and did not open it.

"You are going to ask me whether it was worth it," she said.

"You put a card on that counter on the eighth," he said.

"I did."

"Was it worth it."

Tessa Okonkwo did not answer for about four seconds.

"Yes," she said. "That is the answer and I hate it and I am going to give it to you without softening it, because I am the one who wrote the card and I priced my own name against that building on the eighth and I did not price hers, and I did not price hers because at ten to eight on the eighth I had been running that hatch for nine years and I had not once looked down the queue and counted who came at the same minute.

"That is the whole of what I got wrong and it took a man with a warrant to show me. I made the building findable on purpose and I made myself the most findable thing in it, and a building is findable in two ways, and I only priced one of them.

"And the other one is the two hundred and three hundred people who come to it, who have never given this building their names and gave them instead to the regularity of their own habits, which is the same thing, and I did not see it in nine years.

"Nine years." She shut the book on her thumb. "And I want you to understand what I am not going to do about it, because you will want me to and I have already thought about it.

"I am not going to stop that woman coming. I am not going to move the hours, because the hours are what two hundred and three hundred people a day plan their day around and they have been planning around them for nine years and they are not going to be told by me on a day off.

"And I am not going to go and stand in an office and tell a man in an establishment section that he has got a person. He has not got a person. He has got a quarter of eight and a building with one door and he will get a person out of that in about six weeks and he will get her out of it because I wrote a card."

She stood up and put the register under her arm.

"And the thing that has happened is not that a woman has been taken. It is that a woman who came to a hatch at eight in the morning for nine years without giving her name has been found.

"And the finding is a sentence with a building in it and no name, and the sentence is going to sit in a file until somebody fills the name in, and when they do it will not be a man with a warrant on a day off. It will be an ordinary working morning and it will not feel like anything at all."

"That is a bad thing to have done."

"It is a thing I did at a counter in four minutes in a queue and I have had the best part of a day to sit with it," she said. "Open the hatch tomorrow at eight."

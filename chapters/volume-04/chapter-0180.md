# The Month Ends

It came up on the wire at the Vestry market at about half past eight in the morning of the thirtieth and a boy brought it up sixty-one steps to Ferrier Street at about ten, and it was a roll, and it had to go up on a wall.

That was in the printed instruction at the top, in a sentence with no feeling in it at all. A standing roll is kept at the premises and is displayed, and is produced to any person who calls at the premises and asks for it, and the reason is printed underneath in three lines, which are the same three lines that are printed at the foot of every permission circular in this city.

A PERSON WHO CANNOT BE FOUND CANNOT BE ASKED. A PERSON WHO CANNOT BE ASKED CANNOT BE PRODUCED.

Sun Meiling read the instruction twice on the third step and then she came into the kitchen and put it flat on the table in front of all of them.

"There is a space on it," she said.

There was a space on it.

The roll is a sheet about two feet by three on stiff board, and it has a rule down the left, and it has seven ruled rows.

Seven.

Six of them had a name on them and a standing against each one and a band against each one, and every one of the six names was one of the six women who had written an answer onto a form in their own hand between two and six in the afternoon of the sixteenth of this month, unconsulted, because a woman had written a sentence in pencil on a wall of a landing on the tenth.

Above the seven rows, printed, in a heavier line:

THE HEAD OF THE HOUSEHOLD.

And under that heading there was a name, in his own hand, which was the hand a clerk at a counter on the second floor of the Fourth Branch had initialled at about twenty to nine on the morning of the twenty-seventh.

The seventh row was empty.

It was not marked. It was not crossed. It had a number against it, in the left-hand rule, the way a seat on a tram has a number, and it was the seventh, and there was nothing in the other three columns at all, and it was the same four-inch space that had been at the foot of two forms and that had been empty since the sixth of April.

Sun Meiling put her hand flat on the board and did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"That is mine," she said.

"Yes," said Ines Duarte.

"That is my row and it is ruled and it is numbered seven, and I answered a question on the twenty-third in my own hand and put six, and the Office has written my row out for me because I am the seventh person of reading age in that building and I will not write my own name on anything.

"And it is going to be on a wall. That is what you said at this table on the twenty-third. You said that a household finds out. This is it finding out, and it took a week, and no office had to do anything, because the Office printed a number in a margin and wrote the rest of the line for me."

"Say what you want done with it," said Ines Duarte.

"I want it on the wall."

The room did not move.

"I want it on the landing, at eye height, where anybody who comes up sixty-one steps can be shown it." Sun Meiling took her hand off the board. "Not in a drawer. Not in the notebook. Not under a table. On the wall.

"We have spent four months being a place that cannot be reached, and it turns out that a place that cannot be reached is a place that can be lost, in instalments, by people who never have to say they took anything. And I signed a stub on a doorstep on the thirteenth and it turns out that was the cheapest door in this city, and every paper since has come to me.

"So I want the roll on the wall, in the open, at the top of sixty-one steps, with my name missing off it in a ruled line with a number against it, and I want it to be the first thing anybody sees who comes to that door with paper."

"That is the opposite of everything this house has done since the first of December," said Beatrix Halm.

"Yes," said Sun Meiling. "It is the opposite of everything this house has done since the first of December and it is the first decision any of us has made since March that I believe in."

They put it up at about eleven in the morning and it took three of them and it took about twenty minutes because the wall on that landing is old plaster and a board two feet by three does not want to go on it.

It went up over the pencil.

The sentence in pencil on that wall had been there since the tenth of April and was undated and had no name near it, and it was a level lower and to the right of the roll, and it stayed, and it was under the roll, and any person who came up the stairs and read the roll would be standing about four feet from a sentence in pencil that said the same thing about the door.

The room did not say anything about that.

At about eleven and twenty Ola Bright came up the stairs with the copybook, which she has kept since the sixteenth of March and which is the only record of that house in Ostara that has ever been kept without being asked for, and it stopped being that on the tenth of this month when a man asked for six sentences to be copied in six hands.

She put it on the third step and opened it and wrote six lines and a seventh, in seven hands, over about four minutes, and the seventh was his, and it was the first time his name had ever gone into that book in any position at all, and his signature had been refused three times.

She wrote the date underneath and then she shut it and did not write anything else, and she said, to the landing, "Seven," and went down.

At about two he went in to work and pressed his plate into the socket on the ground floor of the Fourth Branch, because it is the last working day of the month and the strip comes back on the last day of the month and there is a strip in the frame in the reading room with every number on it since the winter.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

Three grains above a floor that had not moved either. Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two under a band that was sitting on a piece of paper in a file in a room he had not been in since the seventh of April.

Nothing had changed and he stood in front of a machine in a lobby and let that be the last fact of the month for about four seconds, and then he went and did six floors.

At about half past five he was on the landing at Ferrier Street.

The roll was on the wall and the light from the stair window came across it at that hour so that the sixth name was in it and the seventh was not, because the seventh row is at the bottom and the window light stops about two inches above it.

Six names and a space.

Runa Sato came up at about six with her kit on her shoulder, which it had been since the thirteenth and which she had carried through the whole of this on a day when she had been somewhere else entirely, and she stopped in front of the roll for about four seconds.

"Seventh," she said.

"Seventh."

"That line is not going to be filled in."

"No."

"Good." She put the kit down against the wall under it, which was the first time in four months that anything had been put down in that house rather than carried, and she went up. "For sixty days it will be the only line on that roll anybody looks at twice."

He was still standing there at about seven when Agnete Lindqvist came up the sixty-one steps, which she had done twice in the last ten days, and she stood in front of the roll with her hands in her sleeves and read all of it.

"There is nothing for me on it," she said.

"No."

"That is correct and I have been awake about it since the twenty-third." She read the seventh row again. "Eleven thousand and some hundred people holding no plate and no audit, and a ruled line with a number on it on a wall in the Slough, and the only person in Ostara who could ever be put in it is the woman who is not on it."

"There is not one column for you," said Sun Meiling, from the fourth stair.

"There is not one column for me in any document in this city," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have known that for eleven years, and I said it on the steps of the Vestry on the twentieth of this month in front of six of you, and it has taken ten days for a piece of paper to say it back to me and I would like it noted that I am not going to make a speech about it because I said I would not."

She went up.

He was on that landing with six women at about eight and Wren Kallo came at about half past eight because Wren Kallo comes to Ferrier Street on the last day of a month, which is not an arrangement and is a habit.

She read the roll. She read the seventh row twice.

"The leak stopped on the twenty-eighth," she said. "And you know why it stopped, and I know why it stopped, and Tessa Okonkwo knows why it stopped and it is not because any of us did anything."

"It is not."

"Four people deep and three told, and all four of them kept every one. And the thing that came out of it came out of a chart a doctor signed at five to midnight in a washroom on the seventh of this month and handed to a woman to carry up a stair."

Wren Kallo put her hand on the roll's edge. "I have spent four days waiting to be the reason something, and I was not the reason, and I have never in my life been less the reason for something. I would like somebody to write that down and I do not care where."

"It goes in Ola's book," said Sun Meiling.

"It does not go in Ola's book, because the second a woman in that book can be named next to a thing I have carried for four days, the book stops being the thing you keep when you cannot write anything down."

He looked at it for about four seconds and then he said, "It is not going to be a thing anybody can use. That is the actual sentence. The certificate comes at the end of sixty days and there is a space at the top of it, and whatever you wrote on the wall of that landing stays on the wall of that landing until somebody sands it off, and I am not going to pretend that is a large thing to be offered in a month like this one."

"That is the whole of what you have," said Wren Kallo, "and it is more than it was, and you paid for it in nine seconds and in a lie you told four people for three days. Be careful about the order of that."

The month went out at about eleven that night with a rain that came in from the west and went out again about twenty minutes later, and he did the last of the six floors and got the half-hourly and got off at the junction and walked the last eleven minutes, and the Board was repainted that morning and the pale rectangle was still on it, about four inches by two, with nothing in it and no file in this city recording that it was empty.

He went up the sixty-one steps.

Six names on a board on a wall at the top of a landing, and a seventh row with a number against it and nothing in the other three columns, and a sentence in pencil four feet below it that would outlast him, and a certificate at the end of sixty days that would come to that address because his name was the first thing on it.

The certificate is not a rank. It is not a band and it is not on the wall and it will not appear on the Board on any Thursday in any month. It is a piece of paper that says that six women live at an address in Ferrier Street under a head of household, and the head of household is findable now, at that address, by anybody, in one line, in about four seconds, for the rest of his life.

He had wanted a household for four years and he had got one on the last day of the month, and the plate said eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and the light had stopped about two inches above the seventh row.

# The Signal Cabin

The cabin at the far end of the Ferrier line is a room eight feet by eleven with a window on three sides and a stove in the corner and a frame of thirty-one levers along the north wall, and it is where the line ends and does not so much stop as run out of reasons.

She was in it at ten past ten at night when he came up the outside stair, which is a stair a person climbs on the outside of a building because the inside of it is forty-one steps of a house over a chop-house.

"Shut the door," she said. "All of it. There is no draught in here at eleven and there is a draught in here at nine, and I am not working a cabin with a draught in it."

He shut it.

She had the lamps on and the block instrument ticking over on the shelf and a lever frame with the road clear for the last two miles, which on that line at that hour means the road is clear because there is nothing on it, and the frame is there anyway, thirty-one levers, each one a place a train could be that is not.

"Sit down," she said. "Not there. There. The chair is under the shelf and the shelf is for the block and the block is for the frame and I do not have a conversation about where a man puts his feet."

He sat down.

"She put her hand flat on the frame, not on a lever, on the frame.

"It is thirty-one levers and it is a telephone made out of iron. Every one of them is a piece of line. I put one down and a signalman at Bellhouse sees a lamp go and reads a letter. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it since before the Veil.

"And what I do not need anybody to know is that I can feel where a piece of line is before the lamp goes. Not the letter. The line. Which way the current is running in it tonight and whether there is a lump in it somewhere between here and the Yard." She let go of the frame. "Say your thing."

They had driven in at about four that afternoon. That was not what she had expected to be doing with a night off, and she had not told him it was a night off because he had not asked and she had not said, and the arrangement between the two of them about what was said in vehicles had been made in a cab at the end of a shift and had not been revised since.

The clerk had been a woman of about fifty called Perlmutter who had kept the records for the line for twenty-six years and who had come out to the cabin at four with a folder and an air of doing somebody a service.

She did not open the folder.

"There is an instruction come down from the office this week about records kept at a signal cabin. It is about the register, which is the train book, which is mine and is correct. And it is also about a second book."

Beatrix Halm did not stop breathing, which he noticed, because a person who does not stop breathing has decided something before they speak.

"What second book," she said.

"You have had one in the drawer under the window since about the June before last," said the clerk. "It is not the register. It has no number on it and it is not in the inventory and I have known about it for two years and I have never once asked you about it, and I am not asking you now."

"Then what are you asking."

"The office cannot protect a record it has never been told exists," said the clerk. "That is not the instruction. That is the only sentence I have got and it is mine."

"And the instruction."

"That a person in a service who keeps a record of their own which is not an entry in any book of the service may be required in writing to discontinue it, and is not to be questioned upon the content of it."

"By whom," said Beatrix Halm.

"That is all it says."

"It does not say by whom."

"It does not say by whom," said the clerk, "and I have read it four times and I have decided that this is deliberate and I am not going to tell you what I think it is because I would only be guessing in front of you."

The clerk went down the outside stair.

She shut the door.

Then she stood in front of the frame with her hands behind her back and looked at the thirty-one levers and did not say anything for about a minute and a half.

"Say the thing you have worked out," she said.

"I have not worked anything out. I have been in this cabin for ten minutes and I have not done anything at all."

"You have done about nine seconds' worth and I have watched you do it." She turned round. "He was kind about it. He gave me the folder and did not open it, and he has known about that book for two years and has never asked, and he said one sentence of his own that was not in the instruction, and he said it so that I would know it was his own.

"That is the kindest thing anybody has done for me in about two years and it has been done by a man in a company coat who is frightened, and it is going to cost me a book."

She had not raised her voice once in four months and she was not raising it now.

"Two hours ago I went into a street in the Slough and got a man out of a car and kept him standing," she said. "Three hours ago I had a man in a corridor tell me a thing about my own house. I struck myself off that roll on the second of this month at eleven at night, in a hand, with a date on it. I am not on it. I have not been on anything since the eighth of April."

"I know."

"You do not know what it means and I am going to tell you, and then you are going to be in this cabin and it is going to be too late to be anywhere else.

"That roll is the only thing I have got that an office has to look at before it comes for me. While I am on it, an instruction like the one in that folder has to be answered by somebody with a head of household's name on it, and the head is a man who cleans washrooms and who cannot stop it either, but it has to be asked.

And on the second of this month at about eleven at night I struck myself off it in a hand with a date on it, because I would rather be unfindable and writing than findable and not.

"And that was a correct decision. I still think it was correct. And it has had exactly seven days to be a correct decision and this is what a correct decision looks like: a woman from a records office comes out to a cabin with a folder and is kind about it, and there is not one single thing in this city I can point at and say, and she was kind about it, and she is going to stop asking because she has said the kind sentence."

Beatrix Halm put her hand on the stove.

"I am going to burn the book," she said. "Here. In this stove. Eleven months of it. And first I am going to tell you why I have not written in it since the seventeenth of April, and I am going to tell you the true reason and not the one I have been giving the kitchen."

She turned round.

"The true reason is that I cannot write it. I have sat down nineteen times with the pen and I can feel the line going somewhere out in the city and I cannot get it down on the page.

I have been writing it down since the June before last and it is the only record I have ever made that was mine, and since the second of this month I have been unable to do the one thing it is for,

"and the reason I am unable to do it is that there is a man asleep in a room in Ferrier Street with four of something broken in his side that I found in a hall in March, and I have not been able to write a line since the end of that month."

She said it flat, in the register of a signalman reading a lamp.

"And the reason I have been telling the kitchen is that I did not want to say that out loud in a room with six people in it. So there it is in a cabin at the far end of the line at ten past eleven at night and you can do what you like with it."

He got up off the chair.

"Right," she said. "Here are the things and they are not going to be a speech.

"We are two people who are awake and sober and have not eaten and are not going to be tired, and if either of us is either of those things I will stop, and I have not stopped for anybody in four years and I am not going to start being the woman who has to be asked.

"Nothing is given to either of us tonight. Not a grain, not a cold minute, nothing out of anybody in this quarter. If you put your hand on anything of mine I will put you off this frame and you will go down sixty-one steps and you will not do it twice.

"There is one thing on you I am not going near and I have never asked you about and I am not going to tonight.

"And I am going to do most of this myself, because I am the one who can, and because a man who has been looked after by six other people in four months has been getting up every single time to be helped and I would like to be the last person in this city who does that to him."

She had unbuttoned the front of her tunic before she finished the sentence.

She was not a big woman and she was built out of nine years of standing in one place on a night shift and she had forearms like a woman who pulls iron, and she took the tunic off over her head and dropped it on the floor by the stove because there is nowhere in a cabin to put a tunic.

The stove was still in.

"Right," she said. "Over here. The lamp swings and I am not having you under it. On the floor by the frame, back to the levers, and if you put your hand on a lever I will break it off at the boss and you will go down the outside stair and I will write that up."

He got on the floor with his back to the frame.

"It is not a comfortable floor."

"I am not going to tell you it is. I am going to tell you that it is a floor a signal operator has spent nine years on and that I know every inch of it and that I can see the whole of this room from where I am standing and you cannot see anything, and I am going to use that about four times."

She was not wearing a great deal and she did not take long over it.

"Hands on the frame above your head," she said. "Not on the levers. On the frame. That is iron and it is bolted and it will not move and you will not be able to help yourself with it, and if you try I will make it harder, and I would rather you did not find out how."

Then she came down onto him.

She had not said that she was going to and he had not asked and he understood afterwards, about an hour later, going back over it, that she had done it because she had been in charge of a frame of thirty-one levers for nine years and a cabin is a room where a person who knows the room wins.

She told him what she was doing about nine seconds before she did it every time, and not because she was being careful. She was being accurate.

"Here," she said. "That is the whole of it. You have been going since about the thirteenth of April. Your left side is not like your right side and it is not like the other people in this room have it and I am not going to ask you about it and I am going to keep off it."

"What is on my left side—"

"Do not." She put two fingers under his jaw and turned his head a quarter turn. "Do not tell me. I have been telling people not to ask me about a headache for four years and I know exactly what it costs the other person to be asked and I am not doing it tonight."

She said that at some point in the first ten minutes and he understood it about four minutes later and did not think about it again until the following week.

She was fast and she was not careful, and there was a moment about a third of the way in when the lamp swung on the frame and she stopped and put her hand over his eyes and held it there for about two seconds and took it away, and neither of them said anything about it and that was the only time in the whole hour that either of them was gentle.

"Nine seconds," she said, later, with her mouth against his ear and one hand flat on his chest. "That is what I do. I tell you nine seconds early. It is not a kindness. If a train is coming you get nine seconds and if a man is coming you get nine seconds and it is the same reflex and I have never been able to turn it off and I am not going to try with you tonight."

He put his hands on her and she let him for about a minute and then told him to leave them exactly where they were, which was two places, and he left them, and she used both of them exactly where she had put them and told him so out loud as she did it, in the register of a woman reading a lamp.

The floor was hard and the frame was at his back and the stove was nine inches from his foot and the lamp swung about twice in the hour, and at some point she got up onto her knees and took her weight off him entirely for about four seconds and came back down, and the four seconds were the only four seconds of the night when anything about it was deliberate.

He said her name once, about two thirds of the way through, when she had stopped for a moment and was sitting back on her heels.

"You have not said his," she said.

"No."

"I am not going to make you. And I am going to tell you why I have not said it, because I have been not saying it for about four months now and it has got to that stage where it has its own weight, and if I do not say it out loud tonight it will be a thing I did instead of a thing I decided."

"It has weight."

"It has weight and it is not modesty and it is not what people think it is. A name in this city is a commitment made before the evidence, and there are two answers to what you are and I do not have the one that is right, and I said that to you in a cab at the end of a shift, and you did not take it back and neither did I."

She came down onto him and finished it in about nine minutes with her own hand and then with her mouth and then, when he was finished, with her hand again, and she was unhurried about that half in a way she had not been about the first half, and he understood about two hours later that the second half had not been for her at all.

Then she lay still on the floor of a signal cabin with her head on the frame and her knee against the stove and neither of them said anything for about four minutes.

"I have not been thanked," she said. "I know. I am telling you before you do it so that you can do it anyway and I can be unpleasant about it, and then here is the rest of it.

"Do not thank me for tonight. Not because I did it for you. Because I did it for the line, and a thing done for the line does not get thanked, it gets logged, and there is no book left to log it in."

She got up and put her tunic on and went to the drawer under the window and took out the book.

It was about the size of a hymn book with a soft black cover and no number on it and eleven months of a signal operator's week in it, ruled in her own hand on the first page, with a date at the top of every left-hand page and three lines under it and sometimes four.

She stood in the middle of the cabin with it in both hands for about half a minute.

"You are going to want to read it," she said.

"I am not going to read it."

"I know you are not. That is the whole of what I have wanted to say about you since the first of April and I am not going to make a speech about it at half past midnight in a cabin." She held it out flat.

"I am going to open it at the front and I am going to let you have about four seconds of the first page and then I am going to burn it, and the four seconds are because you have been in this room for an hour and a half and you have not asked me one single question about it in seven weeks, and four seconds is what that is worth and it is more than most people get."

She opened it at the front and held it out.

The first page had a date at the top of it in June and under it, in her hand:

THE ELEVENTH OF JUNE. WEST END, BELLHOUSE TO THE YARD, CLEAR AND WRONG FROM ABOUT ELEVEN. THE WRONG IS NOT THE LINE. IT IS A SHAPE AND IT IS THE SAME SHAPE I SAW ON THE EIGHTEENTH AND IT HAS A DATE IN IT AND I AM WRONG ABOUT THE DATE MOST TIMES.

Then she took it back and opened the stove door.

"Move your foot," she said.

He moved his foot.

She put the book in on its side with the spine in, because a spine burns last, and she put two of the lamp wicks in with it, and she shut the door, and then she stood with her hand flat on the door of the stove for about four seconds the way a person does at a door they have shut on purpose.

Then she opened it and put the match to the lamp wicks.

It went the way paper goes, which is fast and then slow and then a long time at the end with the spine standing up black in the middle of the ash, and the smoke went up the stove pipe and out of a chimney at the far end of the Ferrier line, and outside there was nothing on that road at all and the block instrument ticked over on the shelf and the thirty-one levers stood where they had stood all night.

Beatrix Halm watched the spine go.

"That is eleven months," she said. "And I have got three weeks of nothing in my head instead, and that is what I am going to have for the rest of my working life, and it is going to come to me at four in the morning at the far end of a line with nothing in my hands, and there is not one document in this city that will ever be able to check me on a single one of them."

She shut the stove door and latched it.

"There is one thing I am going to say and then I am going to walk you down the outside stair and you are going to take the half-hourly, because a man waiting for a tram at the end of a line at half past midnight is a man anybody can ask about."

"Say it."

"The ninth of September." She did not look at him. "I told you that in a cab at the end of a shift on this line, and you have not asked me once what it is, and I have noticed every time that you have not asked.

"I am not going to tell you. And I am going to tell you why not, and it is one sentence and then I am done.

"I burned eleven months of the only record of this city I have ever been able to keep, in a stove, in about four minutes, and I am not going to stand in this cabin holding a date that I have never explained to you, because a woman who has just thrown that away has no business holding anything else she cannot say the price of."

She opened the door.

The river was on it and there was wind off the water for the first time since six, and thirty-one levers stood along the north wall with the road clear for the last two miles because there was nothing on it, and it was the quietest he had ever heard her be, and she went down the outside stair ahead of him with her hands in her pockets and she did not say one word on the way down.

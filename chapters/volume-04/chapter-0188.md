# The Unfunded Clinic

The hatch opens at eight and by half past there is a queue along the wall, and on the morning of the eighth there were twenty-one people in it and one of them had a warrant.

He waited his turn.

That was the thing about him.

He came at about ten to eight and he stood at the end of the wall with his hands at his sides and he did not go to the front and he did not ask, and he was there for two hours and forty minutes with a folded paper in his inside pocket

while a woman with a chest that had been going for eleven days was told to come back on the first, and a man who had been waiting for a fortnight was told to come back on the fifth.

Tessa Okonkwo saw him at about half past nine and did not stop and did not hurry and did not look at him again until it was his turn, because a clinician who looks at a man with a warrant in a queue is a clinician who has told every person behind him what he is.

At eleven minutes past ten she said, "Next."

He put the folded paper on the counter, unfolded it, and turned it round.

It was one side of a sheet with a form number and a date on it, and the date was the seventh of this month, which was yesterday, and the sentence on it was longer than the notice that had come up on the wire on the twenty-eighth of April.

An establishment recorded in connection with the file of a member of the Bureau, and not otherwise funded by the Bureau, in which that member is entered as having been treated. Such an establishment is required to furnish a schedule of persons treated. The requirement takes effect on the date of this sheet.

"There is no name on it," she said.

"No," said the man.

"There is no address on it either."

"No."

"Then say what it is for and say it in one sentence, because you have been in this queue for two hours and forty minutes and I would like to be useful before it is my fault."

"I am to furnish a schedule of persons treated for an establishment in this quarter," he said, "and there are about eleven unfunded establishments in this quarter, and that is the description under which you come."

Tessa Okonkwo read it a second time.

She read it standing up with the queue behind her and she did it in about eleven seconds and then she set it down square on the counter with her hand flat on it.

"You have narrowed eleven to one."

"That is not mine to say."

"It is not yours and it is mine, and I worked it out on the morning of the twenty-eighth of this month past in about a minute, and you did not work it out at all." She did not raise her voice and she did not smile. "Do you know what you are holding?"

"A sheet."

"You are holding the reason a man is entered in a file about a building, and the man is a member of the Bureau.

"He is in my chart. My chart went into a wing file on the morning of the seventh of April and three weeks later a woman in an establishment section read the front of it, saw the second name on it, and sent you to a counter at ten to eight in the morning to wait in a queue for two hours and forty minutes."

She took the sheet off the counter and turned it over and showed him the back, which was blank.

"That is not a name and it is worse than a name. A name I could refuse in front of twenty-one people and you would have to write that down. This I cannot refuse, because there is exactly one establishment in this quarter it fits, and a thing that is true and not answered gets decided by somebody who has never been to this building."

"Then I will wait for the schedule."

"You will wait for it for a while."

She went through to the front room and got a sheet of her own and came back and did it at the counter, in the queue, with twenty-one people watching, which is not how anybody does this and which took her about four minutes.

She wrote:

PERSONS TREATED AT THIS ESTABLISHMENT ON AN ORDINARY DAY: BETWEEN TWO HUNDRED AND THREE HUNDRED. NO SCHEDULE OF PERSONS TREATED EXISTS. A DAY BOOK EXISTS AND IS KEPT IN THIS BUILDING AND IS PRODUCED TO A PERSON HOLDING A WARRANT ON THE PRODUCTION OF THIS SHEET.

NO PERSON TREATED WILL BE NAMED TO ANY PERSON WHO HAS NOT COME THROUGH THIS HATCH. NO PERSON TREATED WILL BE NAMED IN ANY DOCUMENT THAT LEAVES THIS BUILDING. THAT INCLUDES YOU AND IT INCLUDES THE ESTABLISHMENT.

THE MEMBER NAMED IN THE REQUIREMENT WAS TREATED AT THIS COUNTER ON THE SEVENTH OF THIS MONTH PAST AT ABOUT FIVE TO TWELVE AT NIGHT, IN A WASHROOM ON THE SIXTH FLOOR OF A BUILDING IN WHICH HE WORKS, ON MY CHART, AT MY INSISTENCE, AND HE WAS NOT A PATIENT OF MINE AND I HAVE SAID SO ON THE CHART.

T. OKONKWO. CLINICIAN OF THE HATCH. HOURS FROM EIGHT UNTIL FOUR ON EVERY DAY THE HATCH IS OPEN.

She signed it and dated it and put the hour on it and gave it to him across the counter with both hands.

"Now you can take that to whatever office sent you and you will not have to come back to this counter again," she said, "and if somebody in that office wants the day book they come here themselves, in daylight, and they stand in this queue, and I will hand it to them across this counter and they can read it in this room."

The man looked at the sheet for about four seconds.

"You have just confirmed it," he said.

"I have just answered it. Those are different and you will find out which one they are in about four days, and if I had not answered it then in about four weeks somebody would have written my name on it in a hand that had not been to this building." She took the queue's turn. "Next. You have been in my queue two hours and forty minutes and you were very good about it and I have noticed and I am not going to say so twice."

He went.

She closed the hatch for eleven minutes and then opened it again, and she did not go into the back room, and she wrote on the front of the counter with a card and a pen.

She put it on the counter and stood back and looked at it.

It said:

THE CLINICIAN OF THIS HATCH IS TESSA OKONKWO. THIS BUILDING IS AT THE VESTRY AND IT IS OPEN FROM EIGHT UNTIL FOUR EVERY DAY THIS HATCH IS OPEN. A PERSON WITH A WARRANT MAY ASK ME ANYTHING YOU CAN ASK A BUILDING.

She left it there.

The woman who comes to that counter every morning had read the card from the end of the queue and she was still standing there at a quarter to twelve when the room had emptied.

"Does that mean I have to give my name now," she said.

"No."

"Because it says a person with a warrant may ask you anything you can ask a building, and I have been coming here since before the war with the fever and I have never given my name to anybody in this building, not once, not even when the man came with the paper in the winter."

"You do not have to give your name," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and there is a card on that counter because there is one document in this city now that says this building exists and says a woman runs it, and it does not have a box in it for you and it is not going to acquire one."

"That is all I wanted to ask."

"Then go home and come back at eight."

The woman went home and came back at eight, which she had done every morning for nine years, and she did not look at the card again, which is the correct way for a card of that kind to work.

For nine years that hatch had been a place where two hundred and three hundred people a day came who were not on a plate and where not one of them had ever been asked to give a name to anybody, and it had worked on the whole of that arrangement, and the arrangement had been that the building itself was not in anything.

From the eighth of this month there was a card on the counter with a woman's name on it, and a person with a warrant could come and be told where she was and what hours she kept and what she would answer for, and what she would not answer for was the only thing in the building that had ever been worth anything.

"Twenty-one people watched me do that," she said to the room at about half past four, at the bench outside with the brass plate on the end of it, which is a bench he had never once read the plate on and had decided a long time ago that he would read.

"You are going to say something about the fourth line."

"The fourth line is the one you should be angry about and you are not, and you should be, because in about four days there is going to be a sentence in a district file that says a band-five member of the Bureau was treated at a hatch in the Vestry on the seventh of April at five to midnight in a washroom on the sixth floor of his own place of work."

"That is what it says."

"That is what it says and it is the first document in this city that has put you and that building in one sentence in anything, and I wrote it, and I have been aware of it since about eleven o'clock this morning and I chose to be aware of it at eleven o'clock this morning instead of at four in the afternoon, and that is the entire difference between a clinician and a person who signs things."

She picked up the bench's brass plate off her knee, which she had done on the twenty-eighth without noticing she had picked it up.

"And I am going to say the last of it and then I am going to open the hatch tomorrow at eight.

"Nine years this building has been unfunded and unentered, and I have told myself every month of the nine years that that was a virtue, and it was not a virtue, it was a hiding place, and I have been the one putting people in it, and about two hundred and three hundred people a day have been trusting a woman with no standing to be the reason they were not written down.

"And from today that woman has a name on a card and a schedule with a number on it and a member of the Bureau in a sentence with her in it, and I can stand at that counter and be asked about anything except the people who come to it, and that is the strongest thing this building has ever had, and I would like it noticed that I got it by answering a notice with no name on it in four minutes in a queue."

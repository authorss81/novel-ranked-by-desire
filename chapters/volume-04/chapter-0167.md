# Beatrix Halm

She came off the line at half past six on the seventeenth and she had the visitors' book in her bag, and she had told not one person in that house that she was taking it out.

She had put it in her bag at ten past five in the afternoon, while six women were in the room, and had walked out with it under a coat, and that had taken her about nine seconds of deciding and the whole afternoon of feeling sick about it.

The counting had started on the day before last and had gone on for two days.

It was not difficult work. A hundred and eleven entries, forty-one of them asking after a person, and she had read them all out loud four times in a week and had told them at that table on the eleventh that she was starting to hear the surnames before she got to them.

She had not been exaggerating and she had not been mad. She had got a handbill out of a bin at the end of the platform, the back of one, and she had written the forty-one surnames on it in the order they appeared with a mark against each one the second time it came, and by the time she had done the thirty-first she had six marks and by the thirty-eighth she had nine.

Forty-one names. Eleven surnames, and the eleven between them accounted for thirty-four of the forty-one, and every one of the eleven had come to that gate more than once, and the busiest of them had come four times between the eighth of February and the twelfth of March, which is a month, which is the rate of a man who is not looking.

Not one of the eleven was in the seventeen.

That was the part she could not get past. Whoever was asking had not asked about the household once. He had asked about a woman, at that gate, more than once, over a period, and had never once asked whether there was a house.

At half past eight that night she went into Bellhouse.

There is a room off Ash Row with a woman at the door and a card, and it is neither of the two things a stranger assumes.

The woman at the door has been at that door for eleven years and has never once asked a man where he works. On a week when the yards pay there are forty people in that room, and it is the only room in Ostara where forty strangers sit and listen to a woman read out loud from a piece of paper.

She put a shilling on the table and said she wanted the room for about an hour and that she was not selling anything.

"Mind your own business," said the woman at the door, and took the shilling.

She stood on a table, which she had not expected to do and which she did badly.

She read the whole hundred and eleven. She read the dates. She read the entries as they had been written, in Ola Bright's copy hand, because Ola Bright had been keeping it since the house above the chop-house took callers and the entries had a sound to them, and a room of forty people in Bellhouse at half past nine at night can hear a hand.

She read the forty-one asking after a person first, because she wanted to see them react.

Most of them did not. Most people in a room like that do not react to a list of other people because they have not got a stake in it, and eleven of them went still at a name, in the ordinary way, and she marked every one of those eleven down and she got seven of her eleven.

The eighth one came at the thirty-eighth entry.

A man on her left, at the back, at a table with his coat still on, put his hand flat on the table.

"That is my sister," he said, not loudly. "That is the thirty-eighth one. She went there in February and I have not seen her since and I did not know that she had written anything down."

The room heard him. She read on, because stopping would have made it worse, and she finished the whole hundred and eleven in about eleven minutes, and the woman at the door took the shilling's worth of nothing off the table and put it in a drawer and that was that.

He was on the step when she came out.

He was about fifty and he had a band four and he was not a fool, and he was shaking, and he said: "You read that out in a room."

"Yes."

"You read a list out in a room that had my sister in it."

"Yes, and I am sorry about your sister, and I am not going to pretend that is why I did it."

He looked at her for a while. She had the book under her arm and she did not put it behind her, and he could see what it was, and it was not much bigger than a hymn book.

"Who are you."

"I am not going to tell you that."

"That is a stupid answer and you have given it to me anyway, and it tells me you are somebody who is in it and not somebody who was sent." He put his hands in his pockets. "I have been buying a paper about that street for five weeks."

"I know you have. I know how many times."

"Then you know it is not only me. There are eleven of us."

There were eleven of them and Beatrix had known that for two days, and hearing it in the mouth of a man on a step in Bellhouse was a different thing entirely from a mark on a handbill at the end of a platform.

"How many times each," she said.

"Three of us four times. Four of us three times. Two of us twice, and one of us once, and that one is me." He said it without any pride in it at all. "I have bought it since the twelfth of March because that is when it started being for sale, and I have read it at a table in my own front room, and I have not known one thing in it except that it exists."

"What do you want."

"I want to know if my sister is all right."

"Then go and knock on the door."

"I have not knocked on the door." He said it as though the sentence were a different shape. "I have been at that gate once and I was told there was no landing, and I have believed it for two months because a stranger on a step told me there was not."

She got in at about ten past one.

Six of them were at the table because it is a house where six people are always at the table, and Runa Sato was asleep sitting up with her arms folded, which she had not done before.

Beatrix Halm put the visitors' book in the middle of the table with the spine cracked and said, "I have been out since half past eight and I took that book out of this house without telling any of you, and I am going to say what I found and then somebody is going to tell me what to do about it, and I want to say first that I have done a stupid thing and I would do it again and those two facts are both true."

She told them about the handbill and the eleven surnames.

She told them about the thirty-eighth entry and the man on her left and the flat hand on the table.

"And there are eleven of them," she said. "Eleven households in this city have bought a shilling's worth of paper about this street between the twelfth of March and the fourteenth of April, and every one of them has come to that gate at least once, and not one of them has ever asked whether there is a house. He said that. He said they have not known one thing in it except that it exists."

The room had been still since about half past ten.

"Say the rest of it," said Sun Meiling, from the stair.

"There is no rest of it. That is all of it and it is not enough to do anything with." Beatrix Halm put both hands flat on the book. "Which is why I read it out in a room in Bellhouse, and I will tell you exactly why, because you are all going to work it out inside about four minutes and I would rather it came from me.

"Forty people in a room do not react the way a page does. A page can be bought and read at a table and thrown away, and not one reader knows whose mouth it came out of. Forty people in a room hear a voice.

"And I have just given that document a voice, and the voice is mine, and I work a signal cabin at the far end of the Ferrier line, and there are two people in Bellhouse who have been in that room and one of them knows the name of every cabin on that line because he prints the timetable."

Marguerite Vale said, "Say what you have cost."

"I have cost you the visitors' book."

"You have not."

"I have cost you the only thing in this city in which six women are the same six women all the way back. A woman who knows what an audit does said so at this table the day before yesterday.

"And it is now a thing that has been read aloud in a public room by one of the six." Beatrix Halm's voice had not changed once in twenty minutes and it changed now. "I heard it on the eleventh. I wrote it down.

"I did it in a room in Bellhouse with forty people in it, to find out which line of it moves a room. It has moved a room. It is my mouth now."

Runa Sato unfolded her arms.

"What can be done about him."

"That is the part I have had since half past ten on a wet street," said Beatrix Halm, "and I would rather say it out loud than have anybody find out that I have been sitting on it.

"Nothing. There is not one thing in this city we can serve him with. He is not the Bureau and he has not written to the Reading and he has not asked a division for anything, and he has not broken a rule, because a man may stand at a gate and ask whether a woman is all right and there is no instrument anywhere in this city that makes him answer.

"I can threaten him. Ines can go to a counter and ask whether he has bought an exhibit and a clerk will tell her that a shilling's worth of public paper has been sold to a member of the public and will not tell her to whom, because it does not know. Marguerite can price him and a price only works on a thing that can be sold and there is nothing here to sell him.

"And we cannot stop him, and we cannot frighten him, and we cannot refuse him, because a man who is refused at a gate comes back, and a man who comes back twice can tell anybody he wants to that there are six women at that address and one of them is asleep in a chair at one in the morning."

The range let go of its heat.

"What happens," said Nadia Ferrous, "is that he comes."

"He comes," said Runa Sato.

She had not been asleep. That was clear from the way she said it.

"He comes to that gate, and he is entirely within his rights, and he is not a villain and he is not a fool and he has a sister who went there in February and he has a shilling's worth of paper, and every one of us in this room is going to spend four months making sure we cannot be found, and there is a man in this city who has spent five weeks trying to find out whether his sister is all right.

"That is the whole of it. That is the shape of the thing. There is no version of this in which we are the wrong ones."

Sun Meiling came down three steps.

"Then we answer him," she said.

"We do not have to."

"I am not offering it as a position. I am telling you what the instruction says, because it is in five hands in this room and it is in a pencil on that landing, and it is due to be used." She had her arms folded. "A paper comes for this house. It comes to me. I answer it. I do not ask anybody else in this house what the answer is.

"That man is going to walk up that gate with a piece of paper in his hand and the instruction will say what happens next, and none of us will have decided anything, because we did not decide it. We decided it on the tenth of this month and we did not decide it for an office."

Runa Sato got up and went to the window and stood at it with her back to the room, which Nadia Ferrous had done once, on the eighth of this month, and which had been noticed by everybody then.

"Say it out loud, though," Runa Sato said, to the glass. "All of us. Say the part where a woman who is not on a roll and not in a file and not in a service, and who signed a stub on a doorstep on the thirteenth, is the woman who answers a private man at a gate about his sister.

"Say that out loud, because on Thursday morning somebody in an office is going to look at a return with six answers in it and see a name against a signature at half past ten, and they are going to work out that this address has one point at which every paper has to go."

She turned round.

"And say that this time I am not the reason, and that I have been off a roster since ten past two on the twelfth, and that the one who answers it now is a woman who spent four months making sure there was nothing to answer, and that in four days she is the only door."

The room was quiet.

Beatrix Halm said, "I did a stupid thing on a wet street tonight and I would do it again and both of those are true. I am going to say the other half of it so that not one of you has to work it out.

"If he comes tomorrow, I am the one who goes to the gate. I read it out in a room, so it is mine, and I am the one with a mouth that forty people in Bellhouse have heard attach itself to a list, and there is no version of tomorrow where the woman on the other side of that gate is somebody else."

# To Be Withdrawn

The roll had been on the wall of that landing for two days and it had not been taken down once, and it had been looked at by four people who lived in the building and by a man from a gas office who came up for a different reason and stood in front of it for a while.

Beatrix Halm came off the line at half past six and went straight up the sixty-one steps instead of down to the room she slept in, which she had not done before in four months.

She had her hand on the rail at the eighth.

"Take me off it," she said.

The landing did not answer him.

"Not this evening," said Beatrix Halm. "This evening it is a thing I have said in a room. I want it struck tonight, in ink, with the date on it, and I want one of you to initial it, and I do not care which one of you and I would rather it was not him."

"Why," said Sun Meiling, from the fourth stair.

"Because I have worked it out and none of you are going to like it, so I am going to say it in the order it has to be said in and I am going to say the bad part first and I am not going to be tender about it, because five of you have been being tender with me since December and I have been sick of it."

She put her bag down on the third step and turned round and looked at the board.

"That thing is two days old. It has my name on it in a column, and a standing, and a band, and a number in the left-hand rule, and above all of it a man's name in his own hand.

And in about fifty-five days it is going to be certified and go to an office, and from that day there is a document in this city that says that six women live at a known address in Ferrier Street and can be produced through a man who cleans washrooms at the Fourth Branch.

"And I am the only one of the six of you who writes things down."

The landing did not move.

"I have written them down since June last year," said Beatrix Halm. "Eleven months. Every week. What the line is doing and where it is wrong and which way the current has gone in a quarter, in a book I bought at a bookseller in Cordwain with my own money, and it is at the end of my own line and no part of the tramway has ever asked to see it, because a signal operator does not keep a book.

"A signal operator keeps a register. The register is the trains. That is the whole of what the office knows I have, and it is the only document about me that anybody has ever had to produce, and it says where a tram was at six o'clock."

She picked her bag up again.

"On the seventeenth of last month I stood on a table in a room off Ash Row and read a hundred and eleven names out loud to forty strangers, because a document that has been read in a room has a voice, and the voice is mine.

"And there is a man in Bellhouse who prints the timetable for that line and he knows the name of every cabin on it, including the one at the far end, and I did that on purpose on the seventeenth and I would do it again and both of those are true, and I have been not thinking about it for two days because it is easier not to."

"Stay on it," said Runa Sato.

"No."

"You stay on it. That is the whole of what it is for. A head of household answers for the premises and the premises are asked before anybody in them is taken, and if you are not on it there is nothing at the top of you, and a requisition of a person is served on a person and there is no head to answer it first."

"That is true," said Beatrix Halm. "That is entirely true and I have had two days to think about it and it is the reason I want off."

The kitchen door opened and four of them came out onto the landing because the landing is where anything that matters in that house happens.

"Say the reason," said Ines Duarte, from the fourth stair.

"The roll is the only thing in this city that pairs an address with a person in nine minutes," said Beatrix Halm. "That is what it is. It is not a shield, it is a road, and it is a road with a house at the end of it, and I have spent four years being a person who cannot be produced, and two days ago a man wrote his name at the top of it in nine seconds and made the road.

"And I am the only one of the six who has a second address. Not a plate. An occupation. A woman at the end of a line who writes down where the city's current is wrong, and who is deaf in one ear so that on a busy evening she cannot hear herself think, and who has been writing that book down for eleven months with the date and the hour and the section of line, in a hand that gets steadier when it is bad."

"So it is a fact," said Sun Meiling, "that you are not findable without a roll. And that is why I have refused to be on one, and you have been listening to me for four months."

"Yes."

"And you have understood it better than the rest of this room has understood it," said Sun Meiling, "and you have also worked out that it does not apply to you, because you are not refusing a document. You are writing one."

"Yes," said Beatrix Halm.

Marguerite Vale had come up last and had not said anything, and she said it now.

"Price it," she said.

"I have priced it. It is a hundred and eleven and seventeen and a timetable and a book, and I have worked out the order of it." Beatrix Halm did not turn round. "Marguerite, you are the only person in this house who can tell me what a roll costs a person who has one, and I already know, and I want to be off it before somebody in an office works out what it costs a person who has been taken off it."

The landing went quiet in a way it does not usually go quiet.

"So there it is," said Beatrix Halm. "The whole of the argument, in one sentence, and you can have it now and you can all try it.

"I am asking to be a person who cannot be found, on purpose, after four months of a household that has been finding itself on purpose, and I am not going to be argued out of it in this kitchen, and I would like that written down as having been tried."

Nadia Ferrous came down two steps.

"You cannot," she said.

"I have just said I am going to and not one word has been said about how."

"You cannot, because the roll is not a list of members, it is a list of the premises." Nadia was not being loud, which was the worst sign.

"Beatrix, I put forty-one names in a document on purpose in March and I have been waiting since the fourteenth of March for somebody to write down that I chose it, and there is not one line in any paper in this city that says I chose it, and it is the single thing I have ever done that I would do again, and it has cost me forty-one people who did not consent to being in a list.

"And the way it gets fixed is not by coming off. It is by being in one. If there is a document anywhere that I am in, then there is a document that says I chose it, because a person who is on a roll has chosen to be on a roll, and that is the only sentence in this city that puts my name and my decision in the same place."

Nadia stopped.

"So come off," she said. "Come off and be a woman on a roll once, in a week, in front of nine operators, who put her hand to it and said why. And be unfindable after that, with a document in Cobb Street that says she was findable on purpose once. That is a different thing entirely and I know which one of the two I want."

Beatrix Halm stood on the eighth step and did not answer that for about eleven seconds.

"That is a good argument," she said.

"It is."

"It is the best one anybody has made at me about anything in four months." She put her hand flat on the rail. "And it is in the wrong direction, and here is why, and then I am going to bed.

"Nadia, you handed over a page and the page is what people are looking at. When a person is on a roll, the roll is what people are looking at. I do not want to be looked at. I have been looked at for four years by people who wanted me to stop having the headaches, and in March I got up on a table and made forty strangers look at me on purpose, and that was worth it because it was about a list and not about me.

"And in two days there is going to be a piece of paper in a district with six names on it and a man's name at the top of it, and the only thing on that piece of paper that has not been in an office before is me. In a week I will be a woman on a board who can be produced.

In a month somebody in the tramway who reads a book about the current will want to know what a person at that address knows about where the current goes wrong, and he will have my cabin, because there is a printer in Bellhouse who knows the name of every cabin on that line and it is my fault that he does."

She stopped on the ninth step.

"I have been not writing things down since the seventeenth of last month and I have got a fortnight of missing pages, and it is the worst I have felt since December, and I would rather be unfindable and writing than findable and not."

The landing did not say anything for a while.

"Say the thing you are all thinking," said Beatrix Halm. "Somebody say it."

"He has been the least findable person in this city for four years," said Ines Duarte, from the fourth stair, "and it cost him nothing and it cost every one of you something, and he has now made himself the most findable man in it in one line in a clerk's hand, and he did that in nine seconds and did not tell five of you."

"I am not asking to be like him," said Beatrix Halm.

"I know that you are not. That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I am not going to make a speech on a landing at seven in the evening." Ines Duarte came down two steps.

"Struck in ink, dated, and initialled by one person, and the person is not you and is not him, and I will do it, and I will do it in my own hand because I am the only one in this house who files anything and there is no point in the only one pretending."

"Tomorrow morning," said Beatrix Halm.

"Tomorrow morning," said Ines Duarte.

"Not tomorrow morning. Tonight, and before any of us goes to bed, because if it is done tonight then I have chosen it and not arranged it, and if it is done in the morning then I have had a night to think about it and I do not want to have had a night to think about it."

She took her coat off and hung it over the back of a chair.

"Sit down, all of you," said Ines Duarte. "I am not doing this at a table with a queue at the door."

It was done at about eleven that night, at the top of the landing, with the door open behind her so that anybody who came up could watch it being done, which was not required and which she did anyway.

She struck one rule from the left margin to the right edge of the sixth row and put the date against it and initialled underneath, and then she wrote four words at the end of the row in the same hand: WITHDRAWN BY THE MEMBER.

Then she stood back and looked at it.

The board had five names on it and a head above them and two rows at the bottom with numbers against them and nothing in the other three columns.

"She is not on it," said Sun Meiling.

"No," said Ines Duarte.

"Then it is on that wall that she is not on it, in a hand, with a date on it, which is more of a record than a blank ever was, and either of us can say so out loud and neither of us wants to."

"That is correct," said Ines Duarte, "and it is hers and not mine, and I am not going to argue with her about it after I have written it down."

Beatrix Halm looked at it for about four seconds.

"Right," she said. "Now say the other part, Ines, because it is true and it is the price and I want it said to my face and not at a counter in four days."

"There is no certificate yet," said Ines Duarte. "A return opens sixty days and the certificate at the end of them is what makes it a household, and until that paper exists you are not behind anything. You are simply a person who is not in it. Anybody may serve a requisition at this address from this evening and there is nothing above you but a row with a rule through it."

"Good," said Beatrix Halm.

She went down sixty-one steps at about a quarter to midnight and slept, which she had not expected to do.

The light came across that landing at that hour and stopped about two inches above the seventh row, and the line Ines Duarte had drawn was the lowest legible thing on the board, and it had been on the wall for two days.

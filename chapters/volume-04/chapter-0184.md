# The Long Form

He had been in the building a dozen times in four years and had never once been above the second floor, and on the morning of the fourth he went up at twenty past nine in his ordinary clothes with a coat on, and a man at the second-floor counter read the plate he put down and then read his face.

"Sit there," the man said. "The long one. Four pages. It takes about forty minutes and you cannot take it away and bring it back."

The long one is what the counters call it. Every composition return in this city is one sheet and can be done standing up. The certification schedule is four pages and has to be completed in a room in front of a clerk, because a certification is not a return. A return is an assertion about a premises. A certification is a thing the Office grants, and the Office grants it by reading four pages in front of itself and writing down that it did.

The room was on the second floor and it was about ten feet by fourteen and it had a table, two chairs, a window with the blind half down, a clock on the wall that was about a minute fast, and a clerk called Doyle who had been doing it for about thirty years.

"You have been in here before," he said.

"No."

"You have not." Doyle squared the four pages and put a clip on the top one. "Everybody says that. Not one of them has."

Page one wanted the premises. The street, the number, what was over the shop, how many rooms, and the number of persons of reading age who keep an address at the premises. He wrote six.

"Five," he said. "I saw the roll."

"You saw the roll on the landing and the roll is four days old and it has a line through the sixth row with a date on it. The premises answer is not the roll." Doyle did not write anything down and he did not look up.  "You are the head and you are answering a question about the premises and the premises answer is how many persons of reading age keep an address there. Six is correct and the roll is not the instrument.

You have had a stroke of the pen and I am going to let you have it twice."

"Then six," he said, "and one person is not on the roll."

"That is a permitted answer and there is a line for it and you are the first person in four years who has used it." Doyle wrote one word in the margin. "Carry on."

Page one went on to the head of the household. A name, which he wrote in his own hand, and then a plate number, which he read off the plate he had put down, and then a standing.

Doyle stopped.

"The figure is not required," he said.

"It is optional and I want you to hear the sentence before you put anything in it, because about one in four of the heads who come through this room leaves it out,

"and every one of them who leaves it out gets it filled in by the office out of what the office holds, which is your plate, which is in the socket this morning, which is a number you did not choose and cannot argue with and will be quoted back at you in whatever office cares to ask."

He wrote it.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

Doyle copied it onto the sheet and then did a thing with the corner of the sheet that he had clearly done nine thousand times, which was to put his thumb at a line lower down and run it back up, and his thumb stopped about a third of the way.

"That is band five," he said.

"Yes."

"That is three grains above a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, and it is the whole of what you have, and I am going to say the rest of it once and then I am going to stop saying it because it is not my job to sell you a thing."

He put the pen down.

"A certification does not make a household strong. A certification makes a household askable. And when a member of yours is requisitioned, the requisition is served on the head first, and the head may be asked, and the head's privilege for being asked is the band on this sheet. That is the whole of the mechanism and there is nothing else in it.

"Everyone at that address is standing behind eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight grains a week, and it has been that way since the beginning and it is not going to be a different way by the tenth of June or any other day in this city."

"That is what I bought," he said.

"That is what everybody buys," said Doyle. "I have watched nine hundred households come through this room and not one of them has ever objected to the figure, and about one in three of them have not understood that it is the figure."

Page two was the members.

It wanted them numbered, and then a name, and then a standing, and then a band, and then the date each of them began to keep an address at the premises.

He had five to write, because the sixth row had a line through it and a date on it and four words at the end of it in Ines Duarte's hand. He wrote five names in five columns and he did not ask one of them anything, and the reason he did not ask was a sentence in pencil on a landing wall written on the tenth of April: answer it in writing in your own hand and do not consult each other.

That sentence was for papers that came to the house. This form had not come to the house. It had come to him, in his own coat, twenty-five feet from a corridor he had walked on the twenty-fourth of April.

He wrote what he had heard them say about themselves in rooms, and it was not what any of them would have written, and he knew that inside about two minutes of the second name.

A service file that goes to a counter. A clinic that is unfunded and a chart in a Wing file. Two rooms on Lantern Row kept for eleven years and a plate of her own. A guild and a set of terms declared off on the fourteenth of March. A Field division at Cordwain.

The last of those he wrote because it was written on the roll, and it was on the roll because a woman in that house had written it in her own hand on the sixteenth of April because it was true, and it had stopped being true at ten past two in the afternoon on the twelfth.

He looked at it for about four seconds.

Then he signed it.

"Not that one," said Doyle.

"It is on the roll."

"It is on the roll and the roll is four days old and that line is under a correction that is due at this office on the seventh, and I have got a date on it in my tray with a name on it." Doyle wrote nothing.  "Sign it. I am required to enter what is on the sheet in front of me.

I am not permitted to enter what I think about it afterwards, and I have thought about this one and it is not going to be in anything I write."

He signed it.

Then Doyle initialled every one of the five and read the page back at him in the voice of a man reading out a form, and at the end of it he said, "That member's standing is entered as at the twenty-seventh of April and I have entered it as at the twenty-seventh of April.

When the correction comes on the seventh I will enter the corrected one as at the seventh and both dates will be on the sheet and anybody who reads it in nine years can see that there were two."

"That matters," he said.

"It matters enormously," said Doyle, "and it is the only protection this office offers anybody and it is offered by accident."

Page three was the premises and the page four was the signature and the date and the hour.

It took forty-one minutes and he was in the room for fifty.

At the door of the room Doyle put the four pages into a tray and clipped them and wrote on the top sheet, in the ordinary way, that he had attended at the second-floor counter of the Reading at twenty past nine in the morning of the fourth of this month and that the form was complete.

"You are not going to be able to give that back," Doyle said. "I would like to say that to you at the door and not in the room, because in the room I am doing a job and at the door I am being decent, and you have about ten years of being the sort of man that office clerks notice."

"I know," he said. "Somebody told me on the twenty-fifth."

"Then somebody did their job." Doyle picked the tray up.  "The certificate runs sixty days from the twenty-seventh of April. You are welcome to work that out on a stair.

I have worked it out for about nine hundred people and I have never once been asked what it comes to, and I am not going to tell you this time either, except to say that it is not in this month and it is not in the next one, and that everything you have bought so far is going to sit on a tray in this building until it is not on a tray."

He went down the public stair and out into the street at about a quarter past ten, in daylight, in a street with trams on it and a thousand people in it, and he was the least remarkable thing on the pavement.

There is now a piece of paper in this city, in a clerk's hand, saying that on the morning of the fourth of this month a man came into a building a mile and a half from his front door and sat in a room for fifty minutes with four sheets of a form in front of him and a name at the top of it that is his own.

Ten days ago he had stood in a corridor on the third floor of that building for ninety seconds with a card in his hand, and had put it back at the same angle with the stub facing out, and had left, and there had been a figure against a column headed persons off the public stair and it had been two.

The figure had not been him. He had not gone up the public stair. He had gone in the main door at a quarter past ten on a working morning, with his coat on, and asked at a counter for a form.

He had not asked for a card. He had asked to be entered, and a woman had written down that he had come, and that was a different act by a different man in a different door, and it cost him nothing at all, and it cannot be given back, and it is the only thing he has done in five years that he cannot undo.

One hundred and eighteen lines on the ledger, and nine of them turned, and the ninth still himself, and nothing spent on anybody in this month at all, and the promise made in a hall in March still standing and untested by anybody with a hand.

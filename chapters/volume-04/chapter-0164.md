# Nadia Ferrous

She came off the cars at ten past two in front of nine operators, which she did once a week and had done for four months, because a clerk in the Reading wrote to her service every month and asked her to identify the operator of a lift, and Nadia Ferrous had decided a long time ago that if she was going to be looked at she was going to be looked at in front of people.

She did not go home at ten past two. She went up to Cobb Street and she stood at a counter on the public side of a grille for about a quarter of an hour and worked out that the exhibit was a shilling, and then she paid it.

The clerk was about sixty and had a bad cough and did not look up twice.

"Exhibit roll, the twenty-ninth of March, the sitting in the Cobb Street hall."

"That one."

"I have read it over a girl's shoulder at the yards in March," Nadia said. "I would like to buy one."

"It is a shilling. There is no name taken on it and there never has been, because it is a proved exhibit and a proved exhibit is public paper."

"So you sell it to anybody."

"I sell it to whoever stands there." He pushed a sheet across, face up, and it was warm, and that was the first thing she noticed. "It is set up fresh each morning. That is the only thing about it that is worth a shilling."

She took it out into the street and sat on the low wall at the end of the arcade where the trams come through and read the whole of it in about eleven minutes.

It was nine items on two sides. Item four was the one everybody in the building above a chop-house in Ferrier Street had been dreading for a month, and it said exactly what a clerk had said it would say, in the flat register of an office that had never seen any of them.

SIX LINES A DAY IN SIX HANDS SINCE THE TWENTY-FIRST OF MARCH. NO SIGNATURE ON ANY OF THEM. A HOUSEHOLD OF WHICH THE COMPOSITION IS NOT DISCLOSED.

And under item four, because the household had done the arithmetic out loud in a hall on the fourteenth of March and had not worked out that arithmetic was part of the exhibit, three more lines.

VISITORS TO THE PREMISES: ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN. ASKING AFTER A PERSON: FORTY-ONE. ASKING AFTER THE HOUSEHOLD: SEVENTEEN.

Nadia Ferrous read those three lines four times.

Then she turned the sheet over, because a man who has spent four months on a stage puts his hand on the back of a thing before he reads the front of it again, and there was more.

The back of it was half the exhibit. It was the standing of the exhibit, and it was set out as a date and a line, the way a till roll is set out, and it ran from the twenty-ninth of March down to the line at the bottom, which was the fourteenth of this month.

TWELFTH. MEMBER SATO, R. — IN THE MATTER OF A WRITTEN DISOBEDIENCE. MEMBER DECLINED A COUNT ENTERED BY THE MEMBER. COUNT INCOMPLETE.

THIRTEENTH. PREMISES ABOVE THE CHOP-HOUSE, FERRIER STREET. NOTICE OF STANDING SERVED. RECEIVED BY S. MEILING AT HALF PAST TEN.

She read the second line nine times.

A man had stood on a step at half past ten yesterday morning and put a stub on a half-door and held it flat with his own hand against the wind, and she had watched that from the third step of a stair in a house in Ferrier Street, and the whole of it had taken about ninety seconds and had not touched anything else in her life.

It had taken ninety seconds yesterday and it was in print this morning and it was for sale at a shilling and the only thing on the sheet that said S. Meiling was a surname and an hour.

She got up off the wall and walked to the gate of the arcade and stood there for a while with it in her hand.

Then she went back and bought a second one, and the clerk took the shilling without looking up and did not ask her why.

"You can have a third if you want one," he said.

"Would you stop me."

"There is no rule that stops it. It is a shilling and it is set up fresh each morning, and if you came in here every morning for a year you would be the biggest customer I have and I would still not have your name." He coughed. "That is the whole design of it, and not one clerk on this counter is proud of it. A proved exhibit that sat unbought would be a waste of a clerk's morning. It is meant to be bought."

She carried two of them home in a paper bag and came in at about half past four and put one of them face up in the middle of the kitchen table with the back of it showing.

"That is us," she said. "That is a shilling and that is the whole of the last four months. Every line in the top half of it is a thing we said out loud in a room with two hundred people in it, and every line in the bottom half is a thing we did at this table, and both halves are for sale in Cobb Street and there is not one name on either of them that is any good."

The room said nothing.

"Runa was entered as refusing at ten past two yesterday afternoon in a room that four paid people sat in, and it was in print this morning before I had finished my breakfast."

She put her finger on the line. "Sun, this line is you. Yesterday. Half past ten. It took ninety seconds and it cost you four months, and it was set in type some time after that and it went on sale for a shilling, and the man who set the type has never heard of you and never will."

Sun Meiling, from the stair, said: "Say the other thing."

"I am saying the other thing. The forty-one and the seventeen are on the top of that sheet because Marguerite read the book out in that hall and Tove Marlin read the whole roll to the people it was about, and Runa has been reading it out in this kitchen once a week since the eighth of this month, and Beatrix read the whole hundred and eleven out on the eleventh of this month—"

"That is enough," said Beatrix Halm.

"It is not enough, and I have not come to the end of it."

Nadia Ferrous did not take her finger off the sheet. "I have been in this house four months telling every one of you that I am not the reason, and that you do not need to protect me, and that I am not here for the standing. I have said it about nine times.

"You have all let me say it. That was very decent of you, and it has meant that nothing has ever been tested."

She straightened up.

"So I am going to say the part I have never said, and then I am going to go and wash my hands, and then you can all be furious with me.

"I have wanted you since the second of March. Not since the museum, not since the lift, since the second of March, when you came into a house above a chop-house with a plate and four days of nothing in your face and you did not look at any of us for about a minute and a half, and then you sat down and asked whether there was a kettle.

"I have said that about nine times too, in about four different rooms, and not one time did you let me finish it. Every time, you did something enormous in front of me instead.

"On the tenth of this month you wrote one sentence six times in six hands and gave five of them out and kept one back from me, and you told me why, and the reason was kind. It was the last time anybody in this city was going to be kind to me for nothing."

"Say the rest," said Sun Meiling.

"There is no rest. I came up that street this afternoon with two copies in a bag because I wanted to be the one who went, and I was, and I am the only person in this house who can go anywhere and come back, and every single one of you is stuck.

"That is the whole of what I am for. I have been telling the table that for four months in about four different ways and not one of you has ever sent me for anything." Nadia Ferrous picked up the second copy out of the bag and put it down again. "And there is not one line on that sheet about me, and there is not one line on it about you, and that is correct, and it is also the whole of what either of us has."

She went and washed her hands.

Ola Bright, from the third step, said: "I am going to copy it."

"No," said Sun Meiling.

"It is two sheets and it is four paragraphs and I can do it in about eleven minutes and not one living soul is going to see it."

"No," said Sun Meiling. "It goes under the table with the other two of them from the ninth and it does not come out again, and if a woman comes to that door with a copy of this in her hand we will know where it came from, because there is one counter in this city that sells it and it is two hundred yards from my front step and I can see the roof of it from the top of that stair on a clear morning."

The room did not reply to that either.

"The fifteenth is tomorrow," said Runa Sato from the table, where she had been sitting since four o'clock with her hands flat in front of her and had not moved.

"From tomorrow any office in this city can come through that door about me. That is a thing I put in writing in the fourth box of a return on the eighth of this month with my own hand, and the woman who set the type on the sheet you have just put on that table did it because of a rule on a wall in Cordwain that was there before I was born."

She put one finger on the second line at the bottom of the sheet.

"And the same printer has it in type by about four o'clock in the afternoon." She looked up. "Which means that in about six hours there will be another copy of that line, and it will be the fifteenth, and the rule will be printed in the margin of every street in this quarter by tomorrow evening, and there will not be a person in Ostara who can read it who does not know there is a house in Ferrier Street with a woman in it who signs for things."

He had not said anything since eleven o'clock the previous morning.

"A shilling," he said.

"A shilling," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is what it costs. I have got two of them in a bag and I can go back and get eleven more, because they will sell me all the ones I want and they will not take my name, and the clerk told me that like it was a favour."

She sat down at the table for the first time since she had come in.

"I would like somebody in this room to say one sentence about what we are going to do about the fact that this exists," she said, "and I would like it to be somebody who has read the whole of it, and I do not care which of you it is."

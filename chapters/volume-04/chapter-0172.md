# The Waiting Room

The third floor of the Reading had a public stair off Cobb Street, and the stair went up past the ground floor and stopped, and the third was the top of it, and a woman in a grey armband was sitting on a chair at the head of the landing with a card on her knee.

He had walked past the door of that stair about two hundred times in four years and had never once gone through it.

"Sabine Rooke," she said, before he had said anything. "You are on no roll and you are not a party and you are not an observer, so you are a member of the public, and a member of the public is the only person who can sit in this room without anybody having to decide anything."

"That is the first thing anybody has ever let me in on."

"It is the only thing you will be let in on, and I have been letting people in on it for eleven years." She stood up and opened a door. "Come in. Take a chair at the wall. Not the table. The table is where they put things when they come out."

The room was about thirty feet by twenty and had the look of a room that had been assembled rather than built. There was a long table down the middle of it with nothing on it and six chairs on each side, and against the far wall a row of chairs with arms on them, eleven of them, which is two more than there need to be. There was a window with the sash down, looking out at a roof.

On the frame of the door at the height of a man's shoulder there was a card.

PARTIES AND OBSERVERS ARE ADMITTED UNTIL THE HALL IS CLEARED. MEMBERS OF THE PUBLIC ARE ADMITTED AT ALL TIMES. MEMBERS OF THE PUBLIC MAY NOT SPEAK TO A PARTY.

"You may not speak to a party," he said.

"And a party may speak to you, and a party will, and it will be about how long it has taken them to get used to you being in the room." She sat down at the head of it. "That is not procedure and it is not cruelty either. It is what happens in a room where eleven people sit for four hours.

"Leave your plate in your pocket, and if anybody asks you for it, say you left it at home, which is a lie, and it is the only useful thing anybody in this building can do with a lie."

There was already a man in the far corner.

He had a bandage round the outside of his left forearm and his sleeve was pushed up over it, and he had been there long enough that there was a coat-shaped hollow in the dust of the wall behind him, which is not a thing four minutes buys.

Lin Hao sat down four chairs away from him, because four is as close as you can sit to a stranger and still be on the other side of something.

Then he did the thing he did, which was to count, because it was the only instrument he had and it had never once let him down.

Eleven chairs on the wall. Two cupboards, one with a glass door and one with a padlock. Four windows, three of which had been painted over at some point in a decade before he was born. A rail of pegs with one coat on it. A clock on the wall whose second hand ran about a second fast, which he established in four minutes against the clock on the stair and then gave up on, because the stair clock was worse.

The man's boots were wet. That was all he knew about him for the first half hour.

At about five to ten a clerk came to the door with a slip of paper and said a number.

"Ferren."

The man got up. He did not hurry and he did not take long either. He stood out of the corner and put his coat on and came down the room and out, and he passed the table, and Lin Hao watched his hands, because Lin Hao watched hands.

The left one was shaking. It was not fear exactly.

"That was his," said Sabine Rooke. "He has been here since about a quarter to nine."

"How long was he in there."

"He was not in there. He was told at the desk that his was not on the sheet for today, and to come back at the next sitting." She turned the card over on her knee, which was the first time she had moved it. "That happens to about nine people a week in this building and it is the cruellest one, because it costs the Office nothing and it costs him a week."

He came back in at about ten past ten and sat down in his corner and put his hands between his knees and stayed like that.

Lin Hao sat with his coat over the chair back and did nothing for about an hour and a half, and at about half past eleven he got up and filled the jug at the cistern at the end of the room and put it back on the side table, and then he filled the second cup, which had been upside down on its plate, and set it down in front of the man in the corner.

"I am sorry about the sheet," he said, which was the true sentence and the wrong one.

The man looked at the cup for about four seconds.

"My count came back wrong at the start of March," he said. "One thousand nine hundred and something, and I have held four thousand since the autumn before that, and I did not do anything to bring it about. I did not move house. I did not sell anything, and there is nothing in my file anybody could point at.

"A plate I have had since my first year in the trade has been saying the same thing for sixteen years, and a count says I am a different man, and I cannot tell you which of the two is right.

"And my daughter's place at the school in Bell Row runs on my band and not on hers, because hers does not exist yet, and in September I am either four thousand or I am not." He turned the cup round without lifting it. "That is the whole of it. And I have been coming up to this room since the start of March, and this is the second time anybody has said one sentence to me in it."

Lin Hao stood there with his hand still on the side of the table.

There are things a hand can do.

In four years he had found out that if he laid it on somebody and they were somebody it came from, something they were owed arrived early and settled, and their face changed, and it was the only thing in Ostara he was actually good at. It moved nothing whatever on a stranger. It cost him grey at one temple and a grey night and a night of sleep like being woken on the hour, in proportion, and it never once improved.

And in a hall on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth of March he had said, into a microphone, in front of two hundred and eleven people, that he would not move it on himself again. He had said it before the instrument was run, so that nothing could be said afterwards about a room he had not been ready for.

So the hand was on the table, and it was his, and it was nailed shut, and it would stay that way for the rest of his life, and he had done that to it himself in front of everybody.

"I cannot help you," he said.

"I did not ask you to." The man took the cup. "Since March, and the asking is the part I have not been able to do. You are the first person who has said the word sorry at me in this room, and I would rather you had not, because now I know that you can see it, and there is nothing at all you can do about that."

He drank the water and put the cup down and said nothing else for the rest of the day, and Lin Hao sat four chairs away for four hours and got it exactly right, which is to say that he did not look at him again until the door opened.

Nothing happened in the room.

That is what a parties' room is, and it took him until about half past one to understand it and it did not go away afterwards. Nothing happens in it. Six of the people in the corridor go in and come out, and one of the nine waits, and the waiting is not a stage before the thing, and it is not a punishment, and it is not suspense.

It is the largest part of what the state does to a person who cannot get anybody to move, and it happens in a room with a jug of water in it and eleven chairs against a wall and a rail of pegs with one coat on it.

In four years he had never once been inside a room where somebody was waiting on him.

He worked out, sitting there, that if a clerk had walked in at half past nine and said a number, he would stand up. That was all it would take. He would come down the room with no answer ready, and the answer would not be the thing anybody wanted, because the thing anybody wanted was four thousand that had stopped being true in March.

Four years of being the least findable man in Ostara, and he had never once found out what it was worth.

That was the whole of what four years had bought him. Not a band. Not a plate that moved. A thing so thorough and so unglamorous that no office in the city had ever had a reason to send anybody to wait for him in a room, and he had not known it was a thing he had, the way you do not know you can breathe until a man comes into a corridor and takes a chair out of it and points at it.

At about ten to two the door at the end of the corridor opened.

Four people came out of it over about four minutes, and the woman in the middle of them was about eleven steps away from him and then about four, and then was at the bottom of the stair with her coat over her arm.

Ines Duarte looked at him.

He had about nine seconds in which to have said anything at all.

"Do not ask me," she said.

And she went down the outside stair, and he stood in a corridor on the third floor of the Reading and watched a woman walk away down eleven steps, having gone into a room on the fourth floor at ten and come out of it at two with four people, and having said two words to him in a corridor, and one of them had been a request.

He did not follow her down. He had promised on the fifteenth not to tell the table, and he had been in a room for four hours and had not been told anything, and there was not one thing in him he could have told anybody even if he had been let.

At the top of the stair on the way out he counted the doors on that landing, because he counted things and because he had nothing else to do with a corridor.

Four. One was the parties' room he had just come out of. One had a chair in it and a stack of grey folders and a woman who did not look up. One was shut with a plate on it and a bench outside about four feet long.

The fourth door was shut, and it had a small window in it with a grille across the bottom third, and a brass plate, and the plate said DRAWING BOARD. LATE RETURNS AND PERIOD BUNDLES. IN BY THE PUBLIC STAIR.

There was a bench outside it about the same length as the bench outside the second door, and there was nothing on it, and nothing went in and nothing came out of it between about half past nine and the time he left at ten past two.

Forty feet from where he had spent the day.

He stood at it for about a minute. He could not hear anything through a door and did not try, because a man who cleans doors for a living knows what the other side of one sounds like from the hinge.

Then he went down the public stair and out into the square and stood in the open with the light on the tiles about ninety feet away and four hundred people going across it, and did not go back up.

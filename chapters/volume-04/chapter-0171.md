# Household In Law

The second issue of Form 4-C was on the kitchen table with the first one on top of it, and the delivery stub from the thirteenth underneath both, because Ola Bright had put the stub there herself on the morning it was signed and had moved it back into place every morning since, which was the ninth time.

"Six days," said Runa Sato.

She said it without being asked and in the flat way she said every figure she had ever said out loud, which was as a woman counting something for other people.

"Six from today is the twenty-seventh. The first issue went out on the sixth with twenty-one days on it and the second came on the sixteenth with eleven, and both of them want the same morning."

Marguerite Vale, at the end of the table, moved a pencil a quarter inch and did not look up.

"It is a shorter clock on the same date," she said. "The Office is not thinking about the date. It is thinking about how much of itself it has to spend on Ferrier Street."

Agnete Lindqvist was at the table and had not been at that table seven days ago, and she had been outside in daylight twice.

She had come up the steps from the market at about eleven, had stopped on the second one, had come on, had stopped again on the fourth with her hand on the rail, and then had come all the way in and sat down where she could see the door.

"I have been reading that form since the fourteenth," she said. "Seven days, and most of it is the same page, and I want to say the thing I have got out of it before anybody in this room starts arguing with it."

"Say it," said Ines Duarte.

"It is not a questionnaire." Agnete Lindqvist put one finger on the table, not touching the paper, the way a woman indicates a line she is reading. "That is what everybody thinks it is when one arrives, because it arrives with questions on it and a box at the foot and a clock. It is not a questionnaire.

"A composition return is an instrument of acquisition. It is how this city acquires the right to serve a household. Everything else on the sheet is arithmetic to get a box filled in, and the box is the whole of it."

She stopped, and looked at the box at the foot of the nearer sheet, which was four inches wide and had a heading over it and had been empty on two forms and for a fortnight.

"Seven people at this table have never read one of these before the sixth of this month, and I have read about nine hundred, and I want to say where they go, because it decides what the sixth of April did to us.

"One. A return that is answered and goes back before the end of its period is a return. It goes to the district. It is a household that can be served, and it is read by a person whose job it is to act on it, and it is acted on within a period.

"Two. A return that comes back after the period has turned does not go to the district. It goes to a shelf on the third floor of the building we are all sitting under, on a shelf with three others, and it is shelved, and it is read by a woman in the morning who is clearing a period bundle and who does not read what is on the front of it.

"Three. A return that comes back with the box empty is not a return. It is a set of answers to a form. It is not a household. It is nothing that can be served, and there is nothing to act on, and it is not an error, because the form was answered correctly.

"That is the entire thing seven days of reading has produced, and I have been reading it in a building where I cannot go back inside, and I would like somebody at this table to tell me which of those four is the good news."

The kitchen made its own noise for about four seconds.

"How long did you receive them," said Ines Duarte.

"Eleven years, the last four in composition," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I had a stamp and a wire basket and I sent baskets up, and I never once entered a basket in a register, which is the only reason I can be sitting at this table and you cannot."

The range let go of its heat behind her.

"And the other thing," she said, "which I have been not saying for seven days and which is the reason I read it twice.

"At the foot of that form there is a heading and the heading wants a head of household. Not a member. Not the eldest. Not the woman who pays the rent. It wants a person of reading age who may be required to answer for these premises, and may be answered for.

"That word is the same word the Office used about Runa Sato on the wall in Cordwain, and it is the same word it printed over the schedule in the margin of the second issue, and both of those institutions meant that somebody could be made to produce a person."

Sun Meiling had come down four stairs and was standing on the fourth one with her arms folded, which is where she stands.

"Say the part about the answer," she said.

"There is no part about the answer. That is what I have been not saying for seven days." Agnete Lindqvist turned her hand over on the table. "Every one of the five in that margin is a standing — a person's, a firm's, an employer's, an unmeasured association's. One of them is eleven thousand people. And a standing cannot be answered for. It cannot be served, produced, fined, appealed, or made to send for anybody.

"So if that box is filled with a standing, the premises has no head. And a premises with no head is not a household in law, it is a description, and I have been reading for seven days about a description in a building where the only organisation in this quarter that has never once filed one has eleven thousand people in it."

She stopped.

"And I have not said the last part yet and I am going to say it now, because I have been sitting in a room for eleven years and I am not going to be the second thing to happen to eleven thousand people because a form went to a door."

"Then say it," said Sun Meiling.

Agnete Lindqvist looked at her.

"Say it outside," said Sun Meiling. "I will come down. You are not going to say a thing like that in front of six people and a man and then go up a stair."

They went down to the yard at the back of the chop-house for about two minutes, well out of earshot of the house, and it came out in the end that it was four sentences and none of them was a threat.

When they came up Sun Meiling went back to the fourth stair and Agnete Lindqvist sat down and put her hands flat on the table in front of her and left them there.

"Right," said Beatrix Halm. "Who."

The room did not answer, and he found afterwards that he had not answered either, and that it had gone round.

"Ines cannot," said Beatrix Halm. "Ines files nothing. That has been the position since the eighth of March and if it is in a return in her own hand then the return is a lie with her name at the top of it, and she has refused it in writing twice and I am not going to watch her do it a third time."

"Ines could, in fact," said Ines Duarte. "A person of reading age who keeps an address at the premises does not have to be a member of the household to be answerable for it. That is a whole line of this city's law and it is why I have kept rooms on Lantern Row for eleven years and never once lived in a household I could not leave. I have been doing this for years without knowing it was worth anything. It is worth something today."

The room did not argue with that, because it was correct, and because of what it meant, which was that if Ines Duarte went into that box then six women became answerable to a woman who reads records for a living.

"Runa cannot," said Nadia Ferrous, "because she has a process open and a member with a process open is entered on a return with the process attached. I have already been entered once on one of these and I wrote the truth, and I would like this room to stop counting my process as a favour to anybody."

"I cannot," said Beatrix Halm, "because I have a posting and a cabin at the end of a line and a yard roster with my name on it, and because from the moment I am answerable for that building I am the woman whose name they read first, and I would rather be the woman they cannot find. We have been here before."

"I cannot," said Tessa Okonkwo, "because the clinic is unfunded and toleration is the only thing the Wing tolerates it on, and the second that building is answerable by anybody it is inside a file. I put my name on a chart on the seventh. That was enough. That was one chart and it was about him."

"I cannot," said Marguerite Vale, "and I am going to be exact about why, because I have priced everything else in this house. The Vant is in my life. A household that can be pointed at is a household that can be pointed at by a guild. In August the answer is no, and in June, if there has not been an answer, the answer will be yes, because that is what happens to a thing that stays."

"Sun will not," said Ines Duarte, "and has said so since December, and if I put her in that box after what she signed on a doorstep on the thirteenth I will do it in front of all of you so that you can see me do it."

"I cannot," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and that is the fourth one in this room in eleven years and I have stopped finding it remarkable."

Seven. And he had not answered, because he had been sitting at the end of a table in overalls with a delivery stub pushed under a form and he had understood the sum before any of them did and had understood it at the point where Ines Duarte talked about Lantern Row, which is to say he had understood it in the first minute and had let it run for eleven minutes so that he would not have to be the man who said it.

The oven came on and went off again.

Sun Meiling came down the four stairs for the first time all afternoon.

"Do not," she said.

"Sun—"

"You have been doing it since the fourth stair and it has been very well done and every one of them was honest and I would like it written down that every one of them was honest." She stood at the table. "The person at the top of that box does not go in it for a reason. She goes in it because there is nobody else, and six people at one table have just proved it to themselves, and that is not a reason, it is a shortage.

"And the day that name is on a return, then six women are answerable to a man for a piece of paper, and he is answerable for six women, and neither of those is anybody's house. It is a chain."

"It is a chain that holds," said Ines Duarte.

"It is a chain that holds and it is not a wall, and we have both been told what a chain that holds is worth by a man four doors up with a shilling's worth of paper." Sun Meiling went back up the four stairs. "I am not voting. I have said the shape of it and I am going upstairs."

At about six he got his coat.

"Third floor," said Ines Duarte, in the corridor at the back of the kitchen, with her bag on her shoulder. "The drawing-board room. Everything that missed its period is on four shelves and the third one is where it goes, and I have read about nine hundred returns and I have never once been into that room, because I do not work there and neither does the woman who sent you to it.

"If there is anything about this address that exists and is not on this table, that is where it is. And I am telling you it on the twenty-first because I go in the morning and I am not going to be able to tell you anything on the twenty-second, or after the twenty-second, or ever, and that is not a threat, it is a procedure, and I have already given you one piece of information today and I am not going to be able to give you any others."

He stood in the corridor and understood two things at once and enjoyed neither.

The first was that he had a floor and a room and a shelf, a mile and a half from his own front door, and that it was the only place in the city where the answer to the question in front of him was written down in a hand.

The second was that he was not going to walk in there and ask for it, and that the reason was not the door.

He cleaned six floors of the Fourth Branch and could have found a stranger's written address in that building in nine minutes without trying, and if he had gone up to the third floor of the Reading that afternoon with a question about a return, the question would have been written down, in a book, in a hand, by somebody, and it would have said that a man at those premises wanted to know what the city already held about him.

For four months he had been the least findable person in Ostara. It had cost him nothing and it had cost everybody in his house something, and he had been extremely careful about it.

He went down and did the sixth floor and the second and he got the tram and he did not go up.

It was not because of the doors. He made himself say that on the tram, in his own words, twice, because he knew what he was: the third floor's public stair was open until four, he was not at work until seven, and he could have gone there in his coat and come back with a shilling for a cup of tea and been at the Fourth Branch for the half-hourly at a quarter to six.

He did not go because he would have had to ask.

She had told him on the fifteenth and again on the twenty-first, and the room had gone round the table at half past four that afternoon and arrived at him, and the correct version of that is that he had been sitting at a table in his overalls being the shortest person at a table where eight people had just found somebody else to be.

He got off at the junction eleven minutes short of the house and walked it, because there was nothing else to do with the time, and he thought about a shelf he had never seen for the whole of that evening and did not go.

On the twenty-second he would be forty feet from it.

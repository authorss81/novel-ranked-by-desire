# The Invoice

The invoice came up the sixty-one steps on the morning of the eighteenth in the post and it was a single sheet in a good weight of paper with no form number on it at all, which is the first thing that told Ines Duarte to read it twice.

It had a date at the top, the sixteenth of this month, and a heading that said EXAMINATION OF STANDING HOUSEHOLD — PROFESSIONAL FEE, and a line ruled across the page, and on the line a description and a figure.

Description: THE EXAMINATION OF A STANDING HOUSEHOLD AT PREMISES IN FERRIER STREET, HEAD'S NAME ENTERED AT THE TOP OF PAGE ONE, MEMBERS SIX, ONE ACCOUNTED FOR ABSENT WITH REASON. Six members and one absent is a description that only one person in this city could have written, and she had written it in a ledger in a room on the second floor of a building off the Vestry market with the pen still chained to the desk, and he had been in the room.

The figure against it was not money.

It was a column headed VALUE OF STANDING ASSUMED, and it had a rank in it, and the rank was two.

"RANK TWO," said Sun Meiling, from the fourth stair, reading it upside down. "It is a bill for a rank."

"It is not a bill," said Marguerite Vale. "And I am going to correct all four of you about it before anybody in this house says anything else about it, because you have all four of you got it wrong and I have been in that room and I did it."

She was standing at the table and she had not sat down when she came in, which none of them had seen her do in four months.

"It is not an invoice. It is a receipt, and it is a receipt for a permission I bought on the afternoon of the sixteenth on the second floor of the Exchange, and I paid for it out of my own entitlement, which is the only thing in this city that can be spent, and I have been spending it for nineteen years and I have never once spent it on a house."

"You bought it," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I bought it." She put her hand flat on the sheet and did not turn it round. "And I did not buy a rank. The rank was mine before I walked in there and it will be mine at the end of next year and it is the standing of a guild broker and it belongs to a guild of thirty years, and I did not buy it and I cannot sell it and it is not mine in the way that sentence sounds.

"What I bought was the examination. Every standing household is examined once a year and the examination has to be entered against somebody with standing, and where the head's standing is band five then the examination is entered against a man who cleans washrooms, and where the head's standing is a broker's then the examination is entered against a broker and the broker is named on it and the broker is consulted."

"So the invoice is for asking you," said Ines Duarte.

"The invoice is for the answer," said Marguerite Vale. "And I am going to read you the figure because you are all going to work it out in about nine minutes and I would rather you had it in the correct order.

"It is rank two. Which means that when that household is examined, once a year, for as long as it stands, the person examined is me.

"Not the certificate. The certificate issues in June and that is the Office's business and it has nothing to do with this. The examination happens the year after, and the year after that, and the year after that, and in each of those years somebody has to be there, and it is going to be me, because that is what rank two on a household means and it has meant it for about two hundred years and it is the same rule the law uses."

The kitchen did not move.

"And in the fourth year," said Marguerite Vale, "the guild will have replaced me. They will do it at the annual sitting, they have done it to a man in nineteen thirty-one and to a woman in forty-eight, and when they do, the examination goes to whoever holds my book, and there is a woman called Solveig Rask who holds the books of four guilds and who has never met one of you and who will be named on that document as the person answerable for six women."

"You knew that," said Runa Sato.

"I priced it," said Marguerite Vale. "That is not the same word and I am going to insist on it. I priced it on the sixteenth on the second floor of the Exchange in about four minutes standing at a window, and I knew it, and I did it, and I have not hesitated since, and I am not going to be forgiven for it either, because I was not asking."

"Then why," said Beatrix Halm, from the stairs.

Marguerite Vale looked at the sheet on the table and did not answer for about four seconds, which in four months was the first time any of them had seen her do that.

"Because it is the only thing I have ever wanted that could not be taken off me in a court," she said. "I am thirty years inside a guild that will replace me the moment a woman called Rask decides to, and I have been looking for nineteen years for one asset in Ostara that a court cannot seize, and I have priced every one of the things I have ever owned and not one of them survives an argument.

"A man survives an argument. I have had that since about nine o'clock last night and I am not going to discuss it and it has nothing to do with the sheet on your table.

"A house does not survive an argument either. It has an examination once a year and the examination is a document and the document has a name on it, and once that name is on it the arrangement is lawful and it is bought and it is signed, and there is no judge in this city who will unwind a standing household because its broker turned out to be difficult."

"So you did not buy it for us."

"I did not buy it for us," she said. "I bought it for me. And six women are in it, and the certificate in June will be issued on my standing, and the examination that follows it is mine, and every one of those is true at the same time.

"And every one of you is in this room and none of you asked me for any of it, and I have been waiting nine days for one of you to make me say that out loud and you have not, and that is the correct outcome and I would be ashamed of both of us if you had."

Ines Duarte had the sheet by the corner and had turned it round and was reading the bottom half, and there is a thing she does, which is that she reads a document the way a person reads a document they intend to be cited from, in silence, for as long as it takes.

"There is a second line," she said.

"There is a second line."

"What does the second line say."

Marguerite Vale did not look at it.

"It says EXAMINATION SUBSEQUENTLY ENTERED AGAINST THE HOLDER OF THE BOOK IN THE EVENT OF THE DEATH, RESIGNATION OR REPLACEMENT OF THE BROKER OF RECORD," she said. "It is the standard second line and it has been on that sheet for about two hundred years and I have signed eleven hundred of them and I did not think about the second line when I bought this one because it never applies to the person signing."

"It applies to you."

"It applies to me," she said, "in four years, or in nine if I hang on, and it is the only thing in this piece of paper that frightens me and I did not see it until this woman read it out in her own kitchen at ten past nine in the morning, and she read it in about four seconds and I have been reading that class of document for nineteen years."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"Then take it back," said Sun Meiling.

"I cannot take it back."

"Sun—"

"I cannot take it back because I told you on the seventh that a standing household cannot revive a set of terms that are down, and that is true of my terms and it is also true of this.

"An examination once entered against a broker is not an examination that can be un-entered by the broker asking nicely, and there is not one instrument in this city that will do it, and I have looked, because I looked on the afternoon of the sixteenth while I was still paying for it."

"So the Vant has it," said Nadia Ferrous.

"The Vant has it," said Marguerite Vale. "And I am the one who gave it to them, and I gave it to them for nothing, and I have been giving that guild things for thirty years and this is the first thing I have ever handed over that I kept a copy of."

She took her hand off the table.

"And I want to say the last of it before this room decides what it thinks of me, and then I am going to go to the Exchange at midday because there is a thing I have to do with a book on the second floor, and I am not going to be asked about it and I would rather it was in the room now.

"Six women are about to be bound to a guild. That is what happens on the nineteenth when he signs, and it happens because I signed, and the reason I signed is in a drawer on Lantern Row and it is not in the Vant's book.

"They are going to use it," she said. "Not today, not this year. In about four years, when they own something they want secured, they will come to this household and ask it to stand behind something, and there will be a woman in that room who says yes because it is in the book, and she will not know that the reason she can is a piece of paper a man signed in a clerk's office on the nineteenth of this month.

"That is what I have bought. And I would buy it again on the sixteenth of next month at the same price, and I would like one person in this room to understand that I am not asking to be forgiven for it, because a person who has to be forgiven has not made a trade."

She picked up her gloves.

"You will not tell him I made a speech," she said to the room. "He was there for it. He heard it. He priced nothing and got everything and he knows it and I have watched him not say so for two days, which is the most decent thing he has done in four months and it is not going to cost him anything, because he was not asked."

Twenty-one of the sixty were gone. Thirty-nine of them were left. One day to the nineteenth.

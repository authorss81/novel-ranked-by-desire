# On The Scale

He asked her to come and she came, and the coming was the part of it he had not been able to arrange in his head in four days, because a woman who says yes to a thing in a room with furniture in it is a different proposition from a woman who says yes to a thing in a building after hours.

The Fourth Branch shut at six and the cleaners came in at seven, and by eleven the building was empty and the water was off on three floors and there was one bulb over the sinks on the sixth.

She came up the back stairs at about five to eleven with a case in one hand, and he heard it on the stairs before he saw her, because the case had a metal frame and it knocked every third step.

"What is that," he said.

"A balance." She set it on the shelf under the window, which is where they keep the window-cleaning things, and opened it.

It was the old kind, with a beam and two pans and a sliding weight in the middle, and the pans were brass and badly polished. "It came off a wall in a room on the fourth floor of a building in Bellhouse that has been shut since the winter. I paid a man four pence to carry it up the stairs and he thought I was going to use it on a body."

"Which you are."

"Which I am not, yet." She straightened up and looked at the sinks, and the taps, and the row of four doors, and the light. "You have brought me to the floor you clean."

"I have brought you to the only room in this building I can be certain is empty, and it is the one room in Ostara I cannot stop anybody taking off somebody, because rank six may requisition property and property is anything a person is using, and this is a floor of sinks in a Bureau office."

"Yes," said Tessa Okonkwo. "I had worked that out two days ago. Sit down on the bench if you want to. There is one bench and it is wet and I am not going to apologise for it."

He sat on the bench because it was wet.

"Four things," she said, "and then we will not do this in a cold corridor, because a cold corridor is where people do things they have decided not to decide.

"One. Neither of us spends anything on the other. I do not want to be given anything tonight and you are not to give me anything, and if either of us starts that, I stop and you go home.

"Two. I am not touching the ribs. I am going to tell you why in about an hour and you are not going to like it and I am not going to argue about it tonight.

"Three. You ask me, out loud, every time, and if I say no you stop and you do not take it personally and you do not come back with a better question. I have had a month of better questions.

"Four. Not one person is told, and I am not writing anything down until afterwards, and what I write down afterwards is mine and goes in a book in the clinic and not in a book anywhere else."

"That is four," he said.

"I have been doing this for nine years and I have never once been asked for fewer."

She took her coat off and hung it on a door handle, and she took the pin out of her hair and put it in her pocket, and neither of those was for him.

She did not begin with kindness, which he had expected, and she did not begin with want, which he had been hoping for.

She began by looking at him.

Not at his face. Down, and back up, the way she had looked at a man's knee in a queue two days before, without embarrassment and without any interest in making it flattering. He had been looked at for four years by people who could not see him and twice in a month by a woman who could see all of him, and it turned out that the second thing was much harder to stand than the first.

"Frame is fine," she said. "Shoulders are wrong for your trade, which is because you have been carrying water up six floors for four years and it has gone into the top of your back. Everything else is what it is."

"I am not sure that is a compliment."

"It is not. It is a set of observations." She came and stood in front of him on the wet floor. "Now I am going to do this and I am going to keep telling you what I am doing, and that is not because I think you need telling. It is because a clinician who stops narrating has started improvising, and I do not improvise with a person I care about."

He did not know what to do with the middle of that sentence, so he put his hands on her hips.

"Yes," she said. "That one is allowed."

The bulb over the sinks is a hundred and ten watts and it is the only light on the floor and it is directly above and slightly behind her, which meant he could not see her face properly for the first twenty minutes and could see everything else, and that he understood afterwards that this had been arranged by a woman who had chosen the room.

She took her blouse off over her head the way a person does when they are in a hurry for a reason, and then she stopped, and looked at him, and said, "Right. Ask me."

"May I."

"You may."

He got his shirt over his head and she looked at the place on his left side without going near it, the way a person looks at a wall they have been told about and do not intend to touch, and he felt the whole of the third and fourth ribs announce themselves and she saw that it happened and gave him nothing.

"Now say it back," she said. "Ask me properly."

"May I have you."

"Twice. Once for each thing. This is not a form and I am not a clerk and you are going to get used to it or you are going home."

"May I have you here." Then, about four seconds later, with his mouth against her collarbone and his hands still on her hips: "May I have you now."

"Good." She had him by the back of the neck. "Knees. Bench is wet, floor is tiled, I am not spending the whole of tonight on my knees on tile, so the bench."

It was a bad bench and they made it work.

She was not fast. That was the thing he had not been ready for, because he had spent four months not touching anybody and had built a version of this in his head about nine separate times and every one of the nine versions had been about speed.

Tessa Okonkwo took her time the way she did everything, and she watched, and she said things. She said *there*. She said *again, slower*. She said, about four minutes in, entirely without any apparent reason other than that she wanted to say it, "You are much less careful than I thought you were and I have never been so glad to be wrong about a man."

He got his hand into her hair and she let him and then said, "If you pull, I bite, and I am telling you that so that it is not a surprise and so that you do it anyway."

He did it anyway.

She had a scar on the inside of her left thigh, four inches long, and when she caught him looking at it she said, "A tram, four years, on the way in from Kestrel Hill, and I was on the platform side because I was being rude to somebody. It is not interesting. Keep going."

They went on.

At some point she got him off the bench and onto the tiled floor with his back against the wall under the window, which was cold, and she put his knees up on either side of her and held him there with a forearm across the chest, and she looked at him the whole time with her weight on him and her mouth about four inches away, and neither of them said anything for about a minute and a half.

Then she said, "There. That is the one I have been thinking about for two days, and it is not yours, and I would like it noted that it is mine."

"It is noted."

"It is a thing you cannot buy and there is not one counter in this city where I could put it down in a schedule, and I have wanted it since a hatch at half past nine two days ago, and I have wanted it for about six reasons, and none of them is the one you would pick."

He got a hand up her back and she let him, and after that neither of them said anything for some time.

She came first and she did not make a sound, and he held on and let her, which was the single most expensive thing he had ever done for anybody and cost nothing at all.

She was thorough about him afterwards. That was the part he would remember and the part he would not have been able to describe to a single person in Ostara. She checked him the way she would check a hand. She looked at his pupils and asked him a question about the month and watched him answer it. She pressed with two fingers above the third rib and asked him what that did, and when he said *nothing* she pressed again and said *that*, and made him feel the difference.

"You can feel it," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it is not gone and it never went, and whatever is landing in the third and the fourth is landing there and has been for a long time." She sat back on her heels. "I am not going to touch it and I told you I would not and I am not, and here is the reason and you are not going to like it.

"The ribs are the part of you that a person can point at. Every other thing about this — the account, the finding, the four descriptions, the ledger, all of it — is paper, and paper can be taken off you by a clerk in a minute with a form.

"Those two ribs are the only part of it that is on your body. If I put my hand on them tonight and something moves, then I am a clinician who has put her hand on it, and I cannot un-know that, and I would spend the rest of my life unable to write a chart on you without my own hand in it.

"So they stay. And I will write on a chart tonight that there are two of them and where they are, and I will not write anything else about them, and that is me declining to be the instrument, and I would like you to notice that it is exactly the same refusal you made in a hall on the twenty-ninth and that we have never once discussed it."

She stood up and got the cloth off the last clean tap and turned the cold on for about ten seconds and wiped her hands and forearms to the elbow, which she did without hurry and without making a thing of it.

Then she brought the balance over from the shelf.

"Stand on the pan."

He stood on the pan. It went down further than it should have for how he felt.

She moved the weight out along the beam with her thumb, reading the notch, and then she moved it back a quarter inch and stopped, and then she put her hand flat on the beam to stop it moving at all.

"Stand still," she said, "and do not help me. I have not weighed you properly in two years and there is no reason on earth for me to be able to do this twice."

He stood still. She read it, wrote it on her sleeve, read it again with the weight back a quarter inch, and wrote that down too.

"Seven stone and two pounds over."

"Seven—"

"Over what you should be, and I know what you should be because I have a chart on a hundred and nine men of your height in this district and I have done this for nine years, and you are seven stone and two pounds heavier than the frame that is standing here holding this beam." She wrote it down. "And there is no swelling, and no fluid, and your heart is not doing anything I do not like, and I have looked, and there is nothing there.

"So the mass is real and it is not water and it is not a tumour and it is not anything I have a name for, which is a sentence I have never had to write on a chart about a person and which I am going to write on one this evening in a washroom in a branch office with one bulb on."

She took the chart off the top of the case. It was a single sheet with a printed head on it and a column down the left and a rule down the middle of the page.

She wrote the date at the top. She wrote into the column, in a small upright hand, the weight, and the frame, and the two ribs, and the heart, and then she stopped with the pen about a third of the way down the page and turned it round.

"Now the last part," she said, "and a chart does not take a name until the end, and I have done this for nine years and I am not going to do it out of order for a man standing on a scale in his own building.

She put the pen on the line.

"Lin Hao." Then her own name under it, with the word that she used for herself on every chart she had ever signed, and then the time, which was five to midnight.

She tore it off the pad and did not give it to him.

"That is mine and it goes in the day book for tonight in the dispensary hatch at the Vestry, and it is the first document in four years with both our names on it, and I know what that is, and I said no to it on the third of this month and I have had four days since, and a form in the post since the sixth, and the form is what changed it and not you.

"So here is what it has cost and I am going to say it before it costs, because that is the deal and you agreed to it.

"From tomorrow morning this building holds a chart with your name on it. The Vestry is tolerated by the Classification Wing on one ground, which is that we are unfunded and have never been entered in anybody's file. A chart is a file. Any office that wants to know what this building knows about a person now sends one piece of paper and gets it, and the paper does not have to name me, and it does not have to name the clinic, because a chart names both.

"And I have thought about that for four days and I have done it anyway, and I want you to understand why, because you will get it wrong otherwise." She folded it once and put it in the case and shut the case.

"Because a woman who will not be written down cannot be defended, and you have four months of proof of it and I have two years.

"And because there are six people on the third floor of my building who come to a hatch in the morning who are not on a plate, and if this building goes down they have nowhere in the Slough to go that the Correction Office has not already heard of.

"I am not doing it for you. I have done everything I have ever done for this building and none of it for you."

She picked her coat up off the door handle and put her hairpin back in and then took it out again, and left it in her pocket.

"The bulb goes at midnight, by the way," she said. "The caretaker does it at midnight and he does not care who is standing in here. So whatever you have got left, you have got about four minutes of it."

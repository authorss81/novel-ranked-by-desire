# The Tray On The Second Floor

The address trays are on the second floor, under the north windows, in a run of eleven drawers with brass pulls, and they are alphabetical by street and then by number and there is no other way in.

He had cleaned under them for four years. He knew which two stick, and that the third drawer from the left has a card in it for a house on Ferrier Street that was pulled down in the winter before he started, and that the card has never been taken out to prove it, because a tray is a current state and not a history, and a tray does not keep things that are true.

A woman came in at about ten with a question about a man.

He was on the second floor with a cloth, working round the queue that forms along the wall while the machines run on the first, and he was close enough to the counter to hear all of it, and he had heard it four thousand times in four years without ever once stopping.

"Teodor Marn, Cordwain, the yard gate."

"There is a Marn at eleven, Cordwain. There is a Marn at four, Ash Row, and it is not that one, because that one is a woman. Do you know the trade?"

"He drives the gate."

"Then it is eleven, Cordwain."

The clerk wrote a line in a book with a date in it. That was the whole of it.

He stood there with the cloth in his hand and did the thing he had never done, which was to look at the clock.

The woman said the name at eight minutes past ten. The clerk wrote the address at about twenty to nine. He had not gone and got a card out of the tray, because the clerk had not gone and got a card out of the tray either — there is a printed list of the streets by the counter, and eleven is Cordwain's yard gate, and the clerk knew it.

So there were no nine minutes. There were eight minutes and about forty seconds from a stranger saying a man's name to a piece of paper with that man's house on it, and nothing clever had happened anywhere in that transaction.

It had simply never occurred to him that knowing a street was the same as knowing a person.

He stayed on the second floor until the queue went and then he came back at half past two with a sheet.

This was not a liberty. A request from a floor to a tray is a request from a floor to a tray, it goes on a form, it gets a time on it, and the time is the point. You cannot learn a thing about the building you work in without putting your name against it, and he had known that since his first week, and the entire arrangement of his working life had been built on never once doing it.

He wrote four lines. A request from the fourth floor, ground attendant, for a name: Sarn, I., certification, the reason being a bench plate not returned to the tray and a nameplate out of the rack.

Then he signed it and put it in the tray that goes down at four, and it came back at ten past four with a clerk's line on the bottom of it, and the line said the drawer had been emptied at the end of the week for the period and the cards were with the returns and would be re-filed on the first working day.

Which was the first.

He stood in the corridor outside the second-floor windows holding that piece of paper for about a minute.

A tray holds plates. It does not hold men. A man is removed from a tray by a card coming out of it, and the card goes into a returns bundle, and the returns bundle is with the period returns, and there is a clerk in a room somewhere who knows exactly where it is and whose whole job is knowing exactly where it is.

Eleven drawers along from the one with the dead house on Ferrier Street, in the F, there was his own.

He had known that for four years. He had counted it as a comfort — the idea of a man being looked through so thoroughly that there was a card about him on the second floor which in four years had never once been read — and he had never once run that sentence backwards, which is this: a card stays unread for exactly as long as the person it is about is content.

The third drawer from the left had a card in it for a house on Ferrier Street, and he had cleaned under that drawer four times a week for four years.

He thought about it for longer than he meant to.

That is the honest account of it. There was a piece of paper in his hand with his own name at the top of it and a time on it, and in a building he was employed in, there was a bundle in which a card sat that would have taken a band-seven officer out of the only publication this city has.

He could have gone and looked. Not stolen — looked. A drawer is a drawer.

He put the sheet in his apron and did the third floor instead, and he did it because he wanted to know how it worked and not because he wanted to do something decent, and he was careful, while he was doing it, to be honest with himself about which of the two he was doing.

That was the answer to a question he had been carrying for four months, and it arrived in about nine seconds on a wet floor on the second floor of a building in which he had no standing.

If he could find a man who had never been written down, then so could anybody else who wanted to, and the finding would not be difficult, and the reason it had never been difficult was that he had spent four years perfecting a thing that had never once been hard.

At the third-floor counter on the same afternoon a man called Piet Ossick had sat down on the second step of the stair at a quarter past three, and had asked for nothing, and had sat there for the better part of forty minutes with his hat in his hands.

He ran the loading-gear counter at the Cordwain coal wharf and had been in the city thirty-one years and had a band three and a bad knee and had come in that morning to have his plate read because the notice said it was the week.

When he got up he said, out loud, to the stairwell, that the steps were colder than the yard had any right to be.

Nothing about a man sitting on a step for forty minutes goes into a document. It went into the drawer at the back of his own head that evening, on the back page, after the hundred and fifteen, with the stair and the hour on it.

[Ledger. One hundred and sixteen lines. Nine turned.]

He did not write the name down and he did not write the knee down, because a line is a face and not an act, and a ledger that started keeping deeds would stop being a ledger inside a month.

He got home at about six. The landing light was off and there were four women at the table and the notebook was open in the middle of it with the day ruled and five of the six lines filled in and the sixth one waiting.

Beatrix Halm had the visitors' book open in front of her and a pen in her hand and was not writing anything with it.

"Two more," she said, before he had his coat off. "One asking about the household and one asking about a person, and he wanted to know whether the woman on the landing was still taking callers."

"How did he know about the landing."

"He bought a shilling's worth."

"Tall or short."

"Neither. Ordinary. He wanted a description and not a name, which is what people want when they have bought something, because a description is easier to match and a name is a thing you have to be right about." She put the pen down. "He asked about the landing because somebody told him there was a landing. That is not in the transcript. The transcript describes six lines a day. It does not have a room in it."

Nadia Ferrous said, from the end, "It has a building in it. Ferrier Street is not printed but a chop-house in the Slough that takes callers at night is one street, and anybody who has stood at that gate has said out loud that there is a woman on the landing who takes calls."

"Then somebody has stood at that gate."

"Or somebody has been asking in Bellhouse for four months," said Beatrix Halm. "The book is a hundred and eleven, and forty-one of them asked about a person and seventeen asked about the household, and Marguerite reads every one of them out at the end of every week and attributes none of them, and the ones asking about a person stopped being strangers about the middle of February and became men looking for somebody specific."

The table was quiet for about three seconds. The oven ticked.

"That is what I have been waiting for," said Sun Meiling from the stairs, without coming down.

"It is a man asking about me."

"It is a man asking about me and about five other women and it is the reason you have never been in a hospital in this city and the reason the clinic has never had a Bureau form with your name on it." She had her coat on where she sat, which she did now. "Lin Hao. There is a thing at the bottom of this that you are going to walk past, so I am going to say it once and then I am going upstairs.

"There is one person in Ostara who can put your name on a piece of paper and make it a fact about a body instead of a fact about a man. She has had two years to do it and has not done it, and she has told at least one other person in this house that she is not going to, and every one of us let her keep that.

"And if you go up to that clinic and ask her to do it you are not asking her for a piece of paper. You are asking a woman who has spent two years being the only person in this city who never writes you down, to put down the first word about you that has your name in it. Do that in whatever way you are going to do it. Do not do it gently."

He stood in the doorway with his coat still on his arm.

"Sun."

"I said I would say it once."

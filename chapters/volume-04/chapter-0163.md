# Off The Roster

Runa Sato did not come in at seven on the morning of the thirteenth, which was the first morning in two years that she had not, and the household spent the first hour of it not saying so.

He went down to Cordwain at about half past seven and stood in a corridor with a cold candle on a ledge.

The frame was back on the wall. The four column rule was back on the wall above it, painted back in white and slightly out of true, and in the body of the roster, in the second column, on the line that had said R. SATO, there was a rule straight through it from the left margin to the right, and no new line under it, because there was nothing under it.

A strip of card had been taped to the frame's bottom rail, and on it, in the duty clerk's hand, four words.

STRENGTH RECONCILED. SEVENTH.

He stood there for about two minutes with the card in his eye and then went to the desk.

The duty clerk had the collar of a different coat on and a bundle of frames on his own table, and he did not look up.

"Seventh," he said, without being asked. "There were eight on that wall yesterday at ten in the morning and there are seven now, and the seventh is a division officer at Kestrel Hill who wrote a letter to this office in February and said it was not a matter for this office.

"That was before your woman put REFUSING in the fourth column. That letter is dated the fifteenth of February. You are welcome to it."

He squared the bundle.

"And that is the part that gets said wrong in rooms like yours," he said. "The fourth column did not take her off anything. She is on this roster and she is paid and she is in the division, and on Thursday morning her name goes out with the rest of them.

"I ruled that line off the wall myself, yesterday, at about four o'clock, because I have owed this building a strength count since the fifteenth of February and there was one name too many on it when I came to do it. So you can say whatever you like to your six women about that column. It was the count that did it, and I did the count, and I am telling you that before you go up those stairs and tell her it was her own word."

"And take the piece of card down if you are going to stand and read it. That tape has been read by forty men in nine years and not one of them took it off."

He went down the corridor and out into the yard, where the gates were open and a tram was going through, and he stood there for a while in the noise, which was the thing he had come out for.

Then he went in to the branch and did six floors.

The knock came at about ten past ten.

There were five women in the room and Sun Meiling was on the fourth stair with her coat on and her hand on the rail, which is where she sits and has sat for four months.

"Post," said Ola Bright from the kitchen, and did not move.

"It is not post." Beatrix Halm had got up. "Post does not knock like that. Post knocks and then goes. That was a man standing there waiting to be answered, and he knocked twice and then waited, and in four months the only knock on that door has been the man who comes for the rent money."

She went down and opened it.

The young man on the step had a satchel and a cap and a folded sheet in the top pocket of it, and he took the sheet out and held it up, and he had the face of somebody who had done this perhaps two hundred times and had got very good at not enjoying it.

"Notice of premises standing," he said. "For these premises."

"Give it to him," said Nadia Ferrous from the table. "He is the one the finding is about, he has been in and out of this door for a year, he will know what a notice is."

The young man looked past her at the room, at the six of them, and then at the stairs, where there was a person standing.

"That is not how it works," he said. "It is not served on a person. It is served on premises, and premises are served by a person of reading age at the premises, and I take a name and I take a signature, and I do not have a choice in it and neither has the person who signs."

Beatrix Halm said, "Then go and find another building," and Sun Meiling came down four stairs and said, "Give it here."

She took it off him in the doorway and did not bring it in, and read it standing on the step with the door open behind her, and he watched her get to the second line and then stop, and then go on, and then go back to the first line and read it again.

It was one page. He read it upside down from the table and could get the shape of it, which was four short paragraphs and a signature line and a date. The only line on it in capitals was the third paragraph, and the third paragraph was the whole of the notice.

FROM THE FIFTEENTH OF THIS MONTH, ANY OFFICE IN THIS CITY MAY SERVE A PAPER AT THESE PREMISES. NO WARRANT IS REQUIRED. NO REASON IS REQUIRED.

Sun Meiling folded it once, in half, along the crease it already had, and came in and shut the door and stood with her back against it.

"It is dated the twelfth," she said. "It is dated the twelfth because it was made the moment that word went into a column on a wall in Cordwain, which was at about ten past two yesterday afternoon, and somebody in an office that does not know me looked at a form four women had answered and did arithmetic on it."

She put it on the table face down.

"The form said that these premises are held under the standing of a person whose premises are held by the Field division in Cordwain. That was true on the eighth of this month and it has been false since yesterday afternoon at about ten past two.

"The consequence is printed on the sheet and I am going to read it out because you will all read it in about nine seconds anyway and I would like the room to have it from the beginning. From the fifteenth of this month, any office in the city may serve a paper at these premises. Not may ask. May serve."

The range shut itself off behind them.

"They have written the fifteenth because a paper about a building has to have a day it starts, and they gave us two days, and two days is not a kindness. It is the shortest span the schedule allows."

He came up out of the chair.

"Who signs for it," he said.

"He does." She did not look round. "There is a stub. He came to the door with two pieces of paper and one of them is the notice and the other one is the delivery and it wants a name on it in a hand, and he will not leave the second one."

There was a silence of about four seconds.

Five women in that room could each have gone down those stairs and taken that stub.

Ines Duarte had an audit appointment in the Reading and a room of her own on Lantern Row and a plate. Tessa Okonkwo had a clinic in the Vestry with her name over the hatch and three hundred patients in a day book.

Runa Sato had carried a Field division identity for nine years and had lost it at ten past two the previous afternoon. Nadia Ferrous was in a service file that went to a counter in Cobb Street on Fridays, and Beatrix Halm had a posting and a signal cabin at the end of a line and a yard roster with her name on it.

And one of them had nothing anywhere else, and had spent four months making certain of it.

"He picked me because I was on the stair," said Sun Meiling.

She said it without any expression at all.

"He picked me because I was the only person standing between that door and the kitchen when he knocked, and because I came down, and because I have never once in four months been anywhere that this notice could have been delivered to instead.

"You can be got at anywhere. That is what the last four months were for." She put her hand flat on the notice. "I am not anywhere. And a piece of paper about a building does not have to find me. It only has to find the building, and then it only has to wait for somebody to answer the door, and the person who answers the door is always the one who has not got anywhere else to be."

She opened the door.

"Sir," she said. "I will sign it."

The young man took the stub out of the satchel and put it on the half-door and put his own hand flat on it to stop it going in the wind, and held the pen out, and she took the pen and wrote her name on the line, which is the only name she has written on any document in four months and the first document of any kind that has carried that name and that street together since March.

She gave the pen back and read the stub where he was holding it, which is what her hands do.

"It wants the hour."

"Half past ten," he said. "Put whatever hour it is."

"It wants the hour." She wrote it. "It does not want my band."

"It does not want your band. There is nowhere on it to put your band." He was almost kind about it, in the way that a man being accurate is almost kind. "That is the whole reason this one is a notice and not a return. A return has a column in it for a band and it is four inches long and it is the only part of it anybody ever fills in."

He put the stub in the satchel and put the notice on the step where it had been delivered and put a weight on it out of his own pocket, and put his cap on.

"You will keep that sheet," he said. "It is not a demand for anything. It does not want a reply and there is no place on it to put one. It is telling you what the date is."

He looked at the step for a moment.

"I have done about two hundred of these. Do you know what the ones I remember are? The ones where somebody asked me whether they had to sign. Not the ones where they did not."

He went down the street at about ten past eleven.

The room did not move for about a minute.

Sun Meiling picked the notice up off the table and put it face down under her hand.

"Two things, and then I am going upstairs and I am not coming down again today.

"One. There is nothing on that sheet to answer. I have read it three times and it is a statement of a date and a consequence, and a household that answers it has taken a position on it, and a position is the only thing that a paper about this address can take off us.

"So we do not answer it, we do not discuss it at this table, and it is not going in any book in this house, and if a woman with a pen comes in here and asks to copy it she is going to be told no by me and not by any of you."

She looked at Ola Bright on the third step.

"That includes you. And I know what it costs you to be told that by me, and I would like it written in the room that I know."

Two. And this is the one that is mine and is not going to be discussed again either.

"For four months I have been the one person in this house that a piece of paper could not touch, and it turned out that that is not the same as being safe. It turns out that it made me the cheapest door in the city, and it has taken four months and a man with a satchel to find that out, and I would like it noted that not one other woman in this room could have had this happen to her."

The room was very quiet.

"Sun," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"You can say it."

"I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to go and open the hatch, because it is eleven and there is a queue."

She was already getting her coat. "What you have just bought is not safety. It is the opposite of safety. It is a woman who can be reached at one address.

"It has taken this house four months to make you that and one morning to undo it, and it is the only name in the house that is on anything at all."

"Yes," said Sun Meiling.

"Give me the notice on your way past so that I can see the print on the back, and that is all I am asking for."

She went out.

He came back to the table about an hour later and the notice was gone and the table had been cleared and there was a stub of pencil and a sheet of paper in his place.

She had written two lines on the back page of the notebook, under the day's six, in the seventh space, which had held one sentence since the thirty-first of March and two lines on the eleventh and had never been anything else.

The date was on it. The fifteenth, which was in two days, and which was the first day of anything that had ever come to that door with a reason attached to it.

And above the date, in her own hand, one sentence.

IF A PAPER COMES FOR THIS HOUSE, IT COMES TO ME, AND I ANSWER IT, AND I DO NOT ASK ANYBODY ELSE IN THIS HOUSE WHAT THE ANSWER IS.

He read it and did not go up the stair, and after about a minute he read it again, because it was the same sentence as the one in pencil on the wall of the landing, with the same construction and the same shape, and it had been written by the woman who had refused to have a copy of the other one and had then gone down four stairs and signed a piece of paper in a stranger's hand because he was standing on the step.

Sun Meiling had gone back up and shut her door and had not come down for the rest of the day.

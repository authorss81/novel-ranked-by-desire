# The Counting Room

He had worked in the building for four years and had never once been past the second-floor counter, and on the morning of the twenty-seventh he went past it at ten past eight in his overalls with six women.

The counter runs the width of the floor and behind it there is a room about twelve feet deep with a table in it, two chairs, a rack of pigeonholes on the wall going up to the ceiling, and a door with a glass panel in it and COUNTING painted on the glass in the ordinary way, which is to say it had been painted by somebody who had been told to paint a word.

The clerk behind the counter was a woman he had had a conversation with on the fifth of this month that had lasted ninety seconds and had ended with a refusal and a slip of paper.

"Twenty-one days," said the woman. "And you again."

"And six of them this time."

"Then there will be six sets of hands on this counter and I will have to count all of them." She looked past him. "How many of those six are on the premises."

"Seven women keep an address in that building and I am not one of them."

"That is not what I asked. I asked how many are on the premises. Today. In the building."

He had not thought about that. He had been thinking about the box at the foot of the form since the seventh of this month and had not once thought about the first question, which was how many persons of reading age keep an address at the premises, which is above the four questions and is the thing a clerk reads before she reads anything else.

"Seven," said Beatrix Halm, from behind him. "And one of them is asleep in a chair and one of them is standing on a step outside a building in the Vestry because she cannot go back in, and neither of those two has ever been on a piece of paper, and you are going to want the figure and the figure is seven."

Wray wrote down seven.

"Say the names," said Wray.

"No."

"Then I count the returns and I get the names off the returns, and if the returns do not agree with each other then I send it back with a second issue and a shorter clock, and there is no third thing."

She counted for about four minutes. She has a tally frame and she used it, one answer at a time, and she read each answer in the hand it had been written in, which is a thing she has done ten thousand times and which does not depend on anybody explaining anything.

"Six. Six and a note underneath. Not mine. Six with a footnote about a space at the foot of a book that is not a document. Six, then six again, and underneath it seven women in the house, and that she is the seventh and is not answering for herself." She did not look up. "A Field division address in the second space, which is not an answer to this question."

"I wrote that on the sixteenth," said Runa Sato, "because it was true."

"It is true and it is in the wrong box." Wray went back three lines. "One of these is a price. One is a sentence with no answer in it. One came by post with the word DISPENSARY on the envelope and refuses the third question and gives a reason, and the reason is a decision."

"Six hands," said Ines Duarte, "written between two and six on the afternoon of the sixteenth, unconsulted, under a standing instruction, exactly as instructed."

"Unconsulted is not a thing that exists on a form." Wray put the tally frame down. "Six people have given six figures to one question. Four of them say six and one says seven and one has written an address in Cordwain, and I am required to have one figure."

"Seven," said Beatrix Halm.

"That is the figure on the sheet from the sixteenth, in seven hands, and I am going to write seven, because you have all told me seven in a room and not one of you can be got to say it twice in the same hand. It is arithmetic and not agreement, and I am telling you that so that none of you goes home thinking I believed you."

She wrote the seven and turned the sheet round again and put her thumb on the bottom of it.

"Now the box."

It was four inches wide with a heading over it and it had been empty on two forms since the sixth, and the woman put her thumbnail under the rule and turned the page towards him so that he could see it properly, which is a thing a clerk does and does not do.

"PREMISES HELD UNDER THE STANDING OF. Four inches. And it wants the standing of this house, which is a person or a firm or an employer or an association at item eleven, and I have had this form in front of me every working day since the seventh and I have never once had a name in it, and I am required to complete it before the sheet goes down."

"Then complete it," said Sun Meiling, from the stair.

"I will complete it with the standing that is permitted and it is the fifth one, and then these premises are held under a schedule at item eleven for as long as the schedule exists, and not one office in this city will ever serve a paper at this door, because there is no person standing in it to answer one." Wray looked up. "Is that what you want."

"That is what I want a great deal less than anything else that is available."

"Then somebody has to write in it."

They had known that since yesterday afternoon. They had come across Cobb Street and up sixty-one steps past a chop-house at ten past eight in the morning for that.

It went on for about two minutes and nothing was said worth putting in a copybook, and then Marguerite Vale said, "It is him," and Runa Sato said, "Put it in," in the same half second, and Tessa Okonkwo said, "Lin Hao," and Sun Meiling said nothing at all.

He had his hand out before he had decided to, which was the part he would think about later.

He wrote on the ruled line, in his own hand, at the foot of the fourth question of a composition return for premises above a chop-house in Ferrier Street in the Slough, in a room with a glass door in it on the second floor of the branch office where he cleaned six floors of washrooms every working morning for four years.

Nine seconds.

Wray read it, and did not read it twice, which she was entitled to, and then initialled under his name and put the date and the hour on it and said, "That is a return."

That was all it took. Four minutes and nine seconds at a counter on the second floor of his own building by a woman he had had a conversation with three weeks ago, and the thing four months and seven women and a notebook under a table had been protecting was now a document because he had put his name on a line.

"It goes down today. A complete return goes to the district and somebody reads it inside a week. A complete return also opens a certification of sixty days from the date of filing, and the certificate comes at the end of that and not before, and it is the certificate that makes it a household and not a filing.

"So you will get nothing for sixty days and you will be told things for sixty days, and the first thing you will be told is your own address off your own return."

He said, "Thank you," and meant it, which was new.

"Second sheet. The first issue. That one has four questions and no fifth, and the box is on it, and I need a name on that one as well. You will have to write it twice, and the second one is the older sheet, and the district uses the earliest complete."

He wrote it a second time. It took nine seconds and it was much harder, and neither Wray nor the six women behind him said anything about that.

"That is both of them. I have had this counter all morning and not one person has shouted at me, which is not usual."

She put them into a wire basket and then stopped with her hand in it.

"There is one thing on here I want to look at before it goes, and it is not mine to change and I am allowed to look."

Runa Sato's second line carried a Field division address, and it was the only line on either sheet with a standing printed against it in the Office's own type. Wray took a small book out of the tray and opened it and ran a finger down a column and then stopped and went back up it.

"Your standing was entered on the eighth as premises held by the Field division at Cordwain," she said.

"Yes."

"That was true on the eighth. It stopped being true on the twelfth, at about ten past two in the afternoon, when a line was ruled through a name on a strength wall in a building on the other side of this city. There is an entry here. I did not make it and I do not know who made it, and it was made this morning at about seven and it is dated the twelfth."

She looked up.

"So this sheet goes down this morning with six answers in it, one of which is false, and the Office will check the false one against an entry made at seven o'clock this morning by somebody who has read a wall. And it will match."

Runa Sato had not moved.

"So the return completes and the household is certified for sixty days, and in about eleven days there is a line in a district file saying that a person at those premises gave the Office an answer which was true on the eighth and is not true now."

She put the basket on the counter.

"There is nothing I can do about that and I have looked."

"Do it," said Runa Sato.

"I have looked. That is me doing it."

He came out of the room at twenty to nine on the morning of the twenty-seventh and went down the stair and out into the street, where six women were standing in the doorway of a building that had been shut for eleven minutes and was open again.

None of them could tell from the front of his face whether he had been brave or whether he had been fast.

Ines Duarte took his arm about four steps up Ferrier Street.

"You have done something in that room which you have not told this table, and you will tell me what it is on this pavement, and you will do it in about nine seconds, because I have known you nine years and you cannot carry a shape like that for longer than a street."

He told her. It took about ninety seconds with the shutters half down and a queue coming in behind him, and he did not say who had signed it, because he had not said that on the twenty-fifth either and she had let him.

She listened standing absolutely still, in the way she stands still, and when he had finished she looked at the far end of the street for about eleven seconds.

"It comes off the shelf," she said.

"Yes."

"Within the hour. A complete return on an address reconciles every other return on that address, and a return that reconciles is lifted, and a return that is lifted is read. That is not a threat. It is the ordinary condition of a return, and I have watched it happen to a street in Kestrel Hill."

"I did it on purpose. I did it in nine seconds."

"Say why."

He had been asked that by about five people in four months and he had answered it well four times.

"Because the alternative was that the Office fills that box in with eleven thousand people and this house is held under item eleven for as long as the schedule exists.

"Agnete Lindqvist was standing on a step in the Vestry on the twentieth saying that eleven thousand people have survived twenty years by being a shape and not a fact, and I put her shape into her own hand on the twenty-third afternoon, and that was the cheapest door in the city again. This time I did it myself."

She took her hand off his arm.

"That is the true reason and it is not the whole of it, and I am not going to make you find the whole of it at eight in the morning on a pavement. I am going to tell you what you have actually done, because you have not worked it out and you have about four seconds before you do.

"You found a piece of paper you were never supposed to know about, and instead of burning it or hiding it or leaving it where it was, you went and made it true.

"You have not prevented anything and you have not protected one woman. You took a thing that was inert on a shelf and carried it across a city in your own handwriting and put it in the only machine in this city that makes a thing findable, and it took nine seconds, and you did not have to be brave about any part of it.

"And in about eleven days a line will appear in a district file saying that a member of this household gave the Office a standing that is false. That line carries the date, and the thing that completed it was a head of household, and the head of household is named on the same sheet."

"That is all of it."

"It is not all of it and you know that it is not." She started walking again. "Four of the six people in that building are going to find out that you knew about a document concerning them for three days before you completed it, and that you did not tell them. Every one of them will be right. You will not be able to give one of them a better reason than the others.

"That is not a cost I could price for you. I would not know what to charge. Go and do the second floor."

He went and cleaned the second floor. It took two hours and ten minutes, and he was better at it than he had been at any point in four years, because a man who has just done the thing he came in to do cleans the way a man does who has somewhere to be afterwards.

One hundred and eighteen lines on the ledger and nine of them turned, and the ninth was still himself, and nothing had been spent on anybody at all, and he had promised on the record, in a hall, before the instrument was run, that he would not spend anything on himself again.

He had not. That was true.

What he had spent was nine seconds and a piece of paper and the last thing he had that a form could not take off him, which was the fact that not one person in Ostara could say where he was on any particular morning.

A mile and a half away, on the third floor of a building with four shelves, the strip of card on the front of the bundle had been filled in at about eleven minutes past five the previous afternoon, against a column headed persons off the public stair.

The figure against it was two.

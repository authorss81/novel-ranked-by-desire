# Wren Kallo

She had been on paper review since the nineteenth of last month and paper review at the second bench means a room on the ground floor of the Reading with a desk in it, a chair, a window that does not open, and a door that is opened from outside by a clerk who decides when.

He had been let in at eleven and the clerk had stood in the doorway the entire time, and Wren Kallo had spoken at the volume of a person who has a witness in the room and has decided the witness is furniture.

"Sit down," she said. "I have written it out and I am going to read it out, and if you interrupt me I will lose the thread, and if I lose the thread I will start at the beginning, and the beginning takes about forty minutes."

He sat down.

"Twenty-sixth of March. Counter desk, records. A man I have never met asked her whether a member who has a finding could be treated at a clinic without the clinic entering anything about him, and whether the clinic would be asked to produce anything if a warrant came.

"He did not use your name. He used the finding. He asked whether a place that had never been asked about a member could be made to produce a record of one."

She turned the sheet round and pushed it through the gap in the desk where the door was not quite shut, and the clerk looked at it and gave it back, and Wren did not stop.

"That is a fifth person. Not a fifth — it is five deep and four of them are outside this room. That is me, him, and Cressida, and my sister, and him."

"How long since you knew it."

"Nine days." She put the pen down. "I have had it for nine days, which is two days longer than I have been able to hold it, and I want that entered, because in March you told me that a rule announced to a room becomes an instruction, and I have been proving it all month without meaning to.

"I cannot keep it. That is the entire mechanism. I have never once been able to keep it, and it is the only reason I am any use to anybody, and it is the reason I cannot sit in a room like this for the rest of my life."

"Who is it."

"A dispensary clerk at the Vestry. Hessa Lund. She has been there nine years and she does the clinic's requisitions and she keeps the sheet of what the clinic has not been able to buy."

"Hessa Lund asked that question."

"Hessa Lund passed it to somebody. She asked a colleague in a corridor and a colleague asked a man in a queue. That is how it reached her and she does not know that is how it reaches anybody, which is the part that should worry you more than the rest.

"She is not a party. She is a woman who has been asked a question in a corridor and repeated it, and a question repeated once in a corridor in this city becomes a document inside nine days. I have watched it happen from the other end for eleven years."

She put both hands flat on the desk.

"Hessa Lund is not a party in anything," he said. "Which means she is not the one, and it has been you since the start."

"No. Being the end of it has been sitting in me since the twenty-sixth, and that is the half of it I cannot put down. A question asked in a corridor is only a question until somebody answers it twice. Hearing it come back is what makes it worth repeating, and the man who sent him to that corridor will know it was repeated by Thursday."

"Then it has one day."

"It has until the fifth, because the Exchange publishes on the morning of the fifth and there is a clerk in every dispensary in the Slough who fills in that circular and Hessa Lund fills in hers, and on Thursday she will have to say what she knows, and what she knows on Thursday is whatever she was told in a corridor."

The furnace in the corridor made a noise and stopped.

"Lin Hao. I want something entered the other way and I want you to refuse it and I want you to refuse it for the reason I am going to give you and not for a better one."

"Go on."

"Put me in it."

The clerk shifted.

"Put me at the head of it, as far as they are concerned. Four people know. If the one they find is me, the chain ends at me.

"You chose the other three on the eighth of last month because each of them was the smallest possible thing you could spend. One is a clerk on a counter. One is a woman who asked how her mother's knee was. And I was the one who gave you the number in the first place, and you had no choice about me, because I would have said it in a corridor whether you had agreed to it or not."

"You are not the head of it."

"Then I am at the end of it, which is worse, and you know it is worse, because an end is a door and a head is a queue." She said it fast and she had clearly rehearsed it. "You put me at the head and I am a counter-forensics officer with forty minutes of work a day and a post I will not get back, and that is a price I can pay in a currency I understand."

"No."

"It is the only—"

"No, and I am going to tell you the actual reason, and it is not a better reason than yours, and I would like you to notice that it is not."

He leaned forward on the chair.

"If you are at the end of it, then the chain starts in this room, and a chain that starts in this room starts with me.

"You become the only door between the thing and the corridor. And in about four months somebody will work out that the door is a person who cannot keep a thing, and they will not need to do anything clever at all. They will wait for me to come and see you, and then they will ask the clerk who opened the door which man it was."

The clerk in the doorway had not moved in eleven minutes. She had not looked at either of them, and she had not written anything down, and Lin Hao understood about half a second too late that a clerk standing in a doorway holding a door open for eleven minutes is a document that has not been written yet.

"That is what paper review does to me," said Wren Kallo, following his eyes. "It is a room with a desk and a chair and a window and a door, and I have not been in it alone with another person since the nineteenth of last month.

"So everything I say to you has a witness and everything you say to me has a witness, and neither of us gets to find out this month whether I can hold anything at all, because there is a woman in the corridor whose whole job is standing here."

"Your sister."

"My sister is the reason I am able to do this and the reason I cannot." She said it fast. "She is a laundress at the Vestry and she asked how their mother's knee was in the middle of a kitchen in February because that is the sort of thing a person says in a kitchen, and Wren Kallo was standing next to her, and Wren Kallo answered it, and then she did not put the kitchen down for three weeks.

"She does not know what she asked. She does not know that she has asked. She thinks her mother's knee is better and she thinks that is the end of it, and the price of my keeping a secret in this city is that I put that in my sister's ear and then she went on with her week as if nothing had happened in it."

Wren Kallo sat very still.

"That is better than me being in the middle of it," she said.

"That is worse for you and better for five people I have never met, and I would rather you were angry with me than grateful, because you being grateful to me is the version where this keeps going."

"That is the whole of it," she said. "A corridor and a dispensary clerk and a question about a clinic."

"One day. The fifth is a Thursday."

"The fifth is a Thursday," she agreed. "The Board repaints and the Exchange circular goes up and there are two things published in Ostara and they happen on the same morning, and there are probably eleven thousand people in this city who have never once noticed that those are the same morning."

He stood up.

"Lin Hao." She had not said anything else and then she said something else. "There is a thing I have not told you and I am going to tell you now because if I do not tell you now I will tell you in about nine days in a corridor."

He stopped with his hand on the door.

"The question he asked was whether a clinic that had never been entered in a member's file could be entered in one. It was not a question about you. There were four people in the queue and he asked it loud enough for all four of them, and I have been sitting in this room for nine days turning that over, and I do not think it was about you at all."

"Then what was it about."

"About whether they could get in later," said Wren Kallo, "which is a question you ask about a building and not about a man, and which I have never once seen asked about a person in eleven years, and which somebody asked about this building on the twenty-sixth of March in a corridor in the Slough."

She put her pen away.

"Now go and find out who wants to get into that building, because it is not going to be you, and if it is you then you are the only person in this city who has been told, and you will be the last."

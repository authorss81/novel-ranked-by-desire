# The Pale Rectangle

He went up to the square at half past eight on the morning of the twenty-sixth with the broom on his shoulder, because he was off the early shift and the Reading keeps a man on the square on a repaint day whatever else it does or does not keep, and because he had gone up that wall four times a month for four years and had never once gone at nine.

The men came at nine.

They came with a ladder and a crate and four buckets, and they had been doing that wall for longer than he had been in the city, and one of them was about sixty and had a beard going white in the middle and knew him.

"You are the one from the branch."

"That is right."

"You come up here at half eight every day and you have never once been here at nine, and I have been thinking about it since February." The white-bearded one took the broom off him and handed him a ladder instead. "Hold this, and if I ask you to hold it you hold it, and if I ask you to get off it you get off it fast."

That was the whole of the conversation for about an hour and a half.

A Board is not a list of people. It is a list of bands, printed in ceramic, and it is read by about a hundred and forty thousand people a week who have never once thought about what a tile is. Every Thursday the men take down what the week's list says and put up what the week's list says, and where the list is empty they leave the slot bare, because a tile in it that has not been claimed is a claim.

He held a ladder for fifty minutes and then held a bucket for ten.

At about ten past ten the white-bearded man came down the ladder with a tile in his hands and put it face down in the crate.

It was a white tile with a name on it in black, and he could see the name from where he was standing at the bottom of the ladder, and he did not need to look.

IVO SARN. BAND SEVEN.

"Off," said the white-bearded man. "He is not on the list this week and he has not been on the list since the first, and I have been putting that tile up every Thursday for four weeks on my own because there was nothing on the list either way and a hole in the wall is not a list."

He set the tile in the crate face down and turned round and looked at the wall.

Where it had been there was a rectangle of bare ceramic about the size of a tile, the colour of the biscuit underneath the glaze rather than the colour of the wall, and he had been looking down at that rectangle from about nine feet away four times a month for four years, and until the man took the tile out of it he had not known it was there.

He had a name on the wall from the fourth of March until the twenty-second, which was a stranger's name, and then a second stranger's name until this morning, and now he had a pale rectangle, and the pale rectangle was about four inches by two and it was exactly the size of the thing that six women had refused to fill in for four months.

He stood and looked at it for about a minute.

"Thirty years I have done that wall," said the white-bearded man, coming down and starting to wash his brushes in a bucket. "You want to know what a blank tile is. It is not a shame. It is not a man in a cell. It is a band that did not move, or a slot the list has nothing to say about, and there are about forty of them on that wall at any time and half of them belong to people who are standing in this square."

"How many are about people who are not standing in this square."

"Most," said the white-bearded man.

They got the crate down off the ladder and he carried his end of it with the man, which is a thing a man does on a repaint morning, and it went down the steps and out along the rail, and that was where the other two were.

Wren Kallo was at the rail in her own coat with a reading card in her hand, and the reason she was there is that the Classification Wing certifies what goes on that wall every Thursday and has done for four years, and she was there to sign the week's list, which is the most dull document in Ostara and the only one in the city that is read in public.

Anneke Roos was on the third step of the stair with a folder.

She was the woman who had struck Ivo Sarn's paragraph through at a quarter past five on the twenty-ninth of March and entered a different sentence underneath it and signed underneath that, and his name had been on a tile on this wall for seven days before she did it, and nothing had been printed about any of it.

Anneke looked at the bare slot for about four seconds and then put her folder under her arm.

"That will be bare until somebody takes the slot," she said. "It is not held for anybody. It is not reserved. There is a list of bands and the slot is a position on the wall, and a position on that wall is nothing at all off it."

"Is it in anybody's file that it is empty."

"No. It is ceramic."

That was the whole of what she said about it, and she said it to Wren Kallo, and he heard it, and he understood about four minutes later that he had heard it.

The rest of them came up the street at about ten past eleven and he did not see the street because he was on the wall side of the rail with a bucket.

Seven of them and Wren Kallo and him, which was nine people, and they came from the direction of Ferrier Street in a loose group that was not a group, and the loose group came apart about forty feet out and walked the last forty feet separately, which is a thing a household in the Slough does without being told.

Sun Meiling was in daylight.

She had been out at eleven at night on the seventeenth and at a market at a quarter past one on the twentieth, and this was the first time she had stood in a street with a sky over it since the first of December, and she went the whole length of the rail and then came back and stood opposite the wall and looked up at it.

Runa Sato came last and stood about twenty feet from the wall with her hands behind her back, in a place where the square went past and the rail did not, and he understood what that was, because a woman who cannot be found by a posting or a division or a service has to decide once a month whether she is going to be visible or not, and she had decided.

Agnete Lindqvist stood on the step of the stair with her back to the stone.

She could not get back into a building. She had got to the fourth step of a building off the Vestry market on the morning of the twentieth and had sat down on it, and had been outside every day since, and had gone into the Vestry clinic twice through the front door like a person, and was on a step in the open air on the morning of the twenty-sixth of April looking up at a wall four hundred feet away.

He did not go to her. She had his eye from forty feet and she lifted one hand about four inches off the rail of the stair, which is what you do to a person you can see and not to anybody else, and then she put it back.

Wren Kallo finished signing at about eleven and came and stood next to him with the folder under her arm, and she did not ask, and he told her, because she was one of three people alive who could be told and the other two had not asked.

"Twenty-second of March," he said. "Late return. One address, six names, and the box at the foot empty since the day it was signed."

Wren Kallo did not look at him.

"Right," she said, after about four seconds.

"It was a price."

"I did not ask what it was a price for." She was watching a man put a bucket down at the foot of the ladder. "I am going to tell you the part that is going to cost you and then I am going to go and stand somewhere else, and I would like you to hear it while there are two hundred people in this square.

"An incomplete filing is not a document. It has no force against anybody, it is not producible, and it cannot be cited by an office or by a person or by a division. In this city an incomplete filing is a thing that is true about nothing at all."

She held up one finger.

"A complete filing is a document. It is indexed. It is produced to anybody who asks for it by name or by address, and it is produced once, and the copy goes to the person who asked and not to the person it is about. That is the whole design of it and it has been the design since before either of us was born."

She turned round and looked at him.

"So there is a piece of paper in a building a mile and a half from your front door with six women's names and their bands on it, and the only reason it has never reached anybody is that it was never finished.

"It becomes finished the moment a head of household goes into a box, and it does not become finished for you, or because of you, or on your account. It becomes finished because a form is complete. Then it is a document. Then a district office reads it, and then it can be produced to a clerk with a warrant, or to a man with a shilling, or to a woman who does not want it and has curiosity.

"And the copy goes to them."

She took the folder out from under her arm and looked at it.

"You have got until tomorrow afternoon and after tomorrow afternoon the thing you have been reading is the sort of thing that gets read. Not by the people who hate you. By clerks, on a schedule, because it is due."

That was the whole of what she said and she took it away to the third step and signed something else.

The painters came down and washed the buckets and got the crate up onto a barrow and the white-bearded man washed his hands in the fountain at the corner of the square and said, "You are welcome any Thursday," to him, and went.

By about a quarter past twelve there were four hundred people in the square and nine of the people in it were standing within twenty feet of each other in daylight, at their ease, in a row, looking at a wall.

Not one person came over. That was the thing he kept coming back to on the walk back.

Nine people were standing under the Board in front of four hundred witnesses on the morning the wall was repainted, and not one person in that square walked over to them, and one of the eleven households that had paid a shilling for public paper about that street was four doors up with a boot scraper outside, because a wall with a pale rectangle on it is not a story. It is ceramic.

It is a picture of a household in a city that has never once been interested in one, and it cannot be resold, and it does not have to be defended, and it is the first thing this house has made in four months that it did not have to write down.

He got on the tram at the junction and sat in it.

Tomorrow was the twenty-seventh. Five days had gone by since the twenty-first, and the twenty-first had been the first of these four months on which he had understood something, and the twenty-seventh was the day two forms were due in which the box at the foot of both of them wanted one name, and he had said that name out loud in a kitchen on the previous afternoon and could not get it back.

The pale rectangle would be on that wall until somebody took the slot. And it was not held for anybody, and it was not reserved, and no file anywhere in the city recorded that it was empty, and that was, he thought, in a way he was going to have to sit with for a long time, the most exact description of his life that the only public instrument in Ostara had ever produced.

# The Hostel Room

The Field hostel at Cordwain is a building for people the Field pays for, and it has a yard and a gate and a stair, and the rooms are two floors of timber with a lavatory at the end of each, and the doors are the only lock a person in it has ever had.

Her room was on the second floor and it was about nine feet by eleven, with a bunk, a desk, a chair, a footlocker, and a window that looked at the next building about four feet away.

Her kit was packed and it was by the door and it had been packed since the thirteenth, which was sixteen days. It had not gone anywhere. A Field operative packs a kit the way a person puts a coat on a hook, and Runa Sato had not had anywhere to be since the twelfth.

She opened the door before he knocked, which is a thing a person does who has been listening for footsteps on a stair for sixteen days.

On the desk, face up, was a roster.

It was a sheet on a board about two feet by three feet, and it had four columns ruled on it and a heading over the second one, and no heading at all over the fourth. She had brought it down off the muster room wall herself, which took the strength of both hands and which she had done without asking anybody and without telling anybody.

In the body of it, on the line that said R. SATO, somebody had run a rule from the left margin clean across to the right edge, and there was nothing written under it, because there was nothing to write.

She had laid it on the desk upside down from where he stood, and then she had turned it round, and she had left it there.

"Sit down," she said. "You have got about twenty minutes and I am going to use all of them, and then I am going to want about an hour with you, and then you can go."

He sat down on the chair. It was a chair for a desk and not for sitting in.

"I am going to say this in the order it has to be said in and I am not going to be tender about any part of it, because you have been in a house with six women for four months who are all better at this than I am and I am going to be worse on purpose."

"Say it."

"On the twenty-seventh you put your name at the top of a return. And a head of household answers in his own name for every member of it. That is the law and Ines has read it and it is correct." She put her hands flat on the desk either side of the roster. "Here is what it comes to in the field and I am the only person at that table who has spent nine years being the thing it comes to.

"A household with a head can be asked to produce a member. That is the whole of it. Not to answer for them, not to speak for them. To produce them. And the person the request comes to is the head, and if the head cannot produce them then the request goes back and it goes on a list, and a name on a list in this city is a thing that gets used twice.

"So there has to be somebody in that house who can be produced when the head cannot produce the rest. And there is not one, and there has never been one, because the entire project of that house for four months has been to be a place where a person cannot be produced."

She turned round and looked at him.

"And I am asking to be that."

"You are asking to be the thing they come for."

"I am asking to be answerable for all of them. Not for you. You have got a name at the top of a piece of paper now and you are answerable to a district office, and there is nothing I can do about that and I would not want to.

"I am asking to be answerable for the six of them. I am asking because I am the only one of the seven of you who can carry anything, and because I have been produced once already and I know exactly what it costs, and because it cost me the premises and it cost me the wall and it would have cost me a great deal more if the count had gone the other way."

"What does it cost this time."

"The Field owns one thing off me and I have been not knowing that for sixteen days." She sat down on the edge of the bunk, which made the bunk take her weight

with a sound. "A Field operative is not a person who works. A Field operative is an instrument that can be sent for. Nine years of a division have had my body on a list, and the list is the division, and being on it has been the only thing in the city that could find me, and I gave that up in a corridor on the eighth of this month in exchange for a thing that turned out to be a lie.

"And you cannot do both. A thing that is sent for and a thing that answers are the same wire. If I am the instrument that produces five other people when a request comes, then I cannot be the instrument that is sent for, and the only way in this city to stop being sent for is to ask, in writing, to be struck as available, and that is a resignation and it does not come back."

"How long does it take."

"Three months. It takes three months and a signature and it is the last document I will ever sign that has my name on it and it says that a person who has spent nine years in a division has told the division to stop having her."

She let that sit for about four seconds.

"I would like it noted that I am not doing it for the five of them and I am not doing it for you.

"I am doing it because on the twentieth of this month I stood at the bottom of five steps in the Vestry and asked to stand at the bottom of a stair for a woman who has been indoors for eleven years, and I have been carrying that round ever since, and a woman who has been carrying something for sixteen days needs somewhere to put it down."

"Right," he said.

"Four things, and then we go."

"Say them."

"One. I am awake and I am not drunk and I am not tired and nothing has been given to me and nothing is going to be given to you, and if either of us is impaired I stop. That is not a formality. I have carried two men out of a building in this city who were impaired and one of them died and it was not the building."

"Two. Nothing is spent. Not on me and not on you and not on anybody else in this building, and I do not want your hand anywhere near me for the rest of the evening, and I know what that costs you and it is a price and I am asking you to pay it."

"Three." She stood up off the bunk. "There is something on your left side. Four of them, since about the end of March, and I know how I know, because I was in a hall on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth and I felt all three of them land while I was holding you up, and I have thought about it every day since.

"I am not going to touch them and I am not going to ask you about them. I am a person who carries other people's weight for a living, and I have been told by a doctor with a scale that those four are permanent, and a person who carries things does not go near a thing that is a permanent instrument. You can put them wherever you want."

"Four is that I am going to do most of this with my own body because that is what I am for, and I am telling you that in advance because a man who has been looked after by four other people in four months is going to find that funny, and I have decided in advance that I do not care."

"You said four things."

"I said four things."

She was right about most of it.

There is a thing a person does who carries, which is that they take the weight before they take anything else, and she did not ask him to lie down and she did not ask him to sit down and she came in under him and took him.

He had been got up off the floor of the Fourth Branch's sixth-floor washroom by a woman with a balance she had carried up the back stairs herself, and he had been got off the ground in a hall by six strangers at once, and he had had his weight taken off him by women in rooms all over this city for four months, and none of them had ever had to think about it.

She thought about it. He watched her decide where to stand and how to get her feet and she did it in about two seconds and she was not thinking about him at all, she was thinking about the load, and then she had it and he was a man standing in the air being held by somebody who was paid to hold people, and there was not one moment in it that was gentle.

"Left foot forward," she said. "No. Here. That is better, that is much better, you are on your good side and you did not know you knew."

She was about a stone and a half heavier than she looked and she had said so in a corridor once and had been told she was joking.

They took about an hour and she did most of it with her own body.

She was not fast at the start. She went about it the way she went about a carry, in order, and she told him what she was doing about nine seconds before she did it every time.

That is a habit from a job where the person being carried cannot see and must not be startled. She had not thought about it at all, and he worked it out about two hours afterwards, when he realised she had been narrating the entire time in a low flat register like a woman reading out a load plan.

Then she was not slow either, and that came as a surprise, because on the twelfth of this month a woman had sat in a corridor and told him about a rule on a wall in the wrong tense and he had built a woman out of it who was strong and slow.

He got his hands on her and she let him for about a minute and then told him to leave them where they were, and he left them where they were for most of it.

The bunk took a great deal of weight that it had not been designed for and the frame complained about it once. She did not stop. She worked with it.

She kept talking to him the entire time in that flat register, in short sentences, and he understood about half of it as instruction and the other half as a woman who has been alone in a nine-foot room for sixteen days and has run out of people to say things to, and he understood about a third of that on the second half of the hour and did not know why until afterwards.

"Say something," she said, at one point, and he said something, and she told him that was better.

She came first, which she had decided about in about the first four minutes and had told him about in the first four minutes, and she did not make a sound, and she held on to him afterwards with both arms and about as much weight as she had been doing the whole time, which is what she is for, and which is also the single most expensive thing he had ever been given for nothing.

He did not use her name and she did not use his, and that was not an agreement.

It was that a roster does not take a name. And she had told him on the eleventh of this month, in a building off Cobb Street, that a division clerk would ask him for a figure and not for a feeling, and that a figure is the whole of what could be asked for. There is a thing a person can be in a file and not be in a room.

"There," she said, at some point afterwards, in the dark, with her hand flat on his chest. "You have got four of them and you have not told anybody which."

"No."

"Good." She did not move her hand. "Then we are the same as the other six, and that is the first thing in five months that has made this household normal and I am going to lie here and think about that for about a minute before I say the next part."

She lay there for about two minutes.

"The next part is this and it is administrative and it is not going to take any of it off either of us.

"Being on the answer line of a household means that when a request comes, it comes to me and not to the head, because I am the only body in that building that can be produced, and the division owns a list of bodies and mine is going off that list in three months' time by a signature I am going to give in about four weeks.

"After that, if a request comes, I answer for five people who have all declined to be written down, and I am standing in a room in front of a person with a card, and I say *they are not produceable* and I am believed, because I am a person who has been produced before and has a hearing on the twelfth of this month that says so in an office somewhere.

"All of it is what I am buying, and it is not protection. It is a woman standing in front of a thing and being believed, five times, for the rest of her life, by people who have no idea who any of us are."

"Do you want to be thanked for it."

"No." She said it immediately. "And I want to be precise about why, because I have thought about it and I would rather say it now than have it said for me later.

"It is not modesty. If you thank me for this then it is something I did for you, and if it is something I did for you then it is a thing that can be unpaid.

"And I am going to need it to be paid, every time, for as long as I am alive. It is going to be a debt and I am going to be the creditor of it and I am going to want it. Do not thank me and it stays a position. Thank me and it turns into a favour and I lose it in a year."

"Whatever you want."

"Whatever I want," said Runa Sato, "is in the third column of that thing on the desk, and it is not yours to give and mine to take."

Then she got up and put her feet on the floor and stood over him for a second while she found the tunic she had left over the chair, and she was not self-conscious about any part of it and she was not performing, and she had not been self-conscious or performing at any point in the last hour.

"There is a thing I am going to ask you to do and then I am going to ask you to leave," she said.

"Say it."

"Read me the four lines of the muster-room wall."

He did not want to and did it.

She listened to it with her back to him and her tunic over her shoulder.

She had been made to wait in a corridor for the last twenty minutes of a four-hour hearing on the twelfth of this month because she would have been able to object, and the rule that put her in the corridor is the rule that put a rule through her line, and the sentence painted above the roster in older paint went there eleven years ago and is still the only readable thing on that wall.

"You are the only person who has ever read that aloud to me," she said.

"I am the only person who has been in the room where it is."

"And you are the only person who has ever read it out to me," she said again, "and I have been in that room about four hundred times, and I want you to notice what that sentence is about, and I am not going to explain it to you."

Then she turned round and put her hand flat on the roster, which was still face up on the desk with a rule through her name on it, and she looked at it for about ten seconds the way a woman looks at a thing she has been told to put down.

"Do not thank me," she said.

She turned it face down.

"You can go out now and take the stair, and the yard gate is shut until six and it is shut from the inside, and I have the only key, and I will be here until about six, and tomorrow I will be on the line at Cordwain at half past four in the morning because that is what I do and I have not got a posting any more and I go because I want to be somewhere at half past four in the morning.

"Do not wait for me at the gate. A man waiting at a gate is a man anybody can ask about."

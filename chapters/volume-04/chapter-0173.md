# Sun Meiling

The knock came at about eleven on the morning of the twenty-third and it was the same knock.

Two, and a wait, and two. Not post. Post knocks and goes, and post has never once in four months come to that door, because that door is up sixty-one steps over a chop-house and the man who comes for the rent is the only person in Ostara who knew it was there before the thirteenth of this month.

Beatrix Halm was at the table and Nadia Ferrous had a coat on. Sun Meiling came down four stairs before anybody had said anything, and she had her hand on the rail, which is where her hand goes.

"Give it here," she said.

It was the young man with the satchel.

He took the sheet out of the top pocket and held it up in the way he had held the other one up, and he had the same face he had had on the thirteenth, the face of a man who has done this a couple of hundred times and got very good at not enjoying it.

"It is served on the premises," he said. "It is not served on a person."

"I know what it is served on. I signed the other one." She came the rest of the way down and read it standing in the doorway with the door open behind her, and he watched her get to the third line and go on, and then go back to the first.

It was longer than the notice. Four paragraphs, a heading in capitals, and at the foot a ruled space about two inches deep and the width of a page for names.

"A schedule of the persons of reading age keeping an address at these premises," she said, "required under the notice served on the thirteenth, for delivery within one month of the date of service."

"How long is that," said Runa Sato.

"One month."

"How long is one month from the date of service."

"From today. It is dated today." She gave the sheet back to him and came in and shut the door and stood with her back against it, and then said, "No," to the room, before anybody had asked her anything.

"No what," said Ines Duarte.

"No to all of you, and it is going to take nine seconds and then somebody is going to be angry with me, and I have been in this house for four months and I have earned the right to nine seconds."

She held the sheet up.

"It wants names. It is two inches deep and it is names, and it is served on these premises by a person of reading age at these premises, and I have written on that doorframe since the thirteenth that a paper comes to me and I answer it and I do not ask anybody else in this house what the answer is.

"So I am not asking anybody. I am telling you that I am going to write six names in two inches of space, and that I have sat at that table for three days working out how, and that I cannot do it in the morning with a pen in my hand while six people watch me do it.

"And I am going to do it this afternoon on my own at that table, and if any one of you wants to speak to me about it you can do it now and you will get one sentence each."

The kitchen let about four seconds go past.

"Ask," said Beatrix Halm.

"I am not going to."

"Then I will say the thing you have already worked out, which is that a schedule served on premises that is not completed at the premises is served again." Beatrix Halm had her arms folded and said it flatly, the way she said things she had worked out on a train. "And a schedule served twice goes in front of an assessor, because one lawful thing is left when an office cannot reconcile a count it has been given twice."

"He is coming here," said Runa Sato.

"He has been here." Beatrix Halm looked at the floor. "None of us knows that. I said what an office does."

"You are the last person who can be spoken to about that and I am the reason it is coming." Runa Sato had not moved from the table. "Say the rest of it, Sun."

Sun Meiling stood against the door with the sheet in her hand and looked at the six of them, and he watched her do what she had been doing for four months, which was to count the room and then decide what it was worth.

"I am not going to write six names," she said. "And I am going to be very careful about how I say that, because I can see from where you are sitting that five of you have been about to say something kind about it and I would rather it did not happen in this room.

"Nadia has a service file that goes to a counter in Cobb Street once a week. Beatrix has a posting and a cabin and a yard roster. Runa has a process open in Cordwain and a rule through her line. Marguerite has a guild.

"Tessa has a clinic that is unfunded and one chart signed, and that is the whole of what the Wing tolerates it on. And Ines has a finding of the twenty-ninth of March in a file with a number on it, and any name she writes on a sheet served at this door goes into the same district office as that finding, in the same week, in the same hand.

"That is what is in those two inches. Not six names. Six doors."

She turned the sheet round and put it face down on the table.

"So I am going to write the number. And a number is not a schedule and a number does not complete anything, and the Office will send it again.

"And then I am going to write, in the space underneath, the standing these premises are held under. Which is in the margin of the second issue, printed in three lines at the foot of the page, which arrived in the post on the sixteenth of this month and which six of you have been arguing about for a week."

Marguerite Vale said, "Say the number."

"I am going to write the number, and I am going to write the standing, and the standing is item eleven." Sun Meiling had her hand flat on the sheet. "Associations holding no plate and no audit. Eleven thousand and some hundred persons in this quarter, membership attested, attestation not audited, and no composition return filed by any of them at any time.

"I am going to write that as the answer to who holds these premises, in my hand, in this room, today. And I want every person at this table to understand that I am not choosing it out of modesty and I am not choosing it because it is clever."

"Then choose it," said Nadia Ferrous.

Sun Meiling looked at her.

"I am choosing it because it is the only one of the five that is not a person and cannot be sent for anything and cannot be made to produce anybody," she said, "and because every other one of them is one of you, and I have watched the six of you for four months and I have never once in four months been able to get a straight answer out of any of you about what you can survive."

She picked the sheet up by the corner and uncapped a pen.

"One more thing," she said. "And this one is for him and not for you."

The room turned.

"You have been carrying something since the twenty-second and you have not said it and you have slept on it twice." She was not looking at him. "There is a door on the third floor of that building with a plate on it and you have read the plate, because you read every door you have ever walked past, and you have not gone up, and I have watched you not go up for a day and a half.

"Somebody in this house is going to go and read what is on that shelf. It is not going to be me, because I signed my name on a piece of paper on this doorstep on the thirteenth and that was the last thing I am going to do in daylight for a while."

She wrote for about four minutes and did not look up and did not stop, and when she had finished she read it out in the flat register of a woman reading a clause number.

Six.

And then, in the space underneath, the standing, item eleven, in full, with the number.

She signed it and dated it and put the time on it, because her hands do that, and then she put it on the side table and went up the sixty-one steps without saying anything else.

She did not shut her door. That was the first time in four months.

Agnete Lindqvist was in the yard behind the chop-house when Sun Meiling came down, and she had been out of a building and in the open air for about an hour, and she was sitting on an upturned crate with her hands in her sleeves.

Sun Meiling did not go into the yard. She stood in the doorway and told her in about forty seconds, and then stopped, and stood there.

Agnete Lindqvist did not move for about half a minute.

"No," she said.

"You said no in the yard on the twentieth, before she said a word."

"I said no to eleven thousand being named in a street in the Slough by a man with a worse reason than mine." She got off the crate and stood up, which took her a moment. "This is not that. This is the only lawful answer on a printed sheet to a question this city asked, and it has been printed in a margin since December, and this is the first time one has been used.

"I am not going to say to you that you have done a foolish thing, because it was not a foolish thing, and because I have spent eleven years believing that the only thing that keeps us alive is that we are a shape and not a fact, and you have just found out that a shape can be printed and a fact can be named, and you have done it in a form, with your name on it, in your own hand.

"So I am going to ask you for something and you are going to do it and then I am going back inside a building tonight for the first time since the nineteenth, and that is what it costs me and I have priced it in about four seconds on a crate."

Sun Meiling came out into the yard.

"Ask."

"Go and get the sheet back. It is not gone yet, the young man is at the end of the street. And on the back of it, in the margin, underneath what you have written, write the date, and write that the answer was given by a person of reading age at these premises, and write that no office asked for it."

Agnete Lindqvist was not raising her voice. "Write your name under it twice. Once as the person who gave it and once as the keeper of the premises, because a clerk in some office in eleven years is going to want to know whether one person gave that answer to a house or two, and it was one."

"Why," said Sun Meiling.

"Because in eleven years the only thing that will still exist about today will be that piece of paper, and it will say a number and it will say eleven thousand, and a woman who has never read a form and has never asked to be on one is going to come across it and she will not know whether it happened to us or whether it happened to somebody else."

Sun Meiling went and got the sheet back from the end of the street and wrote it on the back, and dated it, and signed it twice.

The sheet went to a counter at about four in the afternoon and a clerk read the number on it and read the standing under it and did not ask for a single further thing, because there was nothing on the form to ask for.

And that was the trouble with it, and it was worked out at that table in about four minutes by a woman who had spent eleven years inside the only institution in this quarter that files nothing.

Eleven thousand and some hundred persons is a body. A body is not a person. A body cannot be served, cannot be produced, cannot be fined, cannot be appealed, and cannot be made to send for anybody.

A household in law needs somebody who can be answered for.

So the six of them had answered a question about themselves with a standing that could not answer a question about anything, and they had done it in Sun Meiling's hand, and it was on its way to a district office, and there was now a piece of paper in Ostara which said that the premises above the chop-house in Ferrier Street were held under the association at schedule item eleven, and there was not one pair of hands in that house that could be produced for it.

And there was a door on the third floor of a building in Cobb Street with a plate on it that said LATE RETURNS AND PERIOD BUNDLES, and a man in a kitchen in the Slough had read the plate and had not gone, and Sun Meiling had told him in a doorway in front of six people that somebody in that house was going to go and read what was on the shelf, and that it was not going to be her.

# Agnete Lindqvist

The hatch at the Vestry opens at eight and by half past there is a queue along the wall, and she was in the middle of a man with a chest that had been going for eleven days when he came down the line with his hat in his hand.

She finished the man first. Then she put her forearms on the counter.

"You have not come here for me," she said. "You have come because of something that came through that door yesterday and it had a number on it in the margin."

"The eleventh."

"The eleventh." She wiped the counter where nothing had been spilled. "Item eleven. Eleven thousand and some hundred, in this quarter, holding no plate and no audit, with membership attested and attestation not audited, and no composition return in the history of this city.

"I have read that line four times since yesterday and I have not been able to get past the word attested."

"Tessa—"

"I am going to tell you one thing and then I am going to call the next person, and it is the only thing I am going to tell you about this, and I have not said it out loud to anybody in two years.

"A woman came to this quarter on the night before last. She came at about eleven at night, she walked to the end of a corridor in a building off the Vestry market, she knocked on a door, and she asked one question at it. It was the plainest question that can be asked and it took her eleven seconds.

"What is your name.

"And the woman on the other side of that door opened it and said it, and she has not said her name out loud in a corridor in three years."

Tessa Okonkwo looked at him.

"That is all I am going to tell you. I have spent two years not saying anything about that building, because the day this clinic stops being the only doctor in the Slough that the Correction Office cannot say it holds a record of, the six people on the third floor who come to this hatch in the morning have nowhere to go, and I told you what that costs on the seventh of this month and I have not revised it.

"Go and knock if you want to knock. I am not going to tell you which building and I am not going to tell you what is behind the door, because I do not know what is behind the door, and because if she wanted you to have that she would have come out of it on her own two years ago."

The building was a laundry off the Vestry market with a dispensary on the ground floor and a stair at the back and four floors above it, and the corridor on the second floor had a runner on it that had been down long enough to have a path worn down the middle.

There were four doors.

He knocked on the second one from the stair because it was the only one with anything on it at all, which was a brass plate about four inches by two with nothing engraved on it, and then he stood in the corridor for about two minutes and knocked again.

The door opened about four inches and there was a face in it.

It was not a face that had been surprised. It was a face that had been deciding, in the four inches, whether the arrangement it had made eleven years ago was going to hold.

"Agnete Lindqvist," she said. "I was told on the night before last that somebody would come and I have been standing in this corridor since about half past eight deciding what I would say to him.

"You are going to say that you are sorry about something. Do not. It takes four seconds and we have not got four seconds.

"Step in past me or do not, and if you step in past me you will see that it is one room and a chair, and I would rather you saw it than that I described it."

He stepped in past her.

She shut the door and put her hand flat on it for a moment, the way a person does at a door they have not shut in a long time, and then took the hand away.

"Sit down if you like. There is one chair and it is not comfortable. I have not had a visitor in eleven years and I am not going to pretend that this is a room anybody sits in."

"Right," he said. "Say the terms."

"You have not asked me for terms."

"You have had a hand on that door for eleven years and I have been in this corridor for four minutes. I know what a room that needs terms sounds like." He did not sit down. "Say them anyway."

Agnete Lindqvist looked at him for about four seconds and then went and stood against the wall by the window, which did not open, and had not opened in a very long time.

"Four. It is four and I have said three of them to strangers before and the fourth is the one that matters and I have said it out loud once, to a nurse, in the first year.

"One. This is a room in which a person cannot be compelled to testify. Not a lie, not a trick, and not a thing you have to believe in. It has been that since before I came into this building, and it is the only piece of work I have ever done that came out right.

"It works on every person who comes through that door and it has never once stopped working and it is the reason I can sit in a room by myself for eleven years and still be a person.

"Two. Which means I cannot compel you. Not your attention, not your hands, not a single thing you do in the next hour, and I have not been able to compel anybody in eleven years and I have stopped finding it interesting and started finding it expensive.

"Three. Which means you cannot compel me either, and you cannot take a testimony out of this room, and you cannot prove anything that happens in it, and neither can anybody else, and neither can me."

She turned her head and looked at the door.

"Four. So there is no protection in here. Not for you and not for me and not for whatever you came up that stair carrying. You are safer in this room than you have ever been in your life, because not one word of it can be taken from you, and you have no cover at all while you are saying it, because not one word of it can be put behind anything afterwards.

"I am telling you that before anything else in the world because it is the difference between this room and every other room you have ever stood in, and everybody who has ever been in one of the two has lied about which one it is."

"What is it called," he said.

"The Church of the Quiet Number." She said it in the voice of a woman reading out a clause number.

"It is eleven thousand people in this quarter who believe that what you do when there is no witness to it is the only thing that counts. They have been organising to say so for twenty years without once filing a return. We have been perfectly correct the whole time and perfectly illegal the whole time, and that is the entire arrangement, and I have never once been able to make it sound like anything else.

"It is not a church that asks for nothing. It is a church that has been asked for something for twenty years and has never been given it, which is the same thing from the other side of the door."

"And that is what your eleven thousand is. That is not a number on a form about our house. That is a body of people the Office has just printed into the margin of a document as a standing you may be held under, and I have known for a fortnight that it was going to happen, and I have been in this room for eleven years and the door has not been open and I could not get anybody to knock on it."

She stopped.

"Say something."

He said, "You asked for four things and you have given me four things and I have not said one word and you have been standing against that wall for about ninety seconds waiting for me to start asking you things."

"Yes."

"And I am not going to ask you anything."

"That is not possible," she said. "Everybody asks. It is four seconds. It is the single easiest thing in the world and you are doing it on purpose and I can see you doing it and it is the first anybody has managed in three years."

"Somebody told me on the night before last that a woman asked your name at a door, and that you said it. You did not have to. There was no counter and no office and you said it.

"That is the ask. It was made on the seventeenth. I have not come up that stair to take it again."

The corridor made a noise. Somebody went down the stairs.

"I have wanted to be touched since the second year," said Agnete Lindqvist.

She said it the way a clerk says a figure she has checked twice.

"I have had four people through that door in eleven years. A nurse with a chart in the first year. A man from a gas office in the third. A boy from a laundry in the seventh who wanted to see the room. And a woman in the ninth who wanted to know whether I was all right and did not come through the door.

"That is four. I have not been touched since the first of them and I have not been outside since I came in, and I have thought about both of those facts every single day for eleven years, and I have never once had a reason to say either of them out loud, and I have just said them out loud to a man who knocked twice."

She came off the wall.

"I am going to say the last of it and then I am going to stop talking and we are going to have about an hour.

"Do not give me anything. I know what you are — not from anything you have said, but because people come to that hatch every morning who cannot be helped, and a woman who cannot be helped is a woman who has never been helped, and there is a great deal of her in this quarter and every bit of it came from you.

"You were going to try. I watched you not try, which was the correct thing to do and which is also the single hardest thing anybody has done in this room."

"Do you want the door shut," he said.

"No," she said. "I want it open. I have wanted it open for eleven years and I have not been able to open it, because the last time I opened a door I came into a building and I have been inside this building ever since, and a door that a person opens and does not come through is not a door anybody can keep doing."

They took about an hour.

There was no furniture in it that could be used and no light that could be lowered and no rug and no water and nothing on the walls, and there was a runner on the floor of the corridor outside with a path worn down the middle of it, and after the first quarter of an hour she had him against the wall on the left-hand side of the door and after that neither of them cared where the wall was.

She had not been touched in eleven years, and that is not a thing anybody can be ready for.

She came off the start of it fast, and then forgot to be fast, and then stopped being careful altogether. She told him where to put his hands in the first minute, and then stopped telling him and put them where she wanted them. At one point she got both hands behind his head and made him say a thing, and he said it twice.

She wanted it said out loud. That was not in her four terms and she did not put it in them, because a term is a thing you can be held to and this was not a term, it was a want, and she had not had one in nine years and she was not going to file it.

"There," she said, once, with him. "That. I have not heard that from a person in eleven years and I did not know until just now that I had been missing it."

There is a thing a body does when it has been left alone for a very long time and it is not dramatic and it is not slow. It is fast, and it is bad at it, and it goes on being bad at it, and then at about the ten-minute mark it stops being bad at it entirely and becomes a different animal with a great deal of practice in exactly one thing.

She got him off the wall and round and put him on the floor on the runner with her on top of him and her hands on his chest, and she watched him the entire time the way a woman watches a form she has to sign, checking for a discrepancy, and there was not one.

"Right," she said, about twenty minutes in. "You are not frightened of me and I have to say that it is the first time and it is also a little insulting."

"You have not made yourself frightening."

"I know exactly what I have made myself. It has been the whole of my usefulness for eleven years and it is a thing you stand in and it makes you honest and it does not make you like me." She had her mouth about four inches away and did not close it. "You should try to like me. That is not in the four terms. I have simply never had occasion to ask."

"You have not said my name once," she said, about halfway through.

He said, "You have not said his."

"I have been not saying it for four hours and you have not noticed once until now, and I want you to know that I have been doing it on purpose and that it was the most difficult piece of professional restraint I have ever exercised and it is not the thing you think it is."

"What is it."

She was not going to answer that and did not.

Her back was against the wall and his hands were on her ribs and the fourth one on his left had been there the whole time saying nothing, and neither of them had mentioned it and neither of them was going to.

It was not tender. That is the thing that he would remember afterwards and would not have been able to put into a sentence for anybody in Ostara. It was fast and it was expert and at one point she laughed out loud once, in the middle of it, at nothing at all, and then stopped laughing and held on to him.

She came first, and she did not make a sound, and he let her, which was the single most expensive thing he had ever done for anybody and cost nothing at all, and he had done it once before on the seventh floor of a branch office with a balance on the window-cleaning shelf and he had not known then that it would turn out to be the shape of his life.

Then she sat on the runner with her back against the wall and her face in her hands for about forty seconds, and came out of them, and looked at him.

"Right," she said. "That is the second of the two things I have wanted since the first year and I have got it and it is better than I had it, and I am going to be extremely unromantic about the rest of it."

"The door."

"The door." She got up. She was unsteady for about a second and then she was not. "This door, first. It has not been open since the morning I came in and I have opened it about nine thousand times for the nurse and the gas man and the boy, and I have shut it every time.

"And then the other one."

He followed her out into the corridor, and the runner had a path worn down the middle of it and there were four doors and a window at the end that did not open, and she walked to the far end and stopped at the second door, which was not the one she lived behind.

It was the door onto the stair.

She had her hand on it. She did not open it for about half a minute.

"In eleven years I have not been through this door," she said. "I have stood at it. There is a light on the other side of it, because there is always a light on the other side of it, and I have stood at it the way a person stands at a window.

"I am going to open it and I am not going to come down the stair tonight, because it is late and I have not slept properly since the ninth year and I am not a fool about what I am."

She opened it.

The light came in and lay about two feet into the corridor and stopped at the edge of the runner. There was a smell of cold stone and a market and rain.

"That is a market," she said, looking at it. "There is a market on the other side of that door at about this time every evening and I have known that for eleven years."

She left the door standing open behind her. She did not hold it and she did not look back at it once, and when he came down the corridor and stood in the light it was still open, and the air was still moving, and she was in it.

"Sit with me for a minute," she said. "I have got about four minutes before I have to stop being able to do this and I would rather spend them sitting than standing."

He sat on the floor of a corridor in the Vestry with his back against a wall, and she sat down beside him with her knees up, in the two feet of light that came through an open door.

"There is a thing I have to say and I have saved it for the last four minutes because I wanted to do it in the light.

"A woman came to this door on the night before last and asked me my name. I have not said it out loud to anybody in three years, and I did not say it to her because I had decided to. I said it because she asked before I could arrange not to.

"There is a description of a man in a file in this city. Four clerks have typed it and not one word of it is wrong, and it says that he cannot explain what he carries and that he will not try again. It went into the file three weeks ago.

"I have read it. Somebody in this quarter has been reading something out loud in a kitchen in the same voice for months, and I have heard it through a wall on a day of the week, and I have never once gone to the door."

"In eleven years I have read four documents about four people and I kept all four, and I have never once been thanked for any of them, and I have never once been asked for one.

"You have the only document in Ostara that is true and you have not asked me for a copy of it and I would have given it to you."

She got up.

Then she said his name.

She said it once, at the end, the way she said everything else in this corridor, as a word inside a sentence, and it was not an address and it was not a form of words directed at a person standing in front of her.

"I am going to sleep for the first time in about nine hours, and I am going to wake up in a room with an open door, and in about a fortnight somebody is going to come up that stair with a second piece of paper, and I am going to be here for that one as well, and the whole of the reason for that is a woman who asked a question in the plainest words available to her and went home without waiting to be thanked for it."

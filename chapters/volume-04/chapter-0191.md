# The Certification Rule

The man came up the sixty-one steps at about eleven in the morning with a sheet folded in four in his coat, and Sun Meiling established what he was before he was four steps up.

"Why have you come," she said.

"To look at the roll."

"That is the half of it. Say the other half."

He said, "The bench sheet came down on the third and it is on the rail at the junction by the half-hourly and I have read it."

"Then say what it says and not what you have read, because those are two sentences and I am the person at this door."

She went up. He followed her up about nine feet and stopped, which is the ordinary courtesy of a landing and not a rule anybody has written down.

The board is on the wall at the top of the second stair and it is three feet by two and it has a heavy line at the top with THE HEAD OF THE HOUSEHOLD over seven ruled rows.

He read from the top. Then he read the bottom two again.

"Seven rows," he said. "Six names. One space."

"Say the hour."

"I came at eleven."

"You came at about eleven and it is written on your own sheet, and if you write it at that counter down there and you are wrong about the hour then you are the one who is wrong about the hour and not me."

Sun Meiling had a pencil and she wrote on the back of his own sheet, in her own hand, and gave it back to him, and then she read out what she had written, because a woman who keeps a door reads out what she has written.

She read out: the eleventh of this month, at about eleven in the morning, at premises above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, at the request of a person giving no office and no standing.

"Sign that yourself," she said, "because it is your name and I am not writing it for a stranger on my own stair."

He signed it and went down sixty-one steps, and at the bottom of them the address had been given to a man who had come up for it.

"That is the second time," said Sun Meiling, to the landing. "The first was a woman from the district on the tenth. She wrote down five names and two spaces, and she asked why the board was not the instrument, and I told her and she wrote that down too.

"He has written down six names and one space, because he read a rule through a line as though the line were not there."

"A rule through a line is still a name," said Nadia Ferrous, from the fourth stair. "He counted it because it is on the board in ink with a date and four words on the end of it. You would count it too if you had come up here with a sheet and a reason."

"I would count it and I would write down that it was struck," said Sun Meiling. "And he did not, and he is going to write six names and one space on a district sheet this afternoon, and by the end of the week that sheet will be in a file in a building I have never been inside."

The card by the time clock on the ground floor of the Fourth Branch said that the men were coming on the seventeenth, and it had said it since the first of this month, and it was on the list under a day that is not this one.

He did the arithmetic at the sink on the fourth floor at about half past twelve, with his hands in the water, because he had promised himself on the first of this month that he would do every figure out loud in that house and he had extended the promise to this building in the last week without deciding to.

"Fourteen of the sixty are gone," he said, to a washroom on the fourth floor of a Bureau office. "Forty-six are left. Three days to the end of April, and this month has thirty days, and twenty-seven in the next one. The certificate is dated the twenty-seventh of June and it does not care what any of us has done since the fourth."

And then, because he was standing at a sink and had nothing else to do with the four minutes before the staff kettle:

"If it were finished on the twentieth of this month there would be thirty-seven days between the two of them. Ten days left in this month and twenty-seven in the next. Thirty-seven."

It did not tell him anything. It was the first figure he had put into any room in four months that had come out with nothing attached to it.

The Reading is on the corner of a square and it is a building that was a counting house before the Bureau was, and it is four floors and a basement and the lift is slow, and on the second floor there is a counter about forty feet long with a lamp on it and a tray behind it and Doyle behind that, who has been doing it for about thirty years.

He came in at twenty past two on the same afternoon and Doyle was eating something out of a paper bag and did not stop for him.

"Sit there," Doyle said. "The long one is in the tray with a clip on it and you are not getting it back for a fortnight, so before you say anything, ask me the thing you came for, and if you ask me the thing you came for in the wrong words I am going to tell you the wrong thing back."

"What is a certification."

Doyle put the paper bag down.

"Right," he said. "That is the right question and about one in a hundred comes in with it. Everybody else comes in with *what do I have to do* and that is a different question and it has a different answer and you would not like it.

"A return is a filing. You answer a return at a counter and you may answer it badly and I shall take it and put it on a shelf and read it in nine years when a question comes about the premises.

"A certification is not granted on a filing. There is no such thing as a certification that is granted on a filing, and I say that to about four people a week and about two of them believe it." He took a sheet out of the tray and squared it. "A certification is granted on a thing a clerk does."

"What thing."

"The reading."

Doyle put the sheet down and did not look at it.

"That is the word and you may write it down. Four pages go into a room and a clerk puts them in front of the head of the household and the clerk asks the head, member by member, what each member is, and where it is evidenced, and the head answers in the room, out loud, in front of me, and I write down what I heard and I initial it.

"And then I read it. That is the second half and it is the half that does the work. I read what the head said against the four pages, and against the return of the twenty-seventh of April, and against every correction anybody has filed at this counter in the interim. And where it all agrees, the thing is certified. And where it does not agree, I hold it."

"Held."

"Held. Not refused. I want that word in your head and I want it in the right shape, because a refused thing is dead and a held thing is alive and every day it is held is a day off the end of your sixty."

Doyle looked at him for the first time.

"A schedule with an uncorrected standing in it is held. A schedule with a member who will not attend is held. A schedule with a standing in it that cannot be traced to a document is held, and that is the one that will get you."

"What is the source of a standing."

"It is a document."

Doyle said it the way a man says a thing that is so obvious it offends him.

"Every standing in this city has a document behind it. A plate, an entry, a file, a register, a correction at a counter with a date on it. I am not permitted to enter a standing I cannot see the source of, and I enter about nine hundred a year, and about forty of them I have to go and look for.

"You have five on your second page and I could find the source of four of them this afternoon."

He did not say the fifth.

"You told me on the fourth that you would not lend the name on the sheet."

"That was a different sheet and I was permitted to refuse that one, and I was, and I have thought about it every working day since."

Doyle put the pen down.

"I am not refusing this one. I am going to tell you what it is and you may write it down, and then you may leave and think about it, and if you come back and ask me again I will tell you the same thing, because I have never once told anybody two different things, and it has cost me about four friends and one hand."

"Tell me."

"The head of a household answers in his own name for every member of it."

Doyle said it in the voice of a man reading out a heading.

"That is the law and Ines has read it and it is correct and I am not going to improve on it.

"Here is the part that is not the law. The office will not read a member before the head has signed that he assumes the house."

He turned the sheet round.

"It is one side of a sheet. There is a heading at the top that says ASSUMPTION OF MEMBERS and a ruled block under it that wants a head, a date, an hour, and six lines, and at the foot of it, printed, not written, in the same type as the heading:

THE HEAD OF A HOUSEHOLD ANSWERS IN HIS OWN NAME FOR EVERY MEMBER OF IT.

"And that clause is the indemnity, and the indemnity is the whole of the thing, and the six lines are so that the clerk can write who was in the room when it was signed. That is all the instrument is. It is one side of a sheet and it has been on the wall behind me since before you were at that branch office."

"Why has not one of us asked for it."

"Because it is not on the form."

Doyle made a short sound that was not quite a laugh.

"It is not on the return, it is not on the four pages, and it is not on anything the Office sends out. It has been on the wall behind me for the whole of my working life and about four households in a year come in here and ask me what it is, and every one of them has asked me because somebody else in their house read it off that wall, and not one of them has been told to look for it.

"It is not a question anybody asks. You have spent four months finding out what questions exist and you have got very good at it, and this one is not a question, it is an instrument, and it has been sitting on a wall for thirty years waiting for a person who already knew to ask for it."

"And if I do not sign it."

"Then on the twenty-seventh of June this schedule goes up and a certificate issues on it, and the certificate says that six persons of reading age keep an address at those premises under a head, and there is nothing on it anywhere that says the head answers for them."

Doyle put the sheet back in the tray and clipped it.

"And then there is not one instrument in this city that can put a head's answer onto a certificate that has issued. You would not be a household that had not managed it. You would be a household that had managed it and been found wanting, and the difference between those two things is about nine years of somebody's handwriting."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Now. The other half of it, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to soften it because you will get it wrong and then it will be my fault.

"A head who signs that is not simply made answerable. A head who signs that is made askable. When a member of your house is requisitioned, the requisition is served on the head first, and the head may be asked, and the privilege for being asked is the band on page one, and page one says band five, and band five is three grains above a floor and you have known that since the fourth of May.

"So you would be buying six people one question, and you would be paying for it with being a man in this city that a piece of paper can be addressed to."

Doyle squared the tray.

"And at the reading I put the four pages in front of you and I ask you about each member, out loud, one at a time, and whatever you say about a woman goes down on a document that a stranger reads in nine years with no idea who you are.

"You are about to become the only person in Ostara who can be asked about six women. There is not one other route to that and I have watched a good many people go looking for it."

"How many households have signed it."

Doyle did not answer that straight away.

"Nine hundred households have come through this room with a four-page form," he said. "About a hundred and forty of them have a head's answer on the certificate. I have kept a bit of paper in the drawer with the numbers on it and I do not know why I keep it and I am not going to be asked about it in a corridor by a man who cleans washrooms."

He stood up.

"The twenty-seventh of June is the date on the certificate. The schedule goes up the working day before, which is the twenty-sixth, because the Office does not sit on the twenty-seventh of a month and never has, and there is nothing about it on a card."

He said that in the ordinary way, the way a clerk says a fact about a calendar, and did not stop.

"That is the twenty-sixth of June and it is not the twentieth of this month, and I am telling you the date and not doing you a favour, because the second date is mine and not the office's."

He took the tray out from behind the counter and it was heavier than the last time he had carried it.

"Assumptions are entered on a pad. They are entered on a pad of fifty and the pad is numbered and the office destroys an unused pad at the close of a month.

"So your pad is this month's pad. It is on the shelf and it has your name on the stub and it has been on the shelf since the first, and it will be in the stove at the close of the twentieth, and after the twentieth there is no instrument in this building that can enter your assumption on this schedule, and the schedule goes up on the twenty-sixth of June without you on it."

"The twentieth," he said.

"The twentieth of this month. In nine days." Doyle carried the tray to the shelf and put it down and came back. "You have nine days and there is nothing in between that you have to do, and that is the most dangerous thing I have said to you in thirty years, and I have watched nine hundred households go out of this room with a number and a date and go home and wait for a post.

"The thing you have to understand is that the nine days are not for me. I do not need nine days. I would enter it tomorrow and I have entered four hundred of them tomorrow. The nine days are because a man who has nine days goes and looks at what he is signing, and everything I have said to you in this room is me hoping that you do."

He got to the end of the counter and stopped with his hand on the edge of it.

"And that is the whole of what a certification is," he said. "It is not granted on a filing. It is granted on a clerk putting four pages in front of a man and asking him out loud, in a room, what six women are, while he writes it down.

"You have been looking for the instrument this whole month and it has been on the wall behind me the whole month and it is one side of a sheet and it is blank."

# Runa Sato

The visitors' book came down off the shelf at four in the afternoon and Beatrix Halm read the whole of it out, standing up, at the speed of the thing being read.

A hundred and eleven. Forty-one asking about a person, seventeen asking about the household, and she gave the two figures as she went so that the rest of the room did not have to hold them.

When she got to the forty-first she put one finger on the page, said "that one," and carried on, and he knew which one she meant, because it was the only entry in a hundred and eleven that had never been answered and could not be. It was in Ola Bright's copy hand, at the foot of a page in the middle of February, and it asked whether a woman on a landing was still taking callers, and the answer had been that there was no landing.

"They are not going to stop," said Beatrix, when she had finished. "I have read that book out four times now and the same twelve names come up in it over and over, and I have started to hear the surnames before I get to them, and that is not a thing you can unlearn at a table."

"You have started to hear the surnames," said Sun Meiling from the stair.

"I have."

"Then write them down and count them and it will stop being a noise and start being a shape. A noise you are frightened of is only a noise you have not counted yet."

Runa Sato came in at about half past five, wet through, and put her coat over the back of a chair instead of hanging it, which she did when she had been carrying something for a long time and did not want to stop carrying the shape of.

"You are all late," she said.

"We have been here since four," said Nadia Ferrous. "Beatrix has read out a hundred and eleven entries and Sun has told her to count the surnames and I have not moved."

"Good. I want five minutes of it and then I want the room."

She stood with her hand on the back of the chair. "I have been in that building since half past six this morning and I would like an empty room for about ten minutes, and then I am going to want to say something.

"And I want it said in front of all of you at once. If I say it to one of you it turns into a conversation, and I do not want a conversation. I want six people hearing the same sentence at the same time, so that it cannot get edited on the way round."

The room emptied at the speed of a room that has been told to empty.

Beatrix Halm stayed on because she had put the book back on the shelf and had not said whether the surnames were going in the copybook.

"In the book," she said. "Under the table. Where it has been since the sixteenth of March."

"That book has your sentence in it now," said Ola Bright from the stairs, "so it is a different book, and everything I put in it from the tenth of April is a page somebody asked for, and everything I put in it now is a page I am putting in because a woman at this table told me to, which is the same thing and is worse, because at least the first one came with a reason."

She said it from the third step with the copybook under her arm and went up.

Runa Sato came down the stairs at about ten past six and sat on the bottom one, which she had not done in four months, and put her forearms on her knees.

"Tomorrow I go into a room on the fourth floor of the Field division from ten until two with four people in it, and there is no part of that which is anybody's business, including mine.

"Three things, in this order, and I want them said once and I want them not to be argued about.

"One. I am not in the room when you arrive and you are not in the room at any point. You will not be asked for, and if you come you will be put out, and I would rather you did not spend a morning on a stair in a building where a clerk writes down the time people spend on it.

"Two. Whatever is read in that room is read out of a file, and there are four people in it who are paid to read files, and none of them has ever met me and all of them have read about me, and I am not going to be able to object to a single word of it, and I have decided in advance that I am not going to try.

"Three." She looked at the stair. "When I come out of that room at two o'clock, you are not going to tell me what was said in it."

The oven ticked.

"Not the finding," she said. "I do not care about the finding. A finding is a piece of paper and I have read my own file and it is four hundred words of things that happened to other people near me.

"I mean the other thing. The thing you would say.

"You would come down those stairs at about ten past two and you would tell me what that room thought about me, and you would do it because you have been not knowing for four hours and you cannot stand not knowing.

"I have watched you do it to a doctor and to a broker and to six women in this house. Every single time you have been kind about it. Every single time it has been the wrong thing."

"Say the rule," said Beatrix Halm, from the doorway.

Runa Sato turned her head.

"He is going to ask me for it and I would rather he had it now than invented it in a corridor." She said it to the stair rail rather than to any of them. "It is printed at the head of the muster room at Cordwain and I have read it every working morning for two years and I could recite it.

"A member who refuses to answer a count is entered as refusing. A household with a member who has refused is a household with an unanswered count in it."

"That is on the wall," said Beatrix. "That has been on the wall since before any of us were in the Field."

"It is on the wall." Runa Sato stood up. "It is on the wall because in February a division officer at Cordwain put a number in a return that the Office could not reconcile and then sat in a room and declined to produce a second one, and he is still teaching at a desk in Kestrel Hill, and that is the whole of what happened to him, and it is what the rule is for."

She got her coat off the chair.

"So I am going to walk into a room tomorrow morning and I am going to decline to produce a second number, and I am telling you tonight what the wall says about it, and neither of those two things is a surprise to anybody in this room, and that is the last time I will be ahead of this."

"Runa." He had not spoken.

"You are going to say that you will tell me if it goes badly."

"I am going to say that I will be on the stair."

"You will be on the stair." She looked at him for a moment. "There is a bench in the corridor outside that room and it is a hard bench and there is a lamp over it that is on a fuse, and the fuse has been out since the summer. Take a candle and leave it on the ledge by the door and do not go in.

"And Lin Hao." She said his name the way she said everything, which was once, at the end, as though it were the last item on a list she had been working down all day. "I am going to come out of that room at two o'clock and I am going to ask you one question, and you are going to have about two seconds to decide what to do, and I am telling you now which question it is so that you can decide in advance.

"I am going to ask you whether they asked you anything.

"And whatever you say, I will believe, and I have thought about that for four days and I have decided I would rather walk out of that building with a lie than walk out of it with a man who spent four hours on a bench thinking about how to be honest with me."

She was at the door.

"You can say no now if you are going to say no now."

He had about four seconds. The oven ticked twice in them.

"No," he said.

"Write it down that he said it and make him write the date on it."

Beatrix Halm came in from the doorway and wrote two lines on the back page of the notebook under the day's six, in pencil, in the seventh space, which had been used once before and had not been used since.

"You are going to sleep on that bench," she said. "You are not, because you are a man who carries water up six floors and a hard bench in a corridor at one in the morning is a thing that will take four inches off your back inside a week.

"I have written that down as well. And if you tell anybody at that table I wrote it down, they will all pretend to be angry, and then every one of them will bring you something."

He went down to Cordwain on the strength of nothing at all, because there was a bench in a corridor with a lamp on a fuse, and a fuse that had been out since the summer.

A man came out of the gate of the loading yard at about half past eleven with a barrow he had not asked anybody to help him with, and stopped halfway across it to move a stone out of the way that four hundred people had walked around for years, and then went on again, and not one person in that yard would have said at any point in the next twenty years that anything had happened there at all.

He counted them the way he counted washrooms: in order, without turning his head, a figure for every body that came out through that gate, and he did not round it, and he did not decide in advance what a person looked like while they were being counted.

It took him fifty minutes and nothing else, and he understood in the middle of it, standing at a gate in the dark doing arithmetic on strangers, that this was the whole of what a division clerk in Cordwain would be asking anybody for. Not an admission and not a feeling. A figure. And a man who can produce one is a man who can be asked for it twice.

A hundred and eighteen.

He stayed at the yard gate until the gates were shut at about six and then went home, and slept about two hours, and was at the Fourth Branch for seven in the morning.

It went on the back page with the gate and the hour on it, under Beatrix's two lines in pencil, and he put the pencil down and picked it up again and did not write a name, because a line is a face and not an act.

# The Vestry Steps

He finished the sixth floor at about ten past eleven and did not go home.

Agnete Lindqvist was sitting on the bottom step of the building off the Vestry market with her coat over her knees and her hands in her sleeves, and the market was already going, and she had been there since about seven.

"I could not go back in," she said, before he had got to the bottom.

"It is a hundred and ten steps."

"It is a hundred and ten steps and I got to the fourth one at about ten past seven this morning and sat down on it, and I have been sitting on this step for five hours." She said it in the flat register of a woman reporting a figure she had checked twice. "That is the entire discovery. I could not go back in.

"Not because of the room and not because of the light and not for any reason I would be able to explain to anybody in a document. I got to the fourth step and my legs stopped and I sat down on the fourth step and I have been sitting on it ever since, and I have been outside for the first time in eleven years and I cannot get back into a building."

He sat down on the step below her.

"How long did you say the door had been open," she said.

"It was open when I left at about eleven and it was open when I came in at about half past six this morning, because the girl who does the laundry comes in at six and she found an open door and she shut it and then she opened it again because of the draught."

"Ah," said Agnete Lindqvist.

They sat there for about half an hour. She watched the market the way a person watches weather. A man set out trestles and put crates of something on them. A girl went past on the other side of the street with a bread tray on her head and did not look at anybody, which is the thing everybody in the Slough learns to do young and which takes about twenty years of being looked at to unlearn.

He walked back to Ferrier Street at about ten past twelve and told them in about ninety seconds, standing in a kitchen, and six women put on their coats.

They walked out to the Vestry in about fifty minutes, all of them, which is a thing that has happened about four times in four months, and they came up the side street in a loose group that did not look like a group and stopped at the corner of the market because Agnete Lindqvist was standing at the top of five steps with the sun on her and had not moved for eleven minutes.

Tessa Okonkwo went up first. She had a bag from the clinic and there were things in it that she did not explain, and she crouched down on the step in front of Agnete Lindqvist and said something at about the volume you say things at a hatch.

Agnete Lindqvist stood up on the top step and let herself be looked at for about four seconds.

"You are the doctor, and you have been kind to me for eleven years, and I have never seen you in the room."

"I have seen you in the room about ninety times," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"You have seen me through a hatch and told yourself that was a room. It was not." She said it without any unkindness in it at all, which was the thing that made it land. "You have done the correct thing and it has been the wrong thing for eleven years and I am not going to make you feel about it, because it is a quarter past one and you have a queue."

Sun Meiling came up the steps last and stood at the bottom of them and did not go up.

That is how it is now, he thought, and then he stopped thinking about it in those words because she was looking straight at him.

"You asked her her name," he said.

"Yes."

"You went out at eleven at night on the seventeenth, on your own, with a piece of paper in your hand that had eleven thousand printed in the margin of it, and you walked to the end of a corridor in a building you had never been in and knocked on a door and asked a woman you had never met what her name was."

"Yes."

"That is the whole of what the instruction says. It is not a paper coming for the house. It is a woman going out and asking something, and it was the plainest one available, and you did not bring any of them."

Sun Meiling stood at the bottom of five steps in the sun.

"I have spent four months refusing to be written down," she said, "and on the morning of the thirteenth I put my name on a piece of paper because a man on a step could not make anybody else, and yesterday I found out that the reason I could not be found is the reason everything has to come to me, and last night I went and knocked on a door in a building I had walked past nine hundred times in two years and never looked at.

"And there is a woman on the top step who has been indoors since the year the Board last changed and who said her name out loud to me in about two seconds, and she has not been outside since, and she is sitting in the sun now."

She stopped.

"And if anybody in this street says the household has got itself a woman out of a building, I will want it written down that I went and got her."

Ines Duarte had been standing with her back to the wall of the market the entire time and had not gone up the steps, and now she said, "There is a problem with the return and I am going to state it before anybody in this street feels pleased with itself."

"Say it."

"The return is due on the twenty-seventh. That is seven days from today, and the second issue came into this house on the sixteenth with eleven days on it, and both forms are due on the same day, and I have done that subtraction four times today because I do not trust it when I am pleased."

She came up two steps and stopped below Agnete Lindqvist.

"The first question on that form is how many persons of reading age keep an address at the above premises. I wrote six on the second issue on the sixteenth and I wrote it correctly. Sun Meiling wrote six as well and then wrote underneath it that there are seven women in that house and that she is the seventh and that she is not answering for herself.

"There are now eight people who keep an address in that building if this woman agrees to be there, and there is no version of that form on which eight can go in the first box without an explanation that costs more than the form is worth.

"And I cannot write her into it."

"Explain," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"You hold no plate." Ines Duarte said it as an auditor, and not as a woman who had spent the morning helping somebody up five steps.

"Your association holds no plates and files no returns, and it is entered in that office's schedule as an association with no plate and no audit. So the Office holds a record of you and of every person in your association, and there is not one ruled space on that form, or on any other form in this city, where you go.

"You are the only person I have ever met who could not be entered on a return if she tried, and I have spent eleven years trying to get people off forms, and I have never once had to try to keep one on."

Marguerite Vale said, from the bottom of the steps, "Then we do not put her on it."

"We do not put her on it, and we do not leave her off it silently, and both of those cost the same and one of them costs a lie." Ines Duarte turned round to the six of them. "The second issue prints, in its own margin, a fifth permitted answer to the fourth question, and it is a standing drawn from the schedule of unmeasured associations. Item eleven. Eleven thousand and some hundred persons in this quarter holding no plate and no audit.

"And that is her."

Not one person on those steps said anything for about three seconds.

"So the Office has already printed a document," she said, "in which the answer to the question who holds these premises is a body of eleven thousand people, and the woman standing on the top of these steps is one of the eleven thousand, and we cannot write her in, and we cannot leave her out, and we cannot explain her, and every one of those three refusals puts eleven thousand people into a form about this house that we wrote ourselves."

Beatrix Halm said, "Then the second issue is the best thing that has ever happened to us and it has arrived in the post and I would like somebody to say so."

"Say it yourself," said Ines Duarte.

"I am not going to." Beatrix Halm sat down on the bottom step, which put her below everyone. "Because of a man called Ossian Kerr, who is four doors up, who came to that gate on the day before yesterday with six questions in pencil and took an answer in six hands, and who told the room that the next man will not knock twice and will not care whether we are frightened.

"So the next thing that comes is going to be somebody who has read a form with eleven thousand in the margin, and he is going to want to know what eleven thousand is, and there is a woman on that step who would tell him the truth in about ninety seconds."

Agnete Lindqvist came down one step.

"I would tell him nothing," she said. "Not out of caution. Because the moment the Church of the Quiet Number is named in an answer to a private citizen in a street in the Slough, then eleven thousand people in this quarter become a thing that has been named, and we have survived twenty years on the entire fact that we are a shape and not a fact."

She looked down the street.

"I have not been outside in eleven years and I have not read a paper in nine, and I am telling you that standing up in the sun in front of six people and a market. I would like it noted that it took eleven years, a knock, and about four minutes, and not one thing else."

Runa Sato had not come up the steps at all.

She had been standing at the bottom of them with her hands behind her back, in a place where the market went past and the steps did not, and when she spoke she spoke to the woman on the top step and not to anybody else.

"I was entered as refusing on the twelfth of this month at about ten past two in the afternoon," she said. "That is a word in a column. It cost me the premises, and the premises paper is lying on a table in that house with your kitchen on it, and I would pay that again.

"And there is a second thing on that wall that is not the column, and I want it said in this street before anybody says anything else. On the same morning, before I was down the stair, a duty clerk I have never spoken to ruled my line off and wrote a card under the rail that says strength reconciled, seventh.

"That is a count this office has owed since February. It was not done to me. It was done to a building that had one name too many on it, and I happened to be the name."

"So there are two pieces of paper on me and one of them was mine."

"There is no office in this city that can find me by a posting or a division or a service or a band. I have not got any of those, and I gave the last one away in writing on the eighth of this month in exchange for a thing that turned out to be a lie.

"I have got one thing left and it is that I am not findable by anybody at all. Not the Bureau, not the Vant, not eleven households that have bought a shilling's worth of paper about a street in Ferrier Street, not a woman who has a clerk's memory and a reason to want it.

"I would like to stand at the bottom of that stair."

"What for," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"Because there is a woman in a building who has been indoors for eleven years and who is going to have to come in and go out again for the rest of her life, and the first two hundred times she does it she is going to find out what a street is."

Runa Sato did not move and did not raise her voice. "And on the nights when it is late and the market is shut and there is not one living soul on this side of the street, she is going to find out what a street is at two in the morning on her own, and I am the one person in this city who has got the hours and nothing to be at them for.

"So I will stand here. And it will not be on any roster and it will not be in any file, and it is the only thing I have ever been able to offer anybody that does not cost me a posting."

The market went past. A man took a crate off a trestle and put it on a barrow. Two women at the far end were arguing about a price with their hands.

Agnete Lindqvist came down off the top step and stood on the pavement with the sun on her and looked along Ferrier Street as though it were a road she had been told about for eleven years and had only just been given the name of.

"I have been reading about that number since the fourteenth," he said to her, "and I want to say what I think it is, and I am going to say it to you and not to the room, because the room has been arguing about it for three days.

"I have been reading it as a piece of paper's way of saying nothing. I have been reading eleven thousand as though it were the shape of the inside of an envelope, which is what a margin is for.

"It is not a margin. It is people. It is eleven thousand people in this quarter and about forty of them are going past that market before eight o'clock in the morning, and not one of them is on a plate, and not one of them is going to be counted by anything, and every one of them is doing the only thing in this city that does not leave a witness behind.

"That is the first number I have ever read in this city that cannot be taken off anybody with a form."

She did not answer him for a while.

"Seven days," she said then. "You have been carrying that subtraction all morning and so have I.

"It is the twenty-seventh. There is a form in your kitchen with a box at the foot of it four inches wide, and it wants one name. A woman on a landing signed a stub. A man on a white rectangle cannot go in it either. And I could go in it if anybody in this city had a column for me.

"They have not got a column. They printed the reason in the margin, in three lines, at the foot of the page."

She looked back up the five steps at the six of them standing in the sun.

"So there are going to be four names and two blanks by the twenty-seventh," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I am going to be alive on that morning, indoors, which is the one thing in this paragraph that has never been in doubt."

# Beneath The Table

Ola Bright told him at about half past six on the evening of the ninth, standing in the kitchen with the kettle in one hand, before he had got his coat off the second stair, because she had been waiting since four to say it and had run out of ways of not saying it.

"Four of them wrote back."

The table had five women at it and the notebook open in the middle with the ninth ruled and three of the six lines done.

"Say that again slowly," said Ines Duarte, without looking up.

"Four of them wrote back. Over the weekend. On the actual form. I did not know they were going to and I found out last night when Runa told me she had put hers in the pot by the sink to be posted and I said what are you posting and she said a piece of paper, and then I said what piece of paper, and then she gave up."

"Ola." Marguerite Vale set her cup down. "How much of it have you copied."

"All of it." Ola put the kettle down. "I want to be exact about this because you are all going to be unpleasant about it and I would like it on the record that I did it in the way I do everything, which is that I could not keep it in.

"I stopped writing on the sixteenth of March because a thing said before it happens is a thing somebody else can produce. Then I wrote one line on the thirty-first, under the sixth line, and it was a sentence and it was yours and you did not ask for it."

She looked at him on the stair.

"And then four women wrote answers to a form on a weekend without telling you, and if that goes in a book and somebody sends that book to a counter then you have four people who put a thing in writing about themselves and a record that they did it before anybody asked. I could not sit upstairs with that.

"So it is in the book. Under the table. Where it has been since the sixteenth, and where nothing in this house can reach it."

"That is not what you are all going to be unpleasant about," said Marguerite.

"It is the first of it."

He read the four at the table after the oven went off, with the lamp on and the four of them not helping, because four women watching a man read four answers they had written without him is a thing they had agreed to do by writing them.

Ines's was in her own hand and it was the shortest.

Four persons of reading age at the premises. One at band two, holding an audit appointment and not resident. One at band six in a division office. One at band four and self-employed. One at band five and in the service of a private firm.

Then a line under the four, which was not on the form, and which said that the band of the fifth person was that of her employer and was not hers to enter here, and that if the Exchange wished to know it they could apply to the employer in the ordinary way.

That was the whole of Ines Duarte's answer and it was, line for line, correct.

Beatrix Halm's was two words long. She had written NOT MINE on the fourth question and left everything else blank, and underneath, because she could not help herself, she had written a second line that said she would fill the rest in at the yards because it was easier to do it there.

Runa Sato's was the shortest and the worst. She had written the address of the Field division in Cordwain in the fourth space, because the Field division is the standing that holds her premises in the sense that it holds her and pays for her, and she had signed it, and underneath it she had written in a small hand that she was aware of what she had done on the twelfth and was doing it anyway.

Marguerite Vale's had a number on it.

The fourth question wanted a standing. Marguerite Vale had written the standing as though it were an invoice: the right to be asked before requisitioned, valued for one building for one year, with a date on it and her own name at the bottom as the person who had put the price there, and a sentence saying that the price was not payable in grains and had never been.

Four women, four answers, and not one of them had put a name in the space that the form actually wanted filled in, which was the first question, the one that came first on the page, and which every one of them had answered correctly and completely and separately and alone.

He put the four down in a line on the table.

"You have all answered it four times."

"Yes," said Ines Duarte.

"Four returns for one address."

"Four answers for one question, which is what it actually is," said Ines Duarte. "You are looking at it as a return. It is not a return yet. It is a form with four answers on it, and the form says the premises, and the premises is a building above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, and a building does not care how many hands wrote on it."

"The Exchange is not a building."

"No. And what a counter does with four answers is put them in a file under one address and wait for the other three." She put her hands flat on the table.

"Then one of you gives it up," said Nadia Ferrous, "and we answer it properly and we are protected, and the room is over, and I would like to say for the record that I have wanted to say that since about eleven o'clock this morning and have been waiting to see if somebody else would say it first."

She got there first, and the table noticed that she had.

Beatrix Halm said, "I will say the thing that has been sitting under this since the form came and that you are all being very clever around.

"Four of us wrote to a form over a weekend without telling him. On purpose. In our own hands. And every one of us did it for his sake and every one of us told ourselves that was the reason."

She was not interrupted, and would not have been.

"It is a real reason. It is just not the reason we would have given on the sixth."

She turned the book round on the table so the day faced Beatrix and not him.

"We did it because we could not stand in this room and be asked. I wrote mine in a tram because I could not have written it here. Runa put hers in the pot by the sink. Marguerite priced hers. Ines wrote hers in her own language and handed it in like a filing, and it is the best piece of paper any of us has ever produced, and it is a filing.

"Which means the household now has an opinion.

"And the reason we did not bring it to this table is that a table with him at it is a table where we agree with him, and we have all been agreeing with him for four months because the alternative was saying no to each other in front of each other." She looked up.

"So there is a household. That is what happened this weekend. And the first thing a household does without being asked is disagree, and we have done it four times on one sheet of paper, and the sheet is going to a counter."

"That," said Marguerite Vale, "is the first true sentence anybody has said at this table since I started keeping books, and I am going to write it down and I am not going to show it to anybody."

He stood with his hand on the back of a chair for some time.

Then he picked the form up and turned it round, because the four of them had all answered the top of it and the top was where they had been reading from, and he had not looked at the bottom of it in two days.

The bottom of the form had a ruled box across it and a heading over it in the same small print as the heading on the margin of the Exchange's schedule, and it read PREMISES HELD UNDER THE STANDING OF, and the box was for a name.

It was four inches wide and it was for one name, and it sat under four questions and a declaration, and every one of the four answers on that table stopped about an inch above it.

He read it twice.

"That is the only box on the form," he said.

"That is the only box on the form," said Ines Duarte, "and four women have just spent a weekend writing around it, and not one of us has mentioned it, and I have been sitting here for an hour hoping somebody else would notice first."

"Not one of us is going in it," said Runa Sato.

"Not one of us is going in it," agreed Ines Duarte, "which means this form goes to a counter with the box empty, and a counter with an empty box in it completes the return itself, out of what it holds, and what it holds about this address is the exhibit roll of the twenty-ninth, and item four on that roll is six lines a day in six hands since the twenty-first of March with no signature on any of them."

"So the return gets written for us," said Nadia Ferrous, "in an office, out of our own notebook."

"Yes."

"By a clerk who has never met us."

"Yes."

The lamp buzzed.

He put the form down on the table in the space between the notebook and Marguerite Vale's cup.

"Then it is not a form we answer," he said. "It is a form we refuse.

"And refusing it in a room with six people in it is the one thing we have never once done in four months, because refusing it means this house has to say no to a piece of paper in a way that somebody can produce, and the last woman in this house who did that lost a pen and wrote me a sentence instead, and the woman before her went up a landing and would not come down for a month and a half."

He looked at the drawer under the table.

"Except that drawer has not been under the table since the thirty-first," he said, "and there is a woman in this house who cannot keep a silence, and who has had four answers copied into a book before anybody asked her to, and who told me about it standing in this kitchen with the kettle in her hand before I had my coat off.

"So I am not asking anybody to be brave about a paper tonight. I am asking one person to do the only thing in this house she has never refused to do."

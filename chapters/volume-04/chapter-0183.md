# The Bench Order

He was on the early shift that week, which meant seven instead of five, and he went up to the square at half past eight on the morning of the third with the broom on his shoulder, because the Reading keeps a man on that square on a repaint day whatever else it does or does not keep, and because the card by the time clock downstairs had said the third since yesterday morning.

The men came at nine.

They came with a ladder and a crate and four buckets, and one of them was about sixty and had a beard going white in the middle.

"Seven days," he said.

"Seven days," said the white-bearded one, taking the broom off him and putting a ladder into his hands instead. "I have looked at it every morning. Nothing comes down for a slot the list has nothing to say about and nothing goes up into it, because a tile that has not been claimed is a claim. You have the foot of it and I have the top and neither of us is going to be pleased about it."

That was the whole of the conversation for an hour and a half.

A Board is a list of bands and not a list of people. Every Thursday the men take down what the week's list says and put up what the week's list says, and where the list is empty the slot stays bare, and it had been bare for seven days now, and about forty slots on that wall were bare at any time and half of them belonged to people standing in the square.

He held the foot of a ladder for most of an hour and was given a bucket at about ten past ten, when the white-bearded man decided the wall was finished and came down.

Anneke Roos was on the third step of the stair with a folder, and she had been on the third step four weeks running, and she looked at the bare rectangle for about four seconds and then said, "Ceramic," and went up.

Wren Kallo was at the rail in her own coat with a reading card in her hand, because the Classification Wing certifies what goes on that wall every Thursday and has done for four years, and she was there to sign the week's list, which is the dullest document in Ostara and the only one that anybody reads in public.

Something else came down with her.

It came down off the bench at about a quarter to eleven on a wire, one side of a sheet, the way a repaint sheet comes down, and the bench clerk pinned it by the rail under the week's figures, and about forty people read it before the men had the crate off the steps.

He read it at the rail, standing on the outside of it with a bucket.

BENCH ORDER. FIRST OF THE MONTH. HOUSEHOLD ROLLS PRODUCED. A roll kept at premises and produced to a person who calls and asks is to be produced. Where the roll is produced, the person to whom it is produced, the hour, and the number of names and spaces upon it are to be entered on the district sheet and sent in. A roll not produced is not an offence. A roll produced and not entered is an offence.

"That is about you," said Wren Kallo.

"That is about six women."

"That is about you in the way everything is about you now." She had not looked up. "You understand what it does and I am going to tell you anyway, on a rail, in daylight, because it is a thing for a bench and not for a corridor.

"It does not make the roll findable. It makes the roll used. A roll in a drawer in a flat in Kestrel Hill is a thing that is never asked for.

"A roll on a wall at the top of a stair that anybody may come up and look at is a thing a person in this city now has an instruction about, and the instruction says that when somebody does come up and look at it, write down who they were and how many names were on it and send it in.

"So from this week there is a piece of paper in a district office that says a person called at a known address and looked at six names and a space. And a caller needs no warrant, and no reason, and no office, and no standing of any kind. A caller only has to be a person who calls."

She put the reading card down on the rail.

"It is the politest instrument I have ever read. It does not touch anybody. It only counts."

The men came down and washed the buckets and the white-bearded man said, "You are welcome any morning we come," and went, and the square filled up the way a square does.

And Wren Kallo stayed at the rail.

"Two things," she said. "The first one is the pale rectangle and I have had it for seven days and I have not told anybody, and I am telling you because you would find out from the board clerk inside a month and I would rather be the one.

"That slot has been bare for seven days and there is nothing in this week's list for it, and I have pulled the week's list four weeks running to see whether a band has gone, and not one has. It is not a vacancy. There is no band in this city that has gone and is not filled.

It is a slot that a man vacated by leaving a building and the Office has not yet decided whether the leaving was a transfer or a resignation, and while that is open the slot is held open, and the ceramic is doing the holding."

He looked at her.

"That is worse than a vacancy," he said.

"That is a great deal worse than a vacancy and I have not been able to say it to one person in nine days, and I work in an office where people say things to each other every ninety seconds."

"Then say the second thing."

Wren Kallo looked at him.

The square had about three hundred people in it and she did not lower her voice, because she had thought about it and she had decided in advance that lowering her voice was the coward's version.

"I have a name," she said.

"You have a name."

"I have had it since yesterday and I have not given it to anybody, including you, and I am going to tell you that I have it and I am not going to tell you what it is, and I want you to understand that this is the first thing I have ever done in my life."

The rail was about four feet from her and he was on the wrong side of it and neither of them moved.

"On the seventh of April a doctor signed a chart in a washroom on the sixth floor of a building I do not clean in, and it went into a file, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth a notice with no name on it came up on a wire at a hatch in the Vestry and eleven unfunded establishments in this quarter have spent five days working out whether it is about them.

"I have spent my whole working life reading the shape of an edit in a record. I built the thing that finds the hand that changed a document. I have never once had a use for it that was about a person."

She put both hands flat on the rail.

"A chart has a read history. Every document in a Wing file has one, because the Wing has wanted for twenty years to know who looked at what and when, and it does not keep it for the Bureau, it keeps it for itself, and it does not let anybody out of that office see it.

"I am in that office. I saw the history of the chart at about eleven o'clock yesterday morning and there were four entries on it, and three of them are people doing their job, and the fourth is a woman of band four in the establishment section who opened it at four minutes past two in the afternoon on the seventh of April, twenty minutes after it was filed, and initialled it, and whose name is in my head now and has been since yesterday morning."

"Wren—"

"I am not going to say it to you."

"I did not ask you to."

"You were going to." She turned and looked at him properly for the first time. "And here is the thing, and I have gone round it nine times since yesterday and I am going to say it in the order it happened.

"For four years I could not keep anything. Not a document, not a name, not a date, not a thing a person told me in a corridor. It made me the best counter-forensics officer in this city and it made me a woman with no friends at all, and I have never once minded, because the whole of what I am good at is knowing where an edit is.

"And at the end of March a leak went four people deep out of a building and three of them were told and not one of them broke it, and it came out of a form rather than out of a mouth, and I have been waiting seven days for the bill and it has not come."

"That was the twenty-eighth," he said. "The notice came up on the twenty-eighth."

"It came up on the twenty-eighth and I have been waiting seven days, and you have been carrying it since the twenty-fourth, and I have been wrong twice and both times in the direction of expecting to be the cause."

"That was on the twenty-eighth as well," he said.

"I know what day it was. I am telling you I am wrong about intervals and I am right about this."

He put the bucket down.

"Four people deep," he said.  "You, a plate clerk called Cressida, a sister who asked how their mother's knee was, and a dispensary clerk at the Vestry who does the clinic's requisitions. Three of them told. And the question was asked on the twenty-sixth of March in a corridor by a woman who was sent to find something out, and it was whether a clinic that had never been entered in a member's file could be entered in one later. It was about a building.

It was not about either of us."

"That is what I have," said Wren Kallo. "That is the whole of what I have got and it is the answer, and it does not require a name, and I have wanted the name for five days because I am me.

"And yesterday morning I found out that the woman who opened the chart twenty minutes after it was filed is the person who wrote the notice that came up on the twenty-eighth, and that she did it out of a building and out of a form and not out of a corridor, and that she was right."

"So the chain ends," he said.

"The chain ends," said Wren Kallo, "and I am the one holding the end of it, and I have not spent it, and I am not going to."

He looked at her for a while.

"Why not."

"Because I have been the fourth link in four chains in my life and every single time the fourth link was me, and three of those four ended with somebody losing a place." She said it fast and flat and it was not a performance. "Cressida is not in the Reading. The sister is not in her division. The dispensary clerk is still at that hatch and she will still be at that hatch in nine years.

"And I have read four records this morning in this building and I did not write one of them down."

He stopped.

"Your job is the register."

"My job is the register and the annex and the sheet, and I write down where every hand has been in every document in a wing file, and I have not written one line since yesterday morning, because every time I pick up the pen I get out the woman's name instead of the hand,

"and it is a different thing, and she is a person and I am a person, and if I write it in a book in that building then it is a record and it goes in a district file and it has a number on it."

"You have read four records this morning and you have not written one line down, in a building where that is the only thing anybody is measured on."

"Yes," said Wren Kallo. "I know exactly what that is. I am not being brave, and I am not going to be admired for it. I have not been sleeping. I have started counting things, and I started on the first of this month, and it has gone up every day since, and it has no shape at all, which is the part I do not like, because there has never been a shape in my life that I could not get hold of."

She put her coat on.

"It is killing me," she said, brightly. "That is not a figure of speech and I am not asking you to do anything about it and I want you to enjoy yourself at the Board this morning, because the pale rectangle is going to still be there on the tenth and I have already decided that it will still be there on the tenth."

"That is seven days from now," he said.

"That is seven days from now, and you did that subtraction before I finished the sentence, and it is the only arithmetic either of us wanted to do this morning."

She got on the tram. He watched her go down the rail with a reading card under her arm and no folder and no notebook, which is a thing a counter-forensics officer does not do on a repaint morning, and which four people in a wing office had already begun to notice.

# The Exchange Floor

The Exchange is on a street of clerks' offices and it is four floors and it has a name cut in the lintel and a counter on the ground floor that sells nothing anybody walks in for.

He went on the afternoon of the sixteenth because Marguerite Vale said go, and she said it in the ordinary way at the end of a sentence about the kettle, and then she got up and put her coat on over it, which is how she tells you a thing is not going to be argued about.

The sales floor is the second floor and you go up by a stair that smells of oil, and it is not a counter with a man behind it. It is a floor with about forty desks on it and a rail down the middle and shelves behind that go up to the ceiling, and every desk has a lamp and a ledger and a person in it who has been in the city longer than the floor.

One window on the far side had its shutters up and the light came through it in a low bar across the boards.

She went to the window first. She always priced a room before she stood in it, and he had watched her do it enough times not to be surprised by it.

"You have not been in here," she said.

"No."

"You are the only person I know who reads a permission circular every month at a rail in the rain and has not been upstairs." She turned round and looked at the rail. "That is not a criticism. It is how I know you will take this seriously, because a man who has been here before has already decided what a permission is."

A clerk came to them at the rail. He was about forty and he had a card on his coat and he read the card and then read the man's coat, and he did the thing that a man at a counter does, which is to price the person in front of him.

Marguerite Vale stepped half a pace to the left. That was the whole of the introduction.

"He is the head of a standing household at premises in Ferrier Street," she said. "His account is described. It is not classified. It has never been classified. He would like to know what his account buys at this counter and he would like it in writing if there is a version in writing."

The clerk's face did not change, and he put his hand flat on the ledger and said, "There is a written answer and it goes in the margin of the circular and it is the same answer every month."

"Then give him the answer."

"It is no entitlement established."

"I know what the answer is," she said. "Give him the reason. He has read the answer four times at a rail and he has never once been in the room, and he has built four explanations of it, and he has had all four of them wrong, and I would like him to have the true one, which is duller than the other four."

The clerk looked at her for a moment, and then he said, "A permission is a thing you may do. It is not a thing you own and it is not a thing you carry. You may do it, and when you may do it you do not need permission, and so permission is for the people who may not do it and want to.

"A band may do things. A band buys the permission to do a thing it may not do, which is a contradiction, and the reason for it is that a band buys a question. When you are in a room and you ask, you have to be able to say what you are asking about, and if you cannot say what you are asking about then you are a person making an enquiry and not a person exercising a privilege, and enquiries do not stop anybody.

"That is all a permission is. It is a question you are allowed to ask. And to be allowed to ask, you have to be described, and to be described, you have to be on the page."

He turned the ledger round so the sheet faced them.

"It is not your conduct. It has never been your conduct. It is a blank in a schedule, and I am sorry for the phrase, but there is nothing else I can say at a counter. Your account is not on the page. Until it is on the page, it cannot ask anything, and there is nothing on the third floor of this building that will sell to you.

"I have said so to a man about a week ago and he took it as a personal insult, and I am saying it to you now because it is the middle of an ordinary afternoon in a shop and it is not a personal anything."

"I am band five," he said. "That is on the page. I have a plate."

"Your band is on the page because you carry it," the clerk said. "You walk into a room and a plate goes in front of you and somebody reads it. That is a description that travels with you. The one on the circular is an account, and an account is a place in a book, and the place in the book is blank, and there is not one desk on this floor that will sell against a blank."

He closed the ledger.

"Now the second thing, which is the only thing you came for and you should have said so." He looked at Marguerite Vale, not at him. "Your household's account is blank because the head's is blank. That is not a trick. It is the rule, and it is written on the sheet, and it is the reason the household is worth exactly the word you have been reading since December.

"Which means it means this. Every permission a household might be sold is sold on the head's account. And a household with a blank head is a household that cannot be bought anything, no matter what any one of its members is standing, no matter how many of them there are, and no matter what they have each done."

He put his hand on the rail.

"So when people come to me and ask me to sell to a wife, I cannot, and they always say that is absurd, and it is not absurd, it is the only arrangement in this building that makes any of it mean anything."

Then he looked at Marguerite.

"And that is the other half of it, and you have not said it and I am going to say it, because she has paid the elevation and she is entitled to have the sentence."

Marguerite Vale had been looking at the low bar of light on the boards the whole time and had not interrupted once.

"I am rank two," she said.

"You are rank two."

"I am a guild broker, and nineteen years is how long I have held it, which is a longer answer than the question needed. And I am a member of that household, and a member of a standing household is inside it, and the standing of a standing household is the standing of its highest member."

"Which is you."

"Which is me." She took her glove off and put it in her pocket and did not put her hand anywhere. "And I have not told the household that I would sign, because I am not going to, and the reason I am not going to is the point.

"If they certify with me in it then the schedule issues on my rank, and then there is not one clerk in this city who would let them ask a question they were not allowed to ask before, and they would have a standing they had not paid for, and it would be mine, and I would own it."

She looked at him.

"That is the truth of it, and it is why I wanted to see the floor before the seventeenth, and it is why I have not yet told them what I am going to do about it."

The clerk turned a page.

"There is a thing I will say because I do not want either of you to leave this floor having heard only half," he said. "A broker of nineteen years who signs a standing household into a certificate does not get a question. She gets a thing that recurs. Every year the household is re-examined, and the examination is the broker's examination, and the broker is named on it, and after about four years the broker is the only person in the arrangement who is consulted.

"That is not me selling you. That is the shape of the thing. Every band in this city is a person somebody has to keep asking. You have just found out what the shape is when it is a house instead of a man."

The window light moved a foot along the boards.

"Say the last of it," said Marguerite Vale.

"The last of it is that you cannot get out of it," the clerk said. "You can be bound by a rank you did not choose, but you cannot be bound by one you cannot see. It is printed at the top of the household rules and about nine people a year read it.

"So the question is not whether you sign. It is whether you have looked at who is standing in the room when you do."

He put his hand flat on the rail.

"They are not going to let me keep that in the room."

"They never do," the clerk said. "It is the whole of what this floor is for. Go down by the stair, it is quicker, and do not take the lift because the man in it will want to talk to you."

She went first, and he followed her down the stair that smells of oil, and they came out onto a street of clerks' offices at about half past four with the light going and the two of them not saying anything for the whole of the block, and then she stopped at the corner and said:

"I am not going to decide it in a room with a ledger in it. That is what I came up here to find out and I have found it out, and the rest of it is mine and it happens tomorrow and after closing, and you will be there because I am buying it with my own name and a name is not a thing I buy alone."

Nineteen of the sixty were gone. Forty-one were left. One day to the seventeenth.

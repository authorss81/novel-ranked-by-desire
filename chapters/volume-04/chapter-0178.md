# A Patient File

The notice came up on the wire on the morning of the twenty-eighth that goes across the hatch of a dispensary in the Vestry, which is where anything that is not post and not a person gets put, and Tessa Okonkwo read it standing up with a queue behind her because she read everything that came on that wire standing up.

It was one side of a sheet. There was a form number at the top and a date at the foot and a rule under the date and nothing else on it.

NOTICE TO UNFUNDED ESTABLISHMENTS. An establishment recorded in connection with the file of a member of the Bureau, and not otherwise funded by the Bureau, will be brought within the ordinary schedule of such establishments from the first day of next month. Such establishments are advised to prepare and submit a schedule of persons treated.

There was no name on it.

Tessa Okonkwo read it twice and then took it off the wire and put it face down on the counter and called the next person, and she did that for about two hours before she let herself stop moving.

There were about eleven unfunded establishments in this quarter. That was not printed on the notice; that was hers, from nine years of keeping her head above a hatch.

Every one of the eleven read that sentence and every one of the eleven would spend the rest of the day working out whether it was about them, and not one of the eleven could find out. That is not an accident and it is not cruelty either. It is how this city says I know where you are and I am not going to say it out loud yet.

She let the queue go at about half past ten and closed the hatch for twenty minutes, which she had done eleven times in nine years, and she went through to the front room where there is a table and two chairs and a kettle, and she sat down.

Then she did the thing she had been not doing since about four minutes past nine.

It was not about money. That was the first thing she got right and it took her about a minute. The clinic was unfunded and had been unfunded for nine years and the Wing had never once asked it for a shilling, and a notice about money would have had a number and a date and a thing to pay.

It was not about the file either, not exactly. The file would have been a letter with his name on it, and she had been expecting a letter with his name on it since the seventh of April and had told him on the seventh of April what it would look like.

It was about headcount.

A schedule of persons treated.

She had been in this building nine years and she could tell you, without looking at anything, that the single ground on which the Classification Wing tolerated this clinic was that it was unfunded and had never been entered anywhere. Not the unfunding. The entering.

Because the second there is a schedule of persons treated in this building, the Wing has a list.

And if the Wing has a list then it has a place to look anybody up, and if it has a place to look anybody up then this hatch stops being a place people come who are not on a plate. There are between two hundred and three hundred people a day at that counter who have never in their lives been on a plate.

That is what she said to him, at about half past four in the afternoon of the twenty-eighth, on the bench outside with the brass plate on the end of it, which is a bench he had never once read the plate on and had decided a long time ago that he would read.

He had come in with the plate at 8,138 in his coat and something else in his coat, and she had looked at him for about a second and then said, "You have had four days to tell me something, and you have been carrying it since the twenty-fourth, and you have not said it, and I am going to ask you one question and you are going to answer it as a patient and not as a man who cleans washrooms."

"How many people have read it."

He had not been expecting that.

"What."

"How many people have read the document about the address you live at." She had her hands on her knees. "I am asking it in that order because I have been a clinician for nine years and I do not ask a person what they are concealing before I ask them what it is exposed to, and you have had it since the twenty-fourth and you have been in this room twice and you have not said it, and I want the number before I want the fact."

"A hundred and eleven. Forty-one about a person and seventeen about the household."

"I have the day book." She said it flatly. "Three years of it. Everybody who has come to that hatch and what they came with, and I keep it in a book on a shelf in this building because a clinic that keeps a day book is a clinic that can prove what it did, and that is the only reason anybody has ever been able to come here and not be reported.

"Nine of the people in your book come to this counter, and nine of them have come since December, and six of them keep an address in Ferrier Street and hold no plate of their own, and I know their names because a clinic gets told."

She looked at him for about four seconds.

"I signed a chart on the seventh of this month, in a washroom on the sixth floor of a building where you work, at about five to midnight, with your name at the top and mine at the bottom, and I put it into that book.

"And the chart went into a Wing file. And sometime after that somebody in that office did a thing with it which I have not been told about, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth a notice with no name on it came up on my wire."

"So the answer is two," she said. "The Wing, and whoever read my chart and decided a building with my name under his was worth a notice. Two people. That is all of it and it has been two people since the seventh, and it was never going to be one."

"How do you know there were not more."

"Because a notice like that is not made by a person who has been told to be discreet. It is made by a person who has been told to do an ordinary thing and has done it." She picked the bench's brass plate up off her knee, where she had not noticed she had picked it up. "I am not frightened of a man in a corridor. I am frightened of a schedule."

He looked at her.

"It was asked on the twenty-ninth of March, in a corridor, by a dispensary clerk who had been sent to find something out," he said. "And what was asked was whether a clinic that had never been entered in a member's file could be entered in one later. It was about this building. It was not about me and it was not about you."

Tessa Okonkwo did not say anything for about nine seconds.

"Then where did it come from," she said.

"It came from the chart."

"From the chart."

"From the chart, from my own description, which is in a file with a number on it and which four clerks have typed and not one word of it is wrong. Somebody asked whether a building could be put in a file, and a building went in a file, and the Office did it out of order because it does not require anybody to be discreet in order to be ordinary."

That is the entire leak. Four people deep and three of them told and the fourth in a dispensary, and it had not leaked at all, and the thing it produced was a sentence with no name on it that four hundred people in this quarter who run a clinic on nothing were going to read and could not answer.

He had come out of the Vestry at about six and thought about that for about four minutes and then he went and found Wren Kallo, because she was the only person in the city for whom that was a personal fact.

Wren Kallo was at a counter on the ground floor of the Reading and she had been at it for nine days waiting to be ruined.

"I have been waiting nine days for the bill," she said, before he had got to the end of the second sentence. "The fourth name is a dispensary clerk and she asked a question about a building in a corridor on the twenty-ninth of March and I have sat here every day since waiting for somebody to walk into this building and ask her about it.

"Not one person has walked in. Not one person has asked her. The leak did not leak." She looked up. "That is not possible. If a person has been told a thing and they break it, it breaks. There is no version of that where a man who cannot keep a secret ends up not breaking anything."

"There is one," he said. "Somebody asked a question. Somebody else answered it, in a building, out of a book that was already open. Four people are still keeping every confidence they have ever been given, and the thing that got into the file got there off a document one of them signed herself at five to midnight in a washroom on the sixth floor of a branch office."

"That is not a leak."

"No. It is worse. It is a leak where the person who said the thing was right, and the person it was said to has still not found out that it was said."

Wren Kallo sat back about four inches.

"Nine days," she said.

"Ten."

"Nine. It was the evening of the nineteenth and I have been counting since, and I would like it noted that I have been wrong twice and that both times were in the direction of expecting to be the cause of something."

Tessa Okonkwo signed a second chart at about ten past ten that night at the back of her own building.

It was the same form as the first one. It had her name on it and his name on it and the date on it, and under the date, in her own hand, four lines.

THIS ESTABLISHMENT IS UNFUNDED. IT HAS BEEN UNFUNDED SINCE IT OPENED. NO SCHEDULE OF PERSONS TREATED EXISTS. ANY SCHEDULE THAT IS SUBMITTED WILL BE INCOMPLETE AND THE INCOMPLETENESS IS THE PATIENTS AND NOT THE CLINICIAN.

IT HAS BEEN ENTERED IN A MEMBER'S FILE ONCE, BY ME, ON THE SEVENTH OF THIS MONTH, IN A ROOM, DELIBERATELY.

THE NOTICE OF THE TWENTY-EIGHTH IS ANSWERED HERE AND NOT AT A COUNTER, BECAUSE A NOTICE WITH NO NAME ON IT CANNOT BE ANSWERED AT A COUNTER AND A BUILDING THAT IS NAMED BY ANOTHER OFFICE IS A BUILDING THAT SOMEONE ELSE DECIDES WHAT IT IS.

It went into the Wing file on the following morning and it was the first document this clinic had ever produced about itself, and it cost the one thing it had, because from the first day of next month a person with a warrant can stand at that hatch and ask a name.

She did it on purpose and she knew what it was on the fourth of April, and she wrote it on the twenty-eighth, and the reason she wrote it on the twenty-eighth and not on the fourth was that she had wanted to know first whether the thing was going to come from a corridor, and it had come from a form instead, and a corridor she can survive and a form she cannot.

"There is a thing I want to say and I am going to say it to you and not to the table," she said, at the hatch the next morning, with the queue starting.

"Say it."

"That the six of you have spent four months deciding not to be written down, and that I have spent nine years making this building a place where people who have not been written down can be treated, and those are the same project, and it has worked, and it has worked because not one of us is in a file and the whole thing rests on not one of us being in a file.

"And on the seventh of this month I put a name on a chart and handed it to a woman to carry up a stair, and I knew what it was, and I would do it again, and I want it written down somewhere that I have known it for three weeks and I went on opening the hatch every morning."

She ran a cloth over the counter out of a habit that has nothing to do with the counter.

"Go and do your floors. And if anybody in this building ever tells you that a clinic is a place that is kept safe by being careful, you tell them that a clinic is kept safe by being entered on purpose by the person who runs it, and by no other hand, ever."

# The Permission Circular

The Board repainted at nine and the men put a new sheet on the rail at about twenty past, which is what they do, and underneath the sheet on the rail the Exchange posts its circular once a month on the same rail on the same morning, because the Exchange has no other way of reaching a person who is not already standing at a counter.

The new sheet carried this week's figures. Nine hundred and six plates read at the Fourth Branch. Sixty-one people brought up a band. Four plates that came down instead of up. Nothing in any line of it had been on it last month.

Underneath, on a longer sheet with a border printed round it, the April schedule.

He read it standing up, the way he reads everything on that rail, because a rail is the only place in Ostara where a person can read what the city thinks it is selling.

EVERY MEMBER IS ENTITLED TO THE FOLLOWING, AS LONG AS THE ACCOUNT CARRIES A CLASSIFICATION ENTERED AGAINST IT.

Then the bands, one to nine, and against each of them the things.

BAND ONE. Subsistence guaranteed. Assigned work, not of the member's choosing.

BAND TWO. Choice of district. Free clinic, without the merit form. School places without the merit form.

BAND THREE. Own tenancy. Own plate, and not scanned at another person's gate.

BAND FOUR. Travel permit. Ordinary standing at the courts.

BAND FIVE. An account held in the member's own name. A private office. To be asked before requisitioned.

BAND SIX. Sealed filings opened to the member on request. A warrant. Requisition of property. Testimony compelled.

BAND SEVEN. Requisition of merit from a person. The sealed archive.

BAND EIGHT. A correction order that overrides a local reading. The Council bench.

BAND NINE. The seal of the nine.

And running down the right-hand margin of the whole sheet, printed small, in a ruled space with a heading over it that read ACCOUNT — CLASSIFICATION ENTERED, the standard line the Exchange prints against any account that does not carry one.

NO ENTITLEMENT ESTABLISHED.

He read it three times.

There was a woman at the rail on his left who had come up the middle of it reading from the bottom, which is the way to read it. She was a band three, and her line had everything in it.

When she got to the top of band three she put one finger on it and said out loud to the square that she could go to the clinic now without the form, which she had been bringing to the Vestry for two years and being turned away at the hatch once a week. Then she folded the corner of the sheet over the line so she would not lose her place, and went off down the steps with her chin up.

That is what the sheet is for. He stood next to her and understood in about four seconds what he had not understood in four years.

His band is public and it is on a tile in the Reading Square in black-and-white letters that a person can stand under and read from the street. Band five. Eight thousand grains a week. It has been five for as long as there has been anything to be.

And his entitlement is blank. Not refused, not suspended, not held. Blank, in a ruled space on the right-hand margin of a printed sheet, with a standard line printed underneath it for every citizen in Ostara whose account does not carry a classification entered against it.

He was five. He could not be requisitioned without being asked, because band five buys that, and it is the only line on the entire schedule that has ever mattered to him, and the Exchange would not sell it to him, because selling it requires writing down what he may do.

He did the arithmetic standing up, in the open air, and it took him about eleven seconds and it was not arithmetic.

Four months. Four months of believing that what stood in his way was a moral question — that he could not describe the account because describing it would take something from somebody, and that the whole of his refusal was a person refusing to do a thing to another person.

There was a blank space in the margin of a printed schedule. It had been there since the start of the winter, and it had been going down to a counter in Bellhouse every month and coming back with the same line on it, and no clerk had ever once asked him a question, and he understood standing there that nothing in four years had been done to him by anybody at all.

The difficulty had a clerical shape. That was the whole of it.

And the door out of it was the classification.

It sat there in the middle of the sheet as plainly as a name on a wall.

There is a ruled space on the right-hand margin and a form that fills it and an office on the second floor of the Fourth Branch that administers it.

The only reason he had not gone and put his account in that space in the first month of the winter is that a classified account is a held account, and a held account is a room with no window, and everybody who has gone into one in this city has come back out of it with nothing and without a rank.

At the Fourth Branch he went straight to the second-floor counter at ten past ten and asked a clerk a question that took her about four seconds to hear and about ninety to refuse.

"Band five. To have the plate held rather than read. Can I."

"Can you."

"Yes. Today. Is it a permission."

She put one finger on a card taped inside the hood of the counter, which is where they keep the whole of what a counter can and cannot do, and she read down it, and she read it twice, and she came back to him with the apologetic part of her face already made.

"It is a permission and it is not mine to sell, and I am going to tell you what I would have had to write if you were stood here this morning asking to buy it, because I think you ought to hear the sentence.

"I would write the words entitlement not established, and then I would write the band, and then I would write see account. And the man behind me would send that up to the second floor, and the second floor would ask for the classification, and there is not one of those three people who is trying to do anything to you."

"Thank you," he said.

"I have not done anything for you. I have told you what happens, and I have not written anything down, because you did not ask me to do anything."

She put the card back inside the hood. "If you want the words on a slip so that you have them in your hand, I will write them this afternoon. It will take four minutes and it will not help you at all."

He put the corner of the sheet back down and read the last line of the printed schedule twice, because it was the only line on it addressed to a person rather than to a band.

The last line of the printed sheet, again, in full, which was the whole argument of the morning.

Every member.

He read that at about half past nine in the open air with the square going on behind him, and then he went down the steps and across to the tram and sat in it for eleven stops with his coat over his knees.

A permission is a thing you may do. You may not buy one for a man whose account is blank. But a permission is not a thing you carry — it is a thing you are, and it comes off a plate, and it comes off a plate that reads.

A standing household shares one plate.

He had known that for four years the way a man knows the rules of a trade he does not work in: it was true, it was in the ordinary arrangements of this city, and it had never once applied to him, because a household is a thing that requires a name at the top of it, and he had spent four months being asked to sign a notebook and had asked a sixth time in front of six women for a pen.

There were six women on Ferrier Street who had each refused to be written down. Between them and him there were eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two grains of difference between a plate that says five and a band that says six, and not one of those grains could be earned by anything he did in public, ever, in his life, because the account cannot be converted, and the conversion is the thing that is missing.

But a plate can read a household. And a household is read against the highest standing in it.

There was one person in Ostara who might know whether a permission can be held by a person other than the one it belongs to, and she was a guild broker who had spent thirty years making things legal that the city had not thought to ask about, and she had refused once already this year to put a price on him and had put a price on the house instead.

The classification paper dated the nineteenth of last month went up three floors at four o'clock that afternoon, as it had gone up every working day since, and came back down unsigned, as it had done every working day since.

He had a whole month to decide between the most frightening thing available to him and the second most frightening one.

He got off the tram at the junction and walked the last eleven minutes because there was nothing else to do with them.

The thing that made it a decision and not a plan was that he had not told anybody. He had come out of a square on the Thursday morning at about half past nine with an understanding of the whole shape of the month, and there were six women in Ferrier Street who had decided to be in the record with him and not one of them was on the roll, and none of them knew.

And he understood, somewhere between the fifth and the sixth tram, that this was the actual content of the month and that everything else he had done in four years — the description, the four ribs, the promise he made into a microphone before two hundred and eleven people — had been a man arranging the furniture of a room he had not yet been asked to sit in.

He was going to do the second one. He was going to need five women to do it with him, and the first one he was going to ask was a woman who had told him two nights before not to ask gently.

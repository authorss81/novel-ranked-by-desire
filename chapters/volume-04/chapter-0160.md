# Six Carbon Copies

On the tenth, a man on the second floor brought his daughter in at a quarter to nine, because the girl had been off school since the sixth with a hand that would not close, and he stood at the counter for fifty minutes with the forms, and at the end of it the girl was seen and the man's plate was read and he thanked the clerk and not the attendant, and the clerk said the child should come back on Thursday.

That went into the drawer on the back page, with the second floor and the hour on it, and it was the smallest thing that had happened to him in ten days.

A hundred and seventeen.

He found Ola Bright at the kitchen table at about half past nine at night with the copybook open in front of her and nothing else on the table, which in that house at that hour is not a thing that happens.

"I have been told what you want," she said, "by three women, and not one of them told me anything twice, so I am going to assume you know what it is and you are going to have to say it. Because if it is only in my head then it is a thing I dreamed, and I will wake up at four in the morning convinced I invented it, and I will be right."

"Six copies of one thing," he said. "Six hands. And I want the page."

"All six of ours?"

"All six of yours. Not Sun's."

"Because she has said no, and you have stopped asking her." She wrote something in the margin of the copybook. "That is the right thing to do and I want it written down that you did it the right way, because the wrong way would have been to hand her one on the stair and walk away, and you were on that stair two nights ago and you did not."

He said the thing.

He said it standing in the kitchen, in his own words, and she wrote it in the copybook in her own hand, and then she read it back to him, and then she looked at it for about ten seconds with the pen not moving.

"Right," she said. "Now I am going to tell you what that has cost, and I am telling you before I do it, and if you tell me to do it anyway I will, because I have already copied four answers into this book without being asked and there is no version of me that says no to a pen now.

"Since the sixteenth of March this book has been the only thing in Ostara that nobody asked for. Not the notebook, which six women keep because they want to. Not the visitors' book, which Marguerite has kept for four years and reads out loud. Not a ledger, not a day book, not a minute of anything.

"This is the one that has never been asked for by anybody and it has never once been a copy of anything, and it has three hundred and eleven sentences in it and eleven of them are mine and not one of them has ever been produced to anybody."

"Then take it out of the book."

"No. That is the easy one and you knew it was the easy one." She capped the pen. "If I copy six of these into it then the thing that made it worth keeping is gone and the book is a second notebook with worse handwriting, and every sentence in it that came before now has a question mark against it, because the next time I bring it out somebody is going to ask which parts of it you asked for.

"So here is what it has cost. From tonight, nothing I write in this book is unasked. Every page of it from here to the end is a page I wrote because a man in this house asked me to. That is the whole price. It is not a small price and it is not a large one and it is the last thing in this city that was mine that I did not have to explain."

"All right."

"Do not say all right like that." She wiped her pen nib on her apron. "You do that when you have worked out that the price is fair and you would like it settled. I have watched you do it to a clerk twice this week. I am not a clerk and I have a book."

"I am sorry."

"I know you are. That is why I am still sitting here."

She made six copies, which took about forty minutes, and she made them in six different hands on purpose, because she said a carbon copy is six of one person and six hands is six people, and there was a difference and she was the only person in that house qualified to draw it.

She did not let him take them out of the book. She cut them out herself, one at a time, along a ruled edge she had put there on the sixteenth of March for a purpose she had not had at the time.

He gave the first to Nadia Ferrous at about ten past ten and she read it standing up, twice, and then said, "This is an instruction."

"It is."

"You do not get to instruct me. I have been telling you for four months that I am not the reason."

"I know what you are." He said it carefully. "This is not the thing you have been refusing. That one was whether I would touch you. This one is whether a piece of paper that comes to this door gets answered, and I have given that answer away to somebody who is not me.

"And if I had asked you for it you would have said yes in about four seconds, and you would have been owed something for the rest of your life, and I have taken it off you instead."

Nadia Ferrous read it a third time.

"Right," she said. "Say that to Ines, because she is going to make it the whole argument."

He gave the second to Beatrix Halm, who was already at the door with her coat on, and who read it on the step and said "that is going in the yards before the end of the week" and went out into the rain without another word, which was the most she had ever said about him.

He gave the third to Runa Sato, who read it standing at the table with her hands behind her back, the way a serving officer reads a document she has been handed by a person she does not trust.

"On the twelfth I go into a room for four hours," she said, "and on the thirteenth I come out of it and hand this to six people in a kitchen, and every one of them answers a piece of paper about me without me in the room. Do you understand that you have just made me the reason the paper works?"

"I understand that you will be the reason the paper works."

"Then say the rest of it, because there is a rest of it and you are standing there hoping I will not notice."

She put it face down on the table, which she did to very few documents.

"If I am in this, then on Thursday of this month any office that wants through that door can do it about me. Not about you. About me. And I am the one in this house with a band and a hearing and a floor, and I have spent two years making sure that my work and that door had never been in the same sentence."

"I know."

"Then say it and do not apologise for it, because an apology from you at this table is a way of making it your fault again and it is not your fault, it is mine, I am the one with the band." She looked at the face-down paper. "I am going to sign that. And you are going to stop asking me whether I am sure about anything for the rest of the month."

Marguerite Vale had hers in her hand before he had it out of the book, because she had been standing behind him for about a minute.

"Six hands and one sentence and the sentence is an instruction and you have instructed a household." She read it once and put it down. "That is the first time in nine years anybody has handed me a document with more than one signature on it that was not going to a counter. Do you know what it is worth?"

"Don't price it."

"It is worth exactly what I said it was worth in this kitchen two days ago, and I am not going to say it twice." She picked it up again.

"And I want you to know that you have just done the thing that every person I have worked for in thirty years did not do, which is to give six people a standing instruction in writing and then walk away and not be in the room. I have priced that service before and it is not for sale, and you have had it free, and I am going to be unpleasant about it for some days."

The fifth went to Tessa Okonkwo, who read it in the doorway on her way out, and who said, "You could have asked me on the third and I would have said yes and it would have cost me less," and he said that was exactly why he had asked her the night before last in a washroom instead.

The sixth was the last one in the book and he carried it up the stairs.

Sun Meiling was on the landing in a coat, with the hood down, at nearly midnight, which was late for a woman who did not come down.

"You are not giving me one," she said.

"No."

"That is the sixth time you have decided not to hand me a pen and I have watched you do it five times and be pleased with yourself about it and I have never once agreed." She looked at the copy in his hand and did not reach for it. "Go on, then. What is on it."

He told her.

She listened all the way to the end without moving, and when he had finished she was quiet for some time, and the building made the noise it made.

"Write it on the wall of the landing," she said, "in pencil, and do not put a date on it, and do not put my name near it, and when the copy that comes back from a counter in a fortnight has got a blank in it that is me, I want to be able to come up a stair and read the thing I said while it was still true and not read it out of a file with a clerk's stamp on it."

He wrote it on the wall of the landing in pencil. It took him about ninety seconds. He did not date it and he did not put a name near it.

Then he went back down and knocked on the door of the room at the back, which is Ines Duarte's room, and which she rents and does not sleep in four nights out of seven.

She opened the door in a coat over a shift and took the page and read it standing in the doorway in about eleven seconds, which is the fastest he had ever seen anybody read one of his sentences.

"No," she said.

"It is not that kind of instruction."

"It is that kind of instruction. It is exactly that kind." She held it up so he could see her own handwriting on it. "I have refused twice in this city in writing and both times I was right and neither time it cost me anything anybody could put a price on, and I did it because a woman who files nothing is a woman who cannot be got at.

"And you have just handed me a standing instruction that says if a paper comes, answer it, in your own hand, in the post. That is not a household. That is an office, and I have worked in an office, and I know exactly what one of those is for."

"Ines—"

"And you know there is another one of us who is not answering, and you have known it since you came up this stair."

She held the page out at him and did not give it back. "Six copies. Five come back with five sets of hands on them and one comes back with mine on it, and mine is the only one that is not a hand, it is a refusal, and in about nine days there will be a blank in this household that a clerk can point at.

"Which is the first blank in this house that any of us did not put there on purpose."


He stood in the corridor.

"Yes," he said.

"Then that is the correct outcome and I would have said it myself in about a fortnight, and you have saved me a fortnight by being impatient."

She turned the page over and wrote something on the back of it and handed it back, and he could not read it standing there and did not try.

"That is on the back and it is not for you and it is not for the file, and it is four lines and I will not say what is in it, and I have never once broken that in four months.

"Go down. You have made five women answer a piece of paper for the first time in their lives by writing a sentence on a landing, and there is not one hour of this month left to think about what that was."


He went down. Ola Bright was still at the table with the copybook shut and her hands flat on it.

She looked at him.

"Six copies," she said. "Five came back with a name on them in your hands tonight, and I saw you do it, and I did not say anything at the time because it was not mine to say.

"You have handed five of them a thing to do and you have told none of them how to do it and you have left me out of the copies entirely on purpose, because I would have written down what to do. That is four months of you in one evening and I have watched all of it from this chair."

# The Fourth Rib

The back room of the Vestry is eleven feet by nine and it has one window that does not open onto anything but a wall, and a table in it, and a lamp on a bracket, and a chart on a nail, and it is the room a clinician works in and not the room anything else happens in.

She made him take his shirt off at ten past four in the afternoon of the fourteenth, which is the end of the hatch and the hour the room is empty, and she made him stand against the wall first and not lie down.

"Stand there. Do not hold your breath and do not brace. If you brace I will be reading a wall and not a man, and I have had enough of walls."

Four ribs.

They are on his left side, and they are the third, the fourth, the fifth and the sixth, and they have been there since the end of March, and two hundred and eleven people saw three of them go at once in a hall on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth, and the fourth went about ten minutes later.

Ines Duarte made him say the location out loud before she would put a thumb on it, and Sun Meiling never touched it at all and made him say the number three times.

Tessa Okonkwo had refused to touch it for four volumes.

She had given her reason on the seventh of April, in a washroom on the sixth floor, at five to midnight, in the voice of a clinician and not in the voice of a woman, and the reason had not changed in the five weeks since and it did not change now.

If she put her hand on it and something moved, then she was a clinician who had put her hand on it, and she could not un-know that, and she would spend the rest of her life unable to write a chart on him without her own hand in it.

"I am going to touch it," she said.

He did not say anything.

"I am going to touch it because a record of a body is the only thing in this city that is worth the paper it is on, and I have signed a chart in four volumes and every one of them has two ribs on it and nothing else, and a chart that says the third and the fourth and stops there is a chart that says a man has two ribs and not a name of anything.

"And there are four.

"I have refused this for four months and my reason was good and my reason is still good and I am setting it aside, and I am telling you I am setting it aside, and you are going to ask me why and I am going to answer you once and then I am going to work."

"Why."

"Because in five days I am going to be asked to stand in a room and answer for a building, and I have answered for a building on a card at a counter and I am going to answer for it in a room, and I am not going to do that with a hand that has never been put on the thing that brought the man to my hatch."

She pulled the lamp off the wall and set it so it threw across his side and not into his face.

"That is a professional reason and it is the true one and I have not got a better one. Lie down."

He lay down on the table and she put two fingers above the third rib on his left.

"Say it back to me."

"The third and the fourth on the left," he said, "and the fifth and the sixth."

"Say it again, because you will say it three times in this room and I am going to make you say it three times, and the third time is the one that counts and I do not know yet why."

She had her hand flat against the side of him, palm on the ribs, not pressing.

"First," she said. "Where."

"Third and fourth on the left. And the fifth and the sixth."

She moved her hand up an inch and held it there and looked at his face, not at her hand, for about twenty seconds.

"Second. Where."

"Third and fourth on the left. And the fifth and the sixth."

"Third. Where. And this is the one that counts, so say it slowly, because I am going to write down what you say and not what I find, and if we do not agree I am going to leave the page blank."

"Third and fourth on the left," he said. "And the fifth and the sixth. Four."

She did not move her hand for about half a minute.

Then she took it off him and went and washed it and came back and did the whole thing properly, the way she would do a knee.

She put him flat on his back and turned his head to the left and got a finger under the angle of his ribs at the front and walked it along, one at a time, and she said each number out loud as her hand arrived at it and made him say it back, and when her hand was on the third she stopped and held it and asked him what that did, and he said nothing arrived, and she pressed and said *that*, and he felt the difference.

She listened at four places on his left side with the bell of an instrument in her ear, for about two minutes at each, and she listened at two on his right.

Then she took the chart off the nail and wrote the date and the hour at the top and turned it round, and she wrote nothing for about forty seconds with the pen on the page.

She wrote down what the four ribs were doing when her hand was on them.

Nothing.

She wrote that down, and she wrote the frame, and she wrote the heart, and she stopped the pen about a third of the way down the page and turned it round, and put the pen on the line at the bottom and signed it.

"Lin Hao," she said, and then her own name, and then the time, and then she tore it off the pad and did not give it to him.

"That is mine and it goes in the day book tonight and it goes nowhere else, and I want you to hear me say what is on it, because you have never seen it.

"I have written the fourth rib on the left. I have written that it is the fourth rib and that is all it is. I have written that under my hand and under a stethoscope and with my own fingers counted along from the angle of the rib and it is the fourth rib, and it is a fourth rib, and there is nothing about it that I cannot account for and nothing about it that any other fourth rib in this city would not be.

"And I have written that the heart is regular and the frame is what a man who carries water up six floors for four years has, and the mass is what I wrote on the seventh of April and it has not moved by an ounce."

"That is good," he said.

"That is the good news and it is not the news." She sat on the edge of the table. "The news is what it says underneath, and I am going to read it to you because it is a record and you are the only person who can be told it.

"Four ribs on the left, in a man of your trade, in a man who was in a hall on the twenty-ninth of March and watched himself move, are the fourth rib. They are also four of something that a body has been using for a year and a half, and I have been treating them as a curiosity for four months because a curiosity is something that has a name I do not know, and a curiosity is not a diagnosis.

"I have now put my hand on them. I did it knowing what it would cost, because I have four charts in this city with my name on them and I would have had to defend the two ribs on every one of them for the rest of my life, and I would rather have four and no defence.

"And I still do not know what they are. That is what the chart says. I have written the fourth rib and I have written that I do not know what moved in it, and that is a true chart, and it is going into the day book at the Vestry tonight with my name on it and the time on it, and in about nine years a woman in an office will read it and will not know whether I did it or whether it happened to me."

She washed her hands again, to the elbow, without hurry.

"I am not going to tell you what I think it is," she said. "You have four months of people telling you what they think it is, and I am a clinician and I have got a chart, and the chart says the fourth rib.

"And I want you to notice, because you are the sort of man who notices things, that I have said the third time is the one that counts and I did not tell you why. I did not tell you why because I do not know why, and because I wrote it down before I knew, which is the only order a chart is ever done in."

She took the lamp back up to the bracket.

"Put your shirt on. You are going to be here until four because I am going to write this up properly, and because you are not going to walk out of this room and down four flights of stairs and across a market with a chart in me that I have not finished, and if you argue with me about that I will put the pen down."

He put his shirt on.

He said, at the door, "It is the same four ribs."

"It is the same four ribs," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and that is the first thing that has been the same about them in seven days, and I have spent the last hour making a record of it, and the record is the thing. Go home. I will be here at eight."

# The Service File

The fifth was the second Thursday on the card by the time clock, and the men came up to the Reading wall at nine with a ladder and a crate and four buckets, and the pale rectangle was still bare.

Two days. He had said on the third that it would be bare on the tenth, and Wren Kallo had said the same thing, and two women who had never spoken to each other about it had arrived at the same figure from different ends.

The white-bearded man handed him a bucket without asking.

"No list," he said.

"No list."

"That is the fifth week and I have been putting nothing up in that slot since the twenty-sixth of April and there has been nothing on any list since the first, and the day the Office decides whether a man left or was moved the slot goes up or it comes down, and there is nothing either of us can do about it and I have stopped talking about it." He set the crate on the barrow. "You are welcome any Thursday."

At about eleven the six of them were in the kitchen and Nadia Ferrous had a service file on the table with the cover off.

It is a thin book about the size of a hymn book with a cloth spine and a number on the front of it, and it is the document that the district lift service keeps on the woman who runs the cars, and it goes to a counter in Cobb Street once a week, and it has gone once a week since the tenth of March, which is eight weeks.

"Read page nine," she said.

She was already talking, which she had been since she came down the stairs, and which is how a woman who is the fastest talker in a kitchen with six women in it gets to the middle of a thing.

"Page nine is the members of the service. It has got me on it and it has got two hundred and nine thousand hours on it and it has got a box at the bottom that says INCIDENT AND CIRCUMSTANCE IN THE COURSE OF DUTY and it has been empty for four months, which is the best thing in my life and which no document in this city has ever said about me."

She turned it round.

"Page nine is not read. It is read when somebody is after something and then it is read by a clerk with a bad morning, and the clerk reads page nine because page nine is the page with names on it, and then the clerk stops."

"And you are going to write a household on page nine," said Ines Duarte.

"I am going to write an address and a head on page nine and then I am going to write my own name against it as a member of it, in my own hand,

"and I am going to hand it to the counter myself at ten past two this afternoon at the change of shift, and I am going to do that in front of nine people who work with me, because I have been doing things in front of nine people for four months and every single one of them has been a thing I could not tell them about."

The range let go of its heat, which it does twice a day whether or not anybody is in the room.

"Say the reason," said Marguerite Vale, "and say it properly, and then say why it has nothing to do with him, because I am going to ask you that and you have brought it up yourself twice and you have not answered it."

"I have answered it. I am answering it now."

She put her hands flat on the file.

"Three months ago I handed a page of forty-one names to a stranger in a lift on purpose, in about four seconds, because a man said his business out loud in an ordinary voice and I decided I would rather be the one who handed it over than the one who had it taken. And it has been in four documents since.

It has been in the exhibit roll that goes up by the rail, and it has been read out in a hall at Cobb Street to two hundred and eleven people, and it has been read out again in a room in Bellhouse to forty strangers by a woman who is not me and was very welcome to.

"And not one line in any paper in this city says I chose it."

She stopped, and then she did not stop.

"That is the whole of what I have wanted since the fourteenth of March and there is no instrument in this city for it. There is a box at the bottom of page nine and there is a line at the top of a return and there is a schedule served on premises, and not one of them will take a sentence.

"And a service file is the only document I have ever had that goes anywhere on its own. It does not need anybody to send for it.

It goes to that counter once a week whether the Exchange wants it or not, it has gone eight weeks without a single person asking for it,

"and when the certificate comes at the end of sixty days there is going to be a piece of paper about six women at an address in Ferrier Street, and if a household is on page nine then every single week for the rest of that book's life a clerk in Cobb Street is going to be handed a document that says where that household is, without anybody spending a grain on it."

"Without anybody asking for it," said Ines Duarte.

"Without anybody asking for it. That is the entire difference between a roll and a document, and I have been trying to say it since I got in and I have said it badly three times."

"He is not the reason," said Nadia Ferrous.

"He is not the reason."

"You are going to have to be better than that, because he is standing in this kitchen."

"Then I will be better than that." She turned round.  "He is not the reason and he is not even the occasion.

If he were not alive I would have written it in March, and I would have written it in the same words, and I would have handed it to that counter with the same eleven seconds in front of it,

"and the only difference is that in March I would have had to invent a head of household and I could not have done it, because there was not one, and there had not been one since the fourteenth of March when a woman declared terms off in a room and left me standing in a kitchen with nothing."

"That is four months of him in a sentence," said Marguerite Vale.

"That is four months of my own life in a sentence and he is in about a fifth of it and I have been in the rest of it since I was nineteen." Nadia Ferrous put her hand flat on the file. "And the other thing, and then I am going to stop and eat.

"There is a row on page nine and there are about two hundred entries in it going back four years, and every one of them is a lift that did not come, and I have written every one of them in my own hand, because it is my book and not one person has ever asked me a question about it in four years. I can go down as fast as I like. I have never gone up once in my life and I am not going to start.

"And every single one of those two hundred entries is a person in a car who could not leave. I write the time and the floor and the reason and nothing else, because nothing else is mine to write. And a service file is the only place in this city where four years of me being unable to leave is written down in a hand, and not one person has ever asked to see page four."

The kitchen was quiet.

"So write the household on page nine," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"No," said Ines Duarte.

"Ines—"

"He has been in this house four months and every one of you has understood that the standing instruction on that doorframe exists to stop this house from becoming one thing, and Nadia is proposing to put it into a book that goes to a counter once a week for the rest of time." Ines Duarte did not raise her voice.  "It is a good idea. It is the best idea anybody has put at that table since the tenth of April.

And I am going to say the one sentence that is in my head about it and then I am going to stop.

"There are forty-one names in that book already, and not one line in it says you chose them, and you have been waiting since the fourteenth of March for a document with your name and your decision in the same place. Page nine will not do that.

Page nine will put your household in a district book, and it will not put your choice anywhere, and what it will do is put a household of six women on a page in a file that the Exchange is entitled to read, once a week, for ever, with no request and no reason and no person who has ever to be identified as having asked."

"That is what I want," said Nadia Ferrous.

"That is what you want and I have not got one better argument and I am not going to invent one at half past eleven in the morning." Ines Duarte sat down. "Write it. Write it in your own hand and do it at the change of shift and do not let anybody in this house put a single word in it for you, because the moment one of us does it becomes the house's entry and not yours, and it would be worth nothing."

The change of shift is at ten past two.

The car is at the end of the run and nine operators stand in it because they stand in it every week, in the way a crew stands in a thing at the end of a run, and Nadia Ferrous came in with the file under her arm and did not sit down.

She did it in about ninety seconds, standing, in the middle of the car, with the light coming in off the river.

She wrote the address first, and then the words STANDING HOUSEHOLD under a heading she had to rule herself because page nine has no line for one, and then a head of household, and then her own name against the premises, and then she signed it, and then she put the hour on it because her hands do that.

Then she read it out loud.

"That is a household at premises above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, headed by a man of band five who cleans washrooms at the Fourth Branch and who does not live there, and I am a member of it, and I have put it in my own book and in my own hand because I am the only one of that household who has ever chosen to be found and I wanted it in writing before somebody else put it in for me and got it slightly wrong."

Nine people stood in a car.

"Right," said one of them.

"That is all I have," said Nadia Ferrous.

"And I am aware that nine of you have just heard a thing about a house in the Slough from a woman who runs the lift, and I am aware that the Exchange is entitled to read page nine once a week,

"and I am aware that there are fifty-two days left to run on a certificate at that address with six names on it, and that a household in a service file is a household a clerk can be handed on a wet morning with a line under it, for ever, for nothing."

"I did it anyway. I did it in ninety seconds standing up in a car because I have wanted a line under my own name in a document that says I chose it since the fourteenth of March and it is the only one of them left."

The car went in.

She handed the book to a man at the counter in Cobb Street at about twenty past two and he gave her a receipt with a number on it, and she kept it, and it was the first piece of paper in four months that she had walked out of a building with.

At about seven that evening the six of them were at the table and she put the receipt down in the middle of it.

"Eleven seconds to write and forty minutes to explain," she said, "and I have already done the eleven seconds, so the table has forty minutes and I want to spend about nine of them being told I am wrong and then I want to eat."

Runa Sato said, "You put a household into a file that goes to a counter."

"I put a household into a file that goes to a counter once a week and has gone eight weeks without anybody asking for it, which is the only advantage it has, and that advantage is the whole of the argument and I made it at half past eleven this morning and Ines could not break it."

"Two hundred entries in your hand," said Sun Meiling, from the fourth stair, "that not one person has ever asked you about. And you have just put the one thing in it that anybody will ask you about."

"Yes," said Nadia Ferrous.

"And you did it in ninety seconds in front of nine people rather than in this room."

"Yes. If I had done it in this room it would have been the house's decision and there would have been a discussion about it, and there would have been a version of it that was kinder to me." She took her coat off. "I wanted the one where it was mine and it was ugly and there were nine witnesses and none of them asked me a single question about it."

The oven came on and stayed on, which it had not done all week.

"I am going to be on that page once a week for the rest of my working life," she said, "and one day somebody with a band and a bad morning is going to come to that counter and read page nine, and they will read my household, and they will not know that I chose it and they will not be able to find out, because a page in a service file has no place for a woman to say she wanted it."

She sat down.

"That is the price. I have paid it. I would like somebody to say something that is not about the price."

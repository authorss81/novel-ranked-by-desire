# The Shilling Version

He was at the gate at about a quarter past two on the afternoon of the eighteenth, on the same side of the street as the house, with his back to a brick pillar and a folded sheet in his hand, and he had been there long enough that one of the chop-house's customers had put a saucer on the sill for him and he had not touched it.

Beatrix Halm went down at ten past two because she had said at one in the morning that she would, and she went alone, and it cost her four separate women having to agree to let her.

He was a band four and a jobbing printer with a shoproom two streets back off Ash Row, and he gave her his name without being asked for it, and he was not a villain and he was not a fool and he was not nervous, which she had expected him to be.

"Ossian Kerr," he said. "Four doors up. Nineteen years in that room and I have never once been inside your door."

"You have been to the gate."

"I have been to the gate in February and I was told there was no landing by a woman at this step, and I believed her, and I want to say that first, because everything else I am going to say is going to sound as though I have been clever, and I have not been clever. I have been reading a paper."

He held the sheet out.

It was the exhibit roll. Two sides, a warm sheet, type set fresh, a shilling's worth of the twenty-ninth of March, and on the back of it, in pencil, in a small neat hand that had been taught at a bench and not at a school, there were six questions with a ruled line under each.

Beatrix Halm read them standing up on the pavement.

HOW MANY PERSONS KEEP AN ADDRESS AT THESE PREMISES.

WHETHER ANY PERSON AT THESE PREMISES IS A CHILD.

WHETHER ANY PERSON AT THESE PREMISES IS BEING KEPT AGAINST THEIR WILL.

WHO PAYS THE RENT.

WHETHER ANY PERSON AT THESE PREMISES HOLDS A PLATE.

WHY, IN NINETEEN YEARS, NOT ONE PERSON AT THESE PREMISES HAS EVER BEEN ON THE BOARD.

She read the last one three times.

"May I take this inside," she said.

"Yes."

"Will you wait."

"I have been standing here since half past one and I have nowhere else to be, and I will wait as long as it takes, and I want to say one more thing before you go in." He put the sheet back in his own hand. "I wrote those six questions at a bench at about eleven o'clock last night and I have been carrying them about all day being ashamed of them.

"Three of them are nosy. You can tell which three. The fourth one I could have found out by asking the man who owns the ground floor. The fifth one I could have found out at the Reading Square by standing under a wall and looking up."

He turned the sheet round so she could see the six questions again.

"That last one is not nosy. Nineteen years. Six people going in and out of that door, working hours, in a street where everybody knows everybody, and not one tile on that wall has ever had a name from that house on it. Not six. Not one.

"And in February a woman I have nodded at for nineteen years went in there at about two in the afternoon and came out at eight, and she has not been in the street since, and I have thought about it every day for two months."

He put the sheet back down his side.

"There is a paper for sale in Cobb Street for a shilling and it says six lines a day in six hands and no signature on any of them. I am not a clever man. I am a printer. I can tell you what a line of type is.

"Six lines in six hands is not a household. Six lines in six hands is six people writing down what they did, on the same days, for each other, without being asked and without signing. I have set type for thirty years and I have never seen that in a house and I do not believe that is what is happening there."

"What do you believe is happening there," said Beatrix Halm.

"I believe that in nineteen years six people have arranged a life in which they cannot be found, and that they have done it well, and that I have found it out with a shilling, and that is the whole of my business and I would like it answered in writing."

She took the sheet inside and gave it to Sun Meiling without a word, and Sun Meiling read it on the third step in about four seconds and said, "It comes to me," and went in and put it on the table.

The six of them wrote between two and six in the afternoon, in the house, in one room, without speaking to each other about it.

Ines Duarte's was four lines and it was a filing. She gave the first question as six, and attached a footnote about the seventh space at the foot of a notebook that was not a document.

The third question got one sentence and it made Beatrix put her hand over her mouth.

NO PERSON AT THESE PREMISES IS KEPT. THE PREMISES ARE HELD BY SIX PERSONS. THE HOLDING IS VOLUNTARY AND THERE IS NO OTHER HOLDER.

She answered the fifth with a list of six plates, every one of them held by a woman, every one of them held at the Exchange, and not one of them the plate of a person who keeps an address here.

Beatrix Halm wrote hers in a signal cabin at the end of the Ferrier line at twenty past four, standing up, in about ninety seconds, and she did not read the other five and she did not know what the other five said.

Runa Sato wrote the second and the fourth and the sixth.

Tessa Okonkwo wrote her answers at the hatch of a clinic in the Vestry between four and five, standing up, with a queue behind her, and she sent them in an envelope with no note at all, and the envelope said her name and the word DISPENSARY on it, which meant that the first document any of them had ever sent to a stranger was also the first one that named a building.

Marguerite Vale wrote a price for the fourth one and would not give it up, and the fifth, and a fifth answer that is one sentence: THE REASON IS NOT AVAILABLE AND THE REASON IS NOT A SECRET, IT IS A DECISION, AND DECISIONS ARE NOT OWED TO STRANGERS, AND YOU ARE NOT ONE.

And Sun Meiling wrote the first question on the top of the sheet and the sixth at the bottom and nothing else at all, and Beatrix noticed the shape of it afterwards and understood it at about half past six.

"There are six people at that address," Sun Meiling had written. "Six. Not five. There are seven women in this house and I am the seventh and I am not answering for myself and I am not answering for the woman who is asleep in a chair, and both of those answers are yours to work out and not mine to give you."

They gave it back at about ten past seven.

Ossian Kerr read the whole thing on the pavement, standing, in the light, in about six minutes, and he turned it round twice, and he took his glasses off and cleaned them on his sleeve and put them back on and read the last answer a second time.

He said: "Two of these answer a question I did not ask."

"Yes."

"Which two."

"The woman who wrote the fourth one would not give up what she had put in it and the woman who wrote the fifth one has told me I am not owed a reason." Beatrix Halm stood on the other side of the gate from him. "They are both correct and neither of them is a courtesy and there is not one word in either that I would take back."

He folded the sheet and did not put it in his pocket.

"I am going to say three things and then I am going to walk four doors up to my own front door, and I would like you to hear all three before I start, because you will have about ten seconds to decide whether to shut a gate and I would rather you did not."

He did not hurry.

"One. I have got what I came for and I am not going to come back about it.

"You have told me there are six people, that not one of them is a child, that not one of them is being held, that one person pays the rent and it is not the rent man, that not one of them is on the Board, and that the last thing is a decision and not a concealment.

"That is an answer. It is a complete answer, it is the first one anybody has ever given me about anything, and I am going to keep it."

"Two. I was wrong about one thing and I want to be exact about which, because I have been wrong about one thing and I am going to be wrong about another later and I would like it written down which one it was this time.

"I came here with six questions and you have given me six answers in six hands, written in one afternoon, in a room, by six people who had not spoken to each other. I have printed forms for thirty years and I know what a set of answers written under one roof by one household looks like, and this is not it. This is six people who are not managing each other.

"And that is the answer to the sixth question, and none of you wrote it down, and I did not have to pay a shilling for it, and I have got it for nothing, which means I have stolen it."

"Three." He looked at the gate and then at the step above it and then at nothing. "There are eleven of us.

"There are eleven people in this city who have bought that paper, and three of us have bought it four times, and I have met two of the others and I have not met the rest, and every one of us has a different reason and every one of our reasons is a real one.

"The next man who comes to this gate will not be me and he will not knock twice and wait. He will be somebody with a worse reason than mine and a worse question than mine, and he will come in about a fortnight, and he will come because he has read the same thing I have read, and he will not have stood at this gate for nineteen years and he will not care in the slightest that you have answered me in six hands.

"And I want to say one thing about that, and it is not a threat, because I am not that kind of man.

"There is no office in this city I could complain to if I was. There is no office and there is no counter and there is nothing to file. You cannot refuse me and you cannot be reported and you cannot be put in a file, and I have known that the whole time, and it is the only reason I could stand at this gate at all.

"So there is no protection in answering me, and I want that said out loud, because the six of you have written that on that sheet as though answering me were a wall, and it is not a wall. It is a thing that has been done once, and it can be done by anybody, and the next one does not need your permission."

He put the sheet in his pocket.

"Thank you for the answer," he said. "I am going to go home and I am going to put it in a drawer with the other three copies of it that I bought in March, and I am going to keep all four, and if anybody in this city ever tells me that there is nothing at that gate I am going to be able to produce four shilling's worth of paper and say that there are six women there who answer in writing and who are not frightened."

He went four doors up.

Beatrix Halm stood at the gate and watched him get to a door and open it and go in, and she knew which door it was.

It was on the same side of the street and it was four up from the gate and it had a brass number on it and a boot scraper outside it, and she stood there for about a minute with the knowledge of it, and then she went inside and shut the gate behind her.

"They have got four copies of us," she said, "and the man who came has told the room that there are eleven of him, and I have just watched him go into the fourth house up, and I am the only person in Ostara who now knows where that man sleeps, and he knows where we sleep, and neither of us can do a single thing about it."

Runa Sato said, "Then the six answers were not a wall."

"No," said Beatrix Halm. "They were the first time anybody has written anything down for a person who was not going to take it off us. That is all they were."

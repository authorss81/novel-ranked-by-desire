# Ferrier Street

It came on the morning after it was sent, which was earlier than anybody expected, because a document that goes into a bag on one day goes out on that day and is delivered the next morning, and not one thing in this city is arranged to arrive on a working day.

Ola Bright brought it up.

"There's a thing," she said, from the third step, "and I have not opened it and I want it on the record that I have not opened it, because there are four things a person in this house can do with a letter and one of them is to open it and one of them is not, and I am the one who chose the not one."

"Give it here," said Runa Sato, without turning round.

"You are not the post. I brought it up because I was on the landing." Ola held it out. "It came to the door and it is thin and it is not a bill, because a bill is thicker and it has a number in the top corner, and this has a number in the top corner."

It was on the table by four o'clock and the table was full, and sitting down had been decided by six people independently and without discussion, which was how the afternoons in that house worked.

Marguerite Vale read it out. She read it out because that was what she did and had always done, in Bellhouse and at the table and in the hall on the twenty-ninth, and she read it twice, and the second time she stopped at the fourth question and made Runa Sato repeat the words after her.

"How many persons of reading age keep an address at the above premises, and what is the band of each, and under whose standing are the premises held, and are the premises held under a plate." She put it down. "Four questions. That is all of it. And the fifth thing is the six of us, and not one of those four questions asks it."

The table went quiet for about ten seconds.

"It has to be answered by somebody at these premises," said Ines Duarte. She had read it twice in about four seconds, which was what she did. "That is in the instruction at the bottom. A person of reading age at the premises, and failing that any person having the care of the premises. It cannot be answered at a counter and it cannot be answered by him."

"Then that is settled," said Nadia Ferrous.

"That is the first half of it being settled." Ines put two fingers on the paper and did not move them. "The second half is that a return answered at these premises is a return about these premises, and the moment it is answered it is a household, and a household is read against the highest standing in it, and the Exchange is not obliged to publish which standing it read it against."

"That is in his favour."

"That is in his favour. And I want it entered that I noticed that first and said so, because I am going to spend the next two weeks being the person in this room who raised the objection and I would like the room to know that I raised it in his favour and then had to argue against it anyway." She looked up. "There is a better reason and it is worse."

"Go on."

"A member of this household with a disciplinary process open is entered on a return with the process attached." She said it flatly, as an auditor, and then let the room have it. "I have read the schedule. I am not guessing. It is the ordinary rule and it has been the ordinary rule for nine years and no clerk has ever been interested in applying it."

Runa Sato did not move at all.

"That is correct," she said.

"Runa—"

"It is correct and it has been correct since I read it out in a corridor last month for somebody else." She had her forearms on the table.

"My hearing is on the twelfth. It has been in a room with four people in it since February. If I stand on a composition return in this building on the morning of the thirteenth, then this building is a return with a Field matter on it, and any office in this city that wants to come through the door of this house from the fifteenth of this month can do it with a piece of paper about me.

"I am not saying do not answer it. I am saying that the second I put my name to that piece of paper this house stops being a place a paper cannot get into, and I am the reason it stops being one, and I would like the room to be clear that I know that and that I am going to be the reason anyway if you all decide to answer it, because I am not going to be the one who holds it up."

Beatrix Halm said, "There is a forty-one in it."

"There is not."

"There is." She had the visitors' book and she did not open it. "Forty-one people in that book asked about a person and seventeen asked about the household, and a good half of the forty-one were asking about a lift and not about a man.

"And if this building answers that form then this building is a household with a plate, and every one of those forty-one can walk up a hill and look at a tile and find out where we are. They cannot do that now. Not one of them can do that now."

"They already bought a shilling's worth," said Nadia Ferrous.

"One of them bought a shilling's worth. One. And he came to the gate and asked about a landing that is not in the transcript."

Beatrix put the book down.

"That is not a failure. That is one man out of a hundred and eleven, and I have been reading that book out loud every week for four months and have never once been frightened of it. I am frightened of it now.

"The difference is that in four months I have never sat in a room where six of us were frightened of the same thing at the same time."

Marguerite Vale put her hand flat on the paper.

"I am going to price it once and then I am going to stop, because the last three times I have priced something in this house the price came out negative and it came out negative because I have been pricing a thing that is not mine.

"Here is the price. A return that is answered can be asked for by any office that wants to serve a paper on this address. A return that is not answered is completed by an office out of an exhibit roll that describes us and does not name us."

She looked around the table and she was not joking.

"I have priced the right to be asked before you are touched, for a building, for a year. It comes to a very small number and I have never been able to make that number go up, because in eleven years of pricing things in this city not one buyer has ever come to the counter for it.

"That is the entire value of this piece of paper. It is the only thing any of you has been arguing about for four months, and we have been arguing about it from the wrong end.

"It is not protection. It is a chair at a counter where somebody has to say your name before they hand you anything. And right now, this house has the other chair, and the other chair is the one where they can do it to you without saying your name."

The oven ticked twice.

Tessa Okonkwo said, "I have already done it."

The table stopped.

"You have already done what," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I have written a chart. Last night. In a washroom on the sixth floor of that branch because it is the only room in this city with a door that shuts and a caretaker who comes at midnight. It has his name on it and mine and a weight and a date and two ribs." She did not raise her voice and did not put her hands flat on anything. "It is in the dispensary day book at the Vestry as of this morning and it is not going anywhere.

"And I said no to him on the third and a form arrived on the sixth, and I would like it said in this room that the form is what changed my mind and not him, and that without one I would have said no again."

"You put the Vestry in his file," said Runa Sato.

"I put a building that has never been heard of in a file that will be reachable by any office that asks a question in a corridor." Tessa Okonkwo looked at her. "Yes. And I did it for six people who come to a hatch in the morning who are not on a plate, and I will not be told in this house that I did it for him, because I did not."

Nadia Ferrous stood up, went to the window, and stood there with her back to the room for about half a minute, which she had never once done at that table.

"Then it is done," she said to the window. "It is done and it was done last night and we are having this argument about it on a morning, which is exactly what we have done for four months."

"There is one thing that has not been said," said Sun Meiling from the stairs.

They all waited. She had her coat on where she sat.

"There are six lines in the book on that table and there are seven women in this house and the return has room for every person of reading age who keeps an address here, and the question that has been going round this table for an hour is not whether we answer it. It is who answers it and who does not.

"And I have been sitting on this stair for an hour working out that I could answer it. That is what a return wants. A person of reading age at the premises. Any one of us. And if I answer it then the answer is complete, and it goes to a counter, and there is no blank in it anywhere, and every single argument this room has just made becomes a footnote.

"I am not going to do that. And I am not going to say why, because I have said why four times and I said it properly on the thirtieth of March on this landing and I am not doing it again in front of a form."

"So there is a gap," said Ines Duarte, quietly.

"There has been a gap in this house since the first of December," said Sun Meiling, "and the gap is me, and the difference today is that today the gap has a date on it, and it is the twenty-seventh."

She stood up on the stair.

"Somebody read that out again at me tomorrow and tell me a thing about my own country I have not been told, and then leave it alone for a bit. I have had a whole day of it."

The stair door shut, not hard.

Marguerite Vale turned the form round so the blank was at the top of the table and put a pencil beside it.

"Not one of us is filling that in tonight," she said. "And it is not going to be filled in on the twelfth, after Runa's hearing, and it is not going to be filled in by him.

"And if four of us go at it separately then the Exchange gets four returns for one address, and a clerk has to reconcile them, and a clerk reconciling four returns for one address is going to ask a question.

"One return. Six hands. And every one of us writes her own part and hands it in herself, and he does not see it and he does not sign it and he is not in the room when it is done." She looked at the ceiling. "It is going to cost all of us something and he is going to find out what on the fourteenth, and I am quite sure he will find out what and be insufferable about it."

Marguerite left at about half past six. So did Beatrix.

The table did not move for about four minutes.

Then Runa Sato stood up, went to the door and got the visitors' book off the shelf, and put it in the middle of the table on top of the form.

"He says he is not going to be in the room," she said. "Fine. Then the first thing the room does without him is this.

"Forty-one people have asked after somebody in this house and seventeen have asked after the house, and we are going to read the book out from the beginning at the start of the week, all of it, every week, for as long as it takes.

"And we are going to answer the ones we can answer and say so in the book that we cannot, in our own hands.

"And then the answer to that clerk in Bellhouse is going to be a house that says yes out loud, with names on it, and not a house that can be found."

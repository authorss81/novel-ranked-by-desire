# Marguerite Vale

The card by the time clock had said the seventeenth since the first of this month, and the seventeenth was the third Thursday printed on it, and the men paint the Board at nine on a Thursday because that is the whole of their working week.

They came at nine. The pale rectangle was three weeks bare and was still bare, and not one person in the square said anything about it, because the white-bearded man had stopped saying anything about it, and he took a bucket off a man he had never spoken to and went up his ladder.

The Exchange shut at six. The sales floor emptied from the rail backwards, a desk at a time, until there were three clerks left on it who clean, and then two, and then at about twenty past six the last of them went down the stair that smells of oil and shut the stair door behind him.

The one window at the far side had its shutters up. It had been up all day and it was up now, and the light had gone off the boards and there was a lamp on the rail that a clerk had left burning, so the room was one lamp and one bar of the last of the day coming through the shutters.

Marguerite Vale was standing at the window with her gloves off.

"Sit down," she said.

There were forty desks and about six chairs that belonged to desks, and she had cleared one of the chairs with the flat of her hand and put a ledger on the desk in front of it so that anybody coming up the stair in ten minutes would see a person at a desk and not a person at a window.

"You have about forty minutes," she said. "The building empties at half past and I have a key for the stair and I have not used it for anything in nine years and I am not going to start tonight. So we have the floor until they lock the front and then we have four minutes in the dark and I am not going to be doing this in the dark, so forty minutes."

He sat.

"First, the terms, and I am going to say them out loud and I am not going to soften them, because you were at that table on the seventh of this month when I put the whole of this down in front of the certificate in about ninety seconds, and I am not going to have either of us pretend that I told you for the first time tonight.

"I told you at that table. I said the highest standing in this household is mine, and that a certificate at the end of fifty days binds six women to a guild broker's standing, and that it puts the Vant in a position it has not been in for nineteen years.

"I said that none of you had asked me for it, and I said it at ten past one in the afternoon with the certificate in front of us, and every one of you heard me and not one of you asked.

"So I have been waiting ten days for one of you to make me say it out loud as a thing I was offering, and none of you have, and I am going to stop waiting, because I have decided to buy something tonight with my own name and a name is not a thing I buy by myself."

"What are you buying."

"A permission." She said it the way she said a price. "That is the Exchange's own word and it is the correct one and I have not softened it and I am not going to. A permission is a question you are allowed to ask. And the question I am buying is not a door, it is not an hour of a machine, and it is not a hearing moved up.

"I am buying the examination."

"The examination of the household."

"The re-examination," she said.

"Every standing household is examined every year, and the examination is a document, and the document has to be signed by somebody with standing. Where the head's standing is a broker's, the broker's name goes on it, and the broker is named on it, and the broker is consulted.

"That is what I said on the seventh and that is what I am buying tonight, and I am paying for it out of my own entitlement, which is the only thing in this city that can be spent, and I am spending it in a room I have been coming to for nineteen years."

She put one hand flat on the ledger.

"And here is the price, and it is not money, and I am going to say all of it before I take my coat off, because I do not do this after.

"The moment that examination is bought and entered, this household is bound to my standing for as long as it stands. It is a rank-two broker's standing. It is the Vant's standing, because a broker's standing is the guild's and the guild's book is the Vant's book, and the Vant has been trying for thirty years to own the gap between what a thing is worth and what this city says it is worth.

"They cannot get a house. A house is not a thing you buy, it is a thing you belong to. They cannot get a man. A man is a person and a person is not property in this city and the whole of the Exchange is built on that.

"But a household with a rank-two broker's standing on its face, that they did not have to do anything for, that they came into because a woman in it signed a piece of paper for them at a kitchen table when not one person asked her to, that is a thing they have never had. And the gap between what that is worth and what this city will call it is about four times what a man is worth.

"That is the price. Not to me. To the Vant. And I am the Vant's senior broker and it is my book and it is my guild and I am handing it a house for nothing and I have been waiting nineteen years to hand it one for nothing."

She let that sit.

"And you are going to ask me why I would hand my own guild a thing that I cannot take back. And I am going to answer that once, and it is the only true thing I have got, and then we are going to stop talking about the Vant for the rest of the evening, because the Vant is the only party to this that is not in the room."

She looked at him.

"I am not doing it for the Vant," she said. "I am doing it because I have been thirty years inside a guild that will replace me the moment a woman called Solveig Rask decides to, and I am looking for the one thing in Ostara that a court cannot seize.

"A house is not seizable. That is why I am buying it. The examination is what anchors it.

"The moment the Vant's book has a standing household in it and the examination is theirs, then there is a broker in this city who is answerable for six women, and when that broker stops being me, there is a woman called Rask who is answerable for six women, and there is no court in this city that will unwind it, because it was bought lawfully and signed lawfully and a head of household signed for it.

"So I am not giving the Vant a house. I am making the Vant hold a house. And if I am its broker for four years instead of nineteen, then in four years I am still the broker of it and I am not a woman who can be replaced."

The lamp on the rail hummed and the light through the shutters had gone the colour of the street outside.

"That is why," she said. "And it is the worst thing I have ever done and I have done a great many, and I have not hesitated, and I would like you to understand that I have not hesitated and that I am not going to pretend to have thought about it longer."

"Do the others know you have decided."

"No," said Marguerite Vale. "And they are not going to be asked, because if they are asked then they will say no, and if they say no then it does not go through, and if it does not go through then I have not bought anything.

"Sun Meiling has already said no. I heard her say it on the fifteenth at that table, in the middle of a room, and she was right, and she said it about my name and not about herself, and I have thought about very little else since."

He stood up.

"Do not," she said. "Sit down. You have thirty-one minutes and I have not started and I am not going to be hurried by a man who has been thinking about this for four months when I have been thinking about it since March."

"Sun said no," he said.

"Sun said no to me being on the schedule, and she said it in a room and she said it about me and not about her, and she was right, and it does not matter." She took her gloves off and put them on the desk and squared them. "I have been very clear about what I am and I am going to be clear about it again because you are the only person in that house I would take the trouble with.

"I am not signing the composition return. I am not signing anybody's name on anything. I am not filling in a form and I am not answering a question about anybody.

"What I am doing is that I am going to bring a standing to a room that I have every right to bring a standing to, and it is going to be on the household, and the household is going to be mine in every way a guild is a thing, and I am going to do it and be answerable for it and I am not going to pretend that answerability is a gift.

"You, on the other hand, are going to sign an indemnity on the nineteenth in a room where a clerk writes down what is in front of the clerk. That is yours. I am not signing it for you and I have not asked to, and I want that on the record in this room, in case either of us is asked in nine years."

"You are countersigning it."

"I am countersigning it," she said. "Because the head's assumption is not enough. The household is bound to the highest standing in it, and for the household to be bound to me there has to be an instrument with my name on it, and I will put my name on it at the bottom of yours on the nineteenth, and I will keep a copy."

"That is not the same as filling in a form."

"It is exactly the same as filling in a form," Marguerite Vale said. "It is a form, and I will fill in my part of it, and I will do it in my own hand, and I will take a copy away from that building in my own bag, and that copy is mine and it is the only thing I am taking out of this arrangement and the only thing that will still be mine in four years."

She looked at the window.

"And I will keep the copy," she said, "and I am going to keep it in the same drawer as a set of terms I declared off a table on the fourteenth of March, which are down, and unsigned, and out of force, and which I am not bringing back tonight, and which a standing household cannot revive, and which I am not going to mention again."

He looked at her.

"You declared terms off," he said.

"On the fourteenth of March," she said. "At a table, in a room, to about six people. They were my terms and they were the price of a chair and I took them off the table and I have never put them back and I am not putting them back tonight and no certificate in June brings them back. They are down. They stay down."

"Then what is this," he said, "if it is not the terms."

"It is not the terms," she said. "The terms were what I wanted in exchange for letting a man into a room. This is what I am paying for, with my own standing, for six women who did not ask me and a man who is going to sign his name to them in two days."

The bar of light through the shutters had gone off the boards and the lamp on the rail was the whole of the room.

"Twenty-six minutes," she said. "Now we are going to do this properly, and I am going to say the whole of what happens tonight and then we are going to stop talking and do the other part, because I have been standing at a window in a cold room talking for nineteen minutes and I did not put my coat on to talk."

She took her coat off and put it over the back of the desk chair, over the ledger.

"I am awake and I am not drunk and I am not tired and nothing is spent on either of us tonight. Not a grain, not a cold minute, not anything out of anybody in this quarter. If you put your hand on anything of mine and it is not mine I will stop, and I will not have to stop, because you will not, because you have spent four months not."

She came round the desk and stood in front of him in the aisle between the desk and the rail.

"And I am going to do most of this myself," she said, "because I am the one who can, and because you have had five other women look after you in four months and I am the last thing this city is going to put in front of you and I am going to do it my way and I am going to be accurate about it the whole time."

She unbuttoned her coat and took it off the other arm and dropped it on the ledger.

"It is that aisle," she said. "Between the rail and the desk. It is about two feet wide and there is a rail at your back if you want it and a window at your left and I have chosen this floor and this aisle and I have chosen it because a woman who has been in a room for nineteen years knows which way the door is from any point in it."

She put the ledger flat on the desk and turned it face down, and squared it, which is the thing a woman does at a desk she intends to come back to.

"Do not stand up out of the chair yet," she said. "Sit in it. I have watched you stand up when a woman tells you what to do in about four rooms in four months, and it has cost you every one of them.

"You are going to sit in that chair with your back to the window and take your shirt off over your head and put it on the chair, and if you fold it, I will price the folding.

"And when you have done that you are going to look at me and tell me you want me, in those words, twice, and I am going to tell you to say it a third time, and the third time is not for me."

He took his shirt off.

"Once more."

"I want you."

"Twice."

"I want you here."

"Twice more."

"I want you now."

"Good."

She came out from the desk and turned the chair with two fingers on its back so that it faced the window, and then she stood in the two feet of aisle and let him see the whole of what she had decided, unhurried, at a distance, the way a woman shows a buyer a thing she has already valued.

"That is not a formality and I am not going to explain it, and you are going to be glad of it at about the nine-minute mark. I am telling you the number because I have never lied about one to a man and I am not starting at forty-one in a room I have paid for."

She did not hurry and she did not make a production of any part of it, and everything she did she had clearly already costed, and about a third of the way through she stopped and stood absolutely still with one hand flat on his chest and looked at the shutter and not at him and said, quietly:

"That is the one I have been waiting for. Do not tell me what it is."

He did not tell her.

Afterwards she sat sideways on the edge of the desk with one foot on the floor and her hands in her lap, and did not touch him for a while, and when she finally did it was with her mouth and not with her hand, and it went on a good deal longer than the rest of it.

She stopped before he did and sat back and looked at the shutter and said, "There. That is the whole of it, and that is the half I am keeping, and you may have the rest of it whenever you want it and I would like you to want it, because a thing that is handed back is worth half of a thing that is taken."

He said her name once, when she had stopped and was sitting there with her hands in her lap.

"Once," she said. "That is your allowance and it is the correct one. A name in this city is a commitment made before the evidence, and you are about to be the head of a household, and the head's name is going on a certificate. I have priced one tonight and I have not charged you for it, and the figure is in a book in a drawer and not in a room."

"What did you price."

"Your name," said Marguerite Vale. "Third person, as an item in an account, at a figure I am not going to say to you. If I say the figure to you then the figure is yours, and the figure is not yours.

"And I told you on the seventh that I was not going to be thanked for this and I have not been, and I am not going to be talked out of it tonight either, and the price is not free and it is not me who pays it, and I have known that since I was thirty-one."

She came back down onto him once more and finished it, and then she got her own back before he had finished his, which is not a thing she had told him she was going to do.

Then she lay still on the boards of the sales floor of the Exchange with her cheek on the floor and one hand on his chest and neither of them said anything for about four minutes, and the lamp on the rail hummed, and the bar of light through the shutters came back up the boards about an inch as the last of the day went off the street outside.

"I am not going to be thanked for tonight," she said. "Not because I did it for you. Because I did it for a broker's standing, and a thing done for a broker's standing is not a favour, it is an outlay, and outlays go in a book.

"I have taken it out of my own book, and the figure is in it, and there is a line against my name in the real ledger with a date on it, and it says HOUSEHOLD, FERRIER STREET, and it is the first line in that book in nine years that I have entered against myself."

She got up and got her coat.

She got the ledger off the desk chair and opened it and did not take the pen off its chain, and she wrote on the page with the chain still on, which is what a clerk does and what she used to be, nineteen years ago.

She wrote one line. Then she tore the page out along the perforation and folded it in four and put it in her inside pocket and buttoned it.

She did not read it out and he did not ask.

She buttoned her coat.

"Now the last of it," she said, "and then we go down the stair, and I am going to leave the lamp on the rail because I have decided that is the correct thing to do and I have been leaving it off for eleven years and it has occurred to me tonight that eleven years of leaving a lamp off in a room you have stood in is a habit and not a principle."

She turned round.

"On the nineteenth you are going to sign an indemnity in a room where a clerk writes down what is in front of the clerk, and I am going to countersign it under your name and take a copy, and that copy is in this pocket, and it is the only thing in this city that is going to survive me in an arguable shape.

"And when that certificate issues on the twenty-seventh of June, this household is going to be worth a rank-two broker's standing, and the examination is going to be the Vant's examination, and the Vant's book is going to have a house in it that they did not have to do anything for, and that is the thing I have been buying for four months and I did not know until this month that I could pay for it with my own name instead of with one of theirs."

She picked up her gloves and did not put them on.

"Go down the stair before me," she said. "And do not take the lift, and do not thank me, and on the nineteenth do not tell them I made a speech. I did not make a speech. I priced a thing and I bought it and I have a copy in my pocket and I have wanted that drawer for thirty years."

Twenty of the sixty were gone. Forty of them were left. Two days to the nineteenth.

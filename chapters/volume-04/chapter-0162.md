# The Muster Room

The hearing was on a Thursday because the reading was, and the reading was on a Thursday because the Board repaints at nine, and every instrument in this city that runs on people runs on the morning a number goes up on a wall in the open air.

He was on the stair of the Field division at Cordwain by a quarter to ten with his coat on, and there was a bench in the corridor outside the fourth-floor room that was not comfortable and a lamp over it on a fuse that had been out since the summer.

He put a candle on the ledge by the door at about ten to ten. He had brought it from the branch and wrapped it in a cloth, because a candle in a Bureau corridor is a thing a clerk asks about.

At half past nine a woman came along the corridor with a book under her arm and stopped in front of him.

"Name."

"Lin Hao."

She wrote it down. "Address."

"Ferrier Street."

She wrote that too, and then she asked what his trade was, and then she asked how long he had known the member, and he said three years and she wrote that, and then she asked him what his business was in that building and he said he cleaned washrooms at the Fourth Branch and she looked up for the first time.

"Then you are not here on the member's account," she said. "You are here because somebody put a note in the book. Sit down."

She went along the corridor and put her hand on the door of the fourth-floor room and went in, and the door shut, and there was a sound through it of about four people standing up and sitting down again.

The corridor had four doors on it. One of them was open, and it was the muster room, and it was the only room on that landing with anything in it except furniture.

The roster was on the wall of the muster room, end to end, in a frame, and it had been painted rather than printed, which was why it had been done for years by somebody who was not a clerk. It was ruled into four columns. NAME. POST. IN THE DIVISION SINCE. And the fourth column had no heading at all, which was the ordinary way, because a column only gets a heading when somebody thinks it is going to be used.

Over the roster, at the top of the wall, in a smaller hand and a different paint, there was a sentence that had been there since before Runa Sato had come to Cordwain.

A MEMBER WHO REFUSES TO ANSWER A COUNT IS ENTERED AS REFUSING. A HOUSEHOLD WITH A MEMBER WHO HAS REFUSED IS A HOUSEHOLD WITH AN UNANSWERED COUNT IN IT.

He read it four times in an hour and a half.

There was a duty clerk at a desk in the muster room with a card index and a rack of small handbells, and at about eleven he went in there for a cup of water and the clerk looked at him and then went on with his card.

At about eleven twenty a man went along the corridor and into the fourth-floor room and the door shut. At about half past eleven it opened and he came out and went down the stair without looking at the bench.

At noon the corridor was quiet for about twenty minutes and then a woman came out and went down, and she had been crying, and the door shut again.

At about twenty past one the door opened and Runa Sato came out into the corridor and stopped, and he stood up off the bench.

She looked at the door. Then she looked down the corridor at the stair, and then at the door again.

"They have twenty minutes," she said. "There is an item left and it is not about me."

"That is not allowed."

"It is not allowed because I would be able to object to it and an objection from the member reopens the proceeding, and they have four hours to spend and they do not have another four." She put her back against the wall beside the door. "I am going to sit here on this floor until it is finished and then I am going to go down to the muster room, because that is where they keep the paper."

She sat on the floor with her back against the wall, which was the posture of a woman who has been in a lot of rooms.

At about twenty to two the clerk came out again and looked down the corridor.

"Lin Hao."

He went.

The room was eleven feet by nine and it had a table at the far end and four chairs on the near side of it and two clerks, one of whom was the woman from the corridor and one of whom was a man who had not come out at half past eleven. There was no window. There was a lamp on the table and a second lamp on a bracket and the bracket lamp was the one that worked.

They had his file. It was open on the table at the second chair and he could see the top sheet from the door and it was the description, his own four sentences, typewritten, with his own signature at the bottom of it.

"Sit down."

He sat down.

"You are the person this item concerns," said the man. "You are not here to be examined. You are here because the member has declined to give a number and the number is one you would be the only person in Ostara able to supply."

"I do not know the number."

"That is not the answer I need and it is not an untrue answer." The man turned the file round and put a sheet beside it. "Between ten and two on the twenty-ninth of last month, in the Cobb Street hall, in the open, in front of two hundred and eleven people. How many times did something move out of a person and into you."

The room did not make a noise.

He looked at the sheet. It was a ruled form and it had one box on it and the box was about two inches square and the word over the box was AMOUNT.

"I said on the record, before the instrument was switched on, that it moves on me and that I was going to do it three times, and that no person at that rail was to be reached for."

"You said three times."

"I said I was going to do it three times."

"That is a number." The man's pen was not off the desk. "The member was asked for a number on the ninth and gave none. The member is not in the room because she would be able to object to you and an objection from the member reopens the proceeding. You are the last person in Ostara who can supply this and I am telling you that the room is aware of what I am asking."

He did not look at the table.

He thought about a woman on a floor with her back against a wall eight feet and a door away, who had asked him at half past five the previous evening to come out of that room and walk out of the building with a lie.

"Three," he said.

The man's pen did not move for about two seconds, and then it did, and the number went into the box, and then the man read the box back to the clerk and the clerk read the entry out in the flat voice that is the only voice anybody in this city has ever used for the sentence that finishes a person.

"Amount supplied by a third party at the item. Member declined. Count incomplete."

"I supplied a number."

"You supplied a number that is not the number that was asked for, and I am going to tell you why in one sentence, because it is the only sentence in this room that is about you and you are entitled to it."

He put the pen down. "A number answered by a person other than the member is a number with a second name on it. The Office cannot use it, and it cannot go on a record of the member's own conduct.

"So the count stands as refused. The refusal is entered against the member, and it is not entered against you."

He stood up.

"That is the whole of it. Thank you for coming. You will not be asked again on this matter and you are not to attend the next sitting, if there is one, and if anybody tells you that a person may attend a sitting, they are telling you about a public hearing, and this is not one."

He got out into the corridor and Runa Sato was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall where he had left her, and she had not moved, and she got up when she saw him.

The clerk at the muster room desk had a sheet out of the rack and was copying it onto the roster frame's margin sheet, in a column.

She came down the corridor. "Did they ask you anything."

He had about two seconds, exactly as many as she had said there would be on the previous evening.

"No," he said.

She looked at him for a moment longer than the answer needed.

"Good," she said.

Then she went down the stair to the muster room, and the four of them went down after her, and he was last, and in the muster room the roster was on the wall with the frame open and her name in it, in the second column, R. SATO, POST BRUTE-FORCE, IN THE DIVISION SINCE — and in the fourth column, in the duty clerk's flat hand, on a line she had ruled that afternoon, one word.

REFUSING.

The duty clerk did not look up from the sheet. He had ruled a line because there had not been one.

"That is not a punishment," he said. "That is a schedule."

"I know what it is. She quoted it to me last night in about eleven words and she was talking about a man in Kestrel Hill."

"She quoted it and she came in here anyway." He blotted the sheet. "They do that. It is the ordinary thing. About two in this division in nine years and one of them is on the fourth floor of a building in the Slough with an unfiled clinic in it, so do not go telling anybody in this building that you heard the rule from a woman."

He put the sheet into the rack.

"Now. I am going to tell you what the fourth column does, because you have been looking at that wall for four hours and you will go and ask somebody, and if you ask somebody in this building you will get the half of it.

"A member entered as refusing is still carried on the strength. A member entered as refusing is still on this roster and is still paid and is still in the division, and on Thursday morning when this office reads the count out, that name is read out with the rest of them.

"But the premises that member keeps are not in a position to be returned to. A household with a member who has refused is a household with an unanswered count in it, and a count that is unanswered cannot be reconciled by an office, and an office that cannot reconcile a count has exactly one lawful way left to find out how many people are in a building."

The clerk squared the rack.

"It has to go and count. And it will send somebody, and the somebody will be a person of reading age and will not need a warrant and will not need a reason, and the reason is on the wall behind you and you do not have to know what it is."

The room was very quiet.

"What kind of somebody," said Runa Sato.

"An assessor." He closed the drawer. "They are not Field. They are not a division. They come once a fortnight from somewhere with a different letterhead and they ask how many people and they write down what they are told and they go out again, and if the number they are told is the number on the paper, they leave, and if it is not the number on the paper then the paper has been answered badly and somebody is asked why."

He looked at her.

"You wrote a standing into the fourth box of a return on the eighth of this month," he said. "I can see the form on the rail in the corridor outside and it has your hand on it. Whatever you wrote there is not this division's standing this morning. That is not an opinion. That is a line on a form that is now wrong, and the Office finds wrong lines by arithmetic."

Runa Sato did not move.

"How long have you had the form on the rail."

"Since the ninth. It goes out on the eighth for anything at these premises and it comes back when somebody answers it."

"Then four women have answered it," she said.

"Four hands have been on it."

"Four women have answered it, and I wrote the standing, and the standing was the only part of it that was a lie." She said it without any weight at all, which was how she said the things that mattered. "Well. That is the cost of it and I would have paid it in March if anybody had told me the wall in this room was going to be what it was."

She turned round and looked at the frame, at her own name, at the fourth column.

"Tell me what you said to them."

"I told you. They did not ask me anything."

"That is not the question I asked and you know it is not."

He looked at the word in the fourth column, which was in a duty clerk's flat hand and was two days old in a rule that had been on that wall for eleven years.

"Runa," he said.

She waited.

"You told me on the stair last night that you would rather walk out of that building with a lie than with a man who had spent four hours on a bench thinking about how to be honest with you."

He put his hands in his pockets because there was nothing else to do with them. "You were right and I have done it, and I would do it again tomorrow.

"And I am telling you that I have done it. You are owed the one fact about me that I actually know, and it is that I sat on a bench for four hours and then lied to you in a corridor, and you said you would rather have that."

The muster room was quiet in the way a room is quiet when four people have all decided at once not to say anything.

"Right," said Runa Sato.

She went up the stair and he let her get two floors ahead before he followed her out of the building, and on the second landing he stopped and looked back down at the ground floor, where a man in the duty clerk's coat was taking the roster frame off the wall.

He had taken the four column rule off first. It was on the wall in a different paint and it had been the only part of that wall anybody had ever read.

The frame came down, and the wall behind it was a different white, and there was a pale rectangle in it the size of the sentence.

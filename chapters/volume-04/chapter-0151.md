# The Repaint Sheet

The Fourth Branch does not open on the last day of the week. The cleaners come in at seven instead of five, and on the first of this month he had the building to himself from seven until about eleven, six floors and a stairwell and the hot-water pipes going in the wall.

The card by the time clock on the ground floor lists the days the Board repaints. It has listed them since he started.

APRIL. FIFTH. TWELFTH. NINETEENTH. TWENTY-SIXTH. AND THE THIRD OF NEXT MONTH.

He read it standing up the way he reads it every month, because the men who paint the wall have to know which days they are coming and the card is the only thing in the building that says so. The twenty-sixth and then seven days is the thirty-third, which is not a day there is.

He went up at about half past seven with the bucket and did the second floor, and the third, and then the fourth, which he had cleaned every working morning for four years until the standing on that floor went and the floor stayed.

The room off the landing was shut. It had been the machine room in his first year, and then it was a corridor, and then somebody in an office two floors up worked out what it could be used for and it became a desk with a bench against the far wall and a lamp that had a chain on it.

There was a cloth over the machine.

Not a cover. A cloth — the grey heavy bench cloth the instrument men put over a socket when the building's own power is off, which it was, because it was the last day of the week and the switches on that floor go off at noon on the last working day of the week, and the key that puts them back before the week does not exist anywhere in this building.

The nameplate was gone.

That was the part he stopped on. There is a rack on the wall inside the door, eight slots, and every plate in it has been the same height for as long as he has worked here, and seven of them had a name on them, and the eighth was empty, and an empty slot in a rack of plates is not the same as a man who is late.

He stood in the doorway with the mop in his hand for longer than the job needed.

He had heard the man's voice twice. Once in a room four feet by nine off this floor, at a quarter to eleven in the morning on the twenty-seventh of March, when the tool's own specification was read out to him and a clerk wrote it down and the man said the clause number twice to be sure of it.

And once in a hall on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth, standing up behind a bench with nothing in front of him, saying that an instrument which cannot produce what its proponent says it produces should not go on a table, which was true, and which had cost the man the only thing he had said.

And on the afternoon of the twenty-ninth, at the counter on the ground floor, a clerk had struck his paragraph through with one rule and entered a different one underneath it and signed underneath that.

The office did not publish any of it. The sheet on the rail had said a hearing was held and had said the account was described by the member, and both of those lines were about him.

He went down and finished the fifth and the sixth and washed his hands and thought about a nameplate.

Then he walked up to the Reading, because it was ten past nine and there was nothing on the card for today and the wall does not care.

The sheet was still on the rail.

That was not unusual. The repaint sheet goes up on the Thursday morning with whatever the week's reading has to say in it, and it stays up until the next one replaces it, and in the ordinary way that is five days and on the last day of a month it is whatever is left of it.

The twenty-ninth's sheet had been up since before four o'clock in the afternoon of the twenty-ninth, which meant three days, most of them spent unread by anybody at all, and it would come down on the fifth along with the men on the ladder.

He read it because he had read it on the morning it went up and it had not changed.

THURSDAY. A PUBLIC HEARING WAS HELD AT COBB STREET FROM TEN UNTIL TWO. TWO HUNDRED AND ELEVEN PRESENT.

THURSDAY. THE MEMBER'S ACCOUNT IS NOT UNDESCRIBED. IT IS DESCRIBED BY THE MEMBER. NO BAND HAS CHANGED.

Then he walked up the wall, which he did four times a month and had done about two hundred times in four years, because a man who works under a building for four years looks at the wall that tells the city what it thinks of him roughly once a fortnight whether he means to or not.

The rectangle where his name had been since the fourth of March was a stranger's name, and the stranger was not the same stranger it had been on the twenty-second.

IVO SARN. BAND SEVEN.

He stood under it for a while.

Band seven. Requisition of merit from a person, the sealed archive, fifty-five thousand grains a week, which is a man who can take a person's standing away and whose own standing is therefore not a thing anybody prints about in the open air more than once.

And the tile had gone up on the twenty-second of March, because the Board repaints on Thursdays and the twenty-ninth was the Thursday after that.

Seven days. Then three more.

He did that subtraction twice, standing in the open air with his hands at his sides, because the first time he had it and did not trust it.

Seven days from a tile going up to a paragraph going through a rule. Three more from the rule to a cloth over a machine and an empty slot in a rack. Ten days from one to the other, and for the whole of the first seven of them a man was walking into a building every morning and his name was on a wall.

The men painted the tile on the twenty-second. On the twenty-ninth, at a quarter past five in the afternoon, at a counter on the ground floor of the building he cleaned, a clerk struck the man's paragraph out of a book and wrote another one under it and signed it. On the first of this month there was a grey bench cloth over the machine and an empty slot in a rack.

Seven days in which the city had a band-seven officer's name on a wall and the Office printed nothing, and seven days in which the man was in a building every day.

Not one person in Ostara would ever connect the two facts. There is one board in this city and it publishes bands, and the sheet on the rail underneath it publishes plates, and a transfer inside the Certification Wing is not a plate and not a band and has nowhere to go.

Somebody in that building had known, on the twenty-ninth, that striking a line out of a book moves a man, and had cost herself something she had not been told about, and had been right, and had not been able to say so to anybody at all, including the man.

He went down the wall and stood in the street.

The thing he could not get past was not the transfer. Transfers happen. The thing he could not get past was that it had been on the wall for ten days, and that a man who has spent four years making sure that not one page in this city can say where he slept had worked that out in about eleven seconds by looking up, and that the entire skill had been transferable in the other direction and always had been.

A name on a tile is not a person. It is a public statement that there is a person, and it is the only thing this city publishes that everybody reads, and once a name is on it the name does not come down because anything happened to the man. It comes down because the band changed.

He stood in the street for about four minutes with that.

Then he went to the stair and the plate reader and looked at the board on the far wall, where the bands are printed on a strip every week for anybody who wants the number and not the tile, and he found the row he wanted, and it was there: a band seven, an office, a floor.

And he thought, in the ordinary way, about the seven women in the house in Ferrier Street, and about the fact that the machine that put that name on that wall would find every one of them inside nine minutes if anybody in this building put a hand on the right tray, and about the fact that four years of his life had gone into making sure that no hand in this building ever went near the right tray.

They had all refused to be written down. That was the whole of what was keeping them off paper, and it was not modesty and it was not caution, and it had cost every one of them something real, and he had let it stand there for four months as though it were a defence.

It was not a defence. It was a delay.

On the fifth of this month that sheet comes down. On the fifth of this month the city is told about plates and bands and hears nothing else, and in three days after that somebody in an office that does not know anything about a household above a chop-house in Ferrier Street will read a paragraph and think about a form.

And there was a person in Ostara who could put a name onto a piece of paper without it becoming a finding against the person named.

She had been refusing to do it for two years and she was the best diagnostician in the Vestry and she had never once written him down in anything.

He got back to the branch at about ten and put the bucket away and stood in the ground-floor corridor for a minute with his hands still wet.

On the night of the thirty-first a woman in that house had taken a pen out of a drawer she had put it in on purpose, on the sixteenth, in front of six women, and had written one line under the sixth line of a day in a book that has six lines a day and one woman to a line and a seventh space that has never been used.

It was not a signature. It was a sentence. It was the only line in that book that was not a woman's day, and it was his, and it could not be signed and could not be taken back.

He had spent four months not knowing what to do with it.

He was going to ask somebody to be written down.

# His Own Indemnity

He went in at the front door at twenty past nine on the morning of the nineteenth, in daylight, in his ordinary clothes, with the coat on, because it was the same door he had gone in at on the fourth of this month and he wanted it to be the same walk.

The counter on the second floor was the same counter and Doyle was behind it and there was a second chair on the wrong side of the table, which there had not been on the fourth.

"There it is," said Doyle. "Sit down. It takes about nine minutes and it is not the long one and you are not going to like it."

It was one side of a sheet, cream weight, a little heavier than office stock. At the top, in the Office's own type, ASSUMPTION OF MEMBERS. Under that, a ruled block with four spaces: a head, a date, an hour. Under that, six lines, numbered one to six, ruled, for a clerk.

He looked at it for a while.

"It is very plain," he said.

"They are all very plain." Doyle put the tray down and clipped the four pages of the schedule in beside it and squared the pair of them. "The plain ones are the ones that do the work. Come and look at the schedule, because I am going to read it against your sheet as you sign it and I am not going to read it against nothing."

The four pages went round to his side of the table.

Page one: the premises, six persons of reading age keeping an address there, one person not on the roll, permitted answer, first person in four years to use it.

Page one: the head, a name in his own hand, and a plate number, and the figure. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. He did not look at it for long, because he had written it into a legal document seven days ago in this room and had been quoted back to himself about nine hundred times since.

Page two: five names, numbered, with a standing and a band and the date each began to keep an address at the premises.

Page two, at the bottom, a sixth line, in a different hand, dated the sixth of this month: ABSENT WITH REASON. Four hundred words in Sun Meiling's hand.

Page three: the premises.

Page four: the signature, the date and the hour.

"Now."

Doyle turned the sheet round so it was the right way up for the man and the wrong way up for himself, which is a thing a clerk does and which makes a man lean in.

"This is the whole instrument and I am going to read the whole of it, and then I am going to ask you four things, and you are going to answer all four out loud. A head who signs this in silence is a head who has not read it, and a head who has not read it is a head whose signature is worth nothing to six women and is worth a great deal to somebody else."

He waited.

"I have wanted this since the first of April," he said.

"I am aware of it," said Doyle, "and it is the correct sentiment and it is going to cost you, and I am going to let you have it for about as long as it takes me to put my finger on this line, and then we are going to read it."

He put his finger on the line under the heading.

THE HEAD OF A HOUSEHOLD ANSWERS IN HIS OWN NAME FOR EVERY MEMBER OF IT.

"That is the indemnity," said Doyle. "It is printed. It is not written and it is not mine and it predates this building by about two hundred years, and everything else on this sheet is the office being able to prove that you read it.

"I am going to ask you four questions and the answers go down in the tray with your name on the stub.

"One. Do you read that sentence.

"Two. Does that sentence describe what you are about to do.

"Three. Name the six.

"Four. Is it your hand."

"Four questions and six names," said Doyle, "and the six names are not a formality, because the six lines under the heading are numbered and the clerk fills them in, and I fill them in.

"I write who was in the room when it was signed and I write the hour, and in about nine years there will be a woman in an office like this one who will read that a head stood in a room at a quarter to ten in the morning and named six women, and she will not know one thing about any of you, and that is the correct outcome and I am not going to pretend to you that it is anything else."

He turned the sheet round again and put it in front of him.

"Your name," he said. "In your own hand. Not a mark. Not a cross. Your name, at the top of it, where the head goes, and then the date and the hour, and I will fill in the six lines while you are still in the chair."

He picked up the pen and did not write with it. He held it, the way a man holds a pen when the pen is not his.

"Lin Hao," he said, out loud, in a room on the second floor of the Reading, in daylight, in front of a clerk.

He wrote it. It is a small hand and it is even and it did not shake, which he noticed and was pleased about, and which cost him something afterward that he could not identify for about a week.

He wrote the date. He wrote the hour: twenty-five past nine in the morning.

"Six lines," said Doyle.

"Ines Duarte."

"Two."

"Nadia Ferrous."

"Three."

"Tessa Okonkwo."

"Four."

"Runa Sato."

"Five."

"Marguerite Vale."

"Six."

"Sun Meiling, entered as absent with reason on the sixth of this month, in four hundred words, in her own hand, and she is not in this room because she sent him out of the room and I was not going to say her name and sign it for her in front of a clerk."

Doyle wrote the six lines down and the sixth line he wrote in a different way and read it back.

"The sixth member is accounted for and her name is on the schedule of the fourth of this month in a document she gave at a counter on the sixth of this month, and she was not in this room and has not been asked to be."

He initialled it.

"That is in my hand as well as hers and that is my decision and not the office's, and I have been doing this for thirty years, and it is the correct outcome and the only protection this office offers anybody, and it is offered by accident."

He filled in the four spaces under the heading. He wrote the head's name, the date, and the hour, and he wrote his own initials against the block, and then he did something he had not done on the fourth, which was to put his own name at the foot.

The room was very quiet for about four seconds.

"Now you know what you have signed," said Doyle, "and I am going to say it out loud because a head who does not hear it said back to him by a clerk has not had it read to him, and then I am going to put the sheet in the tray and you are going to leave this room.

"You have made yourself the easiest person in Ostara to find. Every form, notice and paper in this city that concerns those premises now has a name at the top of it that leads to a man who cleans six floors of washrooms at the Fourth Branch, and you have been the least findable man in this city for four years and you have made yourself the most findable one in a piece of paper about nine minutes long.

"From today, a paper can reach you by writing to you first. Not by coming to the address. By writing to you. And every one of those five women who answered the sheet on the fourth of April was answering the head, and now the head exists and has answered.

"You have bought six people one question. And the question is worth three grains of margin, because that is what being asked is worth at your band, and every household in this city that has ever been certified has bought exactly that and exactly nothing else, and about one in three of them has not understood it at the time you understood it.

"And you are bound by the highest standing in it, and the highest standing in it is not yours, and you were told that at a kitchen table on the seventh of this month by a woman who priced it out loud and was not thanked, and you have had twelve days with it and you did not ask her a single question, and I have been in this building for thirty years and I would not have believed that of a man with your sheet until I read it."

He put the pen down.

"The schedule goes up on the twenty-sixth of June and the certificate is dated the twenty-seventh and neither of those dates has moved and neither of them cares what you did in this room this morning. You have not brought the certificate forward by one day. You have made it a household's."

He looked up.

"That is the whole of it and I have kept a bit of paper in the drawer with the numbers on it and the number on it this morning is a hundred and forty-one, and I am not going to tell you what the other one was because a man who knows that a certificate is the first thing he has ever had that is not a number should not be given the numbers by a clerk in a corridor."

He clipped the sheet onto the four pages and squared the pair and wrote on the top sheet, in the ordinary way, that he had attended at the second-floor counter of the Reading at twenty past nine in the morning of the nineteenth of this month and that the head had assumed the household and named the members.

"Take your coat," said Doyle. "And go down the public stair and out into the street, and there are about a thousand people on that pavement and you will be the least remarkable thing on it, and that is the last time it will be true of you."

He stood up.

"Sit down one minute," he said. "There is one thing I have to ask you and it is not in the tray and it is not on the sheet and I am asking it as a man and not as a clerk, and you are not obliged to answer it, and if you do not answer it I will not remember having asked.

"Do you know what you signed."

He knew. He had known since the seventh of May at ten past one in the afternoon in a kitchen, when a woman put her cup down and said the highest standing in that household was hers and priced the whole of it in about ninety seconds and said that not one person in the room had asked her for it, and then she had said that she was not going to be thanked for it, and she had not been.

He had had twelve days. He had spent seven of them on a requisition he read out loud in a hall in front of forty cleaners, a caller with a pencil going down a landing at Ferrier Street, a woman refusing in a queue, four questions at a counter, a sheet of paper in the middle of a floor where forty people were standing, and a woman telling him that the Vant's book had a house in it.

"Yes," he said. "I knew what it cost."

Doyle did not write it down.

"Good," he said. "That is the whole of what a certification is granted on."

He went down the public stair and out into the street at about ten in the morning, in daylight, in a street with trams on it and a thousand people in it.

And in his coat, in the inside pocket, on a piece of paper of a good weight that was not office stock, there were two names: his own, at the top of it, in his own hand; and Marguerite Vale's, at the foot of it, in hers, dated the seventeenth, which he had not known was on it until the clerk squared the pair of sheets and put the tray down, and which he had looked at for about four seconds and then not looked at again.

Twenty-two of the sixty were gone. Thirty-eight of them were left. One day to the twentieth.

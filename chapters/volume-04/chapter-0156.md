# The Composition Return

A composition return is how this city says who lives at an address and under whose standing. It is not served. There is no door and no clerk with a paper at the bottom of the step.

It is posted, and then it comes back.

That is the part that never appears anywhere a person outside the Exchange could read it, and it is the part that had him standing at a counter at about eleven in the morning reading over a clerk's shoulder.

"Can a composition return be stopped," he said.

The clerk on the second floor had a bundle of them in a tray and she had been putting them in envelopes for about an hour. She said, without stopping, "No."

"It has not gone yet. It is in a tray."

"It is in a tray and it is going in a bag and the bag goes at noon." She took the top one off the pile and looked at it, and then at him, and then she read it again properly, which was the first thing that had happened to that envelope since it was made. "You are the member in the finding."

"I am."

"Then you will not mind me saying that this one does not go to an address that anybody on this floor has got." She put her thumb on the envelope. "There is no name on it. There is a street, and a number, and a floor, and then it says the household of the member described in the finding of the twenty-ninth."

He looked at the envelope for about as long as a person can look at a thing without it being a question.

Ferrier Street. A number. A floor.

Which meant that the Office had the address, and had had it since the eleventh of March, because that was the day the exhibit roll was proved and a visitors' book with a hundred and six live entries went on it, and a visitors' book is a book with addresses in it if the person keeping it is a broker who has never in her life let a caller leave a house without writing which house.

He had stood at a table in that hall on the fourteenth and read all nine items of that roll aloud to nineteen people, in his own voice, and had sat down again.

"You can read it," the clerk said. "It is a form and it is going to a house and there is nothing in it about you."

He read it.

It was a single page and it took about ninety seconds. It did not ask a name. It asked a number: how many persons of reading age keep an address at the above premises, and what each of their bands is, and under whose standing the premises are held, and whether the premises are held under a plate.

There were four short questions and then a space at the bottom headed DECLARATION, and the space was ruled in two halves with a line between them, one for a member and one for a witness, and the instruction above the space said that the return was to be completed by a person of reading age at the premises and that in the absence of such a person it might be completed by any person having the care of the premises.

"It goes out today," the clerk said. "You have until the twenty-seventh. That is three weeks from the day it is posted and not three weeks from the day you found out about it, and I am telling you that because everybody thinks it is the second one."

"Twenty-one days."

"Twenty-one days." She put it in the tray. "And if it is not answered, the Exchange completes it from what it holds, and what it holds on a household with no return against it is the household that is on the roll."

He thanked her and went back down and put it to her straight, because there was one thing he had not asked and it was the only one that mattered before the form arrived.

"Can it be answered before it arrives."

"Not by you." The clerk did not even look up. "It is completed at the premises by a person of reading age at the premises. Not before, not at a counter, not by the member."

She put the envelope down on the tray.

"If you filled it in in this room today I would have to enter that it was completed at the premises, and it was not, and then it would go back empty and there would be a second form and a date on the second form, and the second form carries a shorter clock than the first one. I have watched that go wrong twice in eleven years and I am not doing it a third time for a man standing at my counter on a busy morning."

That was the sentence. Not a threat, not a threat at all — a schedule. A return that is not answered is completed by an office, from the exhibit roll of the twenty-ninth, which contains item four, which is a description of six lines a day in six hands since the twenty-first of March with no signature on any of them.

So there were two futures and both of them were bad. Answer it, and six women are named on a form and handed to a building. Leave it, and six women are described on a form by an office that never saw them, out of a book Marguerite Vale has kept for four months.

He got on a tram at ten past twelve and went out to the yards.

Nadia Ferrous came off a car at ten past two and found him on the bench by the gate, which is where he had been sitting for two hours, and she came off that car at a change of shift in front of nine operators once a week for four months because a clerk in the Reading wrote to her service and asked her to identify the operator of a lift.

"The file," she said.

"The file."

"Twice today." She put her bag down on the bench. "The file goes at the counter and it always has gone at the counter, and you have known that since March, and today the girl on the counter put a second envelope in the same bag and it was not addressed to the service."

"What was on it."

"A street and a number and a floor. And a line underneath saying that the premises were the premises of a household, and that the household was held under a plate, and there was no name on the whole thing from the corner to the signature line." She sat down. "I read it over her shoulder for about four seconds and she did not stop me, because a person holding a bag open in front of you does not think about who can read.

"And then I asked her what it was and she said it was a composition return and it goes out on Fridays with the service files because they go out on Fridays."

"You did not have to tell me that," he said.

"I have been waiting four months to say that there is a piece of paper with our street on it and not one of our names on it."

She put her hands on her knees.

"I have been not saying it for four months and you know exactly why, and it is because the whole arrangement of this house is that a paper cannot reach it." "There is a plate on the board that says five. I have seen it. I walk past it. And there is no name in this house on any plate anywhere and there never has been and that was supposed to be the whole of it."

"It was the whole of it until Thursday."

She turned her head.

"Thursday the Board repaints. They put the Exchange's monthly schedule on the rail under the sheet." He told her what was on it and what was printed in the margin against an account with no classification, and he told her what the line said, and he watched her get to the bottom of it before she said anything, which took about four seconds.

"No entitlement established," she said.

"No entitlement established."

"So he cannot be asked first. And there is no way on this earth that a piece of paper about six women gets answered by him, because a paper about six women is not his, and there is no version of him that is entitled to stand in front of a door on Ferrier Street and say it is."

"That is the whole of what I have worked out since nine o'clock this morning," he said, "and I have been sitting on this bench for two hours waiting to find out whether I had got it right, and you have just told me I have."

Nadia Ferrous was quiet for about half a minute. A tram came through the gate and went on into the yards and did not stop.

"Right," she said. "Then say the rest of it, because you have got that face on and the rest of it is the part that costs."

"It does not cost me. I want that on the record before I say it and I am saying it here because you are the only person in that house who would take it from me if I made it smaller." He turned and looked at her. "It has to be a household. Not six women who happen to live in a building. A household, with a plate, with a name at the top of it, and the top of it has to be me.

"And the moment that is done, every liability that belongs to six separate women becomes one liability that belongs to me, and the Exchange will sell him every permission on that schedule, and he will be able to say *asked first* on behalf of a building above a chop-house, and that is the whole of what is on offer."

"And what it costs."

"That I am findable. That is all of it. That is the entire price and I have had it in front of me for a day and a night." He looked back at the gate. "For four years I have made sure that there is not one page in this city that can say where I sleep, and I am proposing to put my name at the top of six other people's paperwork."

Nadia Ferrous did not answer for some time. Two trams came through.

"Say the names," she said.

"The return goes out today. It is posted, and it arrives, and there are twenty-one days on it, and every day of those twenty-one days is a day it can be answered with six names and no head." He stood up. "It arrives tomorrow. And tomorrow night is the last night before it is on a table in this house, and I am going to need somebody to be on that table before it gets there."

"Which somebody."

"A woman who can put a name on a piece of paper and make it a fact about a body instead of a fact about a man." He picked up her bag and gave it back to her. "And I have been told twice this week not to ask her gently."

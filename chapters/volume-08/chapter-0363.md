# A Third Mark In A Chained Book

He had not been down the service stair off the Exchange yard since the first of January and he went down it at ten to nine on the first of February with his hands in his pockets, because that is the only way into the counting room that does not go past a public counter and a man who wants to be a fact in a room would rather be a nuisance in it.

Hanneke Ruis had written to him in September, on the seventeenth, that she would not see him again downstairs while there were two figures for one door. One figure was seven, initialled and not signed, dated the sixteenth of September. The other was eight, certified at nine in the morning on the thirtieth of November by a woman who sleeps at that address. Both were still on the books.

So this was the second time in a month he had come down here against a written instruction from the woman who had the keys, and this time he had prepared for it.

He had had an afternoon off Cordwain to prepare for it and he had come down with three things to say and by the time he got to the bottom of the stair he knew that two of them were about him and one was about a piece of paper in her coat, and there is no hearing in this city for a man who brings that.

She did not look at him.

## Three On A Line

Four people were down there. Hanneke Ruis at the desk with the keys through her belt. Solveig Rask at the board with the open book in front of her. A junior on a stool working through a strap of returns that had come in during the night. A man from the plate office who had come down for one figure and would go straight back up.

The book on the board is fastened to it. It is fastened with a short chain and a staple and that is not a decoration, because a mark made in it cannot be lifted off the leaf without a second entry in the destruction register on the shelf beside her, with a date and a hand against it, and a hand that has not been in the destruction register in four years is a hand that people begin to ask about.

The pen Solveig Rask wrote with is on a chain too. It goes round her wrist and back to a staple in the board, and the length of it is set so that a second person could not reach the page, which is how a book can be written in by a person alone and still be a record.

She came to the leaf.

Seven at the top. Below the figure, on the first of December's line, one word: *Called.* Below that, printed, with a bracket at each end, the rule of the room.

Against the first, on the right of the bracket, the first of December. On the left of the same bracket, the first of January.

And under both of them the pen made the third.

*Called. Called. Called.*

Under it, the same rule in the same words, because it is a printed line and printed lines do not vary to suit a man.

Against the third, the first of February.

She laid the pen down in its loop, took her hand off the page, and looked at the keeper.

Hanneke Ruis entered the hour in the ordinary register, which has no chain on it and no staple and is the only book in that room a person can take off the desk, and the hour was twenty to nine.

## Afterwards

They cleared out. The plate-office man went first. The junior counted his bundle on the bottom step.

That left the two women and a man at a rail, and the two women began the end-of-a-valuation sequence, which has an order and is done the same way every morning, and Hanneke Ruis did her share of it with half her face away from him.

She was on the fifth book when she said it, and the fifth book has had a strap across it since the eighteenth of November and has had one every morning since, and the strap goes on last and it goes on at the speed she feels like.

"One hundred and thirty-four days," she said. "Seventeenth of September. You are the second time in a month you have come down that stair without being asked and the first time you had a reason."

"I had a reason the first time as well."

"You had a curiosity the first time," said Hanneke Ruis. "You are here about a carry-forward and I am going to tell you about a carry-forward and then this conversation is finished."

"You will go up that stair and you will not come down it again while there are two figures for one door. That is what I wrote to you in September. It is the standing instruction of this floor and not a decision of mine, and I would like it understood in the room and not only by you."

Solveig Rask did not look up from the destruction book.

## What A Carry-Forward Carries

"A carry-forward sheet carries a figure from one quarter to the next without a valuation," said Hanneke Ruis. "That is the whole of what it is and it is the cheapest thing on this floor. It is not free, because what it carries is the figure the line is carried at."

"Your line is carried at eight. It is priced at seven. A composition of eight certified on the thirtieth of November against a priced line of seven initialled on the sixteenth of September."

She turned round.

"And I am not going to move a figure across a quarter boundary that disagrees with itself. There is nobody in this building who can overrule me about it, there never has been, and it is not a rule anybody made up this morning."

"A sheet that carries a figure has to carry one figure. If it carried a figure and its own address disagreed with it, then in about four years somebody in a room would have to say which of the two the man actually held, and there would be nothing on the paper to tell them."

"So it cannot go on the sheet."

"It cannot go on the sheet," said Hanneke Ruis. "It goes on the sheet on the day your composition says what your priced line says, and not on any day before that, and neither of those figures is mine and I did not set either of them and I cannot move either of them."

## What She Said Last Time

"You told me on the first of January that it would be carried from the first of February."

"I did."

"You wrote it on a slip torn off a plain pad with a ruler in a hand that is not yours and you did not file it and you told me not to thank you, and I have not thanked you, and it is under my coat at this moment and it has been in the lining of my coat for a month."

"Then you have read it," said Hanneke Ruis, "and you have read it correctly, and it says a line called twice may be carried forward by the room that holds the book without a fresh sheet being drafted at all. Every word of that is true and I wrote every word of it and I would write it again this morning."

"And it does not happen."

"It does not happen," said Hanneke Ruis. "It cannot happen, it has not happened, and it is not going to happen on the first of March either. I did not say it would. I did not write the word outcome."

"You read a rule off a piece of paper and you went away and you believed it was a promise. It is not a promise. It is the shape of a door, and whether there is anything behind the door is not a thing I have an opinion about."

She came and stood at the desk and squared the valuation book against the edge of it.

"You stood at this rail on the first of January and I tore a sheet off a pad. You had come down a service stair on your own, and a man who does that has usually worked out that nobody has ever asked him anything and is going to be disappointed."

"I told you what the rule was. I did not tell you what the rule would do for you, because I do not know. You are the first line at this address that has ever been called twice and there has never been a second one here to compare it with."

"And you have been standing in this room a hundred and thirty-four days holding a rule that does not do anything on its own."

"I have been standing in this room a hundred and thirty-four days holding a book," said Hanneke Ruis, "and the book does not care what I would have liked."

## What The Morning Produced

The valuation had not stopped. That was the thing he had not been able to get out of the shape of the room while she was in it.

The man from the plate office had come down for a figure and gone straight back up, and the junior had taken up returns, and in about four minutes that room had produced a valuation sheet, the way it produced one on the first of December and the way it produced one on the first of January, because that is what a room on the first of the month does.

One sheet. The address. The figure. Three marks and a rule and three dates.

And a valuation sheet goes out on the ordinary stream at four in the afternoon in the ordinary way to the desks of the offices that keep lists, and a called line that has been called three times is more legible than a called line that has been called once, and there are six divisions in this city that keep a list which would want it.

He had believed for a month that he had bought quiet with a slip of paper torn off a plain pad with a ruler. The quiet had never belonged to him. The woman who had sold it to him had sold him the correct rule and had never once told him that it worked.

"Time," said Hanneke Ruis.

He looked up.

"Twenty to nine, both dates. The first of January and this morning. A clerk reads that as a man who was there twice." She put the book away. "There is a valuation room in this city that does not enter a time unless somebody asks for the line to be carried. Nobody has asked for your line to be carried and nobody is going to."

"I entered both times because you were standing at my rail with your hands in your pockets, and I am not going to sit in a cold room at ten to nine in the morning and pretend a man was not there."

Solveig Rask shut the destruction book and put the key in.

"Go up the stair," said Hanneke Ruis. "And do not come down it again until those two figures are the same figure, and when they are, come down it and I will carry it, and it will take a keeper and a date and nothing else, and I will not put my name under it and you will not thank me for it, and that is the entire extent of what I have to offer anybody at this address."

He went up the service stair and out into the Exchange yard at about twenty past nine on the morning of the first of February. Carts had started up under the arches and the yard was doing the noise it does by then, and a sheet with three marks on it was going out of a gate in this city at four o'clock that afternoon.

That afternoon it went to six desks, and one of the six was a wing off a third court he could not find again with a map, and there was a woman on the second floor of it who was line one on a sheet of his own and had gone completely quiet since the sixteenth of January, and he could not ask whether it had been opened.

And the only thing in Ostara that can make two figures into one figure is a woman at a counter giving up an address, and there are eight of those women, and they eat at his table every evening, and one of the eight has been counting since about half past ten that morning and has not told anybody.

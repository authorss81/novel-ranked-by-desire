# The Sheet That Names Its Own Ground

There are two tables in that room and the one at the near end has the blotter on it, and on the morning of the twenty-first of February the woman at that table had the requisition sheet out on it with the four parts uppermost and the fifth line ruled and empty under them, and she turned the whole thing round so that a man standing on the near side could read the first line of it.

*Requisition. Subject: the account of thirty years standing at premises 34-6-118.*

He had been in that room four times. He had been let in on the sixth of February, and on the thirteenth of December, and on the twenty-ninth of December, and on the twentieth of the morning, and on none of those four had anybody ever written on that sheet in front of him.

That was the whole of what the day was for.

## A Man At A Side Table

He had thought about it for a night and a half and the thinking had not improved it, and what he had come down the stair with was six lines.

"I'm going to ask you for a paper," he said. "Not a copy. The sheet stays on your table and I go out of the corridor with nothing in my hands, and you keep the sheet and I keep the question, and if the question is bad the answer can be no."

The clerk did not move.

"Sit down there," she said, and pointed at the chair on the near side of the second table, which is where subjects are put, and which is not the chair he was let into last time. "Now say it slow, because I am going to write it in the book and everything that goes in the book goes out on the stream at four, and I do not want to have to take a word out of it."

He said it slow.

He asked her to produce the ground written in the fourth part. He asked her to do it before the last working day of the quarter. He asked her to read into the keeping book that he had asked and that he was the subject named in the first part, and that he was asking out of the ordinary right of a man who has been served with a sheet, and that nobody had ever used that right on that sheet in twenty years.

"Last bit," said the clerk.

"Last bit is not for the book."

"Then the last bit is not for the book," she said, and wrote, and did not ask him twice.

## The Chair Comes Into The Corridor

Nobody came out of the room for about two minutes, and then the door opened and Anouk Ferreira came out into the corridor and did not go more than four feet from it, and stood with her hands at her sides in a corridor that smells of wax and hot dust, and looked at the sheet on the blotter.

"Show me."

The clerk turned it round. The chair read the six lines on it in about nine seconds, which is how long it takes to read a thing twice.

"You have understood one part of this and you have got one part of it wrong," she said. "The part you have got wrong is that you are asking for something."

"I am asking for something."

"You are asking for something the room owes you, and that is different, and if you do not know the difference between those two things by the end of today you will get very little done in this city." She put one finger on the blotter, not on the paper.

"A ground is a document with a day on it that somebody else wrote, and it is the reason a requisition may be done. If it is in this room and it has not been read out in the nine minutes, it is paper in a drawer."

"You have read your own sheet's ground and you have understood it. What you have not understood is that the room has never read it, and that until the room reads a ground out loud in front of nine people, a ground is a piece of paper a clerk put in a drawer on somebody else's instruction."

"So the room will read it."

"The room will produce it. That is yours and it is the ordinary right and I am not going to argue about it with a man standing at my side table." She took her finger off the blotter.

"And now you are going to get the half of it that you did not ask for. You are going to resent it. You are going to get it anyway."

"Because if I carry a requisition on a paper that grounds nothing, then in about four years there is a minute in this room with a document sitting in it that grounds nothing, and the nine of us will all have to sit here and be asked our names."

"A ground has to be tied to the holding."

"A ground has to be tied to the holding it grounds," said Anouk Ferreira. "Otherwise it is a piece of paper about a house, and the sheet on that blotter is about an account. So the ground comes, and the holding comes with it, and whatever the holding stands on comes with that, and I am going to have all of that in the minute in that order."

## What It Costs Him To Hear It

That was the moment he understood what he had bought.

He had come down sixty-one steps and up a stair at half past eight in the morning with six lines of the ordinary right of a man who has been served, and he had thought, and he had been right to think, that the right was a door to one piece of paper: the description made at his own kitchen table by a clerk he had never spoken to.

He had not thought about the holding. The holding was on the blotter in his own handwriting, in the second part, and it said *the account of thirty years standing at premises 34-6-118*, and no person in that room had ever seen the thing those four lines describe, and the chair was now going to ask a division for it in front of a clerk.

He was going to be served with his own account.

"Chair," he said. "I did not ask for the holding."

"No," said Anouk Ferreira. "You asked the ordinary right, and the ordinary right does not come in halves, and if it came in halves then a subject could demand the paper the sheet stands on and sit there while the paper the sheet stands on grounds a thing nobody has ever looked at. That is not a right. That is a delay with a man's name on it, and you would have got it, and you would have had it for a year."

She went back into the room and shut the door, and it shut the way that door has always shut, without any opinion about it.

## The Question Nobody Has Asked In Twenty Years

The clerk turned the book round to a clean page and squared the pen.

"What is the holding," she said.

He had been in this room four times and nobody in it had ever put that question to him, and he understood that it was not a formality, because she had the pen on the line and the line was the second part of a sheet and the second part had never been the clerk's to write.

"It is the account of thirty years," he said. "It has been called three times. It is carried at eight and priced at seven and it has not been paid and it cannot be produced off the door it would be paid at."

"Which door."

"The counting room under the Exchange," he said. "The chained book. The keeper has the keys and she has written to me once and she has not written to me since."

The clerk wrote all of it down, and it took her about eleven seconds, and he watched a description of a place he had never seen in that building go into a book in a room at the end of a corridor on the first floor of a building with no plate on the door.

That was the first time the account had ever been written down by anybody in that room.

She blotted it and turned the page.

## The Twenty-Six Days

"Time."

"Twenty to nine," he said. "I have not the least idea what the time is."

"Then I am entering it at the hour I entered the last one, which is what I do." She wrote it. "You have twenty-six days."

"A demand entered today goes out on the stream at four this afternoon to the division that holds the ground, and a division that holds a document and is asked for it does not have to do it in a day and is not going to."

"You have until the twenty-eighth of the month to have it back in this room if you want it read in this quarter, and the reading has to be before the thirtieth, because the thirtieth is the last working day of the quarter and this room does not sit after the thirtieth."

"So after the thirtieth it is a demand in the next quarter."

"After the thirtieth it is a demand in the next quarter, and it is not yours any more, it is the next quarter's, and the next quarter's does not know you and has no reason to be quick." She put the pen down across the book. "That is the whole of what a demand is. It is a piece of paper that costs nothing and it goes out at four, and everything else about it is people being slow in the correct order."

## The Division That Holds It

He was standing at the window at the end of the corridor at about half past ten when he worked out what he had actually set in motion, and it took him about four minutes and two of them were wasted on hoping.

The ground was the description put over those premises on the twenty-seventh of October by a clerk called Imrie, and it was in the second sub-level of the Classification Wing, in counter-forensics, and the division that held it was the one Wren Kallo had been sitting at for eleven years.

He could not ask her. He had asked her one question on the sixth of December about an address and received a standing answer, and there has never been an instrument in this city that lifts a bar on that.

He was not asking her. The room was asking her, and the room had asked her in the ordinary way, on the ordinary stream, at four in the afternoon, with a date on it and a figure against it, and there is no discretion anywhere in this city in that.

A clerk who is asked for a document does not get to decide whether the man it is about likes the woman who holds it.

He understood, standing at that window, that he had spent seven minutes on the twentieth of February buying a right and had this morning spent one sentence of his own finding out that the right came with a division on the other end of it, and that the division belonged to a woman who cannot be asked about the address her own division holds.

At about four o'clock that afternoon a sheet went out of a gate in this city with his name nowhere on it and a demand on it, and it went to the second sub-level of the Classification Wing, and it did not go to him.

He went up the hill. Eight women were at the table, and Nadia Ferrous was still on her feet with her coat still on, and the table did not start, because it never has.

# The Price Of Two Figures

The Vant's smaller rooms are off a corridor on the second floor and Marguerite Vale has had them for nine years and there is a table in the middle of the front one with four chairs round it that she has not eaten at since the autumn before last, because a woman who takes a commission on an address cannot be a woman who lives at it and her own rooms became that about six weeks after she took the commission on the one on Ferrier Street.

She had the kettle on when he came up at about four in the afternoon on the sixteenth of March and she had not expected him and did not show it.

"Sit down, if you are going to."

"I am going to ask you for the arithmetic of the whole thing and I have come to ask you for it rather than do it myself, because you did it in September and I would like to see whether you have been doing it since."

"I have been doing it since," said Marguerite Vale.

## The Whole Of It, Once

She did not do it out of a book. She did it off the end of her own fingers, the way she does everything, and she went through it once and did not stop in the middle to enjoy herself.

"Sixteenth of September. A line priced at seven, carried into my rooms in the afternoon, initialled by the man who carried it out at about half past eleven at night and not signed by the woman who gave him the figure. That has been the position for six months and it is a bad one and I told him so at the time and he took it away anyway."

"It has been in fourteen places in four divisions since October."

"It has been in fourteen places in four divisions since October," she said, "and four days after I gave it to you a standing table of the trade met at seven and took the figure with them, and they have met at seven every quarter since and they have never once opened it."

"And the composition."

"The composition was eight from the day the premises was a premises until the thirtieth of November, when it was certified at nine in the morning by the woman who sleeps there, in her own division, in writing. Eight from that day to the thirteenth of March. Seven since yesterday morning, at nine forty-one, at a counter, in the ordinary way."

"Seven and seven."

"Seven and seven, at about half past four yesterday afternoon, after a hundred and seventy-seven days of eight and seven." She put the cup down. "And I have been doing the sum you have been doing, which is that you did not win anything. You paid. Nadia Ferrous paid, in an address she had kept for six years, at nine forty-one in the morning, and the rest of it is arithmetic that was already true when the two of you walked in."

## What The Two Figures Were Doing

"What were the two figures doing," said Lin Hao, "for a hundred and seventy-seven days."

"Nothing. That is the answer and it is why a broker like me has been eating standing up for six months." She turned the cup round on the cloth. "They were doing nothing to each other."

"A line carried at eight and priced at seven cannot be carried on a quarter's sheet, so it waits at a valuation and it is called on the first of the month, and being called is a word in a book with a chain on it. That is the whole of what a hundred and seventy-seven days of two figures buys."

"And what does a hundred and seventy-seven days of two figures cost."

"It costs that nobody can use any of it," said Marguerite Vale. "A line that disagrees with itself is not evidence of anything. Not of a price, not of a standing, not of a composition. It is a piece of paper about a house that two offices have described differently and neither of them can be got to say why."

"Nobody has ever laid a requisition against a holding that will not agree with its own figure, because you cannot administer what you cannot count and a man who administers a thing he cannot count gets put off the board inside four years."

He put his hands flat on his knees.

"It can be counted now," he said.

"It can be counted now," said Marguerite Vale. "That is what happened yesterday morning at nine forty-one."

## What A Correct Line Is Worth

She let about four seconds go past, deliberately, which was a thing she had learned to do in nine years of rooms off a hotel corridor.

"And now," she said, "a line priced at seven agrees with a composition of seven, and a line that agrees with itself is a line anybody holding a copy can produce against anybody. It is not a thing that sits in a drawer and keeps a house warm. It is a thing with a figure on it that can be put in front of anybody, and it does not need you in the room and it does not need anybody to have been at a counter."

"There are fourteen copies."

"There are fourteen copies in four divisions, and one of those four divisions does not sit in the Wing, keeps no list of addresses, and has had a strip about the premises on Ferrier Street since October, and the strip went out of my rooms in September with a line priced at seven on the bottom of it, which at the time was worth nothing to anybody and is now worth a great deal."

He could not ask her one question about that and she watched him decide not to, and did not help him.

## What Seven Does To Eight Women

"And here is the other half of it," said Marguerite Vale, "and I have never said it out loud because nobody has ever paid me for it and I have never had anybody to say it to."

She put her cup down.

"A composition of eight is a thing you can be inside of. Eight people at an address are eight people who eat at the same table, and every counter in this city reads that as eight, and eight is what your holding is worth, and nothing anywhere on paper this morning says how many of the eight have been inside the building this week."

"Seven is different."

"Seven is a figure," said Marguerite Vale. "A figure is a claim about a number and nobody can be inside a number. The day your line is carried forward at seven, this stops being a house with people in it and it becomes a holding with a figure on it, and a holding with a figure on it is administered by the figure and not by anybody, and the only thing left of the eight of you is that you live there."

She looked at the window.

"You are not losing a woman. Nadia Ferrous has not lost a room. You are losing the fact that anybody could ever say there were eight of you, and in about four years that is going to be the only thing any of you had."

## The Fourth Thing

"There is a fourth thing," said Marguerite Vale, "and I am going to say it because you have come up here for the arithmetic and you would not have had it otherwise, and I have been sitting on it since the morning of the fourteenth."

She went through to the front room and opened the drawer of the sideboard she had not opened since the sixteenth of September, and there was one unruled slip in it, and it had a figure on it and a time and an address and a line at the bottom that she had ruled herself with a straight edge and her own hand.

She put it on the table he was not going to eat at and turned it round so that he could read it, and did not let go of it.

"I ruled that line myself on the sixteenth of September and I have never known what it was for and I have never opened that drawer since the day I did it."

"There is a figure on it and it is neither seven nor eight, and you are not getting it out of me, because the moment a figure like that is in your head as well as mine this city does to both of us exactly what it did to both of us in September, and neither of us was ever taught which end of it that was."

"Then why are you showing me."

"Because it is signed," said Marguerite Vale. "It is the only paper in this whole business that has ever had my name at the foot of it, and I am the only person alive who can put it in front of anybody, and in six years not one person has ever asked me to, and I have been the one person in this arrangement with nothing standing over her the entire time and nobody has ever thought to tell me so."

She let go of it.

"Take it. I am not giving it to you as a favour. I am putting it in the hands of the only man in this city who has to be able to produce it, and if you keep it in your pocket then it does not exist and if you do not then it does, and either way it is the first time in nine years that something I have ruled has gone anywhere at all."

## The Table She Does Not Eat At

He put it in the inside pocket of his coat and did not look at it again in that room.

The wind was coming off the tram line and the light in the front room was the light that comes in off a corridor at four in the afternoon, and there were four chairs at that table and there had been four chairs at it for nine years, and in nine years no broker in this city had put a client at it and kept them there over a meal.

"Sit down," said Marguerite Vale. "You have been standing up since you came in and there is nobody in this building who is going to look at you."

"I cannot stay."

"I know you cannot stay. That is why I said it." She did not get up and did not open the door. "There is one thing I am going to say about what happens next and then this is finished, because it is not about the arithmetic and it is the only thing in this room that is about a person."

"Say it."

Marguerite Vale has a table in four rooms off a hotel corridor that she has not eaten a meal at since the autumn before last, and on the thirteenth of March the figure she priced in September became the true figure, and there is not one desk in this city that will send her anything for it.

# The Plate Held By A Formula

The plate goes up at half past four once a week and comes down on the next, and a wall in this city is read whole, which is the reason he had taught himself to read it in daylight in front of strangers and had done so a hundred and forty times in four years.

He was not there at half past four. He was on the fourth floor of the Fourth Branch with a bucket and a woman from the laundry who had asked him to take the heavy end of a trolley, which he did, and which no clerk anywhere wrote down, because that is the shape of a week.

He came out into the square at a quarter to five and Beatrix Halm was already on the sill with the notebook open and the pencil down and the expression she wears when she has already written the number and does not like it.

He read it off the wall from about twelve feet away without going closer, the way he had done a hundred and forty times.

Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five.

## Five Days

"That is the floor," he said.

"That is the floor." Beatrix Halm did not look up. "It has been the floor since the thirtieth of December and it is the floor on the wall this afternoon and there is nothing on this wall about you."

He looked at the wall for about four seconds, which is longer than the whole of the north wall has taken him since July.

There were two words on that wall from the twenty-second of November until the thirtieth of December. *Out of band.* Forty-five points down in one week, eight in the next, and the boy had painted the green over with lead, and then a wall in this city had said the thing out loud for anybody who walked past the north end of the square.

Since the thirtieth of December there had been nothing.

Five days. From the card that came down on different paper in a different hand at six in the morning on the thirtieth, to the fifth of January. Five days, and there is no thirty-first in that month, and a month is thirty days, and the five days are the first week of a thing that had not happened to him in seven years.

"What did you write."

"I wrote the time." She turned the notebook so he could see it. Four seventeen, in her hand, and the number under it, and then nothing under the number, and then the space where she had always put something.

"It has been blank since the thirtieth."

"It has been blank twice in my life. Once in the second month of the war and once now."

[Ledger. One hundred and twenty-five lines. Nine turned. The split has not been published.]

## The Arithmetic Nobody Has Published

"A plate is not a memory of a month," Beatrix Halm said. "You have known that since November and I am going to say the rest of it, because you have never asked me and I have been waiting since the third."

"Tell me."

"Attendants score in aggregate to the whole staff." She wrote nothing while she said it, which was the thing she did when she was reciting rather than reporting. "Every man on a branch round is entered into one figure with every other man on the round, and the figure is what goes on the plate. There is no line anywhere in this city with your name on it that a plate is made out of. There has not been one, at the Fourth Branch or anywhere else."

"How is the aggregate split out."

"That is the part nobody has." She looked up. "There is no published split. It is not a secret. A secret is a thing somebody decided to keep. That is an office that has never had anybody ask it for a sheet, because a man on a round does not care what his round is worth, he cares what he is worth, and what he is worth is a number in a book with his name on it that is not the plate."

"So the plate cannot be traced to me."

"The plate cannot be traced to anybody on the Fourth Branch. It has been that way since before you were at that office." She put the pencil behind her ear. "It explains why nothing on that green card can be traced back to you. It does not explain why you are standing on it."

"It is the eight hands."

"It is the eight hands." She said it without any softening at all. "Eight people signed a witness line under a page on the twenty-ninth of December and that carried you fourteen points in one day from the bottom of your quarter to the line of it. That was the whole of the arithmetic. The split put nothing on that card."

"The split is the reason nobody could have told me in December which part of your plate was the branch's, and that is a different thing and it is the only thing that has ever made you safe."

## The First One He Did Not Build

He did not say anything for about a minute.

The square had about nine people in it and none of them were looking at the wall, and that was the sentence the wall was saying, out loud, in the only language a wall in this city has.

Seven years.

He had built his invisibility out of refusals. He had refused a laundry, a gate, a yard and a lift for seven years, one at a time, in about nine seconds each, and the whole of the method was that a man who is not written down cannot be found, and he had been extremely good at it and he had never once been proud of it and he had never once thought it might be taken off him.

Now there was a formula in a district office that he had never seen, that he had never signed, that nobody had ever published, and it had made him the most anonymous figure on a green card at the north end of a square, and it would go on making him anonymous for as long as he kept his round at the Fourth Branch, which was the only thing he had ever done in this city that nobody had made him do.

"You do not want it," said Beatrix Halm.

"No."

"I have watched a man get offered a thing in this square for seven years," she said. "You have turned down nine of them and one of them was me. I did not know you would want to turn down the tenth, and I would not have bet on it."

He put his hands in his pockets.

"You cannot tell the kitchen."

"No."

"That is the part I have been standing here for, and I did not know that was what it was until I said it."

"You cannot tell the kitchen because there is no sentence for it." Beatrix Halm took the pencil from behind her ear and looked at the page and did not write on it. "If you tell eight women that you are safer because of a thing nobody published, one of them will be pleased for you and seven of them will hear *I have been frightened for four months and there is now a reason.* That is not this house."

"This house has never once done that for anybody and it is the only reason there is still a house."

"I know."

"You do not know. You know it the way a man knows a rule he has never had to break." She closed the notebook.

"That is the first thing in seven years I have found that you built and then could not take back, and I have wanted to write it down for about ten minutes and I am not going to, because it would go in a book with your name in it and there is already one of those, signed by eight people, in a room at the end of a corridor."

## Twenty-Five Under

He went home past the square without reading it again, which was a discipline and not an accident.

Twenty-five under the painted line for seven, which is eight thousand one hundred and forty-five against eight thousand one hundred and seventy, and tolerance three, and he was standing exactly on a line that belonged to a different office and had been set by a boy with a paste brush on the last working day of a quarter.

On the third of November he had been eight thousand one hundred and thirty-nine, thirty-one under that line, and the wall had said *out of band* in paint, and Isolde Marchetti had told him that she would do the three mornings again and that it had got her nothing.

Now there was no paint. Now there was a green card with a number on it and a formula behind it that made the number untraceable and a rule that puts two words back up the wall the day a man goes under and inside a week after it.

So the wall was not being kind to him.

The wall had nothing to say, and the nothing was worth twenty-five points and a green card and eleven weeks, and it was held in place by a piece of arithmetic that nobody had ever printed and that he had never asked for and could not have designed.

On the top step of the sixty-one that night, with the notebook in Beatrix Halm's pocket two floors down, he worked out the one number he had not worked out in four days.

On the first of February there will be a valuation and there will be a carry-forward sheet, and a carried figure is a figure that has been entered by somebody else and can be got at by anybody who can get at the sheet.

A carried account is quiet. It is also, for the first time since the sixteenth of September, a figure that lives on a piece of paper with his address on it and his name nowhere in it.

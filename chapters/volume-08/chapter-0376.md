# The Mark In The Same Hand Again

He went down the service stair behind the plate office at ten to nine on the morning of the first of March, and he did not take the short way at the top of it, and he did not go past the counter on the sales floor, because both of those are places where a clerk looks up.

He had been told in writing on the seventeenth of September that he would not be seen downstairs while there were two figures for one door.

There were still two figures for one door.

## The Fourth

The book was open on the board. Four of them were sitting in the room where the marks were, and the mark went in at the minute it always went in at, in a small level hand, under the same printed rule, against a date.

Against the first of December. Against the first of January. Against the first of February.

And under all three of them the pen made the fourth.

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds, which is about the time it takes a room of four people to work out that a thing which was once unprecedented is now a habit, and habits are the only thing in this city that a keeper is genuinely afraid of.

The hour went into the ordinary register at twenty to nine.

He had not asked for it. Nobody had asked for it. She entered it, and it was not because he was standing at the rail with his hands in his pockets this time, because she did not look at him while she wrote it and he understood that afterward rather than at the time.

"One hundred and sixty-four days," said Hanneke Ruis.

"Since the keys changed."

"Since the seventeenth of September." She put the book away. "Say what you came down here to say, because you have come down that stair three times without being asked and a keeper who lets a man keep doing it stops being a rule and starts being a habit, and I have never in my life been a habit."

## The Whole Of It In One Sentence

"Nothing has been paid and there is no production off that door and neither of those two is ever going to be different, and I am going to keep writing that word on the first of every month until one of them happens, and neither of them is in your hands or mine."

"That is what you said on the first of January."

"That is what I said in December, and again in January, and again at the February valuation, and it is what you will get at the April one. I have not improved it once and I am not starting." She squared the book against the edge of the board. "Go up the stair."

"Four marks," he said.

"Four marks."

"There has never been a fourth mark at that address."

"There has never been a second mark at that address either," said Hanneke Ruis, "and I did not get a vote on the second one, and you did not get a vote on the third, and there was no meeting about the first. Four is not a decision. Four is a morning."

Solveig Rask closed the destruction register, turned her key, and began the end of a valuation in the order that floor has always done it in, and the strap went onto the fifth book at the speed she felt like, and he stood at that rail while a junior counted out a bundle on the bottom step and a man from the plate office went up two at a time.

"You will want to know what I am going to do with the book," said Hanneke Ruis, not turning round.

"You are going to keep writing."

"I am going to keep writing," she said, "on the first of every month, into a book that is chained to a board, and in about four years the chain comes off and a clerk scrapes the plate and makes a plate with four marks on it and puts that up in a case in a hall, and there will not be any way at all to tell you which of the four was the one that mattered."

"Do you know which one mattered."

"No," said Hanneke Ruis. "That is the whole of what a keeper is. I write them all down and I do not get a vote on which one was the one."

## What Four Marks Does


The man from the plate office had come down at a quarter to nine for two figures instead of one, which is not a thing that happens, and had gone back up before the mark was made, and that was the only part of the morning that was unusual and it was the part that mattered.

Because a valuation is not a mark. A mark is a word in a chained book that somebody wrote at ten to nine in the morning because a rule printed under it says so on the first of every month.

A valuation is the sheet that comes out of it.

One sheet. The address. The figure. And now four marks and a rule and four dates where there have been one date and no marks since the sixteenth of September.

A sheet that reads that way is not a quiet thing in a cold room. It is the most legible line at that address in four months.

And it goes out of a gate at four in the afternoon to the desks of the offices that keep lists, and there are six of those desks in this city, and one of them is a wing off a third court and one of them is the counter-forensics desk in the second sub-level of the Classification Wing.

He stood at that rail and understood it about four seconds after she had finished the sentence.

"You have told me four times that the quiet I bought was never yours to sell," he said.

"I have."

"It is not quiet now."

"It has not been quiet since the first of December," said Hanneke Ruis. "It was quiet for a fortnight in the middle of January, and that was a clerk's convenience and you read a promise off it, and you were wrong, and I was right, and neither of those is news to anybody standing where you are standing."

She turned round.

"And now there is a requisition in a room at the end of a corridor with a ground in the fourth part and a file number in the minute, and this morning there is a valuation sheet going out at four with four marks on it, and one clerk in an envelope is going to have to read both."

## The One Sentence After It

That was the whole of what she gave him, and she gave it in the flattest voice she has, and then she went back to the strap.

He was back out on the Exchange yard by a quarter past nine with a number he had been carrying in his head for eleven days, four lines he had nowhere to put, and a written instruction he had broken for the fourth time in three months.

Somewhere under him a room was finishing a valuation, and at four that afternoon the sheet it had produced would go out of a gate to a desk that had spent the previous three days reading about a brother he had never met.

He had known the shape of what the account was since he was a boy in a hall in another city.

What he had never had was the other end of it, and on the twentieth of February a woman in a corridor outside a door had given it to him standing up, and this morning a keeper two floors under an Exchange had told him in one sentence that it was never going to be paid and never going to be produced and was going to be called on the first of every month until one of those two things stopped being true.

One of them was going to be the first of April.

There were twenty-nine days to the end of the quarter and twenty days of them were going to be spent by nine people reading a sheet that was not going to be decided.

He had a woman on the third floor who had answered her own question on the twentieth, and a woman at a side table who had read four lines out loud on the twenty-eighth, and a woman at the head of his own household who had been handed a production that morning at a public post in a building with a man above her.

Beatrix Halm was on the top of the sixty-one at about ten past six that evening and she had her notebook under her arm and she had been standing there, by her own account, for about an hour and a half.

"You have been to the Exchange," she said.

"I have been to the Exchange."

"That is the fourth time since the first of January. I have written down the other three and there was nothing under any of them, and this is the fourth and I am not going to write it down." She tapped the notebook against her knee.

"I am going to go up and open this on the table and shut it again, and you are going to sit on the other side of that table from me for about an hour. That is what I have decided and I decided it on the step."

"And if somebody asks."

"Then somebody asks," said Beatrix Halm, "and I will look at them until they stop, and it will be the sixth one and not the first, and I have not decided that I mind."

And there was nobody left in the city he had not already paid for.

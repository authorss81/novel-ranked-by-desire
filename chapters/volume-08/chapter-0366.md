# Two To Call The Sitting

Beatrix Halm was on the top of the sixty-one steps at about half past seven in the morning on the ninth of February with the notebook open and the pencil in her hand and an expression on her face he had not seen there before, which was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic in public.

"I have called a sitting," she said.

He stopped on the step below her.

"I called it at about ten to eight this morning from the top of this stair and I am telling you at half past seven because I have been awake since five working out whether I was allowed to, and I am, and I have checked, and I would like you to understand that I did not do it because you asked me to and you did not ask me to."

"Who co-signed."

"Runa Sato. I went across the city and stood at a Field counter and asked her to co-sign a call and she asked me three questions and then co-signed it." Beatrix Halm put the pencil behind her ear. "Two members of that room may call a sitting. It is two and not one because one member calling a sitting is a member throwing a tantrum. Two is a thing that has been agreed to by somebody who has an interest in not agreeing to it."

"You do have an interest in it."

"I have four blanks in that book and I have started counting them, which I did not intend to do, and I have written down five of them since the twenty-ninth of December and every one of them is a night you went out and did something and came back and would not say what." She shut the notebook. "That is my interest. It is not a good one. It is the only one I have got."

## The Twentieth Of February

"So it is called."

"It is called for the twentieth of February."

"You have written it on the ninth," said Lin Hao.

"I have written it on the ninth for the twentieth, which is the way it is done." She got up off the step. "You will find out on the twentieth what that means."

"Two to call it. It has been called. There is a date in a book with my name against it and Runa Sato's name against it, and those two names are now in a minute that a clerk will copy out for four years."

"That is what I have done and I would do it again, and I am going inside now, because you are going to say thank you and I do not want to be standing here for it."

## What The Room Was Going To Ask

He caught her at the door.

"The objection that room had been building since the second of October," he said. "Say it out loud, once, so that I know I have heard it."

Beatrix Halm looked at him for a second longer than she usually looked at anybody.

"They were going to ask who holds it," she said. "That is what nine people in a room have been saying since October and it is the only question in this business anybody ever really wants answered, and on the twelfth of January a man wrote a sheet of paper with his own name at the top of it and laid it on a table under a window in that room, and eight people signed a line saying they had seen him do it."

"So the book is shut."

"The book is shut on that question," said Beatrix Halm, "and it is the only book that room ever opened, and it has taken four months and a sheet of plain paper and eight women's hands to shut it, and I have not stopped being frightened since the twelfth of January and I do not expect to stop."

She went inside and shut the door on his hand before he could say anything, which she had never done, and which was the closest thing to an embrace the stair had ever had.

## What Two Got

He caught Runa Sato at the counter at Cordwain at about eleven in the morning, because she was not going to be caught anywhere else and had said so in December.

"You asked Beatrix Halm to come and find you," she said, before he had got a sentence out.

"She came and found me."

"She came and found me first," said Runa Sato. "On the eighth of January she came and found me and told me three blanks in a notebook and then she asked me whether a man with no standing could co-sign anything, and I told her what the answer was, and she went up sixty-one steps and thought about it for a day."

"Nine minutes to read a sheet, question it and decide it. Five of nine would carry it. Three are left who would put a seal on one, and the chair will not ask them, and Alma is recused on her own signature and has been refused in one word, and Nikolai Vester went out of that room on the twenty-third of December and has not been back through the door since, and the fourth seat has been empty since the first of November."

"That is correct and I have written all of it down, and there is no part of it you have to correct. That is the first time that has happened since the twelfth of January." She squared something on the counter that did not need squaring.

"Twenty to the twentieth. Eleven days. Nine minutes. A sheet that is not decided in nine minutes is not a sheet that has been refused. It is a sheet that has not been decided, and it goes to the next sitting, and there is no rule in this city that says when the next one is."

"Who numbers the sittings."

"The clerk does, and the number goes on the sheet in the corner above the fourth part, and it has been done since the sixth of December," said Runa Sato. "Ours is the eleventh."

"That is the whole of what two can do."

"That is the whole of what two can do," said Runa Sato, "and I have wanted to say one thing to you about it and I am going to say it at a Field counter where I am standing on my own side of the wood and where neither of us has to be polite for anybody."

"Say it."

"When a sheet has no ground in it, it cannot be carried," said Runa Sato. "And a requisition that is not carried takes nothing off anybody. Not a grain and not a figure and not a name. It is a sheet in a drawer."

He stood there in the middle of a room full of people at about eleven in the morning and found that he could not remember the last time anybody in this city had told him something good.

## The Quarter And The Sitting

"That is the arithmetic," said Runa Sato. "I will give you the rest of it once and then I am going back to a counter."

She held up two fingers off the wood and did not look at them.

"A requisition lies in the quarter it is laid. It does not lie in the quarter it is decided, because nobody ever decides one in the quarter it is laid in. The rule is there so that a room cannot sit on a thing for a year and then produce it in a week."

"A sheet that is not decided when the quarter goes out is a sheet in the next one, and a sheet in the next one is a sheet nobody in any office in this city is in a hurry about."

"Which is the thirtieth."

"Which is the thirtieth," said Runa Sato. "And the carry-forward sheet goes out the same day, and it goes out to the same desks a valuation goes out to, and if the two things ever go out on the same morning in the same quarter then a clerk opens one envelope and closes the other and does not read either, and that is the whole design and it is about four years old and it works."

"You have thought about this a great deal."

"I have thought about the dates in this business for eleven years and there is nothing else to think about at a counter," said Runa Sato. "That is not a complaint. Most people get one date and then somebody moves it."

She put her hands flat on the counter.

"So there are two ways this goes and I have run both. Either the fourth part of that sheet is not written on the twentieth, and nine minutes go by, and the whole thing goes into the next quarter and lies down."

"Or a ground goes in. It is a piece of paper made before you were a holding, and then it is not in the next quarter any more. It is in this one, with a number on it."

"And you would know which."

"I would know which," said Runa Sato, "and so would the clerk, and so would a stranger in about four years."

## The Thirtieth

That was the whole of it, and he had eleven days, and the arithmetic of the eleven days was not a hard one.

A requisition lies in the quarter it is laid. A sheet that is not decided when the quarter goes out is a sheet in the next one, and the quarter goes out on the thirtieth, and a sheet in the next quarter is a sheet that nobody in any office in this city is in a hurry about.

So there was a version of the next eleven days in which nobody did anything at all. The ground stayed out of the fourth part. The nine minutes on the twentieth ran out with a sheet in front of nine people that could not be carried. And on the thirtieth the whole thing went quietly into the next quarter and lay down, and there was no instrument in Ostara that could wake a sheet up early.

He had been given that for free, on the night of the twenty-sixth of December, by a woman in a room telling eight people that nothing had been taken off anybody.

And it had lasted until the first of February, and then a keeper in a cold room had told him that the quiet she had sold him on the first of January had never been hers to sell, and a sheet with three marks on it had gone out of a gate at four in the afternoon, and now a sitting had been called for the twentieth of February by two women who could not be argued with.

He went up the sixty-one steps at about half past six that evening and the table was full and Nadia Ferrous had not sat down yet and nobody started until she had, which is also how she does it.

Beatrix Halm was at the top of the steps when he came up the second time.

"I wrote your time down," she said. "Nine twenty-one. You came in."

"And you are going to say there was nothing under it."

"There was nothing under it," said Beatrix Halm. "That is the fifth one. And now the other thing, and then I am going up, and you are not going to enjoy it."

"Go on."

"You are going to do something between now and the twentieth," said Beatrix Halm. "You have had a piece of good news for the first time in three months and you have had it for about five hours, and I have watched you get a piece of good news four times in this house and three of those times you spent it inside a day."

"You have a sheet that cannot be carried, and you have a ground nobody has written down. One of those two is the best thing that has happened to you since October and the other one is the worst, and I do not know which one you are going to reach for."

"And I am telling you at seven in the evening on a step so that you cannot tell me afterwards that nobody put it in front of you."

# The Two Words Return

Beatrix Halm was standing under the north end of the ranks wall twenty minutes short of six on the morning of the thirtieth of March with the notebook already out and her hands cold round the pencil, and she had been there since half past four, because a wall in this city is read in the dark by a woman with a notebook and has been read that way every morning for nine years.

The card comes down at six on the last working day of a quarter. It is put up by a different office in a different hand on different paper, by a boy with a paste brush and a list of numbers, and the boy does not know whose premises he is reading and is not told and does not ask.

"Two figures on it," said Beatrix Halm, before it was four inches off the wall.

"Read them."

"Eight thousand one hundred and forty-six. Floor." She had the pencil moving before she had finished saying it. "And against premises thirty-four, dash, six, dash, one-one-eight —"

She stopped.

She read it twice more and then wrote it down, and then she wrote the time against it, and then she stood in the dark with one hand flat on the board and said nothing at all for a long time.

## Eight Thousand One Hundred And Thirty-One

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one," she said.

The square was empty except for the two of them and a lamp at the far end.

"Fifteen under," said Beatrix Halm. "Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-six, and the tolerance is three, and I want to say that number twice because it is the only number in this city that has ever been on my side and it has just stopped being on my side by twelve."

"Read it again."

"It is the figure that was on that wall in the dark on the twenty-ninth of November," said Beatrix Halm.

"And it stood at it for the whole of December while eight people did absolutely nothing, and on the thirtieth of December eight women put their names under yours and it went up fourteen points in one day and it has not moved a grain since. Fourteen. Up, on the thirtieth of December, by eight hands on four sheets of plain paper. And down, this morning, on a card, by a figure of fifteen."

"Fourteen," said Lin Hao.

"Fourteen is the whole of it and I have been carrying it since about twenty to six." She turned the notebook round so that he could see it, and then she shut it, which is a thing Beatrix Halm has done about four times in nine years. "It is not the same fourteen by accident. It is the same fourteen because the thing that took you up is the thing that has put you down and neither of them was done to you by anybody."

## What It Is Not

"I want it said in the right order," he said, "and then I am going to work."

"Then say it."

"Nothing was spent."

"Not a grain, not on anybody, not at any counter, at any hour, in any division."

"He said it the way he has said it to that table about once a fortnight since November and he had never once said it to a wall."

"There is no folio and there is no routing and there is no second sheet and there has never been a production off that door."

"No paper in this city has been corrected this month."

"Not one person has been entered this month and nothing has been taken back, and the account of thirty years standing at that address was called four times and not once in the last month, and the reason there is no fifth mark is that a carry-forward sheet was drafted on the fourteenth of March and goes on the first of April and is administered from that first."

"So it is not you."

"It is not you and it is not me," said Lin Hao. "It is a figure on a card that was read at the composition of a set of premises at about six o'clock this morning, and the card was correct, and the card is not an instrument anybody can argue with, and nobody in this city is going to be able to tell me whose fault it is and that is the entire point of it."

"Beatrix."

"I have nine years of that wall in this book," said Beatrix Halm.

"And there are four dates in it I can arrive at without looking anything up, and the oldest of them is the morning the last plate came down."

"And I am going to write 8,131 under a floor of 8,146 at ten to six this morning and two words under that in brackets with the shade, and then I am going to go and stand at that wall again at about four this afternoon when the painter is on it, because I have never once in nine years read a wall twice in a day and I am going to today."

## Six Floors Of Washrooms

He was cleaning the fifth floor of the Fourth Branch at ten past seven with nine men waiting behind him for the door to open, and he did the fifth and the fourth and ate standing up at about eleven out of the staff kettle.

He did not know and could not have known and had no way of finding out that at about nine that morning, in a division off a third court, a woman with two hundred picture hooks on the wall of a side room had been handed a printed sheet about four inches by two with a box on it to fill in, and the premises at the head of it, and nothing else on the whole of it about anybody.

She read it at her desk. She filled the box in. It took her about four minutes and she did it in her own division, in her own hand, under her own name, at the head of it, and she put it in the out-tray, and she carried on with the morning.

She was the only person in that building that nobody was waiting on.

Two floors above her a man with a window on a yard was doing work he had been doing for nine years.

And four lines with her initials on the transmission above them sat in a minute at the end of a corridor in a building she has never been inside in her life and is never going to be inside. She has never read them. She is never going to.

She has been carrying the answer to them up sixty-one steps since the twentieth of February without being asked for it once.

## Four In The Afternoon

The requisition went into the next quarter at four o'clock on the afternoon of the thirtieth of March with everything written on it.

At ten to nine that morning a paper on a chain had gone out of a counting room with the figure for premises 34-6-118 on it, and it had not been entered as called, because a line carried forward on a quarter's sheet is not a fifth mark and the two have never once been confused except by people who did not work there.

At about four o'clock Beatrix Halm was back under the north end of the ranks wall in daylight with the notebook shut in her hand, and a painter was on the last of the ladder at the north end with a pot in his left hand, and the plate was up.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-one, in paint, in the green this square had been using since the fifteenth of November because the weekly takes what it is given and the quarterly shade was not available on a morning when a card came down at six.

Out of band.

"Say it," said Beatrix Halm, "once, out loud, at the volume I used in the dark, because that is how a wall is read and you have never once learned a different way of doing it."

"Out of band," said Lin Hao.

Eleven people crossing the square between the gate and the tram stopped, or did not stop and turned their heads, and it was nothing at all to about nine hundred people going to work.

It was the whole of what had happened since the twenty-ninth of December to eight women in a building with sixty-one steps in it, and not one of the eight knew that any of it was about them.

The green went blue in about forty minutes. It stayed until the rain took it. It was not the quarterly shade.

## Ninety Days

"Ninety days," said Beatrix Halm.

She was not reading the wall. She was reading the notebook.

"Fourteen weeks and six days off it in the autumn, and five weeks and three days back on. The whole of this city's memory is on the five weeks." She shut the book.

"And then ninety days of nothing at all, which is the thirteenth week, and I have written two words on that stone this morning for the first time since the thirtieth of December and I am going to go home and I am not going to be able to tell any of them what it means and they will work it out themselves by the end of the week."

She went down the sixty-one at about ten past five with the notebook against her chest.

He went up it at about ten past ten at night with a bad back and a plate of something out of a pot and eight women at a table under a window, and he told them the two figures and he did not say what they meant, and Nadia Ferrous did the sum out loud at the end of it because that is what she does with a number that has arrived in a room, and got fourteen, and was right.

The first of April was tomorrow.

A carry-forward sheet for an account of thirty years went on it and was administered from it by a woman who had certified the composition of her own household four times a year since the thirtieth of November and did not know it.

A return about a man held in a room with no window went to a dispensary on the Vestry arcade.

A requisition with the standing of three went across into a quarter it had not been laid in.

And a man who had been the floor of his band for ninety days on eight hands and four sheets of plain paper had one day left to put eight of them under a page again.

Seven women live at that address. One of them gave the address up at a counter on the morning of the thirteenth of March and cannot be got off a line she signed before that.

And the eighth name on that line is a broker from the Vant, who is not on a roll anywhere in this city, and who ate at a table with four chairs for the first time in eighteen months on the evening of the twenty-third of March.

# The Woman Who Recused Herself

She was not in her room on the morning of the fifth of March, and that was the first thing that told him anything, because in four months he had found Alma Quayle behind that door on every single occasion he had come down to this sub-level and had never once found her in a corridor.

She was on the bench outside it with a sheet of paper in her hand, face up, and Mrs Imrie was at the counter about eleven feet away with her back to them both, and the sheet was the one that had come back on the twenty-sixth of January with one word on it in the chair's hand.

"You have asked again."

"I have asked three times," said Alma Quayle. "The first two were in the first week of December and the chair said the word that is on this sheet and I did not show it to you."

"The third was on the second of February and I did not tell you about that one either."

"Sit down, because what I have to say is about why I have not been able to tell you about any of them. It takes about four minutes and it is the only four minutes of my life I have spent on this."

## Who Lifts A Recusal

"A recusal in that room is not lifted by the chair."

He looked at her.

"That is the whole of what I have been doing for eleven days," said Alma Quayle. "The chair is not the authority. I have known that since the first week of December and I have gone on asking her three times, and every time she has said *no* in one word, and every time I have gone away thinking that a woman who has been in that room for eleven years was being unkind to me."

"Then who."

"The subject. You." She said it level, which is how she gets the sentences that have cost her out.

"A recusal is not a thing a room takes against an officer. It is a thing the person it is against lifts, and there is no sheet for lifting one and there never has been."

"A room that is asked about it says *no*, because the room is not the office that can answer it. She has said *no* to me three times because I have asked her the wrong question three times, and she has done it kindly, and I did not let myself hear it for eleven days."

He sat down on the bench.

"How long does she wait."

"She will wait as long as you take," said Alma Quayle, "and she will not ask you, and she will refuse me every time I ask her, and she is not going to tell me when you have done it either, because that would be asking you twice."

She has been the head of classification for longer than he had been in this city when he first came and stood in this corridor, and there is not one thing she has ever wanted that she has been refused, and she has told him in the last four months that there is exactly one, and that she is not going to get it.

"It is the smallest thing anybody has ever refused anybody," she said. "Nine people in a room, four of them able to put a seal, and a woman who could do a thing nobody else in that room can do, sitting in a corridor because she signed something in the first week of December. There is no version of my life where I get it back that does not involve asking a man I have never been kind to."

## What He Does Not Do

He had about nine minutes and he used about six of them and he did not get a single sentence out, and Alma Quayle sat on the other end of the bench and let him.

He was going to be asked for the rest of his life whether he had lifted the recusal of the only officer in this city who could answer for his file, and the answer was going to be that he did not, and he was going to have to say why, and there were four reasons and three of them were good.

The first was the one he had expected: lifting it puts her name into a minute on a file he had opened himself on the twenty-third of February, and a recusal lifted by the subject is a recusal the subject asked for, and in about four years somebody reading that minute is going to find out that the man who opened a file lifted the recusal of the woman best placed to defend him in it.

The second was that he did not trust himself to be fair about it. He had spent four months making himself legible on paper, and there is a kind of man who reaches the point of deciding he deserves the best defence available, and he could not tell from the inside whether he was that man or whether he was frightened and dressing it as a principle.

The third was Sun Meiling, who could not be sworn, who was under a bar, and who would have been the first person at that table to ask what he had done.

He did not use any of them.

"You have spent eleven days not telling me and I am not going to ask you why," he said. "I am going to tell you what I am going to do, and then it is yours to carry."

## The Classification Paper

Alma Quayle went through the door behind her and came back out inside of two minutes with a different sheet, which she put on the bench between them without a word.

It was a page of about eleven lines with a heading, a date and a reference, and the date was the nineteenth of September.

"That is what started this," she said. "That is the paper that referred those premises for classification, and every single thing that has happened since the fifteenth of October happened because a man in this sub-level read that on the afternoon of the nineteenth of September and decided that a description was cheaper than a classification. It cost you nothing and it cost the eight women at your table everything and it is the reason a description of your own kitchen exists on a piece of paper."

He read it. He did not understand two thirds of it and he understood the rest.

"There is a box on it," he said.

"There is a box on it," said Alma Quayle, "and the box is headed *author*, and it is empty, and it has been empty since the nineteenth of September, and it cannot be filled."

"That is the whole reason this business cannot be put down in a sentence. Four offices in this city have spent eleven years trying to invent a way to write it down anyway and none of them has managed it, and there is a woman in this city who has told everybody that the not writing of it is the entire point."

He did not say anything about that, and she did not either.

"Now there are two reasons in this business," she said. "There is that one, which cannot be written down, and there is a description made at your kitchen table with four words and an hour against them, and that one can be written down, and it is in a minute in a room at the end of a corridor, and in about four years it is the only one of the two that anybody with a folder is going to read."

## The Sentence She Says Out Loud

Alma Quayle stood up and took the sheet of the nineteenth of September and went and put it on the counter in front of Mrs Imrie.

"Enter this," she said. "Verbatim. In my own hand, in a minute, with the time against it."

Mrs Imrie did not look up.

"That is your October finding, head. You sent that one on the twenty-sixth of January as a finding on method."

"That one went out as an instrument. This one goes out as a minute." Alma Quayle turned her round and made her listen.

"Mrs Imrie, I am about to say a sentence in this corridor and I am going to ask you to write it down. I want you to understand that a minute is a thing a stranger with a folder can read in about four years, and that I am doing it anyway, because a sentence that has only ever been on paper in my own drawer is a sentence I have not said."

"Go on, then."

Alma Quayle said it out loud, in her own building, at a counter, in the ordinary voice of a head of classification making an ordinary finding, and it took her about eleven seconds, and Mrs Imrie wrote all of it down including the words she got wrong the first time.

She signed for it. It went into a book. And at about four minutes to twelve on the morning of the fifth of March, a sentence that a woman had drafted on the nineteenth of October at eleven forty in the morning and had read once and had not sent, and had sent ninety-seven days later as an instrument, went into the standing record of a division in a second sub-level in the form of a minute.

## What That Is

He understood it on the hill and not before.

She had sent it once as a finding about method and nobody was asked to do anything with it and it was safe, because a finding about method is the safest thing in this city and nobody has ever been injured by one.

She had now said the same sentence out loud in a corridor and had it written down as a minute, and a minute is not an instrument and cannot be withdrawn and is not safe, because a minute is the ordinary record of a division and a division's ordinary record is what a stranger is given when he arrives with a folder.

She had said it out loud to be a person who had said a thing and not a woman holding a piece of paper.

And she had said it in a building with four officers in it, one of whom had written four words at a kitchen table and had been asked about them four days ago.

He went up the sixty-one steps at about half past one in the afternoon with a bench in his chest and twenty-five days to the end of the quarter, and he had not lifted the recusal, and the best classifier in this city had just put a sentence of her own into a book where nobody could take it back, and she had done it for him, and he had not asked her to, and there was not one instrument in the city that could have stopped her.

# The Intake Desk After Twenty Years

The intake desk on the Ninth Terrace shuts at four in the afternoon and has shut at four in the afternoon every working day for twenty years, and on the evening of the eighteenth of March he went up the walled road behind the tram line at about ten past six and found the hall dark and one lamp on behind a counter with a book open on it, which is not a thing that office does.

She was still there because the book does not get carried.

He had come up the walled road twice in his life. The first time had been a boy who wanted to see where the top of it was, and a man at the gate had told him the road was not a walk and had walked him back down it. The second time had been the second of December, for a form, at a counter in the daytime with four hundred people going past.

This was the first time he had been there in the evening, and the hall was about four times the size of the room it had to be, and the floor was stone, and there was one lamp lit behind the counter and the rest of the place was not dark so much as unlit, which is a different thing and which he had noticed.

## What An Intake Desk Is For

"You are staring at the room," she said. "Everybody who comes up here in the evening stares at the room. It is a hall for reading a plate into a stone and it has a counter in it because the law says the Ninth Terrace takes an intake at a public post, and a public post has to have a person at it, and there has been a person at it for twenty years."

"Not you. Somebody before you."

"There have been three." She turned a page over in the book without looking down.

"The first two were appointed. Appointed means on a roll somewhere, which means there is a tile on that wall with a version of them on it, and neither of them ever sat here at night. The third one was mine, and I have never been on a roll in my life, and there is no tile with my name on that wall anywhere. That is the best thing that has ever happened to me and I would not know how to say it, so I have just said it."

"Why not on a roll."

"Because a roll is a list a counter reads," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "You take an oath and you go on a roll and it is written down once, and after that you are legible."

"You come in here and you give me a name and a plate and I read it back at you and it goes in a book and neither of us can be got at. I have spent twenty years being a woman that nine hundred people a week hand a piece of themselves to and cannot afterwards say a word about it."

She looked at him for the first time.

"That is not modesty. Modesty is what people call it when they have not got a word. I know exactly what it is."

## Three Things

"You are the man from Ferrier Street," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and there have been two pieces of paper about your house through my hand and one of them was made at this counter, and the other one is not mine to talk about at a desk on a night when I am on my own."

"I have come about my brother."

"I know why you have come about your brother. Everybody who comes up this road comes up it about a brother and about four in ten of them come about something else and lie about the brother for the first ten minutes." She did not get up. "Three things and then I am going back to my book, and they are not terms, because I have nothing to trade with and terms are what you say when you have got something."

"Say them."

"One. You will not ask me for the page."

He had it half formed in his mouth.

"And I will not offer it. It is not a coin. It is not in my gift. That book has never come off this desk in twenty years and it is not coming off it tonight and it is not coming off it for you, and if it came off it for you then it would have to come off it for the next one, and there have been forty-one on the pages in that book since before you were born."

"Two," she said. "You will not write the floor down."

The hall was very quiet and the lamp made one small yellow thing on the counter.

"That is not a strange thing to ask for. That is the most ordinary thing anybody has ever said at this counter and about forty people a year say it. You get to a number and you want it in a pocket. Everyone wants a number in a pocket. And I say no, and the reason I say no is not about you and not about your brother."

"What is it about."

"It is about the only fact this desk has ever had," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "A floor is a room. A floor in a document is a door that has been written down, and a door that has been written down gets opened by somebody in about four years who is not anybody in this story, and he will not be cruel about it, he will simply be working."

"And three."

"You will not come back up this road about it," she said. "That is the third and that is the real one and the other two are manners."

"That is not a small thing to be given by a woman on a first night."

"It is the only thing I have got and you are the first person to ask me for any of it."

She put both hands flat on the open book, one on each side of the page she was on. "If you come back up here about your brother then this counter is a place where a man comes about his brother, and in about four years it will be a place a stranger sends a paper about your brother. Neither of those is a place I want to work. That is the whole of it and there is nothing else in it."

## The Book

It is a bound ledger about two inches thick, the boards gone soft at the corners, and it is ruled in the front office's own hand in a standard that has not changed in twenty years because nobody has ever been asked to change it.

A date. A name. A floor. A direction.

The direction is the only part of it that means anything to anybody but me, and it is the part that made this book a book instead of a list. Every name on those pages went up. That is the whole content of it. A person came up this road or was brought up it, went up to a floor of the walled terrace at the top of it, and did not come down, and I wrote it down, because somebody has to write it down.

There are forty-one lines. He counted them twice.

He is on the eleventh page and the writing is the same writing, small and level and very fast, the writing of a woman who had four hundred names a day going past a counter and learned to write them without looking down.

*Second day of the month. Lin Wei. Seventh floor. Up.*

The page did not move when he put his hand out towards it.

She put her own hand flat on the paper, on top of his, not to stop him and not to help him, and the whole weight of her went through about four inches of paper and a counter.

## The Page She Would Not Give Him

"No," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

"That is my brother."

"That is a name in my hand and a date and a floor and a word, and it is the only thing in this building about him that has ever been written down by anybody who saw him, and I am not giving it to you."

"I am going to tell you exactly why, and then you are going to leave, and I am going to have said more to you in six minutes than I have said to anybody at this desk in about four years, which is a thing I have decided to do and not a thing you have done."

"Say it."

"He came up that stair on his own feet," she said.

He put his hand on the counter.

"He was not brought up. I have been asked four times in twenty years whether people are brought up or walk up and I have said yes three times, because three of them were, and the fourth one was a man in his own shoes with his own hands empty at both sides and nobody touching him anywhere, and I wrote it down and I put the direction up because up is what happened, and I have not said that out loud to anybody since the night I wrote it."

The hall made a sound like that.

"So you have got the floor and the date and the direction," she said, "and you have got them in your head and there is nothing you can do with any of them, and that is the truth and I have never softened it and I am not going to start."

## What She Was Allowed To Say

"Three facts," said Lin Hao.

"Three facts and the order they are in and I am going to say them in that order because after tonight I am not going to say them again and a fact you say in the wrong order sounds like a story."

She took her hand off the paper.

"Seventh floor. Second day of the month, three years ago this month. Up."

He wrote nothing down. There was a pen on that counter four inches from his hand and it had been there the whole time and it belonged to an office and not to him.

"Now say what you are going to do with it," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I am asking you, because in twenty years nobody has ever asked me that and because if you say nothing then I have given three facts to a man who came up here for one page and got one page's worth, and that is the correct trade and I would like it recorded that I thought so."

"I am going to write it down."

"Then come back up this road tomorrow and I will ask you for it again," she said, "and you can stand there and tell me you have written it down, and I will ask you for it again, and there is nothing else on this counter to sell you and I am not going to invent something."

## The One Thing He Had

He had been at that counter about a quarter of an hour and he had got nothing he came for and three things he had not asked for, and he stood there and made a decision that took about four seconds and that he had been carrying since the fifteenth of October.

"Dagmar Oyelowo."

"That is my name and you have not used it until now and I would like you to know that I noticed."

"I can put you on a roll."

The lamp buzzed very slightly, the way a lamp does when nothing at all is happening near it.

"Not tonight and not with a form," he said. "There is an instrument and it wants a rank nine, and there are nine of those in this city, and one of them cleans washrooms. It is one piece of paper and about eleven minutes and a man comes to a counter and reads your name out of a book. After that there is a tile, and a line, and a division that knows where you are, and you can be asked things."

"Yes," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "Go on."

"You would not have to take an intake nine hundred times a week ever again. You could be put somewhere and be paid and be legible, and a woman in a corridor off a third court would know your name in four years."

"No," she said. "I would be legible."

He had expected the no. He had not expected it to be that short.

"That is the entire reason," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and here is the rest of it, once. In twenty years about four hundred people have come up that road wanting a number out of me. Every one of them left with something they can put on a table to prove they had been here, and eleven came back a second time to ask me whether I had written it down, and I said yes to all eleven, and not one of them has ever come back to ask me about myself."

She put one finger on the open book.

"You are the first man who has come up here in five months and had something he could give me. That is not a thing that happens at this counter. It happens in the rooms at the end of corridors where somebody can sign something, and I have never been in one of those rooms in my life and I am not going to be."

"You told me two minutes ago that you wanted to be wanted."

"I did," she said. "And what I want is a man on that road at half past six in the evening asking me whether I wrote it down. I do not want a tile."

## Yes

She said it about eleven minutes later and she said it standing up, in the middle of an empty hall on a walled road above a tram line, with a lamp on and a ledger open and forty-one names in it, to a man whose name is at the top of a page eight hundred yards away and who has never once in five months asked her for a single thing.

"Yes," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

He did not understand it for a moment and then he understood all of it and had to put his hand on the counter.

"There is no tile on that Board with my name on it," she said. "Twenty years at a public post and there is no tile and there is no line and there is nothing on anything, because I have never scored once and I have never been in a room where anybody could have measured me and there is no room in this city where they would put me if I walked in and asked."

"And you are the last line on it," she said, "the top line, the only one anybody looks at, and you came up a walled road at ten past six at night to ask me for a page of a book, and I have wanted somebody to come up this road and ask me for a page for about eleven years, and you are the first one, and you are not getting it."

That was the whole of the yes and she said it at a volume that would have carried to the tram line.

"Do not thank me," she said, "because I have watched this city for twenty years and there is not one instrument in it that can say thank you to a woman and mean it, and I would rather be wanted than thanked and I have said so out loud, in an empty hall, once."

He put his hand out and stopped it about six inches short of the counter and took it back, because there were three things she had put in front of him and he had given back no word to any of them, and one of them still had about an hour left on it, and she was standing four feet away and would have been able to count to about nine in the time it took him to change his mind.

"Say the other thing," said Dagmar Oyelowo. "Whatever it is you have not said. You have said *no* twice tonight and you have not said one single thing out of what you came up here with."

He stood at that counter and looked at about forty-one names in a small level hand.

"I came up here to find out whether there is a way into that floor," he said, "and there is not, and I knew there would not be, and I have known since the twenty-third of February, and I came anyway, because I have spent eight years being a man who is exactly legible in the one thing that costs him nothing and I have never once come up a road for nothing in my life."

"That is the first thing you have said tonight that I am going to keep," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I have kept a book for twenty years, so it is not a small thing to say to me, and I am not saying anything more about it."

She picked the pen up off the counter and put it back down in exactly the same place.

"Now go down the hill before the gate closes, and go home, and think about it, and do whatever you are going to do about it without me in the room."

## Afterwards

He went down the walled road at about half past seven in the evening with a seventh floor, a second day of the month, three years ago, and the word up, and no means of getting to any of them.

There was a form somewhere in this city with a file and a sub-level and a transfer receipt out of a held account in it, and there was a woman in a clinic with a return about a man held, and there was a man on the third floor of a building who had written four lines and asked to be left off the distribution of anything further upon the one.

None of those things could reach the seventh floor of the walled terrace at the top of the tram line, and there was no instrument he had ever met in eight years that could, and the only woman who knew how to get there had just told him not to come back up the road.

He got to the Vestry arcade at about eight and stood at the doors for a while watching Nadia Ferrous work nine hundred people.

She was counting. Of course she was counting; she had been counting since the fifteenth of October, and a woman who counts is not a thing anybody in this city has ever found an instrument for, and on the thirteenth of March she had gone to a counter at nine forty-one and taken herself off a list, and here she was on the evening of the eighteenth doing the only thing she knows how to do with a thing she has given away.

He went home up sixty-one steps and did not tell the table, and then he went back down to his own front door and wrote three lines on the back of a kitchen goods docket with a pencil.

A seventh floor and a date and a direction is not a story anybody is going to believe, and a woman with no gift at all had put three things in front of him and he had given her nothing back, and he was going to have to break one of the three before he had it inside his own front door.

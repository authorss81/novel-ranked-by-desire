# The Fourth Hand On The Line

Marguerite Vale went to the room at the end of the passage off the second landing at about nine in the morning on the twenty-third of March, on her own, and told him about it on the afternoon of the twenty-second, in four rooms off a hotel corridor, with the wind coming off the tram line and a cup she had not drunk out of.

"You will not come with me."

"You will not stop me."

"I will stop you," said Marguerite Vale. "I have been in nine offices in this city over nine years and I have never once been in that building, and I am going on the morning of the twenty-third, at about nine, and you are going to be in a washroom on the fifth floor of the Fourth Branch at that hour, and we are both going to find out in the afternoon what happened."

## Why A Broker Goes To That Room

A requisition standing undecided when a quarter closes neither dies nor lies down. From the first of April it is administered weekly, and administration is a return, and the return has nine ruled lines on it, one for each seat, and under the nine there is a tenth line headed *composition of the holding* with a box on it that has to be countersigned at the address.

The fourth of the nine lines has said *vacant* since the twelfth of February.

"That is the seat a man laid an entry about in a keeping book in February and has not been in since," said Marguerite Vale. "And a line that says *vacant* is not a hole. A line that says *vacant* is a sentence. It says this room is one short of nine, and it has said it in every copy of every weekly return since the twelfth of February, and every clerk who reads it reads the same thing."

"What does it get them."

"It gets them a room that cannot carry anything, which is true, and it gets it at the bottom of a weekly return that nobody reads."

## What She Went There To Do

"And here is what I am going to do about it," she said, "and I am telling you the night before because I am not going to tell you the morning after."

"Say it."

"I am going to write my name on the fourth line."

He put his cup down.

"Not in place of anything," said Marguerite Vale.

"There is no replacing on that line and there is no filling and no electing and no instrument of any kind in this city that would put a woman into a seat of that room, and I am not going to try, because the chair would refuse it and she would be right to."

"I am going to write my name in a line on a return in the ordinary way, as a person who is present, the way any person may write their name in a box at a counter."

"That does not fill the seat."

"It fills nothing. It fills nothing at all."

She turned the cup round on the cloth.

"That is why I want it."

"Every division in this city that keeps a roll of who somebody is has got me on nothing, and there is not going to be one, because the Vant's books are not a roll and there is no instrument in this city that will enter a guild broker's standing anywhere at all."

"If the Vant is gone in two years I am gone with it and not one person in Ostara would be able to say where I went."

I have wanted to be findable since I was thirty-one and I have never once said that to anybody.

"You have said it now."

"I have said it to you, and you are going to keep it, and I am going to go and write my name in a line of a return in a room at the end of a corridor, and if you think those are the same sentence then you have not been listening for nine years."

## The Slip In His Coat

His hand went into the inside pocket of his coat without him deciding to, the way a hand does when it has forgotten a thing is in there, and his fingers found the edge of it and stopped.

She watched him do it and did not ask.

"That is the slip you have been carrying since the sixteenth," said Marguerite Vale. "With a figure on it that is neither seven nor eight, and a line at the bottom that I ruled myself with a straight edge in September because I did not know what the line was for."

"You have not asked me about it in ten days."

"I have not asked you about it because I ruled the line and I did not know what the line was for, and a woman who does not know what her own line is for has no standing to ask anybody." She turned the cup round on the cloth. "Have you looked at it."

"No."

"Not once in ten days," said Marguerite Vale. "You have had the only instrument in this arrangement with my name at the foot of it inside your coat for ten days and you have not taken it out, and I am not going to ask you to take it out tonight, and I would like it noticed that I noticed."

## The Part Of It That Costs

"And the part that costs, since you will work it out on your own in about four minutes and come and ask me," said Marguerite Vale, "is that a name written by a person in a line is entered by a clerk out of a book, and a book in that room is not a document anybody can correct."

"It is not one of the sheets on a table."

"It is a return, and a return is a copy of a book, and the book is the book."

"So you cannot take it off it."

"I could not take it off it if I wanted to and I would not want to and I want that written down, because in about four years a stranger with a folder will read a weekly return with my name in the fourth line, and he will want to know what a guild broker from the Vant was doing in the middle of a requisition about eight women at a kitchen table, and there is no answer to that question that does not cost somebody."

"Which somebody."

"Me," said Marguerite Vale. "And it has been me at some point since the sixteenth of September for six months and I have not minded it once, and I would like it noticed that I did not come up here tonight for this and that I am doing it anyway and that those are two separate things."

## She Said Which One

He did not answer that.

"Nadia Ferrous gave up an address to get off every paper with that address at the head of it," she said. "I watched that happen at that table on the fifteenth and I have thought about almost nothing else for eight days. And there is no version of it where the woman who took herself off the list gets a line put back."

"I am not asking you for anything," said Marguerite Vale, "and I want that said out loud in this room tonight because you have been not asking me things since September and I would rather we both had it in the open."

"Tomorrow at nine I go up a stair on my own and I write a name in a box in a room."

"That is the whole of my day and it costs me the only thing I have and you will have been asleep for three hours of it."

"Then what did you want tonight."

## The Table She Does Not Eat At

She looked at the table.

There have been four chairs at that table for the nine years she has had the rooms and she has not eaten at it since the autumn before last, and the reason is not fastidiousness.

She took a commission on the one in Ferrier Street about six weeks after she moved in here, and a woman cannot both hold a commission on a house and have her own name at the door of one, and that has been the whole of it for six months and there has never been a second reason.

"Sit down," said Marguerite Vale. "No. Sit down. I have watched you stand in this room four times since the sixteenth of September and you are going to do it again in about nine days or you are going to be somewhere in this city where a thing with a number on it goes out, and there is no reason on this earth why you should do that standing up in a room with a cup in your hand."

He sat down. She sat down opposite him, which she had not done in nine years, and there was a cloth on the table and two chairs at it and neither of them had been in them.

There was a plate on the table with the cold of something on it, and there were two plates, because there were two of everything in the press in the second room off a hotel corridor for nine years and nobody had ever come to the table.

She put her hands in her lap for about four seconds and then used them.

"You are going to be different on the thirtieth of March," she said, between two mouthfuls, as though she were discussing a delivery. "I want to say that I have known since the morning of the fourteenth and I have said nothing, and I have said nothing because there is not one thing in this business I can do about it, and because a broker who tells a client about the weather is a broker who has stopped being paid."

"Marguerite—"

"Eat," said Marguerite Vale. "It is a bad plate and I have made it twice and I have not cooked for anybody in this room in nine years, and you are going to be no good at this conversation for about nine days and I would rather you were eating while it happens."

Seven days to the last working day of the quarter, and a woman was going to write her name in the fourth line of a return in a room at the end of a corridor in about fourteen hours, in a hand that was not a clerk's hand, and it would not put anybody in a seat and would not be able to be taken out.

# A Refusal That Goes In A Book

Beatrix Halm was sitting on the top step of the sixty-one with the notebook shut, which she had not done once in nine years, and when he came up at about a quarter to eight in the morning she had it flat on her knee with both hands on top of it.

"A woman came down those steps at ten past seven," she said. "She went in at the bottom and she came out again in about four minutes and she put something under the door of the flat and she went up."

"Did she say anything to you."

"She did not say a word and she did not have to, because I know the back of about eleven people in this city by their shoes and she is not one of the eleven."

Beatrix turned the notebook over on her knee. "And I am going to tell you the rest of it and not the rest of it, because I write the time down against everything you do and I am not writing this against anything, and if you ask me who it was I will put the pencil behind my ear and go inside."

He picked the docket up off the kitchen table, where it had been lying since about half past seven.

The stair had been cold and the doors had been shut and nobody else had been up. A piece of paper under a door at the bottom of sixty-one steps at ten past seven in the morning is the only kind of news this house has produced since the fourteenth of January.

The declaration of holding had been laid in a room and it had cost eight women a signature. This had been slid under a door by a woman who had been in her clothes and had gone straight back up, and nothing on the face of it asked anybody for anything.

The face of it was a departmental docket with a wing stamp in the top left corner and a number under the stamp and a ruled box across the bottom of it in two columns, the first for the hour of an attendance and the second for what was done about it.

In the first column, in a clerk's hand, eleven forty.

In the second column, in the same hand, in capitals, DECLINED.

And under the box, smaller, in the same hand, eleven forty-four.

He looked at that for a while.

A clerk who writes the hour of an attendance and then writes the time at which she entered it has been made to wait four minutes and has written down that she waited, and no clerk in this city writes the second time down unless somebody has made her.

## The Back Of A Docket

He turned it over.

The back of a docket is where routing goes, and the routing had been used, so what was on it sat in the middle where a routing is not. Two lines in a small hand he had read off four hundred filing slips for three years.

*You will want to ask me about this. Ask me nothing before the twentieth.*

*It was mine to do and I did it on my own and I am telling you afterwards and not before, because a woman who tells a man first is doing it for him.*

He read it four times and then put it flat on the table with the printed side up, because that is how a thing goes back where it came from.

There was nothing else. There was no enclosure and there was no copy and there was no note of what had been declined, and no docket number for an answer, because a declined attendance has no answer.

The number in the top left corner was the number of the attendance and not the number of anything that came out of it, and it would be in a book somewhere for four years with a clerk's name against it, and the clerk's name would be the name of a woman who has no reason in the world to know any of this.

## What A Refusal Is

He had known four days that something had gone up that stair and had not gone down.

She had gone quiet on the sixteenth after telling him she would bring a consult to a man on the third floor by the twentieth, and the twentieth had come and gone on the twenty-first without a sound.

He had decided somewhere in the middle of that silence that she had changed her mind or that she had been talked out of it, and he had not gone up to find out, because a man who goes up sixty-one iron steps to ask a question gets an answer about the stairs.

What he had not understood until the morning of the twenty-seventh was that she had done it and had done it in the building.

A consult is a document. An attendance is a person in a room on the record, and the record takes her answers down and initials them and puts them in a file, and an answer that its own drafter will not stand behind in the room it was drafted in is not an answer from a wing. It is an opinion from a woman.

So it goes back.

And a departmental question that goes back is raised again, and a departmental question raised again is raised on the person who drafted it, in the ordinary way, because that is where the drafter is written and there is nowhere else for it to be written.

She had put her own name at the head of a departmental paper about a transfer that carries no name.

That was the price, and she had named it to him on the sixteenth of January in a room with two hundred sheets on the wall without softening it and without explaining it afterwards, and it had taken her ten days and a booking and no discussion at all to pay it.

## What He Did About It

He did nothing about it.

He went down the sixty-one steps at about ten to eight and caught the tram and was at the Fourth Branch at about a quarter to nine, and he cleaned six floors of washrooms for the rest of the day with the office filling around him.

On the third floor a woman with a bad back wanted to know about the water and he told her it was hot, and she said it never was at that hour. That was the whole of what was said to him that day by anybody whose name he could not have told you.

At about two in the afternoon he stood at a rail on the fourth landing with a mop in his hand and did the arithmetic he had been not doing since the twenty-sixth of January.

It ran like this.

On the sixteenth she had told him at about eight in the evening that a man on the third floor had sent for her, that she had gone up to see him for eleven minutes, that she had said she would bring a consult, and that he had four days and nothing he could do about any of it.

The four days ran out on the twentieth. He had gone up that stair twice since and had found nothing, and he had decided somewhere in the middle of the silence that she had been talked out of it.

That was the whole of what he knew at half past seven that morning and the whole of it was wrong. The woman it was about had done the thing on the twenty-sixth at eleven forty-four in the morning, while he was cleaning six floors of washrooms on the other side of the city. She had walked sixty-one steps, put a piece of paper under his door, gone back up, and said nothing at all to the woman on the top step who had been awake since five.

Sun Meiling could not be sworn. She had been under a bar from the first of September and she was never going to be sworn, and she had said so to him in December in front of eight women and had not said it as a complaint.

And a woman who cannot be sworn is a woman who cannot be made to attend anything.

She had just proved it. At eleven forty in the morning on the twenty-sixth of January she had been asked to be in a room and had said no, and a clerk had entered it with the hour and then the minute she entered it, and a booking book on the second floor of a building off a third court now said, in a clerk's hand, that a woman who cannot be compelled had asked to be told twice and had declined both times inside four minutes.

There was no office in this city that could make her go. There was no office in this city that could make her do anything at all, ever, and she had been telling him that since September as though it were a small thing, and it was the only piece of luck this household has had since the fifteenth of October.

And it had cost her the one thing she has spent three years and one bar and one wing protecting, which is the fact of not having a name at the head of anything.

## At The Table That Night

He said it out loud at the table that night, which he had not planned to do and could not afterwards account for, and he read out the two lines on the back of the docket and the hour and the word DECLINED and the four minutes.

Petra Ilyin put her forearms on the table.

"Right," she said. "Say the rest of it and get it over with. You have been sitting on it since seven this morning."

"An attendance is not a consult. A consult is a document and it goes into a queue and a queue is a thing that happens to a paper. An attendance is a person in a room and the room takes down what they say and initials it and puts it in a file with their name at the head of it."

"And a drafter who will not be in the room."

"And a drafter who will not be in the room has written an opinion," said Petra Ilyin. "She has not written an answer. The department will not issue an opinion with a wing stamp on it, and it will not send it back to be corrected, because there is nothing to correct."

"It goes back into the queue with her initials still in the corner of it. And the queue takes a raised question at the front of it, because a raised question is the only kind that moves." She turned her cup round on the table. "Every raised question in this city carries the name of the person who raised it."

Nadia Ferrous had her hands in her lap.

"So it is her name," she said. "Not on the answer. On the question. On a piece of paper that says a departmental question about a transfer that carries no name has been raised by Sun Meiling on the morning of the sixteenth of February, and there is no number on it, and there is an address under it that a clerk will find in about nine seconds if anybody in this city ever decides to look for it."

"There is no number on it," said Sun Meiling. "That is not a small thing and every one of you has just walked past it."

The room went quiet for about a second and a half, which in that kitchen is a long time.

"Say it," said Petra Ilyin.

"A raised question with no number cannot be answered by anybody and cannot be chased by anybody," said Sun Meiling. "It can sit. It can sit for ever. It is the only shape of paper in this city that gets worse the longer anybody looks at it and better the longer nobody does."

"That is why she did it," said Lin Hao.

"That is why she did it," said Sun Meiling. "And I am not going to explain it a second time this evening and I am not going to be thanked and I am going to bed."

She got up and took her cup to the sink and did not turn the tap on.

Beatrix Halm had gone inside by the time he came up at about half past six and had left the notebook on the step, open, at a page with nothing on it.

He did not write anything under it, and he stood there in the cold with a docket in his coat that had a woman's refusal in a clerk's hand on it, and the thing he could not get past was the four minutes.

Eleven forty was the hour. Eleven forty-four was the minute she wrote it down. In between there was a clerk standing at a counter holding a docket and being told no, and the clerk had not put the docket down, and had not gone out, and had waited, because that is what happens if you let the moment pass and the person who has come to refuse you is still in the room.

"Ask me nothing before the twentieth," the docket said.

He had until the twentieth of February. On the ninth of that stretch two women he could not argue with would call a sitting for the twentieth, and on the twentieth a sheet would be read for nine minutes in a room at the end of a corridor.

None of that was on the docket and none of it was hers, and she had known none of it when she wrote *ask me nothing* on the back of a form at half past six in the morning, at a desk two floors up in a building off a third court, and had it under a door on Ferrier Street at ten past seven.

He went in and did not ask.

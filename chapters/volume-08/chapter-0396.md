# The Woman Who Spoke And Was Believed

The clerk who wrote four words down at a kitchen table one morning in the last week of October came to Ferrier Street at about five in the afternoon on the twenty-sixth of March, and she stood on the step in her coat with her hand on the frame a shade longer than a woman with a parcel does.

There was no bundle. She had come about a quarter of an hour and she was going to be given a cup of tea she would not drink.

"It is not a requisition," said Mrs Imrie. "I want to say that before anybody in that kitchen says anything at all, because I know what it is not and I have had three weeks to be certain of it."

"What is it."

"A notice served at the premises. There is a division that does not sit in the Wing and keeps no list of addresses and it has the fourth part of a sheet, which is a description of this address put over it on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October, at about eleven, against the head, and it requires the person who made the entry to attend at the address the entry describes."

She said it the way a woman reads out a rule she has been made to learn. "They cannot reach me. They have never been able to reach me. So they have written to the house."

## What She Was Asked

"It took about eleven minutes," said Mrs Imrie. "That was on the twenty-fourth, at a counter on the second floor, and a man read the entry back at me and then read a question under it, and then I answered it, and then he wrote my answer down, and then he read that back too."

"What was the question."

"He asked me why I wrote it."

The kitchen went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with anybody in it.

"And you said."

"I said because it is cheaper." Mrs Imrie said it to the middle of the doorframe. "I said the words, because they were on the entry, because they were in his mouth at the time and I wrote them in his hand because that is what an entry is. And he wrote that down as well, and read it back to me, and asked whether there was anything else I wished to say."

"What did you say to that."

"I said no," said Mrs Imrie. "And then he thanked me, and I went down, and I walked to the tram, and I am the woman who read it back twice."

Nobody at the table said anything for about eleven seconds.

"You could have said it was somebody else's line," said Nadia Ferrous.

"It was somebody else's line," said Mrs Imrie. "That is exactly what it was. It is not my line and it was never my line and there are four people in this room who were standing at that table when it was said." She took her hand off the frame.

"And I am not going to say that at a counter on the second floor, because the moment I say whose line it was, then this house is an office's way of reaching the woman who made the entry, and I would have handed them a name and gone home and it would have been in a minute by six o'clock."

"Which is what she said would happen," said Wren Kallo, from the far side of the table.

He looked up.

Wren Kallo had not been in the room when Mrs Imrie came and had come in about three minutes afterwards and had heard all of it standing up.

"Yes," said Wren Kallo. "In the ordinary way, on the third of January, with my sleeves rolled and my hands in the water, in a kitchen, in front of seven people, and I said she would go home and be the woman who read it, and she has."

## The Sentence He Was Not Allowed

He got up off the bench.

He had about four seconds in which he could have said the thing, which was that the four words on that entry were the ones he had said at his own table on the morning of the twenty-seventh of October and that everything since had come out of them, and that he had known it for a hundred and forty-nine days and had let a woman stand at a counter and answer for them.

He had been told in October, in a room with three conditions in it, that there was exactly one question he might not put. There were two women in this kitchen to whom he might not put one question and he had been keeping to it since October with about nine minutes a day of effort.

So he said, "Thank you for coming," and Mrs Imrie said, "Do not thank me, I came about a quarter of an hour and I am going," and she went down the sixty-one steps, and the stair did not make a sound under her, because she is a woman who works on her feet and goes down a stair twice a day.

Four days to the last working day of the quarter, and he had a piece of paper in a building off a hotel corridor that a broker had ruled a line under with a straight edge, and he had not looked at it since the sixteenth.

## Half Past Six

He took the tram out past the laundry and walked the hill at about ten past seven on the evening of the twenty-sixth of March.

She put three things in front of him in an empty hall on the eighteenth with a lamp on and a bound ledger open on the counter, and he had given back no word to any of them. Three things: not to ask for the page, not to write the floor down, and not to come back up this road about it.

He had broken the second of them on the morning of the nineteenth with a carpenter's pencil in ninety seconds and had signed it with his own name, and a woman in a kitchen had told him within the hour that a piece of paper with a name at the foot of it can be produced by anybody who finds it.

This was the third one.

She was still there, which is not a thing that office does, and she had not opened the book.

"You said that if I came back up here about my brother then this counter becomes a place a man comes about his brother."

"I said exactly that."

"I have not come about my brother," said Lin Hao. "I have come about your first condition and not about my brother, and it is your counter, and you may send me down the hill now and I will go and I will not come a third time."

Dagmar Oyelowo looked at him for a long time and then she turned the book round so that it faced her again and squared it, and she said, "Say the question."

## The Question She Wanted

"Not about the page," he said. "I am not going to ask you about the page."

"Then there is nothing on this counter for you."

"There is one thing," said Lin Hao, "and you said it to me in an empty hall on the eighteenth of March and you said you had been wanting it for about eleven years and nobody had ever asked you, and I have thought about nothing else for eight days, and I am not going to stand here and ask you what your gift is because you have not got one and there is no tile with your name on that wall."

"Then what."

"What is the first thing you did this morning," he said, "before nine hundred people came past your counter."

## What A Woman With No Gift Does

She had to think about it, and the thinking took long enough that he understood it had never been asked.

"Counted the doors," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

"There are nine people a day at that counter who want a plate read into a stone and about four hundred who do not know they want anything, and every one of the four hundred comes to the same counter and goes past the same desk."

"I count the doors every morning before the queue starts, because if the count is wrong then everything after it is wrong, and it is the only arithmetic I do and it is not a gift and it is not on any roll and it does not cost me anything."

"That is not what I meant."

"I know it was not what you meant." She shut the book. "And I am going to tell you something I have not said out loud to anybody in twenty years and I am saying it because a man came up a hill for the third time and came about this instead of about his brother, and there is no instrument in this city that can record it, which is the only reason I am saying it."

"Say it."

"There are two people alive in this city who know what is written in that book on the page about a man on the seventh floor of a terrace at the top of this road," said Dagmar Oyelowo.

"You and me. You have written it on a docket in pencil under a cloth on a kitchen table in a house with eight women in it, where a woman with a rule about things being asked could read it at any hour of any day, and I have never written a word of it anywhere and I have no gift and there is nothing in this building that would let me remember it for you if you lost it."

She put both hands flat on the closed book.

"So that is what you broke your own condition for," she said, "and I am not going to say thank you for that either, because you did not do it for me and I have been watching men in this hall for twenty years and I know exactly what it looks like when a man does a thing for himself and puts it in front of somebody else so that it looks like it was for them."

He put his hand out and took it back.

"I have said your name once this evening," said Dagmar Oyelowo, "and I did not say it because I wanted to. I said it because you came about me and there is not one other word for that, and you may write that down or you may not, and if you do write it down I will know, because I will be the one who reads it back."

He went down the walled road at about a quarter to eight having broken two of three things in eight days, with the first the only one of hers still standing, and the seventh floor of a terrace was three years and one month old and stood in a direction nothing in this city travels in, and it was in the head of a woman who had no gift at all and would not be here for ever.

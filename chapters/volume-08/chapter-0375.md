# The Signature Under The Answer

*S.M.*

He had seen them twice before in his life and both times were in the same month.

The first was at half past eight in the evening on the sixteenth of January, in a side room off an iron stair, out of a drawer that had not been opened in three years, initialled in the margin against the fourth line of a dead draft in a woman's own hand.

The second was at ten past nine on the morning of the twenty-sixth of February, at a side table at the end of a corridor, read out by a clerk who read out everything the same way, against the fourth line of a reply that had stopped a question dead three years before.

He had spent a night and a half of it not being able to put the three characters down anywhere.

## The Stair

He did not write it down. He was the kind of man who had written down four sheets of plain paper in forty minutes in a kitchen and put his own name at the top of them, and he had been unable to write down a file number on the back of his hand, and he understood perfectly well why, because a file number is the one thing in this city that cannot be carried by a man who is described and not classified.

So he carried it. And on the evening of the twenty-seventh he went up the sixty-one steps at about half past six knowing that he was going to have to keep it in his head, and he got as far as the hall.

Sun Meiling was standing in the hall with her coat on.

## She Told Him First

"I have been waiting for you since four," she said, "because I have had it since the twentieth and there is nobody in this city I am permitted to tell and you are going to have to be the one I tell, and I have had a week to decide whether that was a decision or a convenience, and it was a convenience, and I am telling you it was a convenience so that you do not waste any part of tonight on it."

"Tell me."

"Not in a hall." She came in and shut the door behind them and did not take her coat off. "I have already said the part that mattered and it took four seconds and I would like to do it at that table where I have said difficult things before."

They went up. Eight women were eating and the table went the way it goes when two people come in late, and eight pairs of eyes went over her coat and stopped, and she sat down and he sat down and she put her hands flat on the table in the manner of a woman reading an instrument.

"I went up on the twentieth," said Sun Meiling. "With the consult in my hand. Eleven minutes again, and it was the second time in three years I had been on that floor and it will be the last."

"And he answered you."

"He stood in the corridor outside his own door so that we were both standing," she said, "because he would not ask me in, and he answered me in four sentences, and then he thanked me by name for the draft and went back in, and that was the whole of the visit."

## Four Sentences

"Tell me the four sentences."

"No." She looked up. "That is the second thing and I want it said plainly, so listen. I have come home with what he said in a corridor and I am not going to give it to you sentence by sentence, and not because of the discipline, and I am going to tell you exactly why."

"Because it is a document."

"Because it is not a document," said Sun Meiling, "and that is the entire difference between him and me, and it is the thing I have been not understanding for three years. I understood it on the twentieth, standing in a corridor, while he was saying it."

"He gave me an answer in words. He did not give me a sheet. He stood in a corridor and he said four sentences and he did not write one of them down and he did not offer me anything to take away, and when I asked him whether there was a paper he said there was nothing in writing to give me and there never would be."

She put one finger on the tablecloth.

"He asked, three years ago, to be left off the distribution of anything further about that transfer. That is what my four sentences were about. Not the transfer."

"It was about the fact that there was a transfer, and that the question was going to keep coming, and that every time it came somebody in his position would have to do something about it. He asked once, in writing, in advance, to be left off it. And he was. And so he was never asked again."

"And so the last answer anybody got from that floor is the one standing in front of me in a corridor at four o'clock on the twentieth of February."

She picked her knife up and put it down again without using it.

"So I have the answer," she said, "and I got it in about eleven seconds in a corridor from a man who asked to be left off the list, and there is no paper in this city that shows it."

"And I have carried it up sixty-one steps tonight to give to the only man I am allowed to give it to, and he has just heard it from me in a kitchen in front of eight women who will know inside a minute that something has happened, and that is my fault, and I did it anyway."

## What He Did With It

He had four lines in a minute in a room at the end of a corridor and a woman across a table who had the same answer in a different shape and did not know there was a piece of paper anywhere in this city.

Beatrix Halm had her notebook open on the cloth and she had not written in it, and she turned it round so that the table could see that it was empty, which she has done twice in nine years and both times it meant that something had happened that she was not allowed to put a time against.

Sun Meiling read the turned notebook and closed it with one hand.

"Not tonight," she said.

"Not tonight," said Beatrix Halm. "I am writing *the twenty-seventh* and a rule, and there is no room on the page for what the rule is, and I have been doing that since the twelfth of January."

She wrote the date and shut the book, and nobody at that table asked her what the rule was, because there has never been a version of this house in which anybody at that table asks Beatrix Halm what a rule is.

He could have said it.

He had been holding it for a day and a half and he had had all the time in the world to work out how to put it down, and there are two ways and he knew both.


He could have told her at that table, and then nine people in a room and eight women in a kitchen would have had it inside a day, and a woman who is under a permanent bar from the first of September and who is never going to be sworn would have been sitting in a room where a stranger with a folder could find out what she had read.

Or he could have taken it up to a room at the end of a corridor, alone, and asked for a minute that recorded that a man had read a reply to a departmental consult and said nothing about it, and every person in that room would have been entitled to open it again in about four years.

He did neither of those things.

He sat at a kitchen table in a building on Ferrier Street and he kept it, and the woman who told him everything saw him keep it, and neither of them said one word about what he had done.

A man who carries a document in his head cannot be made to produce it. That is not a trick and it is not a discipline and it is not anything he decided. It is simply the shape of him after eight years of being the most anonymous figure available to himself, and he has got very good at it, and it has never once cost anybody anything.

It cost somebody something on the twentieth of February and it was going to cost somebody something on the third of March, and both of those had come out of paper.

Runa Sato had not looked up. She put her fork down first, which she does not do.

"Lin Hao." She said his name the way she puts a case down. "One question. I get one question out of an evening like this and I am spending it now rather than saving it, because if I save it I will have decided by the end of the week that you have already had it."

"Ask it."

"Are you carrying something."

"Yes."

"How much of it is in your head."

"All of it," he said, "and it is the first time in my life that answer has been the wrong answer and I have watched it be the right one at four different counters since November."

Runa Sato nodded once and went back to her plate, and she did not ask one further question, and that was the whole of what she had come down the sixty-one steps for.


## Three Days

There were three days to the end of the month.

The thirtieth is the last working day of the quarter, and on the thirtieth a sheet that has not been decided goes across into the next one and lies down.

And then the account of thirty years would be called again on the first of April at ten to nine in a cold room two floors under a sales floor, with a fifth mark in the same hand on the same printed rule against it, and he would be at that rail because there was nowhere else in the city for him to be.

He had a file number he could not write down and four lines he could not put anywhere and a woman who had just handed him an answer for nothing in a kitchen in front of seven other people.

"Isolde," he said, across the table. "Pass the bread."

She passed the bread.

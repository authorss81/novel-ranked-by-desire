# The First Time He Refused Her

The Vestry arcade takes about nine hundred people up and down past its doors on an ordinary afternoon and Nadia Ferrous works them, and on the afternoon of the twelfth of March she worked the last two hours of them from the inside with her coat already on, because she had decided at about three o'clock that she was going to catch a man before he went home and she had decided to do it standing up.

He came through the west door at about half past four with his coat over his arm and a roll of stock under it, because he had collected a delivery from the branch store on the way and because a man who is carrying stock at half past four does not have to explain himself to anybody.

"You are late," she said.

"I am not late, I have been working since seven."

"You are late for me, and I have been standing here since about twenty past." She did not move out of the doorway and the doors kept going past her. "I want one thing and then I am going to go home and eat, and I am going to say it in one sentence so that you cannot spend ten minutes working out how to answer it."

"Say it."

"Will you come with me tomorrow morning."

The arcade went past. Two women with a basket came through, then a man with a rolled floor plan under his arm, then a child who was not being held by anybody.

"No," said Lin Hao.

## What He Felt

What he felt was relief.

That was the part he had not expected and he stood there and let it happen to him in full, in a doorway, at half past four in the afternoon, while nine hundred people a week went up and down past a woman in a coat.

He had been on a dispensary floor for five hours the night before and had slept for about two of them. Everything since February had been building to a morning on which a woman he was going to have to look at for the rest of his life would walk into a counter and say an address out loud.

He had wanted it. He had wanted it the way he wanted the plate to be a number he could read, and he had wanted it for about nine hours a day for three weeks.

And she had just offered to let him stand in the room while she did it, and he had said no, and the answer had come out of him faster than the question had come out of her.

"Was that a no about tomorrow," said Nadia Ferrous, "or was that a no about something and I have walked into it."

"That was a no about tomorrow."

"Then say why, because I am going to hold on to it and I would rather hold on to the reason than to the word."

## The Reason He Did Not Say

The reason he did not say was this, and he did not say it, and it was not a noble one.

He needed her to want it.

Not to be fond of it and not to have agreed to it in the abstract at a kitchen table in February. To want it, in the ordinary way, on the ordinary morning, for no reason at all — the way a person walks into a counter and gives up an address because they have moved out or they never lived there or they have simply stopped keeping it.

If he asked her, it would stop. The second it was a thing he had asked for, the counter and the clerk and the day and the hour would all still be exactly the same, and the act would be his with her hands on it, and a stranger in about four years reading that line would find a man's fingerprints all over a woman giving up the only address she had ever kept.

There is no instrument in this city that can stop a woman giving up an address. She has been entitled to do it since before any of them were born. The only thing anybody has ever been able to do about it is stand close enough to spoil the shape of it, and he had worked that out at about half past six that morning on sixty-one stairs and had known for six days that it was the whole of what he had.

## What He Said Instead

"You should know what you are paying for," he said.

"That is a change of subject."

"It is a fact and then it is a change of subject, and you can have both." He put the roll down on the sill beside him. "Seven is not a quiet figure. If the composition of that address says seven and the line is priced at seven, then the two figures are one figure, and then that line goes on the quarter's carry-forward sheet with nobody's name under it at all, and it does that every quarter after this one for as long as the account is open."

"I know. I have known since the first of February. I said it at that table on the fourteenth."

"Then you know what a carried line is, and I am going to give you the rest of it, and nobody has ever given you the rest of it, and I would rather you had it from me here—"

He stopped. Two seconds.

"I would rather you had it from me here than find it out in a room in four years."

## What A Carried Line Is

He said it to the doors and not to her, because it was easier.

"A carried line is administered," he said. "Not held. Administered. A line that is waited at a valuation is waited at by a keeper with a pen and nothing happens to anybody. A line that is carried forward stops being a thing that is looked at once a month and becomes a thing somebody answers for. Somebody answers for it every quarter, out of her own hand, in her own division, with her own name at the top of a sheet that somebody else built."

"She has to sign it."

"She has to answer for it. Which means she has to sign something. Every quarter. And she does not get asked whether she will and she does not get a vote on it and there is no sheet for saying no to it, because there is nothing in the city that has ever let a person say no to being the answer for something at their own address."

He turned round and looked at her.

"And she is not appointed."

## The Question She Asked

"Then who," said Nadia Ferrous.

The arcade went past. The doors went over and over.

He did not know.

That was the honest position and it was the worst thing he had done all afternoon, worse than the word no, because a man who says no and gives a reason has a floor under him and a man who says no and then cannot finish his own sentence does not.

"I do not know," he said.

"You do not know."

"I know that it is somebody at that address and I do not know which of you and I have not worked it out, and I have had four months to work it out and I have been not working it out."

"Then do not tell me the other half of it," said Nadia Ferrous. "Do you understand what you have just done? You have stood in a doorway and made a thing frightening to a woman who was going to do it anyway, and you did it because you did not have the sentence to the end, and the part you did have was enough to put a stone in it."

"I know."

"You do not know. You know that you have said it. You have been doing that since November and so have I and we have both been extremely good at it." She took her hood down and put it up again, which was nothing and took about two seconds.

"I am not going to be frightened off a counter by a man who cannot finish a sentence in a doorway. I would like you to understand that about me before tomorrow, because I am not going to be able to say it to you afterwards."

## Why She Started Counting

She sat down on the low wall by the doors with her coffee going cold beside her, which she never does while the doors are open, and she told him a thing she had never told that table, and she told it because he had just said no to her and had run out of room to be polite about it.

"I started counting on the fifteenth of October at about eleven in the morning because a man came through the west door and gave a wrong number out loud, and a hundred and forty people watched him say it and went to work in the afternoon."

She did not look at him. "I was working the doors that day the way I work them now, and I have never worked them any other way. That is the whole of what happened at this arcade and nobody has ever asked me about it."

"That was my brother."

"I know it was your brother. I did not know it was your brother then and I would not have known it was your brother now." She turned the cup round on the wall. "I counted them for a week before I understood what I was counting, and then I counted for three months after, and the number I got was not about him at all, which is the part that took me the longest."

"What was it about."

"About whether anybody would do it," said Nadia Ferrous. "A hundred and forty people and not one of them. They were all busy. That is the entire finding and it has taken me five months to be able to say it in one sentence at a table."

She got up and picked her coffee up and drank it cold.

"That is why I offered on the fourteenth. Not to save you. You were already inside the building, you have been inside it since I met you. I offered because I have been standing at a counter for five months watching people walk past a thing that costs them nothing, and I am not going to be one of them on the one morning I am actually asked."

## Nine O'Clock

He picked the roll up off the sill.

"You should not have asked me to come."

"No," she said. "That one was mine. Everything else you did yourself."

She went back to the doors and stood in the gap where the two streams of people split, and he went out into the street with a roll of stock under his arm and the east end of the arcade going dark behind him.

Then she said it to his back, over the top of about forty people, in the ordinary voice of a woman calling something across a room to somebody she knows well.

"Ground floor of the Vestry. The counter that is not the clinic one. About nine. Come if you like."

He stood in the street.

He was not going to be there. He had known that on sixty-one stairs at half past six that morning and had said so out loud in a kitchen in front of eight women, and he had known it again at half past four this afternoon in a doorway, and both of those had been before the word come if you like.

He stood in the street for about a minute anyway, because a woman had told him where to be, and then he went home and told the table nothing and went up the sixty-one and lay on top of the blanket with his boots off and did not sleep.

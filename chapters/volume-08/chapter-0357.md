# Eight Hands Under A Fifth Page

He had done this once.

That was the whole of what went wrong with the morning, and it went wrong in the way a thing goes wrong when you have done it perfectly once and you are doing it a second time at a table with the same eight people in it and the only difference is that the second time is your idea and nobody made you.

The sheet went round the kitchen on the morning of the fourteenth of January and each of the eight read it in the room and gave one thing back.

Nadia Ferrous read it twice and said, "It is short."

Runa Sato read it once and said, "That is a figure I have seen on nine pieces of paper and I have never once seen a name next to it and I am not going to forget the twelfth of January."

Agnete Lindqvist read it and did not say anything at all and put her hand flat on the paper for about a second, which she had not done to a piece of paper in eleven years.

Isolde Marchetti read it and said, "There is nothing on that page I could use," and put it down, and that was not a criticism and everybody at the table heard it as the opposite.

Beatrix Halm wrote the time against each reading. Eight times.

Wren Kallo read it at about half past nine with her coat still on, standing up, in the manner of a woman reading an instrument, and put it down and said, "Counter-forensics will serve this at every desk of the six that carry a post at this address, on the fifteenth, in the ordinary way, and nobody in this city has ever been asked to hold one back, and I am telling you that as the person who would have to do the holding back."

Sun Meiling read it last and said nothing and gave it back.

## The Gap In The Line

At five minutes to eleven on the morning of the fourteenth of January he was let into the room at the end of the corridor with one sheet of plain paper in his hand.

The clerk at the door said what a clerk at that door says, word for word as she had said it sixteen days before, because it is a rule and not a courtesy: anybody may leave a paper with that room at any hour of any day and the room will keep it.

He laid it on the table under the window.

It lay on a table that had held a page on the twenty-ninth of December, and the page on the twenty-ninth had been four sheets and this was one, and both of them had his name at the top of them.

Anouk Ferreira read it standing up, at the speed she reads everything, and did not sit down and did not read one word of it twice.

There are nine seats and eight people were in them. The fourth has stood empty since the first of November. Nikolai Vester has not walked through that door since the twenty-third of December. Alma Quayle was in the room on her own signature with the recusal on it.

"Chair," said a man in the seventh seat. "Is that a description?"

"No."

"Is it a return?"

"No."

"Then what is it, because I have to be able to say in about four years what I was in this room for."

"That is a holding," said Anouk Ferreira. "In twenty years this room has been asked to hold nine addresses and about four hundred figures, and every one of them was a thing that belonged to somebody and had nowhere to go. It is not holding an address this morning."

"It is holding a man, and the clerk will enter it in the keeping book as a document with no address on it, because a document with an address on it is a thing this room has had eleven thousand times and this is the first one that has come in with a man's handwriting on the top of it."

She put it down square.

"Call the people who were in this room on the twenty-ninth and the fourteen who were not, and tell them what is on the table and nothing else. Eight of them will come. Eight of them signed the last one."

## Eight Hands And Two Fingers

They came and they read it standing and eight of them signed the witness line underneath, in their own hands, saying that on the fourteenth of January a man laid a document into the keeping of the room declaring that he held the account of thirty years standing at premises 34-6-118, in his own hand, with no reason clause on it.

Eight hands went on the line in the order they had gone on in sixteen days before.

And the gap was where it had been.

Two fingers wide, in the middle of the paper, where the fourth seat was, with nothing whatever written in it.

He had looked at that gap on the twenty-ninth of December and thought it was a hole in his eight. He looked at it on the fourteenth of January and understood that it had never been his eight.

"That is not our line," he said.

Nobody spoke.

"That has never been our line. Eight of the nine who signed on the twenty-ninth of December signed as people who sleep at that address. There were nine seats and one of them had been empty since the first of November, and the gap is where a member of this room signs, and he has not signed because he cannot."

"That is correct," said the chair.

"And nobody in this room has ever said so out loud."

"Nobody in this room has ever had to," said Anouk Ferreira. "Until the twenty-ninth of December the line was not being kept up to. It was a form people put their names on. On the fourteenth of January it is a line in a book, and a line in a book with a gap in it is a thing a clerk copies out every quarter for four years."

## What It Costs

Sun Meiling had not spoken since the table under the window.

"That instrument takes the desk of anybody at this address who holds a post," she said, "and puts a paper in their hand at that desk. There is no discretion in that anywhere and nobody has ever been asked to invent any."

"I am going to say that once, in this room, in front of eight people, and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to explain it afterwards, because if I explain it afterwards it sounds like a warning and it is a bill."

Eight people in a room heard a woman price an instrument the way a clerk prices one.

"That is the cost of the twelfth of January," said Sun Meiling, "and it is not mine."

"He wrote it and he will answer for it in four years, and the four of us who sign a line about it will be asked about it in four years. Alma Quayle has been carrying the twenty-ninth of December in her own book since before any of us knew what it was going to cost, and there is nothing anybody in this room can do for her about that,"

"and I am the only one of the eight who has never signed anything about him and I signed this one at about four minutes to eleven this morning without being asked and without discussing it."

"You did not have to come," said the chair.

"No," said Sun Meiling. "That is the whole of what happened on the fourteenth of January and I would like it written down in whatever room this is."

## Five Minutes To Eleven

The clerk entered it at five minutes to eleven.

The copies go out and they go to every person named on the thing, at their post, in the ordinary way, and there is nobody in this city with the standing to hold one back and nobody is going to be given the standing today.

The desks get theirs on the fifteenth. The stream takes the rest at four in the afternoon on the nineteenth, the same as it took the four sheets on the twenty-ninth.

He came out of the room at about ten past eleven with nothing in his hands, which for the second time in sixteen days was the shape of a morning.

## The Top Of The Sixty-One

Beatrix Halm had taken the top step with the notebook open and had come off a car early and had not gone in, and she was still there when he came up.

"The plate has not moved all week," she said. "Eight thousand one hundred and forty-five, and it has read that every morning this week, and it goes up again this afternoon at half past four. The wall has not said one word about anybody since the thirtieth of December and it is not going to say one today either."

"Thank you."

"I am not being kind, I am being a roll." She turned a page. "I want to write down one thing while it is fresh because you are going to come down that hill in about five days in a state and I am not going to be able to get it out of you then."

"Go on."

"You laid a paper in there this morning at five minutes to eleven and I do not know what was on it and I am not going to be told and I have written the time down because I write the time down against everything you do, and that is the entire reason I am any use to you and it is also the entire reason nobody at that table can sit down." She wrote. "Eight forty-one. He went down. Ten past eleven. He came up."

"Nothing under either."

She shut the book.

"Two more things and then I am going to bed."

"Go on."

"Fourteen to the nineteenth is five days. The stream carries it at four on the nineteenth, the way it carried the four sheets on the twenty-ninth, and it is bound for eight addresses inside one building on this street." She put the pencil behind her ear.

"And it is bound for six more, because six of the eight of you hold a post and a declaration goes where the post is."

"And one of the six is a wing off a court you could not find again with a map, and there is a woman on the second floor of it who is line one on the page you have just put in a room." Beatrix Halm stood up off the step. "I am not going up that stair for you and neither is anybody else in this building."

"But you knew that on the twelfth when you wrote it, and you wrote it anyway, and that is going to cost the two of you and not the other six."

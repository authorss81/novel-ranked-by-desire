# The Seat That Has Been Empty

Runa Sato found him on the ninth and gave the whole of it across a table that night in about eleven sentences without softening one of them.

"Two rooms over a lock-up on a lane off Cordwain, and he has been in them since the first of November. He gets up at about seven and goes to a counting-house on the same lane that pays him four hundred a month and asks him to check returns."

"He was in that room's fourth seat for six years. He signed the keeping book in that room every quarter for six years. He has not signed anything for anybody since the morning of the first of November."

"What happened on the first of November."

"Somebody queried a return," said Runa Sato. "That is all a re-read is. A figure gets queried and it goes back down to a valuation and a man from the floor looks at the line again with fresh eyes. If the fresh eyes come back with the same figure, the return goes through."

"And if they do not, the man who queried it is the one who gets looked at. A man who queries a figure and is wrong has wasted an afternoon of a division. A man who queries a figure and is right has cost a division an afternoon and a face."

"And he was right."

"He was right about the figure and wrong about the week," said Runa Sato. "The figure did not move. The week was his error and he said so in the room before anybody else had, in writing."

"And then he would not sign the return, because a return is signed by the man who agrees with it and he did not agree with it. That is the whole of it. He was right, he said he was wrong, he would not sign, and a man who will not sign a return does not get re-read for about six years."

She put her knife and fork together.

"He has no standing. A man with no standing cannot hold a seal. And that seat is a piece of furniture."

## Coming Up The Sixty-One

He came up the sixty-one steps at about four in the afternoon on the eleventh of February without being asked to and without telephoning, which is not a thing that has happened in this house since the twenty-ninth of December.

He was a narrow man with a bad coat and the hands of somebody who has been checking figures in a cold room since November, and he stood in the hall and did not come in until Petra Ilyin said come in, and then he came in and stayed standing.

"I have heard there is a sitting on the twentieth," he said. "I have not come about the sitting. I have come because I am in this room's fourth seat on paper."

"There are eight hands on a line under a sheet that a man laid on the fourteenth of January. The gap in that line is the width of two fingers. It has been my seat since the first of November and none of the eight of you has ever once said so to me."

"Sit down, Bertram."

"I would rather stand," said Bertram Sallow. "I have not been in this room since the first of November and I am not on a roll and I would rather not get used to a chair."

## The Gap

"The gap is not a hole in your eight," he said, to Lin Hao, across a kitchen with eight women in it. "You said that on the fourteenth and you were right and nobody in that room had ever said it out loud, and I have thought about it every day since, because a hole in eight would be your business and a gap in nine is mine."

"Whose signature is it."

"It is nobody's," said Bertram Sallow. "It is the fourth seat's. And I could fill it if I could sign, and I cannot sign, and that is not a matter of will."

"A witness line is a line in a book, and an entry in a book is an entry against a standing, and a man with no standing does not put entries in books. I have not been able to sign a piece of paper since the first of November. I could not sign a receipt for a meal. I tried, about ten weeks ago, for a coat."

The kitchen did not say anything.

"So the gap stays," he said, "and it is the only part of that document that cannot be forged. Any clerk in this city can produce a page with eight signatures on it. Nobody in this city can produce that page with a two-finger gap in the middle of the witness line, and the moment somebody tries, there is a clerk who has to write a word under it explaining it, and the only word available is *vacant*."

## What The Seat Is For

"What is it for," said Isolde Marchetti, without looking up from the table. "Six years. What does the fourth seat do in that room."

"It reads," said Petra Ilyin. "That is all it does. Nine people and nine seals, and every one of them reads the thing before anybody votes on it. The fourth seat has read every sheet that has gone across that table for six years, has said about four sentences in ten years, and every one of the four was correct."

"That is not nothing," said Isolde Marchetti. "That is a man who sits in a room and reads a thing carefully and mostly says nothing. There is no instrument in this city that does that to anybody else, and half the people in it cannot read a docket at all."

"It is not nothing," said Petra Ilyin. "And a man with no standing cannot hold a seal, and a seat in that room without a seal is not a seat. It is a chair somebody is allowed to sit in."

"They did not turn him out. They left him a chair and took the seal off it, and he has been the only person in this city for three months who could read a room and had no way of doing anything about it."

Isolde Marchetti looked up.

"Then he has been doing the same thing I have been doing," she said, "except I get paid for it and he gets nothing, and he did it in a cold room under an Exchange."

## What He Is Worth

"Then what is the point of it," said Nadia Ferrous.

"The point of it is in about four years," said Bertram Sallow. "Somebody is going to open that sheet. They will get to the middle of the witness line and there will be a space and they will ask why, and the answer will be that there are nine seats in that room and one of them has stood empty since the first of November because the man in it said he was wrong when he was wrong and would not sign a return that was not true."

"They will ask who."

"They will ask who," he said, "and they will be told, and the man they send to is me, and I will say exactly what I have just said to you, and it will be true, and it will be the only part of that whole business anybody in it can stand behind."

He looked round the kitchen.

"I lost six years on the first of November by being correct. If that is what is going to stand under eight women's signatures in four years' time then it cost exactly what it was worth and I would like somebody in this room to understand that I am not being brave and I am not asking for the seat back."

## What The Room Did

What the room did about it took about four minutes and it happened the next morning.

The clerk at the door of that room at the end of a corridor takes a paper from anybody at any hour and keeps it, and on the morning of the twelfth of February a man with a bad coat and no standing laid a sheet of plain paper on the table under the window and asked for it to be entered in the keeping book.

It said that he held the fourth seat of that room, that he had been re-read at a valuation on the morning of the first of November, that he came out under his own floor, that a man with no standing cannot hold a seal, and that the seat had stood empty since and would stand empty until the standing came back.

The clerk entered it at eleven minutes to nine with the date against it, and a copy went out on the stream at four in the afternoon to the divisions that keep a list, because that is what happens to every paper that enters that room and nobody has ever been asked to invent an exception.

So the gap stopped being a space in the middle of a witness line and became a line in a keeping book with a date on it.

## The Part He Would Not Let Go Of

He said one more thing at the door on the way down the sixty-one and he said it to Lin Hao and not to the room.

"You did not save me and nobody in this house asked you to, and I want that said where it can be heard, because in about four years somebody will decide that we are all in this together and we will not be."

"I know."

"And one other thing," said Bertram Sallow. "I have been coming to the door of that room twice a week since the first of November and the clerk has turned me away twice a week since the first of November, and I have never once got as far as anybody in it finding out, and I have not mentioned it because for three months I was quite sure somebody was going to fill that seat and get it back."

He put his coat on at the bottom of the stairs.

"Nobody filled it," said Bertram Sallow. "It is going to be empty in four years and you had all better get used to the shape of it, because that is the first time anybody has said out loud, including me, that a seat in that room is going to stay empty, and the man who said it is the man it is empty for."

He stopped in the doorway with his coat half on.

"One thing before I go, and it is for him and not for the table."

"Say it."

"You put your name at the top of a piece of paper in December and again in January. You did both of them in your own hand with nobody helping you, and everybody in that room thought it was the bravest thing anybody had done there in years."

Bertram Sallow did not turn round.

"It was on paper. Everything from the twenty-seventh of October to the twelfth of January in this business has been on paper. Paper is the part of it that does not hurt anybody, and I have watched you do it in that order."

"It was also necessary," said Lin Hao. "I would do it again."

"I know you would," said Bertram Sallow. "That is what has been wrong about you since October, and it is not a thing a man gets out of by being careful with paper."

He went down sixty-one steps to the pavement and the door came shut behind him on its spring.

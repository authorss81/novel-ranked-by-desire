# The Line Called Twice

The counting room is two floors under the Exchange sales floor and it stands about nine degrees colder than the street on any morning of the year, and the only way down into it that does not go through a public door is a service stair off the yard that smells of wet stone and old string.

He came down it at ten to nine on the first of January.

He had been awake since five, not because there was anything to do at five but because a called line is called at ten to nine on the first of the month and he had spent three months failing to think about anything else at six in the morning.

## At The Rail

The valuation was not a meeting. Four people were in the room: Hanneke Ruis at the desk with the keys on a ring through her belt, Solveig Rask at the book, a junior on a stool with a bundle of returns that had come in overnight, and a man from the plate office who had come down for a figure and would go straight back up.

A valuation that nobody stands at is not a valuation. That was the rule of the floor and it was the whole of why he was allowed to be in it, and he had read it out of a notice on the wall on his second day down here in October without understanding what it was for until this morning.

Hanneke Ruis did not look at him.

She had written to him in September that she would not see him again downstairs while there were two figures for one door, and there were still two figures for one door, one of seven dated the sixteenth of September and one of eight certified on the thirtieth of November, and she had kept to it, and he had come down a service stair at ten to nine on the first day of a month in direct breach of a written instruction from the woman who held the keys.

He stood at the rail with his hands in his pockets and let it be true.

She had been in that room a hundred and four days. Seventeen of September to the seventeenth of December is ninety, and seventeen of December to the first of January is fourteen, and a hundred and four days is a long time to hold a room that has one thing in it that you would rather somebody else was holding.

The fifth book on the shelf behind her had a strap across it, buckled, and had had the strap since the eighteenth of November.

## The Word Goes In Again

The chained real book lies open on a board that is chained to the frame by a link the thickness of a finger. Solveig Rask holds it with her left hand and writes with her right, and the pen she writes with is on a chain that goes round her wrist and back to a staple in the board, which is the whole reason she can write anything at all without a keeper standing over her.

The mark cannot be scraped off. A mark in that book cannot be scraped off without a line in a destruction book and the name of the broker who wrote it, and there is a destruction book on the same shelf, and a broker who has not written anything into it in four years is a broker somebody notices.

She turned to the leaf.

The figure was where it had been since the sixteenth of September. Seven. Initialled and not signed, which meant a man had put his initial to it and had not put his name to it, which in this city is the difference between a figure that exists and a figure that can be argued with.

Under the figure, on the line where it went on the first of December, there was the word.

*Called.*

Under the word, the rule, which is a printed line with a bracket at each end and is not the rule of the Exchange and is the rule of the room: a called line waits at a valuation until it is paid or produced.

Against the rule, on the right, the first of December.

And now, on the left of the same bracket, in the same hand, on a chain, the pen made the second mark.

*Called. Called.*

Under the second one, the same rule.

Against the second one, the first of January.

She did not say anything. She put the pen down in its cradle, which is a leather loop the chain goes through, and she took her hand off the book, and she looked at Hanneke Ruis.

Hanneke Ruis wrote the time in the valuation book, which is a different book and is not chained, and the time was twenty to nine.

## A Slip She Did Not File

Nobody left for about a minute. Then the man from the plate office went up, and the junior took his bundle up, and the two women did the things two women do when a valuation is over, which are to square the books and to put the covers on in a particular order.

He was still at the rail.

Hanneke Ruis pulled a pad out from under the desk — a plain pad, unruled, the kind that comes by the hundred — and she tore a slip off the top of it with the edge of a ruler, and she wrote on it in a hand that was not her own.

He had seen her hand four times, in September, on the backs of his own questions. This was flatter and slower and had no hook on the nine at all. It was the hand of a woman who has decided that whatever she writes next is not going to be identifiable as hers.

*You stood at a valuation and you were not asked to. I have not reported that and I am not going to.*

*A line called once waits at a valuation until it is paid or produced. That is the rule and you know it.*

*A line called twice may be carried forward by the room that holds the book, without a fresh sheet being drafted.*

*No line at that address has ever been called twice. This one has now been called twice. From the first of February it can go on the quarter's carry-forward sheet, and it will be carried, because a carry-forward sheet is not an instrument and nobody has to draft it and nobody has to put their name under it.*

*You have bought something. It is not a protection and I did not sell you one. It is that the account stops being a paper that travels and becomes a figure sitting on a shelf in a room with two keys, and a thing on a shelf in a room with two keys is a very difficult thing for a room with nine chairs to get at.*

*I am not going to say this a second time.*

*You are welcome to it.*

*Do not thank me. If you thank me then in four years somebody reads that the keeper of this room was thanked by the man whose address is on that book, and a keeper who has been thanked by a man is a keeper who has been useful to a man, and I have been in this room a hundred and four days and I intend to be in it in four years.*

She turned the slip face down, and then she picked it up again and held it out, and when he had it she put the pad back under the desk with the torn edge showing.

Then she said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, the only sentence she gave him that morning.

"The carry-forward goes out on the thirtieth of the quarter. If a room ever comes at that line again, they will have to come through this floor and not through a door in Ferrier Street, and there is a great deal of difference between those two sentences and none of it is in your favour."

## What It Is Worth

He stood on the service stair for a while with the slip in his coat.

It was eleven minutes past nine and the Exchange yard above him was beginning to make the noise it makes, and he had been awake since five, and in the whole of his life there had not been a morning on which a piece of paper was handed to him by a keeper as a gift.

He wanted to be sick about it, and he could not work out whether it was because of the gift or because of what it said underneath it, and it turned out to be the second.

A called line that has been called once generates a valuation. A valuation generates a sheet, and a sheet goes out on the ordinary stream, and the ordinary stream goes to the desks of the offices that keep lists. That has been what has been happening to him since the first of December, and it is why a man whose whole working life is six floors of washrooms has been the most legible object in a city of two million people for three months.

Called twice, it can be carried instead of valued.

Carried means it does not generate a sheet.

Which means the first of February and the first of March both pass without a single piece of paper going anywhere, for the first time since the sixteenth of September, and the account that has been following him around the city like a dog with a collar goes quiet.

And in exchange for that, the only instrument in Ostara that could reach it is now inside a room with two keys, and the only way to get at it is to come to that room and ask the keeper, and the keeper has written down that she will not be thanked.

He did not thank her.

## The Rule Of The First

He walked up the service stair and out into the yard at about twenty past nine with the slip folded into the inside of his coat where the lining had been mended twice by somebody else.

The rule under the second mark said the same thing the rule under the first mark said, and it said it in the same words, and it does not vary, and there is no reading of it under which it stops.

*A called line waits at a valuation until it is paid or produced.*

He had known for three months that he could not produce. There is no production off premises 34-6-118. It is not that nobody has offered; it is that the door does not have a production in it, and the room where nine people sit has been told so in four parts on a sheet with a figure of seven on it.

And he could not pay either. Paying a called line means paying at the figure on the line, and the figure on the line is seven, and the books that carried the priced line went to a guild table at seven in the morning on the sixteenth of November into the hands of nine people, and there is no clerk in this city who can take that money off a person who does not have it.

So it waits.

It waited on the first of December. It waited on the first of January.

It will wait on the first of February and on the first of March, and it will wait on the first of April after that, and it will go on waiting until somebody pays it at seven or somebody produces something off a door that has never had anything produced off it.

He stood in the Exchange yard and worked that out for the fourth time in three months, and it had not got any better, and he had come down a service stair at ten to nine on the first of January and gone back up with a slip in his coat.

The quiet had been free. It had cost a woman a hundred and four days of standing in a cold room and one sheet of a plain pad torn off with a ruler, and she would not be thanked, and there was no day in the rest of his life on which he was going to be able to do one thing about it.

He walked to the Fourth Branch and cleaned six floors of washrooms while the office filled, and on the third floor a woman with a bad back asked him whether the water was hot yet and he told her it was, and his name was not on anything she wrote down.

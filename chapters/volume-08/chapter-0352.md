# The Week She Kept To Herself

She came back to the table on the evening of the third of January, which was four days, and she had asked for seven.

He had not gone up. That was the whole of his week: the staff kettle at six, six floors of washrooms, the gate, the tram past the square at four, and sixty-one steps at about ten at night, and on each of the four nights he stood on the top step for a while and then went down again, and no woman in that building knew that except him.

She came in at about half past six with her coat on and stood in the kitchen doorway with her hand on the frame for about a second longer than a woman who is at home stands.

"Four days," she said. "I said seven."

"You said seven."

"I could not do seven." She took her coat off, dropped it over the single chair, and did not sit down. "That is not a complaint about you. I want that said before anybody at this table decides it is."

"I could not do seven because I sat in my own rooms on the second and worked out what I had done, and then I sat in them on the third and could not work out what I was going to do about it, and a woman who cannot get to a table in four days has not earned the fifth."

Nobody said anything, because nobody at that table was going to say anything about it, and because eight women who have watched a man fail at an ordinary sentence in this room know exactly how long a silence can be left.

## The Shape Of It

She sat down third from the end, where she always sat, and she put her hands flat on the table, and she said the thing she had come to say.

"I have chosen."

Beatrix Halm's pencil stopped.

"Not the office. I am not going to tell you the office and I am not going to tell you the man in it, and if anybody in this room spends the rest of the evening trying to get it out of me by being kind to me I will take my coat and go down sixty-one steps and I will not come back until the second of February."

"We will not," said Sun Meiling.

"I know you will not. I said it so that I would not have to spend the evening deciding whether to trust it." She turned her hands over. "It was done on the first. I went in at about eleven in the morning and it took nineteen minutes and I will not be giving you the nineteen minutes either."

Nineteen minutes, on the first of January, and he had been at the Fourth Branch cleaning six floors of washrooms from half past six.

Then she said the third thing, which was the one she had actually come down for.

"They asked me a question."

"What question."

"That is a question about the office."

He shut his mouth.

It had come out of him as a reflex, in a kitchen, in one syllable, and he heard it hit the table and he felt all eight of them go still, because he had done the thing he had promised in October not to do, in front of them, without deciding to.

Wren Kallo did not use it. She did not even mark it.

"It is not about the office," she said. "It is about me. They asked me a question that could only be answered by putting a name on it."

She put her thumb on the table and pushed a little, the way a person does when they are checking that the wood is still there.

"There is a shape. It is the shape I told you about in December and it has not changed and I have not added to it. Two documents about the same eight women at the same door, made inside a month by the same pair of hands, in one of which I am an officer of counter-forensics certifying a composition from a room I am not standing in, and in the other I am the fourth line of a list of persons at my own address."

"And the question."

"And the question was: whose hands." She said it flatly. "That is the whole of what they wanted. Not what the shape is. Whose hands. Because a shape with no hands on it is a rumour and a shape with hands on it is a finding, and this office does not keep rumours."

"What did you say."

"I said the shape." Wren Kallo looked at the table and not at one person in it. "I gave them every part of it that does not carry a woman. Two documents. One pair of hands. Eight women. One door. That is what I have handed over and I handed it over in nineteen minutes and it was accurate."

"So you told them the shape and not the woman."

"I told them the shape and not the woman, and I did not put a name on it, and I did not put a name on it in a way that could be got at." She put her hands back flat. "That is what I have."

"If anybody in this room wants to be angry with me about that, be angry in the ordinary way and do it at me, because I have been the one holding this since a quarter to two in the morning on the night of the thirtieth of December and it has been mine alone for three days."

## The Price Of Not Asking

Not one of them asked.

That was the part he would think about afterwards, and it was the part of the evening that was actually about him.

Sun Meiling, who is the woman in this house who has never once let a silence go past the point where it was doing work, twice looked at him. Runa Sato put a cup down in front of him without being asked and did not say anything at all, which from Runa Sato is a speech. Beatrix Halm wrote the time and wrote nothing under it, as she had on the thirtieth of December and as she would on the nineteenth of January.

And he sat at the head of his own table with a question in his mouth and did not ask it, because in October, in this room, with three conditions in it, he had promised, and on the sixth of December he had asked it once and been given the standing answer and had said he would not ask again.

*You may not put one question to me about that address and I will say I do not know what you are talking about.*

He did not say the words. He came within about a second of saying them, and everybody at that table saw him come within about a second of saying them, and nobody helped, and it was Isolde Marchetti who made it worse by looking at him.

"I want to say something and I am not going to say it kindly," said Isolde Marchetti. "You have been the reason I am frightened for four days and you have not done one single thing to cause it and you are also the reason I cannot say who frightened me, and both of those are true at once, and I have sat at this table for a week working out which of the two I would rather you were."

She did not raise her voice. She never raised her voice. In a room that agrees to what she sings, raising it would be a waste of the room.

"I am not angry at you. I am angry at the arrangement, and the arrangement is that you cannot help me and I cannot be helped and it is the same arrangement that has kept me on a list instead of a wage."

"It is."

"You are going to say *it is* to everything in this room for the next hour and then you are going to go up sixty-one steps and be alone with it," said Isolde Marchetti. "I have watched you do it since November. It is the only thing in this house you are good at and it is going to cost you the only woman in it who sings."

## Cheaper The Wrong Way

Later, with the plates in the sink, she said the third thing.

"You told me on the thirtieth that it costs nothing to be wrong about you and a great deal to be right." Wren Kallo had her sleeves rolled and her hands in the water and she was not looking at him.

"I have had three days with that sentence and I have come to the conclusion that it is the only sentence anybody has ever said to me in this business that is both true and useless, and I would like you to know that I said it first."

"That is not what I meant."

"It is exactly what you meant and it is exactly what you got." She shut the tap. "Being wrong about you costs them nothing. Being right about you costs a person. Not you. A person."

"Somewhere behind that shape there is a woman who keeps a list and who would have to put a name on a page and then stand in a room while somebody read it back to her in the flat voice, and she would not be paid for the afternoon and she would not be thanked and she would go home and she would be the woman who read it."

She dried her hands on a cloth that had not been dry since the sixteenth of November.

"I think I have told the wrong people."

The kitchen went quiet in a way that was different from the other quiet.

"I think I have handed a shape to an office that will use it, and I think that office cannot reach the thing the shape is about, because the thing the shape is about is a woman on a list, and an office cannot reach a woman on a list, it can only reach the building she is in." She hung the cloth over the rail.

"And I think that whoever I should have told is somebody who could get at the one document out of the two, and there is exactly one such person in this city and she has been sitting four feet from me since November with her hands in her lap."

She did not look at Sun Meiling and Sun Meiling did not look at Wren Kallo, and neither of them moved, and Lin Hao understood that a thing had just happened at that table which nobody would ever be told about and which he had been present for.

## The Division

She took her coat off the back of the door at about half past ten.

"One thing, because you will otherwise spend tonight and every night after it working it out, and I would rather you spent it on the four minutes it takes." Wren Kallo stood with one arm in her sleeve. "The division I went into is not one that sits in the Wing. It has no floor in the Wing and it keeps no list of addresses of its own and it has never in its life held a description of premises."

"I would not have found it if I had not been sent a strip about it in October."

"What division."

"That is the question."

"That is not a question, that is a description of a thing."

"It is the only description of it I am going to give you at this table." She put the second arm in. "Goodnight. I am sorry about the four days. I am not sorry about the rest of it and I would like somebody in this room to write that down so that I cannot come back in a week and pretend it was an accident."

She went down the sixty-one and the stair did not make a sound under her, because it never has.

He stood at the top of it for a while, and then he went to the window at the top of the landing and looked out at the roofline toward the square, and he did the only thing he was permitted to do with what she had given him, which was to think about it once, on his own, without putting it to anybody.

A division in this city that does not sit in the Wing, that keeps no list of addresses, and that somebody sent a strip to in October.

An office that cannot describe a thing does something worse to it than an office that hates it, and a division that does not keep lists of addresses does not want an address.

It wants an author.

There is exactly one reason clause in this business that has never had anybody's name on it. It was made in public on the fifteenth of October, it cannot be written down, and nine hundred people in this city are carrying it about in their heads for nothing.

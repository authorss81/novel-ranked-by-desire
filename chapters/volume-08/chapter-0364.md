# The Filing Run After Nine Years

He went up the iron stair off the third court at about ten in the morning on the fourth of February, for the second time since the sixteenth of January, on the outside of the lamps, because that is how a man walks to a ninth door and not to an eighth.

The corridor on the second floor runs eleven doors and a window at the end and it is the only route in this building between the third floor and the basement records store, because the service stair goes up and not down, and every bundle that goes into the basement for the year goes past eight doors on its way.

He had been up here twice in three years and he had gone into the side room twice this month, and he had not told anybody he was coming and he was not going to, and there was a chair under the window at the far end of the corridor that had been there since he first came and he sat down on it.

At about ten past ten a man came out of the stair head at the top with a bundle on a strap across his chest.

## The Four Hundred And First

It was not a big bundle. That was the first thing he registered, because a man who has come down a corridor four hundred times does not carry big things and this one had twenty file-slate in it and it was not heavy, and he carried it the way a man carries something he has carried four hundred times, low, on the left hip, with the strap across the right shoulder.

Nine years in that building. A window on the yard. He had never once come down this corridor in three years and he was coming down it now at ten past ten on the morning of the fourth of February.

He stopped at the eighth door.

"Sun," he said. Not her title and not her name. Sun.

The door was shut. It opened.

## The Question

"It is a small thing and it is about a box," he said, from the threshold, with the bundle still on his hip. "Which of the two boxes the departmental drafts went into, the gray one or the brown one. Both of them have a research wing stamp on the lid. The record office has them on two shelves and the shelves are a floor apart and a man of my age does not want to do it twice."

"The gray one," said Sun Meiling.

"You are certain."

"Gray. Third shelf down from the top, and the lid is marked with a date and not with a wing, and whoever marked it did it with a chin because the pen was in their other hand." She had not come out of the room. "Third shelf down. Not the shelf it looks like it should be on."

"Thank you."

And then he said, "Give me your name for the box record."

## Her Own Name

There was a pause of about two seconds on that landing, and Lin Hao, eleven feet down the corridor in a chair under a window, watched a woman decide something.

"In a box request the name of the person asked goes down," he said, "and the record office keeps them. It keeps them for the life of the store. If you give me a wing docket number and nothing else, then the record office will come up here and ask the wing who drafted it, and the wing will look at the booking book on this floor, and I would rather the record office had a name off me than have them come up here and find one."

"Sun Meiling," she said.

And she said it to him, at the doorway, in a corridor with a window at the end of it, in the ordinary voice of a woman answering a question about a box.

She did not look down the corridor. She had not once looked down the corridor in three years and she did not then, and the whole of what she had just done she had done to a man with a bundle on his hip and not to a chair eleven feet away with a man sitting in it.

## Why She Gave It

She gave it because on the morning of the twenty-sixth of January she had gone into a booking on that floor and declined an attendance, and a clerk had written down the hour and then the minute at which she wrote it down, and a booking book is not a private document and never has been.

If she had refused him the name, he would have gone up to the third floor and asked there, and the third floor would have gone to the record office with a docket, and the record office would have come to the wing, and the wing would have gone to the booking book on the second floor.

That book would have said that the woman who drafted the departmental answer three years ago declined to stand behind it on the morning of the twenty-sixth at eleven forty-four.

From that minute the refusal stops being a woman saying no in a room and becomes a fact about a document, with a date and a docket number on it, and a woman under a permanent bar who has been under it since the first of September.

She had put her name on the box record instead. Six letters of it, in a corridor, to a man who asked for it as though it were nothing.

## What She Said Afterwards

The door opened again about a minute after the stair head had gone quiet and she came out into the corridor in the same clothes, and she shut the door behind her and stood against it with her back to the boards.

"I know you are there," said Sun Meiling. "You have been in that chair since ten o'clock and you breathe like a man who is holding himself still."

"I am."

"Then you heard me give a stranger my name on a landing." She did not move off the door. "I did not do it for you and I want that said before either of us says anything else about it."

"I know you did not."

"You do not know that," said Sun Meiling. "You know what it cost and you have worked out a shape that fits the cost, and the shape is always the man. If I had given him the name on the sixteenth of January it would have been you. On the fourth of February, in a corridor, to a man with a bundle on his hip who had never once been in this room, it was a box record and I wanted a box record."

She looked down the corridor at the window and at the one chair under it.

"He is going to come down that stair again. He has asked me a question and I have given him an answer and a man who has been given an answer by a person in a building does not stop at one, and he will be back, and the day he comes back I will not have to decide anything at all and that is the entire reason I answered him."

"Sun."

"I am not angry. I want you to hear that I am not angry, because you are standing on that landing making a face you have made about four times since November, and every one of those times you were about to tell a woman not to do a thing."

She pushed off the door.

"I did it on the twenty-sixth at eleven forty-four and I have been doing it every working day since, in daylight, at a desk. Nobody in this building can make me stop and nobody in this city can make me start, and that is not a triumph. It is a job. I have one and I have done it for three years."

"Then why did you tell me not to ask."

"Because there is a man coming down that stair who works things out and does not leave them alone, and he was going to have to have a name for me within a fortnight whether I gave him one or not. I would rather it was mine and given."

She went back inside.

"And because you would have asked him. You would not have asked me. You have not asked me one single question about anything since September, and if you had asked that one you would have been a man who interrogates the people at his own table, and I have not got the hours to keep you from becoming that as well."

## What Changed

Nothing happened.

That was the part he could not get past for the rest of the morning.

The man on the third floor said thank you, shifted the bundle back onto his left hip, and went up the service stair, and he was gone before Lin Hao had worked out that he had said nothing else at all, and there had not been a question in the whole of it that was about a transfer that carried no name.

Lin Hao sat in a chair under a window on the second floor of a building off a third court and did the sum he had been avoiding since the sixteenth of January, and he did it badly, because the window at the end of that corridor faces the yard and a man sitting in it on the fourth of February is visible from about forty feet away to anybody on the ground who happens to look up.

She had said on the sixteenth that she was the only one of the eight that nothing in this house could cover, because she could not be sworn, and that a copy of his page had come to her own desk at nine in the morning and she had signed for it with her own name in a book that keeps the times.

On the twenty-sixth she had gone into an attendance and declined it, and the booking had gone into a book.

On the fourth of February she had given her own name to a man with a bundle so that no clerk would come up and find the first of those in the second.

She had done three things since the morning of the twenty-sixth of January that put Sun Meiling's name at the front of three separate records, and she had done every one of them on her own, in an office, in daylight, at a desk or a counter or a doorway, and she had told him about one of them on the back of a docket and had told him about the other two by doing them and letting a docket arrive at his door.

And there was nothing at all in the world he could do about any of it.

## Ten Past Ten And Eleven Forty-Four

The run had come down at about ten past ten.

He got that out of the door at the end of the corridor, where a board with the day's runs chalked on it hangs, and he read it standing up in a coat that was on the back of the chair because he had not thought about the window.

Then he went down the iron stair on the outside of the lamps and out into the court with his hands in his pockets.

The decline was entered at eleven forty-four on the morning of the twenty-sixth. The run came down at ten past ten on the morning of the fourth.

Nine days. A man who has been in a building for nine years, who has never once in three years come down a corridor he walks every working day, came down it on the fourth of February at ten past ten because a clerk on that floor had written a woman's refusal into a book and the department that holds a box record has to be able to say who it asked.

Perrin Sowe had not come down that corridor to find out about a transfer that carried no name.

He had come down it because Sun Meiling had said no, and somewhere in the eight days since that refusal had been read by somebody who needed a name against it, and the only name there was hers, and he had come down the only corridor in the building that leads to the basement to ask her for it before anybody else did.

Lin Hao stood at the bottom of sixty-one iron steps off a third court in the middle of the morning and understood that a woman who had gone quiet for eleven days had not gone quiet at all.

She had gone to work.

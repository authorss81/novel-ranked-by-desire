## The Room Is Not A Term

"I have given terms before. I gave four to a man in a kitchen in November and they were excellent and they are the reason nobody has ever asked me one single question since. "There is one thing and it is not mine and I did not build it and I could not switch it off if I tried. In this room nobody can be compelled to say a single word. "And the price of it."

## What Am I To Them

## The Room Does Nothing

## Afterwards

She drank a glass of water from a jug on a shelf and drank about four of them.

"Four hundred people in this quarter know your name," she said. "I have got a list of what they said you were. Not names."

"I want you to understand what that is, because I have been careful for eleven years and I am not going to hand you a register. It is a list of sentences. *The man with the thing. The one who gave it back. The one the Office uses. The one who does not score. The quiet one from the tram depot.*"

"Forty years of them, and twenty years of this chapel. I have written down what was said about you and I have never once written down who said it. If I write down who said it then it is a register, and this building does not keep one."

"What good is that to me."

"None whatever," said Agnete Lindqvist. "It is worth nothing in any room in this city. It could not be produced, it could not be served, it could not be read out to a clerk, and if a stranger from the Office came in here in four years with a form I would hand it to him myself, because it is a list of sentences and sentences are not evidence of anything."

"Then why have you got it."

"Because you came up sixty-one steps in the cold," she said, "and because in about four years somebody is going to ask me what the people in this quarter believe about a man, and I am going to want to have been the one who wrote it down before anybody came with a form."

## The Two Days

He got up to go at about ten to seven and she let him, and she walked him to the mat in the dark corridor because the lamp in the chapel is a bracket lamp and she does not leave it burning.

Then she stopped him with a hand on the arm that stayed about two seconds and let go.

"I am going out," said Agnete Lindqvist. "On the eighteenth. In the morning. To the front of the arcade, where there are nine hundred people a day and a wall I have not seen from the outside since before you were at that branch, and I am going to stand on it and say what is on my list, out loud, without naming anybody, and then I am going to go back in."

She said it the way she said everything, which was as a thing that had been decided and could not now be undecided.

"They will not believe that either. About four hundred of them will and eleven thousand will not, and the four hundred will include every woman at your table and not one of them will be able to do anything about it."

Then she opened the door and the cold came in off the arcade and she shut it again at once, because she had not been out of it.

He walked back up the hill with about an hour of the forty-eight he had asked for left.

The arrangement at that kitchen table stood until the morning of the twentieth of February. On the morning of the twentieth, nine people were going to read a sheet for nine minutes in a room at the end of a corridor. On the morning of the day before that, a woman who had been indoors for eleven years was going to stand on a pavement and say what four hundred people believed about him, and neither of them was going to know about the other.

# The Ninety Seconds She Spent

Marguerite Vale had two rooms over a chandler's on a street off Cordwain and she had not been inside the premises since the tenth of December, and when he came up to her at about four in the afternoon on the thirtieth of January she had a fire going and did not offer him a chair, which was the first thing about that room he had ever understood.

There was a table against the wall with four chairs at it and a lamp on it and nothing else on the table at all, and a woman who eats alone for seven weeks stops putting things out, and you can see it on a table like that the way you can see a score.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "Sit down if you are going to, and do not sit down if you are not."

He sat down.

"You are not going to be thanked and you know it," said Marguerite Vale. "Do not begin. Gratitude has cost this city nothing in twenty years and it has cost me a great deal, and I said the whole of that in December in front of eight women, and you are not going to say it again tonight in a worse room than the one I said it in."

"I did not come to say it."

"You came to ask me a thing you could have asked me in October," she said. "Go on. I have been sitting here since the twenty-second."

## Two Instruments

"Two things have come out of ninety seconds on the sixteenth of September," she said, "and you have only ever counted one of them, because you have been busy since December."

"The figure of seven."

"That is the first one. A priced line, initialled and not signed, dated the sixteenth of September, carried at premises 34-6-118, gone into a real book and copied into four divisions by the sixteenth of October and into fourteen by December and called at a valuation on the first of December and again on the first of January and again, by everything I have heard, on the first of February."

"Then what is the second."

"The second one is everything you have written since." She put her hand flat on the table.

"A man may only declare a holding where a figure has already been priced. There is no such thing as a declaration at a door nobody has ever put a price on. You could not have written the four sheets on the twenty-ninth of December. You could not have written the fifth on the twelfth of January. I priced that address in ninety seconds and you have been living on the invoice for four months without ever having seen it."

He looked at her.

"You told me in December that the only person alive who could put anything into that box was the man it was about."

"And I was right and you heard the wrong half of it," said Marguerite Vale. "The only person alive who could put anything into that box is the man it is about, and the only person alive who could make his having put something there mean anything is a broker who has already priced it. Both of those are me and you, and the second one is me, and you have never once said so out loud."

## The Drawer

She got up and went across to a chest of drawers with a lock on it that had been oiled at some point in the last forty years.

"This one," she said. "I have not opened it since the sixteenth of September."

She took a key out of her bag and turned it and pulled the drawer out and it came out easily, because a drawer that has been opened a great many times comes out easily and a drawer that has not been opened since the middle of September comes out as if it had been oiled too.

There was one sheet of paper in it and it was a slip, torn off a pad, unruled, the kind a person writes a figure on before they carry it to a book.

She put it on the table face up.

It had a figure on it in her own hand. It had a time on it, eleven minutes past nine in the morning. It had an address on it. And under the address she had ruled a single line herself with the straight edge of a ruler, which is what a broker does when she is pricing something she intends to hand to somebody else and expects the hand-off to be arguable.

"Nothing on it," said Lin Hao.

"Nothing on it at all," she agreed. "That is what I have kept for four months. I did not keep it because it is evidence of anything. I kept it because it is the last piece of paper in this city that exists before anything happened, and there are eleven thousand people in a quarter who would give a great deal for a piece of paper like that and would not know what to do with it when they had it."

## The Initial

He waited. She knew he was waiting and she made him do it.

"You want to ask me who put his name to it."

"I want to ask you who put his initial to it."

"A priced line is initialled by the man who brings the figure and not by the broker who gives it," said Marguerite Vale. "That is not a courtesy. It is the difference between a number that has a man's name against it and a number somebody can pick apart in a corridor."

"If the broker initials it, it is an opinion a woman had in ninety seconds and anybody in the building can take it apart. If the man who came in with the description initials it, then he has made it a fact about himself and it is on him and he cannot get behind it."

The fire moved and neither of them looked at it.

"You came in on the sixteenth of September at about eleven minutes past nine in the morning with a picture of a house where a figure ought to have been, and I priced it at seven, and the sheet went into the real book that afternoon, and you initialled it on the way out."

"I did not put a name to it."

"You did not put a name to it," said Marguerite Vale. "You put an initial to it, on the sixteenth of September, at about half past eleven in the morning, in this room, on the way out, because a broker's figure initialled by nobody can be argued with by a man who was not in the room and you are the only man who was in the room."

She put her hand flat on the slip.

"Fourteen offices in this city hold a copy of that sheet and every one of the fourteen carries your initial and not my name. It went to a guild table at seven on the morning of the sixteenth of November and nine people read it, and I was not one of them and I have not seen a copy since December."

"And it has been called at ten to nine on the first of December, and on the first of January, and on the first of February, and every one of those three marks is a mark against an initial that is yours."

## The Whole Of It

He said nothing at all for a while and she let the nothing stand, because the room is the sort of room that has been left alone for seven weeks and can hold one.

"For three months I have believed the figure of seven belonged to a woman who would not be thanked," he said.

"It did not."

"It belongs to me."

"It has belonged to you since the sixteenth of September," said Marguerite Vale, "and you put it there by accident, and I have watched you build four months of paper on top of an initial you do not remember making, and I have never once told you, and there is a reason for that which is not kindness."

"Tell me the reason."

"Because you would have thanked me." She turned the cup she had not been drinking from round twice without lifting it. "And the minute you knew, you would have thanked me, and then it would be finished between the two of us, and a thing that is finished between two people cannot be used by either of them afterwards."

"That is how a broker's arithmetic works. It is not a kindness. It is that I am the only person in this city who can hold a figure you cannot disown, and I have been holding it since September and I am not going to hand it back for a sentence."

He looked at the slip on her table with his initial on it in the middle of an address.

"One more thing and then you can go," she said. "You wrote your name on the twelfth of January and you have been treating that as the brave thing since. It was not the brave thing. A name can be denied by a man who is not in the room. An initial cannot."

"An initial can be looked up by anybody with a folder in about four years. The day-book that records who initialled a priced line is at the Exchange, and it does not know or care whose books the figure came out of, and there are eleven thousand eight hundred copies of it."

"So the brave thing was September and I did it by accident."

"You did it at about half past eleven in the morning on a working day in September because I was busy," said Marguerite Vale. "That is the entire history of it. There is nothing else in it and you may stop looking at it as though there were."

He got up and put his coat on and did not say the thing.

At the door he stopped and looked at the chest of drawers with the drawer still open.

"Do not put that back in and shut it," he said.

"It is a slip of paper with a figure on it and a figure of seven is not going to hurt me."

"You kept it for four months," he said. "Shut it."

He went down three flights and out onto a street off Cordwain in the dark with about eight hours of the thirtieth of January still to get through, and a called line sits at a valuation until somebody pays it or somebody produces off the door, and in about seventeen hours it was going to be marked for the third time at ten to nine in the morning, and he had no idea in the world what that did.

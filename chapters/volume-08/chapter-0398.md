# The Shape She Put In A Room

There is one room, one chair, one plate and one knife in it, and a bed against the wall under a window that gives on the back of a yard, and a stove that has to be lit at half past five in the morning and again at nine at night, and a photograph face down on the shelf where it has lain since the autumn, of two men standing on the step of a tram.

Wren Kallo stood in the middle of it at eight o'clock in the evening on the twenty-eighth of March with her bag in her hand and did not sit down for about four minutes.

"There is one plate."

"There is one plate."

"And there is one chair, and I am not going to sit in it until I have said the three things, and then I am going to sit in it, and you are going to hear me find out whether it is a good chair."

## The Sheet

She took the sheet out of the bag. It was one piece of paper, folded twice, and it was in a clerk's hand and she had had it in that bag for eighty-eight days, and there was a pen across the top of it and the ink had gone into the fold.

"There are five things on this sheet and four of them stand and I am going to tell you which one does not, and why, before anything else happens tonight."

"Go on."

"One." She turned it round so that he could see it and did not give it to him. "*Nothing is spent. Not on me. Not on anyone. Not tonight.* That is the one. It is crossed out and I crossed it out at about eleven o'clock this morning with a pen off my own desk, in my own division, and I did it in about four seconds and I have been carrying the four seconds for about nine hours."

"Why."

"Because it was true on the night of the thirtieth of December and it was never about the thirtieth of December."

She put her hand flat on the paper.

"It was about the two of us. It said that nothing in that room was holding either of us, and it said it in a room I had made sure was not holding either of us, and there has been a third person in that sentence since the first of January and I am not going to write a sheet in a business where I have spent a woman."

"She was at a counter for eleven minutes."

"She was at a counter for eleven minutes because I gave a shape to a room in nineteen minutes on the first of January and I gave it accurately and it was accurate and it reached a woman on the third floor of a building through the only door that room had, which is a door on this address."

She did not raise her voice.

"So that one is out. And I am not going to fold the sheet round the four and hide it, because four things read in a room with one plate in it would be a woman being careful."

"The other four."

"I am not reading you the other four."

She put the pen down on the plate, which was the plate with his supper on it, and moved it.

"They were written on the thirtieth of December for a night in a building off a third court where there was a jug of water and a washstand and no stove."

"Four of them still stand. You know what they are; you read them once in a room in December and you have never asked me about one of them and you have never needed to, and I am not going to do a clerk's voice on a piece of paper in here, because you have one plate and I have a photograph and neither of those is a formality."

## The Thing She Is Saying Before Anything Else

She sat down in the chair. It was a good chair and she found that out by leaning back on it about four inches.

"Three," said Wren Kallo. "And I am going to say this before a hand moves in this room and I am going to say it in about forty words and then I am going to stop."

"Go on."

"I told them. On the first of January, in nineteen minutes, a shape with no woman on it. It cost a clerk called Imrie, on the twenty-fourth of March, at a counter on the second floor, eleven minutes and the word cheaper in her own mouth."

She looked at him and not at the sheet.

"I am the one who says so. I have been going to say it to somebody for eighty-eight days and I have picked tonight and I have picked you and it is not because you are the man it happened to, and I am not asking you for anything, and I will not be asking you for anything at any point between now and about eleven o'clock tonight, and if I find that I have started then you are to stop it."

"I have been not asking you one question since the sixth of December."

"I know. It is why tonight."

## What She Was Going To Have Done Last December

"Now the fifth one, and you have to say it, and then we stop talking."

She did not read it. She held the sheet upside down and let him look at the bottom line of it and she waited.

"I am not here because you are in trouble," said Lin Hao.

"I am not here because you are in trouble," said Wren Kallo. "That was true on the thirtieth of December with a card on a wall in the square and it is truer tonight and there is less on the wall than there was."

She folded the sheet in half and in half again and put it in her bag and pushed the bag under the chair with her foot, and he understood that she had put the shape out of her hands in the one movement that did it and he was not going to see the paper again until she decided to.

## The Thing She Is Not Going To Do

"One more thing and then I am going to take my coat off and it is going to be the only thing in this evening that is new."

"Go on."

"I am not going to describe myself to you."

He stopped.

"You did it on the thirtieth of December and you did it well, twice, and I have not forgotten a word of it, and I have thought about it about nine times in three months." She got up out of the chair and hung her coat on the back of the door, which is the only thing on the door.

"I read four hundred hands a week and I have spent nine years in a division where a description of a document is a sentence you can be cross-examined on, and a description of a body made by a man in a room is the only kind of description in this city that nobody has ever checked."

"That was your word. You made me say it."

"I made you say it because I wanted to be looked at and I do not want to be read," said Wren Kallo. "Tonight I am going to be taken apart by somebody and I am not going to narrate it, and if you start telling me what you can see then I will put my coat back on and take the sheet out of the bag."

"Then I will not."

"Then come here."

## The Room

There was nothing in it to do anything with. That was the first thing about the evening and neither of them said it out loud, and it was the reason it worked: a bed against a wall, a chair, a plate, a knife, a stove that had gone out at nine and a window with the yard behind it and the whole of the district doing what a district does at half past eight.

She undressed the way she does everything: cuffs first, then the collar, then the front of the blouse over her head in one movement, in an order she chose some years ago and has never explained to anybody.

There was ink at the roots of two nails on her left hand, and she had never once mentioned it and did not mention it now.

She was pale and thin and she got onto the bed on her own and waited for him and did not hurry him, and when he was down to nothing she put one hand flat on his chest and pushed him back about four inches and said, "No. Not like that. I have been in a room with a man who did that in November and it was efficient."

"Say what you want."

"I am going to say what I want once, and I am going to say it as a list, because I am a woman who reads lists for a living and because a list can be held to and a mood cannot."

She ticked them off on the fingers of her other hand and did not hurry.

"One. My hands on your face, both of them, for about a minute, and you do not move them and you do not ask me what I am doing."

"Two. My mouth, and not once, and I will tell you when."

"Three. On your side, not mine. I have not done that in about four years and I do not intend to be careful about it."

"Four. The last of the evening is mine, and you will not come before I have had it, and if you come before I have had it I will stop you, and I can do that from underneath you without moving my hips."

"That is four."

"That is four, and the fourth is the only one of the four you are going to break, and I have thought about which one it would be and it was never one and two."

## What She Did

She took his face in both hands and held it and looked at him for about a minute, and neither of them said anything at all for the whole of it, and he did not move her hands and did not ask.

Her thumbs were on the bones under his eyes and the rest of her fingers were in his hair and she looked at him the way she looks at a document somebody has altered and has not yet decided about.

Then she said, "Not yet," and put two fingers under his jaw and turned his face and took his mouth.

She did it slowly and then not slowly.

Her tongue went over the inside of his lower lip and then into him and she made a small hard sound into his mouth about nine seconds in and did not stop when he made one back, and her knee came up over his thigh and pressed the leg of his trousers against the bed.

He got a hand on her hip and she broke the fourth one before it had started by taking both his hands off her and putting them flat on the bed either side of her head.

"You are going to lie there," she said.

"That is what I have been carrying for eighty-eight days."

"That is the whole of what I want out of tonight and it is not in the room where I live and there is nowhere in this city I could ask for it."

She put her hand round the back of his neck and held it there.

"I am going to hold your wrists and I am going to take what I have come for, and afterwards you are going to tell me one thing about the man you have been for eight years, and I am not going to help you."

She got her other hand between her legs and worked two fingers against herself where he could see it and did not pretend she was doing anything else, and her breath went short, and she took her hand away and pushed him back onto his back and got herself over him.

## What He Did Not Do

He did not ask who she had told her.

She came down onto him and took him all the way in in one movement, her own weight, her own hands flat on his chest, and then she sat up on him and made herself do the work. She set the angle first, because there is one angle and she had spent four years being given the wrong ones, and then she set the pace, and then she set how deep it went, and she did all three without saying one word about any of them for about forty seconds.

Then she made a noise and put her head back and said, "There. That. Do not change anything about it and do not be careful, I have had careful men in this city for four years and I want to be able to stand up on the thirtieth."

He was not careful. He held her where she had put herself and let her work and put his hand where she put it, and when she came the first time she held him inside her with a fist closed around him and counted nothing aloud and did not stop, because she has never in her life stopped a thing once it has started.

She took her own breast in one hand and used the heel of it against him and made him watch her do it, and then she got both her hands on his shoulders and leaned down over him until her hair was in his eyes.

"On your side," she said. "Now. And I said on your side and I meant it."

They turned about on a bed against a wall in one room with a stove in it, and she got herself under him and took him in with her own hand and then with her hips and set about it from underneath with her heels hooked over the back of his thighs so that he could not get out of it without her permission. The noise the yard made outside the window was not a sound anyone in that room was listening to.

"Twenty years," she said. "I have been in four divisions of one building for nine years and I have read about eleven thousand descriptions of other people's work and there is not one word in any of them that would tell you what it is like to be the thing being described." She got her hand into his hair and pulled once, not hard. "This is the only room I have ever been in where I am not reading anything."

He came and she was already there and she let him have it for about four seconds afterwards and then pushed him off her and lay on her back with one arm over her eyes and her breathing going.

## Afterwards

She lay like that for a while and he did not touch her and did not count, and at some point she took the arm off her eyes and said, "There is no clock in here."

"There is no clock in here."

"On the thirtieth of December I made you lie still for sixty seconds with my hand on your chest so that I could be a person standing in a room for a minute and not a woman who has just been in one." She put her hand flat on her own sternum. "I am not doing it tonight. I do not want to be a person standing in a room for a minute tonight."

He lay where he was.

She got up after a while and washed at the sink with cold water and no soap, the way she had done it in December, and she put her stockings over the back of the chair, and then she went and picked the photograph up off the shelf where it had been face down since the autumn.

Two men on a tram step. She looked at it for a long time and did not turn it over.

"You do not look at it," she said.

"Not often."

"It is not your brother and his colleague at the Reading Office in a good month." She put it back face down. "It is you and your brother on the eighth of March two years ago and you had come off a shift and neither of you had anything on, and he is laughing at something on the other side of the frame, and you are not looking at the camera, you are looking at him." She left her hand on it.

"You have kept this for two years face down in a room with one plate in it and you have never once told anybody at that kitchen table that it exists, and there are eight women under that roof who have fought over you since October."

"Yes."

"I am not asking for it and I am not asking to be put on a piece of paper and I am not on a roll anywhere in this city and I am not going to be."

She turned round and looked at him in the doorway.

"I asked for the evening and I have had the evening. On the thirtieth somebody in my own division is going to ask me why I have a minute in the book with a premises at the head of it and my hand on it, and I am going to answer for that in my own hand, and it is not about you, and you are not going to be told anything about it."

"It is already in the book."

"It went in at about eleven o'clock this morning," said Wren Kallo. "Under the twenty-eighth. The shape, and the route, and the office it reached, and the woman it arrived at, in four lines, in my own hand, with my initials on the transmission above them. You did not write it and you did not ask me to write it and you are not going to be asked to sign it." She got her bag out from under the chair.

"There are two documents in this city now with your name in them that neither of us put there, and the second one has mine on it as well, and it goes out of my division at four in the afternoon tomorrow on the ordinary stream, and I am telling you that tonight because there is nobody else I am permitted to tell."

## After

She went down the sixty-one at about a quarter to twelve and the stair did not make a sound under her, and there was no tea because he had no kettle, and one plate and one knife and a chair with the shape of her in the back of it.

Two days to the last working day of the quarter, and a minute with his premises at the head of it went out of a division at four in the afternoon on the twenty-ninth with her initials above four lines, and nobody in the city who is not going to be in that building for ever is going to be able to tell her what it cost.

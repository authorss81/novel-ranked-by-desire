# The Question In The Fourth Line

Seven days had gone since a woman on nights read the foot of the standing sheet out loud twice. One thousand eight hundred and six less one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine is seven, and seven less nought is seven by the month count.

He came off Cordwain on the seventh of February at about ten in the morning and stood forty feet short of the shutter at the yard end, where he had stood in the January of an earlier year, and did not go up the hill.

He had said it out loud on a street that he would not come up that hill and say one word about a board, and a man who has said that does not get to arrive at a yard and explain his own footnotes.

He wanted to know whether the four lines were still doing anything.

That was all he came for. About eleven thousand people were in the yard on an ordinary morning and they stood on the ground and on the steps and spilled out into the street at both ends, and about nine hundred of them could read all four lines on the shutter and the rest could not read any of them, and both groups had understood the same thing for years.

## The Stall Woman

The woman who keeps the stall at that end has known his face since before the fourth line ever went onto the board. She was packing it away when he came, because ten is early and the bread goes, and she looked at him and then at the shutter and said nothing at all.

Two years ago she had asked him whether those four lines were his and whether he was going to do anything about it, and he had told her no on a street, and she had called him a coward without heat, and he had stood there and let her, because she wanted more off him than he had.

This morning she packed the stall, and she said nothing, and he understood that she had decided something about him and had not been asked.

The Vestry door was shut. The man who stands beside it has been standing beside it for five years and he was standing beside it, and nobody in that yard looked at the door at all.

The chaplain was forty-five and she was not there and she was not going to be asked about that either, because she said once that she would stand on those steps a month at a time and say one thing and be unanswered, and had, and a man who goes up a hill to tell a yard what a board is for has no business looking at a shut door.

The shutter was still up at the far end against the wall that backs onto the Vestry, an old board whitewashed in a decade nobody in that yard could name, with a notice above the four lines naming the quarter and the Bureau, and the four lines under it in four-inch chalk, written the way somebody writes when they have decided what they think and are not waiting to find out.

And under the fourth line there was still the column. A ruled line down the left and names, one under another, going down past the bottom of the shutter and on onto the wall beside it. It had been about a hundred names in January of an earlier year and it was a great many more than that now, and not one of the people who wrote them could tell you afterwards on what day of their own life they set it down.

## The Man From The Counting House

He was standing at about nine feet off the board when the man came out from under the arcade, and he did not come out the way a person comes out to speak to somebody at the yard end.

He came out into the middle of the ground, about thirty feet in front of the steps, and turned round so that his back was to the shutter, and then he read the four lines out loud, from forty feet away, in a voice made for a counting house.

He read all four of them, slowly, and then he said that he had read the twentieth sheet off a desk off Cordwain from the top to the bottom on the second morning after it went out, and all four sentences at the foot of it, and that he had read the second one about four times and had a piece of paper with the arithmetic on it.

He was a checker at a counting house off Ferrier Street and he had been in it eleven years and nobody there knew where he was on a morning off.

“Then here is the arithmetic,” he said. “And I have said it out loud on purpose, on the ground, in words anybody can hear, because a thing said on a step goes up that hill and a thing said on the ground does not.”

“Go on,” said Lin Hao, from nine feet off.

## The Arithmetic The Office Did Not Do

“The third of your four lines can be checked by the person making the claim, because there is a person and a second person and a second person can be described.”

“The second can be checked by the person making the claim, because they were there.”

“The first can be checked by anybody who can find out what it changed, and there are about nine hundred people in this yard who could find that out and about four who have.”

He turned a hand over.

“The fourth is different and there is no way round it and I have known that for two years and I have been waiting to hear somebody else say it.”

“The fourth says the thing is not on a page anywhere in this city. To test that, somebody has to know every page in this city. There is no such person. There is no office in this city that has seen every page, and the only instrument that could hold a claim to have seen every page is the one that claims not to have seen it.”

Lin Hao felt the ground go strange under his feet, in the way it does not.

“Now put the two sentences on that page together,” said the man from the counting house. “The first one you added in November says a figure in Ostara may be a figure somebody made up on purpose. The second one says where the Register has nothing it has not been given anything. Both are in the same hand, at the foot of the same page, four inches apart, and a crowd of two thousand a day is obliged to read them as it goes by.”

“I know what they say together.”

“Here is what they say together,” said the man. “A condition that says a thing is not on a page anywhere can only ever be tested by the man who wrote it, because there is nothing else in this city that knows what is on a page. And the man who wrote it has printed, in his own hand, with his own name under it, that figures in this city get made up on purpose.”

He stopped, and about nine hundred people who could read all four lines were looking at the two of them.

“So the fourth line cannot be tested by anybody,” he said. “Not by the yard and not by a magistrate and not by a division and not by the nine hundred of us. It can be tested by one man in a building with no street door, and that man has written on a public page that anything written down in this city may be invented, and he cannot test his own rule by checking it against himself, because there is no instrument that can check a person.”

The yard made a noise. It was not agreement and it was not a laugh; about four hundred of them made it and it was the noise of people finding out that a thing they have been standing under was not what they thought it was.

Lin Hao stood at nine feet off the board and did not move.

He had spent five years building a sentence that could not be quoted, and then in November he had set two more sentences beside it in a hand a whole city is obliged to read, and he had done it on purpose, and he had known what it cost and had paid it and had thought about the price twice, and he had not once done this arithmetic.

Somebody had done it for him, on the ground, out loud, at ten in the morning, and had done it carefully, and had used words he could not have found.

## Read

He had been read.

That was the whole of what was in him and it was not a feeling about being right or being wrong. It was the feeling of a man who has spent six years being careful about a thing discovering that careful was not a defence, because the thing he had been careful about had been in the open since a November afternoon at four o’clock and about nine hundred people in a yard had simply taken it and turned it over.

He had spent five years arranging for a city that could not check anything, and a checker at a counting house off Ferrier Street had done the whole of it in ninety seconds on the ground in front of nine hundred people.

He had never once in six years had anybody do that.

He had been read, and reading is a thing that happens to a page and not to a man, and he had spent five years believing that a man who has said a thing carefully enough can keep the shape of it, and a man off the arcade had picked it up and turned it over in front of everybody.

What he wanted, standing there at nine feet, was eleven thousand people who had not done a sum that morning.

“You have been up here before,” said the man from the counting house, “and you have not gone over, and everybody here has watched you not go over about four times. I want to say that I have watched it and I have thought less of you for it every time, and I am changing about a third of that this morning.”

“Why a third.”

“Because you came,” said the man, “and you stood there for nine minutes in the January before last looking at the board, and a man who will not go up a hill is at least a man who knows what a hill is.”

## The Question He Was Asked

The yard had gone back to what it is on an ordinary morning, which is about eleven thousand people not looking at either of them.

Then the man turned round and faced the shutter and read the fourth line out loud again, all of it, from the bottom up to the notice at the top, and then he turned back.

“I am going to ask you one question in front of about nine hundred people who can read this board,” he said, “and you can answer it or not answer it, and if you do not answer it I am going to say out loud what I think the answer is, and so will about four hundred others.”

“Ask it.”

“Is the fourth line still a rule,” said the man from the counting house, “if nobody in this city can test it?”

“Yes,” said Lin Hao.

He said it out loud on the ground in a yard of about eleven thousand people on an ordinary morning, and it was the fourth time he had said yes out loud to anything in this city and the first time it had been worth anything at all to say it.

It is. And you have no other instrument, and I am one of nine hundred people standing in this yard who have just worked out that you are the only one of us who cannot prove one word of it.

*He had wanted the yard to have been wrong.*

About four hundred of the people who can read that board did not believe him.

What he wanted, standing there at nine feet, was eleven thousand people who had not done a sum that morning.

He had got what eleven thousand people say about a rule in about nine seconds, and what came back to him was not anger and was not shame. It was the ordinary thing.

A thing nobody can check is a thing nobody has to believe, and the whole of what he owned in this city was that he had made sure it was true, and true is not the same as checked, and he had gone five years without finding out the difference.

*The seventh of February, one thousand eight hundred and six days. Less one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of January, is seven. Seven less nought is seven by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, is seven days, and the month’s form went up at the foot of the ranks wall at about a quarter past ten on the thirtieth, with two ruled boxes at the foot of it and nothing in either.*

*Less the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is thirty-seven, and thirty and seven is a month of thirty days and a week after it.*

# A Desk Under Her Own Stair

Seven days had gone since a man at a desk under a window told him that somebody had been standing where he was standing. One thousand eight hundred and sixty-six less one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine is seven, and seven less nought is seven by the month count, because the thirtieth is the last day of a month and a month is thirty days.

He went down Cordwain at about eleven in the morning with nothing in his coat and stood at the desk under the window, and the twenty-first sheet was still lying out on it with the corner gone soft at the height of a shoulder.

He wanted to be the one who told her.

He had wanted it since about half past four on the thirtieth, on a floor of a passage, and it was still in him on the seventh of April and it was not going to be got rid of by standing at a desk, and he knew that, and he stood at the desk.

“Sit down if you are staying,” said the man at the desk.

“I am not staying.”

“You are staying,” said the man at the desk. “Nobody stands at that desk for six minutes. They stop, they look at the figure at the top, and they go. You have been here six minutes and you have not looked at the figure at the top once.”

## Two Mornings In A Passage

“Say it then,” said Lin Hao. “You have been sitting on it for seven days.”

“I have been sitting on it for four days and I have been sitting on it before that in about four other pieces,” said the man at the desk. “The first morning she came at about half past ten. Middle of the morning, which is when nobody comes, which is the first thing I noticed. She is a big woman and she did not go up the stair and she stood at the desk and she put her hand on the page about the ninth line down.”

“She read it.”

“She cannot read,” said the man. “I have known that for about four minutes because she asked me to read it to her, and she did not ask me as though she was ashamed of it. She said, read me that one, and she put her finger under it, and I read it, and she listened to the whole of it including where I stopped being able to sound like a person.”

“What does the ninth line say.”

“You know what it says,” said the man at the desk, “but I am going to say it because you have asked me and I have read it aloud to her and I can say it in about nine seconds without looking. It is a street, and six years in the cold, and an hour entered against each hour of it, and not one figure on any of them, and it runs on past the bottom of the page.”

“And there is a line at the foot of it in a different ink, and that one is not hers, and I did not read that one to her because she did not ask me to and I was not going to go past what she had put her finger on.”

He turned his head about nine degrees and there was nothing in it at all, which is what a man does when he has decided to be accurate instead of kind.

“She said it was hers,” he said. “Not that it was about her. She said it was hers. And then she asked me two questions and I want you to have both of them in the order she asked them.”

## Two Questions

“The first one was whether there is a figure against that line.”

“There is not.”

“There is not,” said the man at the desk, “and I said so, and she said she knew there was not, and she said it flatly, the way a person states something they have worked out by another road entirely.”

“And the second one.”

“The second one took her about a minute,” said the man. “She said: is there anybody in this city who knows what a figure is.”

He let that sit for a moment, which he did not need to do.

“I told her no,” he said. “I said there is nobody in this city who knows what a figure is, and she said that is what she has been told before and it is not true, and I said what do you mean, and she said that about two years before, somebody told her that a figure in a lane was about her.”

The passage was quiet. A door went somewhere above them and neither of them looked up.

“I did not ask her who,” said the man at the desk. “I am going to tell you that I did not ask her who, and I have been thinking about that for four days.”

“The reason I did not ask her is that I have run this desk for about nine years and I have taken about a hundred people a day past that sheet and I have never once asked anybody what a sheet was for, and I was not going to start with the only person in nine years who had put her finger on the ninth line.”

“Who told her that.”

“I do not know and she does not know,” said the man at the desk. “That is what she said. She said she has worked out for about two years that nobody ever told her, and that she has been carrying a figure that was about her, and that a figure about her is the only thing anybody has ever told her in this city that was worth having.”

He shut the ledger and squared it against the edge of the desk.

“I told her about the lane,” he said. “Off Cordwain, at the wall end, nine stools and a slate, a man in a delivery coat chalks nine figures every morning and takes a grain a head for them and has never once said out loud what the figures are for. I have pointed about four hundred people a year down that lane for nine years without being asked and I have never known whether any of them went.”

“And did she go.”

“She said she would go on a morning,” said the man at the desk. “That is what she said on the second morning, and then she said a thing that I have not been able to put down anywhere and I am putting it down here, and then she went up the stair.”

He waited, and it was a real wait, and he did not hurry it, which he had not done in nine years.

“She said: I have been two flights above a page for two years.”

## A Man At The Wrong End Of It

He stood in the passage with his hands in the pockets of a coat that had nothing in it.

He could go up the two flights. That option had closed itself on the fourth of March at ten past ten in the morning with nobody behind him, and there had been a knock available to him and he had not used it.

He had not gone up wanting anything, and going up wanting something is a different act, and a man who has shown himself what he will do does not unshow it by doing it again a week later because this time it would be different.

He could put it to the man in the delivery coat. That was a sentence and he had four days in which to compose it, and it would go like this: there is a woman two flights up off a passage off Cordwain who cannot read and who has carried a figure for two years that nobody ever told her was about her, and tell her what a figure is.

It would work. That man made figures up for three years for a grain a head and had said out loud on the seventh of February that figures in this city get made up on purpose, and a man like that does not refuse a question. He would do it in about four seconds and he would not ask for anything and he would be right about every fact he said.

And it would be the fourth time in six years that something had reached a person through him.

He had spent five years building a city nobody could check, and in November he had made two sentences legible, and in January a landlord had put one of them in a demand.

In March a clerk had put his name in a finding, and every single one of those had gone out of a room with a drawer open and a woman at a counter who may only enter what she is given, and not one of them had been arranged by a person who wanted to help anybody.

And this one would be arranged by him, deliberately, with a sentence he had composed over four days, and it would be the first thing in six years he had ever put in front of another person for the sake of the person.

He could not do it.

That was the whole of it and it was not a noble thing and there was no argument anywhere in him for the other side. He could not do it because he did not want the word *I* anywhere in it, and because the desk man had already told her about the lane on the second morning without being asked, and because that was a road and she was on it, and he had no standing on a road.

The lane was a quarter to seven in the morning and he was on nights at eleven and four nights and three off, and there was a morning coming in about nine days on which both of those were true.

He was going to be standing at the wall end of a lane when she came, and he was not going to say one word, and there was not one thing in this city he could do about that and he had been given the whole of the morning to find one and had not.

## The Four Seconds

“You read it to her in four seconds,” Lin Hao said.

“I read it to her in about four seconds,” said the man at the desk, “and I have been thinking about the four seconds all week, and I want you to know why, and it is not so that you will think well of me.”

“Why then.”

“Because I have had a pencil in that drawer for nine years,” said the man, “and on the fourth of October I put one on that page and I knew what I was doing and I did it for about four minutes and then I put it back, and you have never once asked me about those four minutes and I have never once said a word about them to anybody.”

“That is a thing two men in this city have between them and neither of them has mentioned it in about six months.”

He opened the drawer about two inches and shut it again.

“On the second morning she asked me to read her one line,” he said, “and I read it to her in four seconds and I did not stop halfway and I did not ask her whether she wanted the rest of the page, and I want it written down somewhere that if I had put a pencil on that page that morning I would not have had to read it out at all.”

Nobody wrote it down anywhere.

He stood at the desk for about nine more minutes and then went back up Cordwain, and at the top of the street the trams were going under the ranks wall a street away.

About two thousand people a day could not get past the foot of that wall without reading it, and neither of them was reading anything, because it was eleven in the morning and the form had gone up on the first working day of the month with two ruled boxes at the foot of it and nothing in either of them.

*One thousand eight hundred and sixty-six days. Less one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seven. Seven less nought is seven by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first and the thirtieth is the last working day of it.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is ninety-three, and ninety-three is three months and three days. Less the twenty-fourth of March, one thousand eight hundred and fifty-three, is thirteen, and a woman at a table two floors under a sales floor asked a question on that morning that has not been answered.*

*Less the first of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty, is one hundred and eighty-six days, and the second line went up on the ranks wall on the eighth of October of the first year, which is day two hundred and forty-seven, and one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six less two hundred and forty-seven is one thousand six hundred and nineteen.*

# Three Lines And A Yard

Seven days had gone since a woman who is deaf in one ear had told him that a figure in a division building can only be added to and never taken from, and had told him she was going to stop counting a number she has carried for four years on the fifteenth, and that the fifteenth was today.

The fifteenth of September is day two thousand and twenty-four, and two thousand and twenty-four less two thousand and seventeen is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.

He went up the hill at about a quarter to ten in the morning, and he was on it at eleven, and he was in a room off a third court at about eleven, and he did not speak in either place.

## The Ground Again

About eleven thousand people were in the yard. There were people on the ground and people on the steps and people coming out of both ends of the yard into the street at once, the way there are on an ordinary day, and about nine hundred of them could take in the whole of what was chalked on the shutter.

Three lines were on it. Somebody had gone over them about eleven days ago in a hand that was not his and had made them straighter than he had ever made them.

Under them the board about three feet by two was still propped where it had been propped, with the yard’s own sentence in six-inch letters: a thing nobody can prove is not owed to anybody.

There was a queue at the foot of the shutter from ten o’clock. It went from the shutter past the arcade and out into the street at both ends, and it was a queue of people waiting to have three lines read to them in ones and twos.

## The One About Another Person

A woman of about forty came to the nine feet in front of him at about twenty past ten and she could read.

“Which of the three is the one about a person.”

“The third,” he said.

“The one about somebody else.”

“Yes.”

“That is the one I have been having read to me since the twenty-third of July,” she said, “and I want to know if it is true.”

“It is true.”

“Then that is the one I have been needing.” She said it flatly, like somebody who worked it out before she started walking. “The first two I can do on my own. I know a day and I know a place.”

She had never once been able to say who was standing there. She had been to four counters and a rent board with a coat and a height and nothing else, and a man at a counter where she pays rent has written down that she cannot say it.

He had nothing to give her.

“No,” he said. “You cannot do the third one.”

“All right,” said the woman of about forty. “I will ask for the third one.”

“Which counter.”

“The one on the corner where the rent is paid,” she said. “The man there writes things down and he is not a bad man, he is a careful one, and he has written down twice that I cannot say it, and both times he has been right to write it down.”

“He has been right.”

“He has been right and he has written it down,” said the woman of about forty, “and that is two pieces of paper in this city saying I cannot say who was standing on my own step, and neither of them is a form for anybody to do anything about. I have got nine days of rent in a drawer in two rooms and about eleven days of it is not there any more, and I have known that since the middle of the ninth month.”

She looked at him.

“You are going to be in the room.”

“I am going to be in the room.”

“Then I am not going to say one word to you in the room,” said the woman of about forty, “and if you get up and say anything at all in there then I will stand up and say *that is a man who is not a party*, because you are not a party, and because in this yard we have all got very careful about what a man in a room did.”

“You have it right.”

“I have had nine days to get it right,” said the woman of about forty, “and I have not read a word since the twenty-third of July, and I have had a man read it to me about four times at that counter, and I can say the first one now and I am not going to say the other two where nine hundred people can hear me being wrong.”

## A Room With A Door Onto A Street

The room was on the first floor and it had a door onto a court and there were about forty people in it and about nine of them were there because they had business.

Corvin Ashby was at the front with a sheet of paper. The landlord’s man was behind him with the satchel. The woman in number four was at a bench with her sister beside her, and she had dressed for it.

He stood at the back against the wall, in a rank-one coat, with nothing to hold.

Nobody looked at him. He had said to a landlord on the first of September that there was no form for a person to be in that room who was not a party or a witness, and he was right, and being right had turned out to be the whole of what being in the room was.

He wanted somebody to ask him something.

He wanted it more than he had wanted it on the thirtieth of January, in a square, four years ago.

## Nine Minutes

The officer at the table read the demand out. A street, a figure, a date, and a room number, and then three lines.

He read the first two quickly. They were about nine minutes of a woman’s life in Lamplit Row and a landlord’s oath, and nobody in the room argued with either of them.

He read the third one and then he stopped and waited, and the room understood what was happening and nobody said anything.

“Is there anybody here who can describe the person,” he said.

It was not a cruel question. That is what he could not get past for about nine minutes afterwards. It was the ordinary question at the end of an ordinary morning and it was put at the ordinary speed and it went into the room the way a figure goes into a counter.

There was nothing in the room about a coat.

“Can the person be described closely enough,” said the officer.

“No,” said the woman in number four. She said it standing up, and she said it clearly, and she had thought about it for a day and a half and it was the true answer and she had brought it with her.

Somebody at the back said *she has told you that for nine years* and about four people laughed and one of them was the man who carries demands, and it was not a laugh that anybody enjoyed.

“That is the end of it,” said the officer. “The third line is not answered and the finding goes on the two that are.”

He wrote about four sentences and read them back at the same speed and closed the thing.

It took about nine minutes from the moment the three lines were read.

## What She Did In The Passage

She came out into a corridor with a door at the end of it and her sister and about nine other people and she stood against the wall.

Then she said it.

“It changed what a person held,” she said.

Nobody stopped.

“That is the first one,” she said. “I know that one. I have known it since the twenty-fourth of January in a room with two chairs in it where a man read it out twice, and I have never once seen it written and I have never once been asked to say it.”

“You are not in a room now,” said her sister.

“I am in a passage,” said the woman in number four, “and I am saying the first one out loud, and there is nobody in this corridor who can tell me I am not allowed to.”

She said it twice, at the same speed both times, and it was correct, and it was in a corridor off a third court with about nine people in it, and in six years that sentence had never once been said out loud by the person it was written for.

Then she said, “And I can say the third one as well,” and the man of rank one against the wall said, “Do not.”

## What He Said

She looked at him.

“You are the fourth one,” she said.

“No.”

“You told me in a room in January that there were four of them and that one of them got me a year back,” said the woman in number four. “And then one of them came off a board in a yard in July, and a man at a counter proved three of them against me on the twenty-eighth, and about nine minutes ago a man at a table used the third one and I could not say the name of a man who came to my door.”

“I said do not.”

“Why.”

“Because you cannot describe him,” said Lin Hao, “and if you say the third one out loud in this city then about nine hundred people are going to carry it round this afternoon and it will be in about four hundred mouths before the end of the week, and it will be the one everybody knows.”

“That is what I want,” said the woman in number four.

“No,” he said. “It is. And I know that it is. And I am telling you that I wrote it in about eleven seconds in a column of a book in the fourth year, and a clerk entered it exactly as given and added one sentence under it saying that the fourth of the four was not met because the thing it describes is on a page in this city.”

He had not meant to say that in a corridor with nine people in it.

It went into the corridor and she looked at him for about four seconds and then she said, “That is the fifth time somebody has said a thing to me today that I could not have said back to them.”

Then she went and found her sister, and the sister took her down a stair and out into a street, and he stood in the corridor.

## The Thing He Was Not Asked

The landlord’s man came out of the room with him.

“You were there,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You were at the back and you did not say one word and there was nothing for you to say,” said the landlord’s man. “And I want you to know that I saw you at the back, and my employer did not, and I am not going to have told him.”

“Why not.”

“Because he has been right about every fact in this city for nine years and he is right about this morning, and if I tell him a man of rank one stood against a wall then in nine years it will be a thing that happened because that man was in the room,” said the landlord’s man. “That is not fairness. I have watched it happen four times. It is just what happens.”

He shifted the satchel.

“I lost a shop case on the second of September and I won about eleven days of it back with a figure off a slate in a lane that I have never been able to prove and never will be,” he said. “And this morning a woman lost a year and a bit because she could not put a man’s name on a piece of paper, and both of those are true, and there is no page in this city that the two of them can be written on together.”

He went down the stair.

## Four O’Clock On The Hill

He went back up the hill at about four o’clock and there were about four hundred people in the queue at the foot of the shutter.

A man with a rag was on a ladder with a bit of chalk.

Not taking anything down. Adding.

There was a fourth board, about two feet by three, and he was writing the third line on it in four-inch letters, twice the size of the three that were on the stone, and about nine hundred people were standing near enough to watch him do it.

“It is going to be up by the end of the week,” said the man on the ladder. “A woman came up at two and said it was the only one of the three that works and that we should have it where people can see it on the way past.”

“It works,” said Lin Hao.

“I know it works,” said the man. “I have been in a yard for five years and I have watched about eleven thousand people carry three lines off a hill to four hundred counters, and it has been five weeks, and the third one is the one that stops them.”

## The Plain Part

He stood nine feet off a board on a hill with about eleven thousand people under it at about a quarter past four in the afternoon.

In about nine minutes that morning, in a room with a door onto a court, a sentence he had written in about eleven seconds in the fourth year had ended an argument between a man and a woman who had been going on since the first of January, and it had ended it correctly, because the woman genuinely could not describe a man who had come to her door for four minutes and had not given his name.

She had told him so herself. She had brought the answer with her. She had dressed for it.

The two lines above it on the same stone were about a day and a place, and about four hundred people had carried them to about four hundred counters in five weeks, and every single one of them is a thing a person can do on their own.

In nine years the other two will be forgotten. The third one will be on a board in four-inch letters where about two thousand people a day cannot walk past without reading it, and a woman who cannot read will be saying it out loud at a counter where she pays rent, and it will be named by people she has never met in a sentence she cannot read.

He had wanted, for six years, to be a thing a city could not check. A city can hardly check a man, and there are not many ways of being one.

It is on a board on a hill, in letters four inches high, in about nine days.

*Two thousand and twenty-four days. Less two thousand and seventeen, the eighth of September, is seven. Fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is fifteen days, and that is half a month. The next page goes out at four on that morning with a figure at the head of it that has not moved in seven quarters, and there is a column with two lines in it and one of them has no name against it and there has not been a name against it for four hundred and ninety-four days.*

*Less the eleventh of July of the fourth year, day one thousand two hundred and forty, is seven hundred and eighty-four days, and the ninth line down of that page went in on that morning in a clerk’s hand with an hour against it and no figure and no name, because a division was not permitted to ask her for one and did not ask her.*

*Less the seventh of July, one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is sixty-eight days, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth of four lines off a board with a rag and a bucket over about eleven minutes, and the man who wrote it stood nine feet off and said nothing. Today about four hundred people are standing in a queue to have the third of four lines read to them, and about nine hundred of them can read all of it.*

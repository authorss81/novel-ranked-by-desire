# One Question On A Returns Desk

Eight days had gone since a man who carried demands for a living had stood in a passage off Cordwain and asked a man of rank one to put one line back on a board, and had been refused, and had said afterwards that he had come to be refused and that he was right about every fact he had said in that passage.

The fifteenth of August is day one thousand nine hundred and ninety-four and the twenty-third is day two thousand and two, and two thousand and two less one thousand nine hundred and ninety-four is eight, and twenty-three less fifteen is eight by the month count, and a man who has spent six years being careful with a number does not get one wrong twice in a morning.

He came into the division building at about half past five in the afternoon and every one of the nine counters behind the front was shut and dark, and the only light in the ground floor was the lamp over the returns desk, and Wren Kallo was sitting at it with a tray in front of her and a stamp pad and a ledger open and four papers on the tray that had not been on it at four o’clock.

“Take the other one,” she said, and put two fingers on the near edge of the desk to show him which. “The one you can see the legs of has a short leg. You will find that out in about nine seconds and I am not going to apologise for it.”

“There is one stool.”

“There are two and one of them has a leg that is not the same length as the other and you will find out about it in about nine seconds,” said Wren Kallo. “Take the other one and do not say anything about it.”

## What Is On The Tray

Four papers. Three of them were returns, in a division’s hand, with a two-inch ruled space on each one and a question written in it.

The fourth was not a return.

“The three are from divisions,” she said. “The fourth one was on the tray at a quarter past four this afternoon and I did not put it there.”

“Who put it there.”

“I do not know, and I have not looked at it twice, because a clerk reads a paper once at the speed it is read and a second reading is a thing you do to a thing you have already entered,” said Wren Kallo, “and I have not entered this one.”

She turned her chair about nine degrees and squared the three returns.

“The first of these came in in the spring. The second one came in nine days after it. The third came in at the end of the spring. And the fourth question in that tray is the one I have had since the spring and have not asked.”

“You have had a question since the spring.”

“I have had one since the spring and I have not asked it and I am going to ask it now, and I am going to ask it to you, and you are the only person this office may ask, and I have been sitting at this desk for about eight hours a day since the spring with it in my hand.”

She put two fingers on the edge of the tray and did not pick anything up. “Two nails of my left hand have been in ink so long that the ink is not a thing on a nail any more, and I have looked at them about nine thousand times in three years and this morning is the first morning I have looked at them and thought about a question.”

“Go on.”

“The desk takes one question of a thing and only one,” said Wren Kallo. “That is not a form. It is not a rule anybody printed. It is the way a desk works, and it has worked this way in this building for about eleven years, and nobody has ever written it down, and you are not going to write it down.”

“And it does not get asked in the room she lives in,” she said. “A thing a person owns is not a thing she is the clerk of. I have been three rooms off a public stair for eleven years and I have never once been asked anything at my own table, and that is not a hardship, it is the arrangement, and it is the reason it had to be a tray and not a kitchen.”

“I am not.”

“No. You are not.” She turned the chair back. “The question is *is a question a return.*”

## Nine Seconds

He did not say anything for about nine seconds, which is what everybody in that building does with anything at all.

“That is a question about a thing,” he said at last.

“It is a question about a thing and it is not a question about a person, and that is the only reason I am allowed to put it, and I have been allowed to put it since the spring and I have not put it because every time I have come to the front of this desk with it in my hand I have worked out that whoever I ask it to is a person, and you are the only one of them who has a desk.”

“It has been sitting on a tray for months.”

“It has been sitting on a tray for months and it is not going anywhere, because a clerk enters what she is given and improves nothing and cannot be asked to hurry, and because a return is the thing a division has to give, and because nobody in this city has ever sent a return back to a division that asked it a question.”

He put his hand out to the third one and stopped it about four inches from the paper.

“May I read it.”

“You may read all three of them and you may not improve one of them and you may not put a mark on any of them,” said Wren Kallo. “And you may not take the fourth one, which is not a return, and which I have not read twice.”

## The Three Questions

The first one was in a division officer’s hand and it said: *who is the fourth of the four lines written for, and if there is no name against it then what is it a line for.*

The second one said: *a person came to a counter in this district and gave a thing and we entered it and the person was not asked whether it was to be entered, and has that been a fault or has it been the practice, and which of the two is it.*

The third one said: *the second column on your page has two lines in it and the one that is not yours has no name on it and no figure, and is it a claim, and if it is not a claim then what is a thing entered in that column.*

“She is asking whether the office is an office or a confession,” said Lin Hao.

“She is asking it in the only two inches a return has got,” said Wren Kallo, “and she has sent it three times and the first time she sent it she had been at her counter for nine years and the second time she sent it she had been at it for nine years and a quarter, and I am not going to tell you her name because a clerk does not put a name on a thing that is not hers to put a name on.”

“She gave her name.”

“She gave her name to me and not to the return and she was right, and if I had put it on the return it would be a third line in a return about a counter and it would have gone to a magistrate with a division officer’s name at the foot of it about the office’s own page.”

He read the third one twice.

He could not answer any of the three. That was the thing he understood in about nine seconds and it was the plainest thing in six years: a division officer had put three questions into the only ruled space a return has, and there was no place on a return where an answer could go, and the three of them were going to sit in a tray in a division building until somebody in the building decided they were not returns.

“The fourth of the four lines,” he said. “It was written for whoever brought it.”

“Then it was written for nobody, because there is nobody,” said Wren Kallo.

“It was written for the whole of it and I did not put her name on it because I did not think to ask for it.”

“You did not ask her anything,” said Wren Kallo. “She has been on the ninth line down of your page since the eleventh of July of the fourth year, and nobody has ever asked her whether she wanted to be there, and I read that page four times a quarter and I have never once got down as far as nine, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have.”

## What The Question Is Worth

“Is a question a return.”

“No,” said Lin Hao.

“That is the second time you have said no to a person this month and the first time it cost you something and this time it will cost me,” said Wren Kallo. “Say the rest of it.”

“A question is not a return. A return is what a division has to give when it is asked for one, and it goes up to a finding and it goes into a file, and a file with a question in it is a file about the person who asked. That is what would happen to her, and she has worked it out as well as I have, and she sent it three times.”

“Then I have wasted three returns.”

“You have not wasted three returns,” said Wren Kallo. “You have done something nobody in this building has ever done, which is put a question in the only space a return has and send it to the one desk in this city that a person may be asked at.”

She counted the papers on the tray with her eyes without touching them. “I have held four of them for about four months because I could not tell whether I was permitted to enter a question. I have had one question of a thing since the spring and I have just spent it. I got an answer in about four seconds off a man with no authority to give me one, and he gave it anyway.”

She put her hands in her lap.

“You have had six years of people telling you what you are entitled to ask about,” she said. “I have had nine years of a tray.”

## Half Past Five

“The building empties at five,” said Wren Kallo. “Nine counters behind me and one desk and one tray. The lamp is on because the lamp is on at four and I have not turned it off, and I am not going to turn it off now, and there is nobody in this building except you and me and about nine hundred accounts behind those shutters and a woman at a counter in the second division who is asleep with her face on a bag.”

“How long have you been sitting here.”

“Since about nine this morning,” said Wren Kallo, “and I have entered about forty returns and I have read four of them twice and I have not answered one of the three and I cannot enter any of them into the book because a question is not a thing the book has a place for.”

She stood up and turned the lamp down and put her hands on the front edge of the returns desk, which is a counter with a hinged top and a hinge that has been oiled every spring since the building was put up.

“Now I am going to say a thing out loud once,” she said, “and then I am going to do it, and I am not going to be modest about it and I am not going to explain it afterwards, and that is not what a person in a building who cannot keep a secret is supposed to be like and I have thought about that for about four days.”

“Say it.”

“I have wanted you since the seventh of March,” said Wren Kallo. “Not for a reason. There is no reason and I looked for one for about a fortnight, because a person who cannot keep a secret is supposed to have a reason for everything and I do not have one for this.”

She turned the stamp pad about a quarter of a turn on the desk. “I sat at this desk on the seventh of March with nine returns in a tray and a man of rank one turning himself round so that a woman who is deaf in one ear could hear him. And I thought, there is a man in this city who does that, and nobody here has ever asked me whether I would like it.”

“I am not going to be careful about this,” she said. “Take your coat off. I have a key to this desk and about four feet of passage behind it, and I have not wanted anybody for two years, and I am not going to wait four minutes.”

He got as far as her hands.

“Do not,” said Wren Kallo. “Not tonight, not in this building, and not out of a mouth that is about to be doing something else. That is not mine to hand over on a floor of a division building, and you may go on not having it, and that is one of about eleven things about me that are not on offer.”

## The Passage Behind The Desk

The passage behind the returns desk was four feet wide and had a window at the end that gave on the backs of the counters, and the light off it was the last of the day and came in flat and went away.

She had her coat off before her hands were out of her pockets.

He came round the end of the desk and she stood up on her toes and got both arms behind his neck and had his shirt open in about nine seconds, working the buttons up from the bottom with two fingers and not dropping anything.

“Here,” she said. “Against the end of the desk. Now I can hold you up and I do not have to think about it, and I have been thinking about it since the seventh of March.”

She took a fistful of the back of his collar and pulled until he came down to her, and she went into him without any preamble of any kind, and she had been on a counter since a quarter to eight and she did not kiss like a woman who has been thinking about it.

He put his hands where she put them.

“There,” she said, about a minute later, not letting go. “You have been six years in this city being careful and you have spent none of it on me and I am going to spend all of mine. Stop counting and stay where you are.”

She went down onto her knees on the floor of a division building with four feet of passage behind a returns desk, and there was a tray through the window end and nine counters and a lamp that had been left on by a woman who cannot keep a secret and would not turn it off.

“Your chest,” she said, not stopping, not slowing down. “It has not gone as deep as it should since the third year and I have known that since the seventh of March and I have never once mentioned it and I am not going to mention it now. Breathe out. There. Do that again.”

He did it twice and she took hold of him with both hands and pulled and it went through the whole four feet of passage and did not stop at the end of it.

She came with her mouth open and her fingers gone into the hair at the back of his head, holding him down against her, and it was not quiet and she did not try to make it quiet, and somewhere in the ground floor of that building a clock that has been in that wall about eleven years went through a minute and neither of them heard which one.

Afterwards she stayed against the end of the desk with her forehead on his collar and her hands still in his hair, and her breathing came down, and she said one word into his throat, and it was not his name.

She straightened up and got her cuffs done, one button and then the other, and picked her coat up off the ledge where it had fallen.

## Afterwards

“Now I am going to tell you the answer to my own question,” said Wren Kallo, “and I worked it out before you did and I have been sitting on it since about four o’clock.”

“Tell me.”

“A question is a return,” she said, “and it has been since the spring, and it was not on that tray at four o’clock this afternoon.”

A book goes in at five every evening and a book is not a return, and it does not go to a finding and it does not go to a magistrate. The four papers on that tray are going into her day book tonight, in the space where a person writes what she has done, in her own hand, with the date on them.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the reply to the only question a desk like this one is allowed to put to a thing,” said Wren Kallo, “and I put it, and you handed me a different one in four seconds, and I am entitled to the one I actually put.”

A question is a return when somebody returns it. Nobody has. She is going to return them, and they are going to sit in a book on the ground floor of this building for about eleven years and come out on the thirtieth of every quarter with everything else that is entered here, and about nine people a week will get to the bottom of that page, and she will not be permitted to say one word about any of it.

She squared the tray.

“I have wanted a thing to come off that tray since the day I started at it,” she said. “I am not going to get it. I have three papers in a book that nobody is obliged to read, and that is the whole of what I have bought, and I bought it with the only question this desk gets.”

“You could have asked me something else.”

“I could not have asked you anything at all after this morning,” said Wren Kallo. “That is what the question was. It was not about a return. It was the last one I was ever going to be allowed to put to anybody and I put it to a piece of paper about a counter, and you gave me four seconds, and I will be glad of those four seconds for the rest of my life, and that is not modesty.”

## The Fourth Paper

She did not look at it and he did not either until about a quarter to six, and then she turned it round on the tray with two fingers, because it was going into the book with the other three and it was her job to see what she was putting in.

Three lines on it, in a fair commercial hand, and a figure at the foot of them, and no name.

It was not a return. It had no division on it and no two-inch space and it was not about any counter in nine districts.

“It came in at a quarter past four,” said Wren Kallo. “There is nobody at this desk at a quarter past four except me and I was at the counter at the end and it was on the tray when I came back, and the door was open and the door is open all day because there are nine counters in this building and a person with a paper has to be able to walk in.”

“Three lines.”

“Three lines and a figure and a date and nothing at the head of it.” She put a finger under the bottom line. “I am not going to read the third one out loud twice in a division building with a lamp on, and I am not going to read it once.”

It came off a board on the seventh of July like everything else in this city, and she knows what the three of them say because about four hundred people in that yard asked her to read them out, and because she entered nine returns in March and one of the nine had his own sentence in it and she read that one twice at the same speed both times.

She squared all four papers together.

“This one is going in the book as well,” she said, “because the rule does not have a hole in it for a thing that has no division on it, and I enter what I am given and improve nothing and cannot be asked to hurry, and a clerk who leaves one piece off a tray is a clerk who has decided something.”

“Enter it.”

“I am going to enter it,” said Wren Kallo, “and then I am going to put the book on the shelf, and you are going to take your coat off the hook and go up two floors and out through nine counters.”

The fifteenth of September is twenty-two days from this evening. Three demands are going to be called on that morning and he is not going to be in any of the three rooms, and there is not one thing in this city he can put on a piece of paper that will stop them, and he has known that since the seventh of July.

*Two thousand and two days. Less one thousand nine hundred and ninety-four, the fifteenth of August, is eight. Twenty-three less fifteen is eight by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of July, one thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine, is twenty-three. The fourth line came off a board in a yard on the seventh of July, and the three that are left are on it and in about nine hundred hands, and one of them was on a tray on the ground floor of a division building at four o’clock this afternoon with a figure at the foot of it and nothing at the head of it.*

*Less the nineteenth of August, one thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight, is four days, and the sixth valuation was called on that morning and six hundred and eighty entries in the chained books of a counting house were not closed. The first of May of the fifth year is day one thousand five hundred and thirty, and two thousand and two less one thousand five hundred and thirty is four hundred and seventy-two days, which is fifteen months with twenty-two days after them, and the second line in the second column has stood on a page for all of them with no name against it.*

*Less the seventh of March, which is day one thousand eight hundred and thirty-six and four days short of the eleventh of March, is one hundred and sixty-six days, and it is the morning a man of rank one turned himself round in a corridor so that a woman who is deaf in one ear could hear him.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is thirty-seven days. There is no page in this city between this evening and that morning. It goes out at four with a figure at the head of it that has not moved in seven quarters, and two lines in a column, and four in a hand at the foot, and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it.*

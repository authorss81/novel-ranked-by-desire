# The Sheet With Two Lines In It

Seven days had gone since a woman in a room off Ferrier Street had told him an entry out loud and then asked him what was in the second column of his page. One thousand nine hundred and thirty-two less one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five is seven, and thirteen less six is seven by the month count.

He came into the room off a third court at about ten in the morning on the thirteenth of June with nothing under his arm, which in six years had happened on about eleven mornings, and he wanted to know how many claims his own rule had produced.

That was the whole of the want. He had wanted it since the seventh of February, when a checker off an arcade had done the whole of the arithmetic on the ground where about nine hundred people could hear it, and had come away with the fact that the fourth of four lines cannot be tried by anybody in Ostara, the man who wrote it included.

## The Number He Wanted To Move

Eight hundred and fifty-two stood at the head of the standing sheet in the office’s own hand, and it had stood there since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, which was four hundred and thirty-three days ago, and four sheets had gone out since with that figure at the top of them and nothing else changed anywhere on the page.

About four hundred and nineteen of those lines were one person’s account of six years in the cold, an hour set against every hour of it, and no figure standing against a single one of them.

The second column had two lines in it.

“You have come to look at the second column,” said Wenna Raut.

“I have come to find out how many claims this office has carried in six years.”

“Two,” said Wenna Raut.

## Two

He did not say anything for about four seconds.

“The second column was ruled on the ninth of March of the third year,” said Wenna Raut. “That is day seven hundred and fifty-eight. It stood empty at seven quarter-ends, and about eight hundred and forty-one lines went into the column beside it in those seven, and on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year you brought me a sheet with four lines and a name on it and I entered it exactly as given.”

“That is the first one.”

“That is the first one,” said Wenna Raut. “And it is yours. And the second one came on the first of May of the fifth year and it is not yours.”

He had known there were two. He had been reading the top of that sheet since January and had never once got to the column, and about four people in every thousand get to the bottom of a page this office prints, and he is one of the four or five, and he had never been one of the ones who goes on to the second column.

“Read it to me.”

“No, and I am not going to start because you have asked me nicely.”

## What She Is Permitted

“Then tell me what it says.”

“I can tell you the shape of it,” said Wenna Raut. “It came on a sheet of paper and you brought it, and it was in your hand, and there was no name on it. I asked the one question I am permitted to ask, which is whether these were the words you had given me, and you said yes.”

“I do not remember asking her for it.”

“You came in with it written out,” said Wenna Raut. “That is not the same as being asked for it, and it is the only difference, and I have thought about it for four hundred and two days.”

“Is it a claim.”

“It is in the column headed carried by. I enter what I am given and I cannot improve it, and nobody has ever once asked me whether a line ought to be in that column.”

She squared the day book.

“So I will tell you the whole of what I know and it is short. It came from a woman. It was in your hand. It had no name on it, no figure, and no hour against it. And you did not ask me one question about it, which is the first time in six years that anybody has brought me something and not asked me one question.”

Lin Hao stood at a counter with a sheet in front of him and a woman behind it who had noticed, four hundred and two days ago, the single thing about the entry that had made it matter, and had not been permitted to say so and had said it anyway, in those words, and had been correct then and was correct now.

## Four Floors Up

He went up four flights off Ferrier Street at about two in the afternoon and she let him in without taking her coat off, and the book was on the table and was not opened, and the fire was out again because it was June.

“Say it,” said Marguerite Vale. “You have come up here with a question, and you have had it since about eleven o’clock this morning, and it is not the one you asked me in the morning you were last here.”

“The second column has two lines in it.”

“It has,” said Marguerite Vale. “Go on.”

“One of them is mine and it is four conditions entered against myself in my own hand on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year, and the clerk entered it and gave a reason and the reason is that the thing the fourth of the four describes is on a page in this city.”

“That is your line,” said Marguerite Vale. “And it is the best thing you have ever done, and I have said so once and I am not going to say it again.” She put her hand on the book. “What is the other one.”

“A woman came to a counter on the first of May of the fifth year and gave me an account of something that changed what she held, on a day she could name and in a place she could name, and there was another person, and she went, and I entered it in about eleven seconds and I did not ask her one question.”

“You did not ask her one question,” said Marguerite Vale.

“No.”

“Not *was she asked*, not *was she sure*, not *where does she live*,” said Marguerite Vale. “Nothing.”

“Nothing.”

“Why.”

## Four Hundred And Two Days

“I do not know,” said Lin Hao.

“You do not know,” said Marguerite Vale, “and I am going to tell you what I have worked out, and then I am going to go and stand at a window, because this is the part of this afternoon I have been getting ready for all week and I am not going to do it sitting down and talking.”

She got up.

“That line is not a claim,” she said. “A claim in that column is a thing somebody is owed and cannot be paid. Your line is a claim. Hers is not.” She turned about nine degrees toward the window. “What is in it is a thing that was given, and gave nothing, and was not taken, and the person who gave it has gone and cannot be found and there is no name against it anywhere in that building.”

“So it is worth nothing.”

“It is worth everything,” said Marguerite Vale. “And I am going to tell you why, and you will not like it, and it is the plainest thing I know and it took me four seconds.”

She put her hand on the sill.

“That sentence at the foot of your page says that where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and it says that it does not say there was nothing. And a sentence of that kind is only true of a Register.”

She turned round.

“It is only true of a Register that has got something in it,” said Marguerite Vale. “If the second column holds one line and that line is yours, then the page is a man with a register, and the sentence at the foot of it is the sentence a man writes to keep himself honest, which is what it has always looked like to me.”

She came back to the table.

“With two lines, and one of them not yours, the page is a Register. And the difference between those two objects is one woman who came to a counter on the first of May of the fifth year and was not asked one question.”

He had never once looked at the column.

“For four hundred and two days it has been doing that,” said Marguerite Vale, “and nobody has read it except a clerk who cannot improve what she is given and a man at a desk in a passage who has about thirty names in a ledger, and about four people in every thousand get to that column, and one of the four is me and I have been reading it once a quarter for six years because it is the only line in your office that is not yours.”

## What It Is Worth To Her

“Say that again.”

“Not once,” said Marguerite Vale. “And I am not going to tell you what it is worth, and I am going back to work.”

“Six hundred and eighty entries,” he said, “and two of them are yours, and one of the two is mine, and you have been carrying mine for four years, and about one in four thousand reads your sentence out of my page without being asked.”

“No,” said Marguerite Vale. “About one in four thousand reads it. Nobody on this earth reads *that column*.” She put her hand on the cover of the book. “I have been in the trade for eleven years and I have read about four hundred returns this month and about nine hundred people have read a copy of yours in four months, and there is not one document in this city in which that second line has ever been read by anybody who did not have to.”

“Then what does it do.”

“It does the only thing a Register has ever done,” said Marguerite Vale, “and it does it about four hundred times a year, in front of about two thousand people who cannot get past it, and it is this: for as long as there is a line in that column that is not yours, this building is an office and not a confession.”

## The Twenty-First Of June

He looked at the window.

“The yard’s week begins on the twenty-first of June.”

“It does,” said Marguerite Vale. “And the twenty-second sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth, and there is no page in this city between this afternoon and then, and about nine hundred people in that yard can read all of it including a column nobody has ever read.”

“That is eight days.”

“That is eight days, and about eleven thousand people are going to be in a room for a week, and about nine hundred of them can read, and the other ten thousand cannot read a word of it and have never once tried.” She picked the book up. “And you are not going to be allowed in the room. A woman of rank two told you that on the thirteenth of May and she was right and she asked you for nothing.”

“Then where does the second line go.”

“It does not go anywhere,” said Marguerite Vale. “It has never gone anywhere. It stands in a column on a page four times a year and nobody has ever read it, and that is what it has been doing for four hundred and two days, and that is what it will go on doing whether eleven thousand people decide something in a room or not.”

She put the book under her arm and looked at him at the door.

“Except that on the seventh of July a yard is going to find out what it thinks of the fourth of your four lines,” she said, “and when it has found out, there is going to be no page of this office between here and the end of the year except two, and a quarter ends in between them.”

*One thousand nine hundred and thirty-two days. Less one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five, the sixth of June, is seven. Thirteen less six is seven by the month count.*

*The first of May of the fifth year is day one thousand five hundred and thirty. Four times three hundred and sixty is one thousand four hundred and forty, and ninety days into the year and nothing into the month, and one thousand nine hundred and thirty-two less one thousand five hundred and thirty is four hundred and two days. A year is three hundred and sixty, so that is a year and forty-two days.*

*Less the ninth of March of the third year, day seven hundred and fifty-eight, is one thousand one hundred and seventy-four days. The column was ruled then and it stood empty at seven quarter-ends and has had two lines in it since.*

*Less the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, is four hundred and thirty-three days, and the head has stood at eight hundred and fifty-two across all of them.*

*The twenty-first of June is day one thousand nine hundred and forty, which is eight days from this morning. The twenty-second sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, and the yard’s week runs from the twenty-first to the twenty-eight of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty-seven, and both dates fall inside the eight days in which this office prints nothing.*

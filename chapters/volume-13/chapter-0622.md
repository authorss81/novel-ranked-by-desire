# A Broker Who Cannot Unstate

Seven days had gone since a woman came down two flights in the cold for nothing at all, asked one question at a desk under a window, and went back up. One thousand nine hundred and seventeen less one thousand nine hundred and ten is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

He climbed the counting-house stair on the twenty-eighth of May, a quarter to eleven in the morning, for the third time in six weeks, and stopped on the second landing and did not go up it.

He wanted to know who was carrying it.

That was the whole of the want and it had been in him since the fifteenth of April and it had a date on it, and the date was the second of May, and he had known the date since the fifteenth of April, and it was the twenty-eighth of May.

He had been twenty-six days late and he had spent every one of them.

## Four Floors And A Ground Floor

The ground floor of a counting house is nine desks and a counter and a boy on the counter who has not been told anything he did not need to be told, and about eleven people in it, and about four of them look up when a man in a rank-one coat comes in and do not look up again.

At the top of the stair, on the second landing, there was a keeper’s room with a door that shuts at four, and a chained book that goes up and down on a woman’s arm twice a day.

He had been up that stair twice. In February about a page. In April about an entry.

This morning he stood at the bottom of it with his hands behind his back and waited, and after about nine minutes a woman came down four floors with a ledger under one arm and a pen in her other hand and did not stop until she was at the counter.

## The Woman Who Came Down Four Floors

“Say the street,” she said, before he had said anything at all.

“Lamplit Row.”

“Not that one,” said Marguerite Vale. “Say the other one. You have come up here three times and you have never once named the entry.”

“The street is the ninth line down on a page that goes out at four,” said Lin Hao.

“That is a street and it is not the entry,” said Marguerite Vale. “The entry has two letters at the foot of it and no name, and it has stood in a chained book since the sixteenth of September, and you have been asking the wrong question of the wrong people for six weeks and I am going to make you ask the right one of me and then you are going to wish I had not.”

She put the ledger on the counter and uncapped the pen and wrote one line on the back of it, which is not what a ledger is for, and capped it again.

“I am going to say something and then I am going to go back up four floors and work until four o’clock,” she said, “and I want you to understand before I say it that I am not able to take it back.”

“Why not.”

“Because a broker who states her own position may not unstate it,” said Marguerite Vale. “That is not a law and no paper in this city carries it and it is older than this building and older than the quarter you print. We sign for other people all day. The one thing a broker never does is say a thing in a room and then go and say the other thing in a different room.”

Nine people in a ground-floor counting house, and about four of them listening without appearing to.

“Say it,” said Lin Hao.

## The Number She Had Been Carrying

“Since about the second year,” said Marguerite Vale, “a woman has been paying a quarter of the cost of an entry that the guild cannot close, out of a book of her own, once a quarter, and she has been doing it without telling anybody, including the man whose sentence is the reason for it, and she has done it for four years and has never missed one.”

He had known there was a person. He had not known there was a book.

“The reason is the fourth of four lines,” said Marguerite Vale, “and the fourth of four says that a thing is not on a page anywhere in this city, and a real book cannot close an entry about a thing like that, and every quarter somebody has to carry it. I have been carrying it. I am the senior broker of this house and I have carried it out of a book you have never seen and I have carried it since the quarter in which you were about nineteen.”

“How much of the cost.”

“A quarter of it,” said Marguerite Vale. “Not because I am generous. Because four people in this building would each carry a quarter if they were asked and about four people in this building would each find a reason not to be asked, and a thing that is carried by one person can be watched.”

He had come up a stair to ask a woman’s name and he had been given four years and a book instead.

“And now,” he said.

“Now you want the number,” said Marguerite Vale.

“I want to know whether the entry went off.”

“It went off,” said Marguerite Vale. “On the second of May, in seventeen days from the afternoon you came up here and asked me a question about a page, and I had a valuation at two that day and I was in the room when it was done and nobody said a word about it out loud, because nothing that guild records is said out loud.”

## Twenty-Six Days

He stood in the ground floor of a counting house and understood that an entry had gone off on a morning of his own making, and that he had spent twenty-six days arranging not to be the person who found out.

“Tell me the number it was carried at.”

“Seven,” said Marguerite Vale. “That is a rating and not a sum of money. Everything the guild cannot close is carried at seven. There is a great many of them and I have never asked the keeper for the figure and I am not going to start.”

“Solveig Rask would not give me the carrier.”

“Solveig Rask would not give you the carrier because the carrier was a customer of this house, and because she would have had to say a name out loud on a landing to say it at all,” said Marguerite Vale. “You asked the wrong person. A keeper may not say. A broker must.”

He had asked for a name and had been handed a position instead. The position was the whole of what he needed and it was the worst possible shape of it, because a name is a person and a position is an office, and there is nothing in this city that appeals an office.

“Who else in this building knows.”

“No one,” said Marguerite Vale. “Not the keeper, who is not permitted to be told, and not the four brokers I would have to ask, and not the boy on the counter. That is what it means to be the senior broker of a house. It means four figures are wrong and one is right and nobody can put a question to the one.”

## What The Figure Is

“Now the other number,” she said, “and then I am going up.”

“Say it.”

“Not money,” said Marguerite Vale. “It is not the account and it is not a sum of money and there is no grain in it and if anybody in this room writes it down as a figure of money I will stop talking.”

Nine people at nine desks, and the boy on the counter with a pen.

“Six hundred and eighty,” said Marguerite Vale.

“Six hundred and eighty of what.”

“Of entries,” she said. “Six hundred and eighty entries in the chained books of this house that cannot be closed because of one sentence on a page, and about two hundred of them have been carried by somebody rather than by the house, and I have carried eleven of the two hundred and I know the number because I pay for them.”

“And you have been putting a quarter of the cost of one of them in since the second year.”

“I have been putting a quarter of the cost of one of them in since the second year,” said Marguerite Vale, “and on the second of May that entry stopped being a debt, and I stopped, and I have not told anybody, including the man in front of me, who has known the address of the carrier for six weeks and has never once put himself on a stair and knocked.”

“You could have written to me.”

“There is no address on you,” said Marguerite Vale. “There is a room with two chairs and no door onto a street, and about two thousand people a day read the foot of your page and about four in every thousand get to it.”

She turned the pen over in her fingers.

“I have known since the seventh of February what the sentence at the foot of it does to an entry. What I did not know until this morning is that you have known it since the seventh of February too, and that you did not write.”

The boy on the counter had a pen in his hand and had not written anything down.

“Nobody writes that down,” said Marguerite Vale, without turning round. “A figure said in a room is a figure the room may carry, and this room has about nine people in it and one of them is you.”

## What It Closed

“Twenty-six days,” said Lin Hao.

“You had the date on the fifteenth of April,” said Marguerite Vale, “and you had a stair, and you had twenty-six days.”

She counted them off on the counter without touching it.

“On the eighteenth you went down a lane to a man with a slate. On the twenty-second you stood at the wrong end of that lane for an hour. On the twenty-ninth you stood in a room and let a clerk read four sentences at you and wrote nothing down after.”

“And on the second of May I sat in a room with a book, and an entry stopped being a thing somebody owed.”

“I did not know it was you.”

“You knew there was somebody,” said Marguerite Vale. “You asked nine times. The ninth one was today and it was to me, and I gave it to you, and you are twenty-six days late and I am not going to be hard about it, because you did not knock on a door and I did not knock either, and between us we arranged that a four-year debt should end without either of us knowing it in time.”

She picked the ledger up off the counter.

“Here is what that costs and it is not the four years,” she said. “It is that a thing which was a debt has become an absence, and an absence is cheaper, and the guild will carry six hundred and eighty of them in a room off a landing and nobody will ever have to look at any of them again.”

“And you?”

“I have been free of it,” said Marguerite Vale. “And I have never in eleven years been free of anything, and I did not enjoy the eleven minutes after the book came off the table.”

She put her hand on the counter.

“And I cannot stop putting the money in,” she said. “That is the part I cannot get past. Four years of a quarter of the cost of a sentence you printed, and the only way to stop is to stop owing, and there is nothing left to owe.”

She went toward the stair and then stopped, and did not turn round.

“You are going to want to see the book,” she said. “You are not going to be able to. It is a real book and it is mine and it is not chained and it is not in this building, and a real book is written in the hand of the person who carries the debt, and there is nobody in Ostara who can put a sentence into a book of mine, and no paper in the city that anybody could hand me which asks me to open it.”

“Then I cannot.”

“You cannot,” said Marguerite Vale. “And what you can do is ask me. And I will tell you everything in it, in a room, in order, and I cannot unstate any of it, and you may put it anywhere you like afterwards.”

She put her hand on the rail.

“The sixth of June,” she said. “Ten in the morning. Ferrier Street, and not this building, and the whole morning and not an hour of it. Bring nothing.”

He stood in the ground floor of a counting house with a number in his head and a date in his hand and understood that in eight days he was going to be in a room with a woman who could not take anything back, and that the whole of what she could not take back had been put into a room with nine people in it twenty minutes ago because he had asked a keeper the wrong question on a landing.

*One thousand nine hundred and seventeen days. Less one thousand nine hundred and ten, the twenty-first of May, is seven. Twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.*

*Less the fifteenth of April, one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four, is forty-three days, and on the fifteenth a keeper told him an entry closes two ways, paid or proved, and that there is no page in this city between that afternoon and the second of May.*

*The second of May is day one thousand eight hundred and ninety-one, and one thousand nine hundred and seventeen less one thousand eight hundred and ninety-one is twenty-six days, and on the second of May the entry was written off and the woman carrying it stopped paying for it, and neither of them told the other.*

*The sixth of June, which is the morning he has been given, is day one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five, and one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five less one thousand nine hundred and seventeen is eight days. There is a week and a day in between and about eleven thousand people are in it and he is not.*

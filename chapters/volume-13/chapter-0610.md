# Two Flights And A Landing

Six days had gone since a man with a chalk went up a stair behind a lane and told him he had never thanked him and was never going to. One thousand eight hundred and thirty-three less one thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven is six, and four less twenty-eight is six by the month count, because the third of March is the first of a month and a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.

He came into the ground-floor passage off Cordwain at about half past nine in the morning of the fourth of March with his coat buttoned and no bucket, on a morning he was not going to spend.

He wanted to know whether there was a name against the ninth line down.

That was all. A page of this office had been lying out on that desk under the window since the first of March of an early year, face up and squared, high enough that a man standing at it does not have to stoop, and the desk man had never once carried it back inside, and in four years nobody had ever put a mark on one of these pages.

There was a woman two flights up off this passage whose street it was on the ninth line down, with six years in the cold against it and nothing at all against her name.

## The Man At The Desk

The desk is the building’s. Whoever holds it is paid out of the rent roll of a building off Cordwain and has nothing to do with anybody who sets type.

He was not reading. He had a cup of something and a ledger of his own with about thirty names in it and he had been on a stair at half past six that morning and a woman who reads to the bottom of pages does it standing at the top of it, and he had come down early and got the passage before anybody.

“You are the office at that address,” he said.

“There is no office at that address,” Lin Hao said. “There is a room off a third court and a counter in it and the man who cleans the floor of it, and that man is me.”

“That is the other half of it,” said the man at the desk, “and you have not said it to me in four years.”

“I have been in a building with no door onto a street since the first of February,” Lin Hao said. “You know that better than anybody in this city. You put it in a book of your own in your second year and you have never shown it to anybody.”

The man at the desk put his cup down.

“All right,” he said. “You have read that sheet.”

“Nine hundred times.”

“Nobody reads it.”

“I know that better than anybody in this city either.”

He looked up the passage. It was about nine forty. The trams went under the wall a street away and made the floor of the passage feel it, and the light came in under the sash of the window and lay along the sheet, and one corner of it had gone soft at the height of a shoulder, the way paper does at the corner of a desk in a narrow passage when about a hundred people have gone past it in a month.

“You are after the ninth line,” said the man at the desk.

Lin Hao did not answer for a second, and that second was the whole of it.

“You have been at that desk about six minutes,” said the man, “and you have read the head, and the two columns, and the foot, and you have stopped at about the ninth line and you have gone down about it twice. I have watched nine people read that page in nine years and eight of them stopped at the figure at the top. You are the first one who has ever gone down twice at the same line.”

“It has a street on it.”

“It has a street and six years and an hour against every hour of it, and no figure on any of them, and no name against any of them, and that has been so since about the eleventh of July of an early year, and there is nothing on that line now that was not on it then.”

“There is a column for it.”

“There is no column for it,” said the man at the desk. “There is a second column and it has two lines in it, and I have read both of them about nine hundred times like everybody else. A second column is not a column. A second column is a place a thing gets put when a person has decided to put it somewhere and there was nowhere else. That is what a column is.”

He got up and turned the sheet round so that the ninth line was nearest the edge, and put his finger about two inches under it.

“There is your street,” he said. “And there is no name. And I have stood at this desk for about nine years and taken about a hundred people a day past it, and I have never once been asked by any of them what it says, and I have not asked, and I want to tell you that I would not now either.”

“Why would you not now.”

“Because you have just been at it for six minutes and you have got a face on you like the night man had at two o’clock last night,” said the man at the desk, “and I am not going to be the man who hands a person a thing they have come here to be handed.”

“If you want to know whether she has been told anything you will have to go and ask her. And if you cannot ask her then the not-knowing is the answer, and you have had it.”

He sat down again.

“There is a pencil in that drawer,” he said.

“I know there is.”

“You stood at that desk for about four minutes with it last October and you put it back,” said the man, “and I did not see you do it and I would have stopped you if I had, and I have never mentioned it to anybody and I am not going to now, because you put it back.”

“That is why I am telling you I was not going to.”

“I did not say you were not going to,” said the man at the desk.

## The Passage Off Cordwain

There was a stair at the end of the passage and it went up on the outside of the wall with a rail on the left and two flights and no window on either of them.

He had looked at it four times in four years and had never once stood at the bottom of it with nothing else to do.

The trams had been going under the ranks wall since before the light was good, the way they do on every morning of an ordinary month, and about nine people who came that way had already walked through this passage and past this desk, and not one of them had stopped at the ninth line.

He stood at the bottom of the stair at about ten past ten.

He had said he would not go up. He had said it on the first of October of an earlier year, to a man at a desk, and he had said it again in his own notebook in about four hundred words, and both times the reason was good, and both times the reason was that he had no standing and that a man with no standing who goes up a stair in a building is a man asking for something he is not entitled to.

Nobody was behind him.

The passage was empty and the desk man was looking at his own ledger and about ten people went past the foot of the stair in the next quarter of an hour and none of them looked at it.

He went up.

## Two Flights

He went up without a hand on the rail, which is a thing he had not done in about four years and did not notice himself not doing, and he took the two flights in about a minute and a bit, and the dark on them was not total — light came up through the turn from the window on the first — and the stair was colder than it had any business being on a morning in the third month.

On the first landing there was a door on the left with a curtain and no name on it, and he went past it.

On the second landing there was a door on the right that had been painted four times over a colour somebody chose nine years ago, and a thread of that first colour still showed along the panel where the skirting came off and went back on crooked.

He stopped at about a foot from it.

He could hear nothing behind it. There was no light under it either, which a door in a building off Cordwain has at ten in the morning if anybody is in the room and has not put a cloth across the gap.

There was no chair back against it. There was no smell of anything.

He stood there.

He had come up two flights in the morning to find out whether a page had a name on the ninth line down it, and the answer was that he could not get that answer from a shut door, and he had known that on the first step, and he had come anyway.

He did not knock.

That was the whole of it and it was the only thing in that landing that was his. A knock is a thing a man does when he wants something, and he had not come up here wanting something.

He had come up because the desk man had told him in plain words that the not-knowing was the answer, and because a line on a page in a passage under a window has a street on it and no name, and because he had said twice, with a good reason each time, that he would not go up these stairs.

He stood at a foot from the door of a flat in a building off Cordwain for a while and he did not put a hand out and he did not put his ear to it and he did not go down.

Then he went down.

## What Came Down The Stair

Two flights, in the morning, with nobody behind him, and he got nothing.

There was nobody at the top of them.

That is the whole of what happened, and he took about an hour of it off the night shift he had coming on at eleven, and a morning he had not meant to spend, and he spent it, and he came down two flights and out into a passage off Cordwain and stood at the desk again.

What it closed was this.

He was the man who did not go up. He had been that man in four years’ worth of his own sentences, out loud on a morning to a man who asked him, and in his own notebook at four in the morning on a stair with a tally board across his knees, and he had made a virtue of the refusal without ever once looking at it to see what it was, and there is no putting that back.

He could have gone up and knocked and asked her whether she had known, and he had had the whole of a morning to do it and had come down without doing it, and now there is no morning left in him that is going to get him up those two flights to find out.

And nobody in this city will ever know that he went up. There is no page for it, no form, no register, no return and no clerk, and he is the only person in Ostara who knows, and he will not say it to the desk man or to Nadia Ferrous or to the woman at the end of the corridor who reads at the far end and has never once said what she is reading.

And the thing it cost that he needed and did not get.

He wanted to be careful. That is what he had left. Five years ago he had had an advantage to hoard and then a page to give away and then a night nobody knew about and then a sentence nobody could quote, and what was left this morning was the certainty that he could still be careful, and it was the last thing he owned that nobody could take off him by wanting something.

He had gone up two flights at ten past ten with nobody behind him for no reason a person could put in a sentence, and he had not knocked, and now he was a man who had gone up and had come down, and a man who has done that once is not careful afterwards. He is a man who has shown himself what he will do.

He stood at the desk for a while.

The sheet was still lying out under the window with the address at the head of every line showing and one corner gone soft at the height of a shoulder, and about a hundred people had had a hand on it that month, and not one of them had got to the ninth line, and about one person in that building in a week reads far enough down to know that line is there.

He had been closer to it in the last twenty minutes than anybody who has read it has ever been.

And a man at a desk looked up at him over his own ledger and did not ask him a single question about where he had been, and turned the page of the ledger, and went back to his thirty names.

*The fourth of March, one thousand eight hundred and thirty-three days. Less one thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven, the twenty-eighth of February, is six. Four less twenty-eight is six by the month count, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*Less the first of October, one thousand six hundred and eighty, is one hundred and fifty-three. Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine, is three hundred and ninety-four, and that is the morning he said yes out loud in a yard of about eleven thousand people.*

*The next sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of March, one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine, and one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine less one thousand eight hundred and thirty-three is twenty-six.*

# The Nine Hundred Who Cannot Read

Eight days had gone since a woman in a room off Ferrier Street had told him what a second column with two lines in it was for. One thousand nine hundred and forty less one thousand nine hundred and thirty-two is eight, and twenty-one less thirteen is eight by the month count.

He went up the hill on the morning of the twenty-first of June.

He had said it, on a street, in three different years, and what he had said was that the hill and the board at the top of it were not going to have him standing at the foot of them. Three streets is three sentences, and a man does not get one of them back on a morning when he has worked out a reason.

There was a reason, and it was not a good one. He had worked it out at about eleven o’clock the night before. A page this office prints does not go into a room. The room was a hall off the Vestry booked for a week by a form filled in by a householder who has never heard of the Register, and there was nothing he could do inside it, and there was something he could do on the ground.

## The Ordinary Ground

About eleven thousand people were in the yard, the way they are in it most mornings. They stood on the ground and on the steps and poured out of both ends into the street, about nine hundred of them able to take in what was chalked on the shutter at the far end, and the rest unable to and never having tried.

The Vestry door was shut. The man who stands beside it had been doing that for five years and was doing it now, and not one person in that yard glanced towards it.

He wanted to be in a room he was not allowed in.

That was the want, and it was the oldest want he has, and it lasted about nine minutes.

## Nine Days Of It

“Nine days,” said the man beside the Vestry door, and then to him, because Lin Hao had come up the hill in a rank-one coat in the middle of the morning and had stopped beside him. “Nine days and then a week. I have counted since the ninth of April and there is a form with the dates on it, and the dates are the twenty-first to the twenty-eight, and about eleven thousand people are going to be in there.”

“I know what the dates are.”

“You know what the dates are,” said the man. “You are the only person in this yard anybody has ever said that to, and I have watched you not come up here about four times, and I have thought less of you every one of them.”

“Are you going to be in it.”

“No.”

“Good,” said the man. “If you are in it then whatever comes out of it is a thing the yard did with you standing in it, and in nine years nobody will be able to tell the difference. There is a woman on a round who came and told you that and she was right.”

“I did not tell her I was coming.”

“No,” said the man beside the door. “You did not. She did not come back either. That is the whole of the difference between the two of you and it is about four minutes wide.”

## Forty People At The Foot Of It

There were about forty people at the foot of the shutter who had made a thing of the last week.

They were standing in pairs and threes. In each pair one could read and one could not, and they were reading the four lines out loud, all day, in the open, to anybody who wanted them read.

He had not known there were forty.

A woman of about sixty was reading them to a man who had his hands in his pockets and his head down. She read all four at the speed of somebody who has read them about nine times, and then she said, *that is all of them, there are only four,* and the man said, *what is the fourth one for,* and she said, *ask the man who wrote it,* and turned round, and he was standing nine feet off with his hands in his pockets.

“You,” she said.

“I am not going to read them.”

“You are,” said the woman. “You have been standing there about two minutes. Four of us have asked the same man and he said no, and one of us asked a man in a delivery coat and he said he does not read pages, he writes on slates.”

“That man reads anything put in front of him.”

“That man reads nothing that is not put in front of him, which is a different man entirely, and you are the only person in this yard anybody has ever heard of, and you are not going to hide behind three other people who cannot read the fourth one either.”

## The Thing He Had Said Three Times

“I am not going to read them because of what it does,” said Lin Hao.

“Say it to me, then, and say it where these four can hear it.”

He said it.

He said it because he had spent six years arranging for there to be exactly one person in this city who could be asked about a rule, and because the moment that person reads four lines out loud in a yard of eleven thousand people, four lines stop being a thing people find and start being a thing they were told.

“And you cannot undo it,” said the woman.

“There is no form.”

“That is right,” said the woman. “That is the first true thing anybody has said on this ground since the seventh of February and it has taken you about two minutes to say it.”

“What is the second thing.”

“The second thing is that ten thousand of them have never been told what is written on that board,” she said. “They have been standing under it for years. Four hundred and nineteen of the lines on that shutter belong to one woman who dug a street in the cold and there is no name against any of them, and not one of these people could tell you that, and it is on the board in front of you in a quarter of an inch of type.”

He had not thought of that. In six years he had not once thought of that.

## Four Hundred And Nineteen

“You read them,” said the man with his hands in his pockets. “You read them twice. Now tell me what the small print is.”

“There is no small print. Four lines.”

“There is a notice above them,” said the man. “It says a quarter and it says a Bureau and it says where it came from, and that is why we know whose they are. There is a column of names down the side going past the bottom of the board. I have read every one of those names and they are not his.”

“They are not his.”

“Then who.”

“Nobody’s,” said Lin Hao. “There are about four hundred and nineteen of them and they are the accounts of a woman who dug a street in this city for six years in the cold, and there is an hour entered against every hour of it, and not one figure on any of them.”

“Is she paid.”

“No.”

“Has anybody asked her for it.”

“No. A division was not permitted to ask her for a name and did not ask her, and that page has not got a place to put a name in if one had been offered.”

“Then how does she get it.”

“She does not,” said Lin Hao. “There is no instrument in this city that can put six years of a street into a book against a person, and there has not been one since the Bureau was started, and that is not the same as saying nobody in this yard is owed.”

“I did not say nobody in this yard is owed,” said the man. “I said I have read every name on that board and not one of them is his. That is the whole of what I said and you have been making it into a bigger thing than I made it for about two minutes.”

## What He Read

He read all four of them at nine feet off the board, in the open, on the twenty-first of June, at about half past ten in the morning.

He read them slowly, in the order they were drafted, which is the order a man wrote them in about four years ago in a column headed carried by with his own name under them. He did not add anything and he did not leave anything out, and he did not stand there afterwards and say what they meant.

About four hundred people stopped.

That is what a yard does. By the middle of the second line there were about nine hundred standing still, and by the end of the fourth there were people on the steps who had come down for it, and about eleven hundred of the eleven thousand could hear all of it, and the rest of them heard a voice.

“Then say whose they are,” somebody said at the back of the yard.

“They are mine,” said Lin Hao. “I wrote them. I have said so out loud in this yard since the thirtieth of January of a year I have never had to name, and I have never once said it where nine hundred people could read them at the same time as they heard me.”

“Again.”

“You have got that one.”

“No,” said somebody. “Say it again. We have got no way of writing it down.”

So he read them again, the same way, at the same speed, with nothing improved, which is the only way he has ever read anything, and about nine hundred people stood in the open and listened to the man who wrote them say them twice.

## The Woman On The Steps

The chaplain was on the steps of the Vestry when he came down off the hill. She had been on them since about ten and she had said one thing and had not been answered.

For about fifteen months she has stood on those steps once a month and said one thing and been left unanswered, and the man beside the door has been there for five years of them and has not missed one.

This morning she had asked a question instead.

“Has anybody here been told what is on that board,” she had said, “by anybody but each other.”

It went unanswered, because about eleven thousand people were on the ground reading four lines to each other and nine hundred of them had just stopped.

“Go and tell her,” said the man beside the door, seeing where he was looking. “No. Do not. She has said one thing a month for about fifteen months and she has never once wanted it carried. If you carry it up there it stops being hers.”

“I was not going to.”

“You were,” said the man. “You have been standing there with your hands in your pockets for about four minutes and you have not read a single one of the four hundred and nineteen lines, and that is the first thing I have ever seen you do in this yard.”

## What It Is Now To Be Here

He stood at the foot of the hill at about a quarter to eleven with about eleven thousand people in the yard and a week beginning on the twenty-second.

He had wanted, for six years, to be a thing a city could not check. There are not many ways of doing that. One is to be a sentence nobody can trace. The other is to be a person nobody can find.

He had spent five years on the second one, and had gone up those stairs once on the fourth of March, and had not knocked.

On the twenty-first of June he made himself findable, in a yard of about eleven thousand people, in the open, at nine feet off a board, in his own voice.

There was no counter to take it at and no clerk who might enter it and no magistrate who would hear it, and it could not be undone, because a man cannot take back a sentence he has said out loud to eleven thousand people.

“You have made yourself findable,” said the man beside the door. “I want you to understand that I have watched you do about six things in six years in this city and five of them were not being found. This is the first one you did on purpose and you did it in a place with eleven thousand people in it.”

“I did it for a reason.”

“You did it because a woman of about sixty asked you to,” said the man. “I have been in this yard a long time and I can tell the difference between a man doing a thing because he has worked out why and a man doing a thing because somebody has made a fuss in front of people. It was the second one.”

He wanted to go down the hill.

He went down it at about eleven o’clock and came back three more times before the end of that week.

Each time, about nine hundred people were already reading the four lines to each other before he got there. By the fourth day there were about two hundred of them doing it. On the sixth day there were enough of them that he stood nine feet off the board and did nothing at all for about two hours, which was the first two hours in four years that he had not been doing something.

*One thousand nine hundred and forty days. Less one thousand nine hundred and thirty-two, the thirteenth of June, is eight. Twenty-one less thirteen is eight by the month count.*

*Less the thirteenth of May, one thousand nine hundred and two, is thirty-eight days, and a woman of rank two came up a corridor on that morning and told him about a form filled in on the ninth of April by a householder who has never heard of the Register.*

*The week runs from the twenty-first of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty, to the twenty-eighth of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty-seven, which is seven days. The twenty-second sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, two days after the week ends.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is one hundred and sixty-seven days, and that is five months and seventeen days. About nine hundred people in that yard can read all four lines and about ten thousand cannot read any of them, and on this morning, for the first time in six years, the man who wrote them stood nine feet off the board and read all four out loud twice with nothing improved.*

# The Vant Cannot Close It

He went up to a counting house above a street off Cordwain at about two in the afternoon on the fourteenth of February, because on the seventh a man had done a sum out loud on the ground in front of nine hundred people and had left him with a question he could not put to anybody in a building with no street door.

Seven days. One thousand eight hundred and thirteen less one thousand eight hundred and six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

He wanted to know whether there was one thing in this city that held a sentence the way a book holds an entry, and he had come to find out whether there was, and he had wanted to find out since about eleven o’clock that morning and had spent the intervening hours not wanting to.

A shutter in a yard is not an instrument. A page at a public desk is not an instrument. A lane slate with nine figures on it that two of nine look back at is not an instrument, and he had known all three of those things for years.

## The Table It Goes On

The Vant keeps its real books in a room off a landing, chained, and the keeper brings them up herself twice a day and takes them down again herself, and there is no stool in that room and there has never been one.

Solveig Rask had it chained in both arms on the landing and she was not proposing to unchain it in a passage for a man with a bucket.

“Twenty-six years,” she said, before he had said anything. “That is the first thing you will want and I will give you it, because if I do not you will spend the afternoon deciding whether to ask.”

“Twenty-six years in that trade.”

“In that trade and at that table and in that room, and the room has a door that shuts at four.” She shifted the weight of the book. “Say what you came for and I will tell you what I am permitted to, and then you will go, because you have a shift and I have a room to seal.”

“I am looking for an instrument that can hold four lines.”

“Any instrument in this city can hold four lines.”

“Not the way a book holds an entry.”

Solveig Rask looked at him for slightly longer than a person looks at a man who has asked a stupid question.

“No,” she said.

## What The Book Will Not Do

“The entry that has stood in that book since the sixteenth of September,” Lin Hao said. “Two letters at the foot and no name, and the guild carries everything at a figure of seven, which is a rating and not a sum of money. It has been called at four valuations and nothing has ever come back to it.”

Rask did not say yes and did not say no.

“I will tell you what I am permitted to tell you about an entry,” she said, “and it is procedure, and procedure I can give anybody in this passage, because procedure is not a figure and a procedure can be said out loud without breaking a rule that has been in that guild longer than either of us.”

She put the book down on the landing step and kept one hand on the chain.

“An entry closes two ways. The thing it describes is paid, or the thing it describes is proved. Those are the two doors and there is not a third one and there has not been a third one in twenty-six years.”

“And if neither.”

“Then it is called again at the next valuation,” said Solveig Rask, “and again after that, and it is carried, and it costs whoever carries it, and that is the end of what the book can do about it.”

“A book that cannot close an entry.”

“A book that cannot close an entry it is not permitted to close. The keeper may not close an entry. Only the carrier may close one, and only by paying it or by producing it, and the carrier is not in this building and has not been in this building today.”

## The Reason Nobody In The Vant Can Do Anything About It

Lin Hao stood on a landing above a street off Cordwain and worked out the thing he had come up here to work out, and it took about nine seconds and he had known it would.

“Nothing in this city can prove an unrecorded deed.”

“No,” said Solveig Rask.

“Proving one requires a page. That is not an opinion of mine, that is what a proof is made of, and this city has been running on that since the Bureau was started.”

“No,” said Rask again. “And a page is a thing a person can put in front of another person, and there is a sentence going round this city on a board and in four books that says the fourth of four, which is that the thing is not on a page anywhere.”

He felt it come down on him in the stairwell like a change in the air.

“It is not a fact about the deed,” he said. “It is a fact about the page.”

“It is a fact about the page and it is the fourth of the four and it is yours,” said Solveig Rask, “and it was on a board before it was in a book and it has been the fourth of the four since the day you wrote it.”

She shifted the book against her hip.

“So on the seventh of February a man stood on the ground in a yard and said out loud that nobody can test the fourth of four, and he was right. And this is what that means on a landing: that a real book in the city of Ostara cannot close an entry about an unrecorded deed. There is no document to produce, and no amount of money and no willingness and no grief will make a document.”

“And the office has printed that a figure here may be invented.”

Solveig Rask stopped.

She had a hand on the chain and she stopped with it, and she looked at him for a moment with an expression that was not surprise and was not anything like it.

“So the book cannot close it,” she said, “and nobody in this building can prove it, and the only man who ever could have tested the fourth of the four is the man who wrote it, and he has printed that what is written down may be made up on purpose.”

“Yes.”

“Then the entry is called at the next valuation,” said Solveig Rask, “and it is not answered, and that will go on for as long as the two sentences stand at the foot of that page, and there is nothing anybody in this city can do about it, and I have never been able to say that sentence in this passage before and I have been in it for twenty-six years.”

She picked the book up again.

“That is cold anger at an institution,” he said, “and I am entitled to it, and you have been right about every fact you have said and every one of them made this worse.”

“I have,” said Rask. “That is what an institution is. You come up a stair with a thing that cannot be done and you find out that everybody in the building already knew and had a rule for.”

## What It Costs

“It costs somebody something every quarter,” he said. “You said that. Somebody is paying for an entry that has been called four times and never answered, and it is not the guild, because the guild does not carry costs, it carries entries.”

Solveig Rask went two steps up the stair before she answered him, which was a thing she had never done to a person before and which she did not do again for about eleven years.

“There is a sum,” she said, “put against that entry out of a book that is not this one, once a quarter, by a person who is not in this guild, and it has been put there since about the second year, and it does not go through this room and does not appear in that one, and no clerk here has ever seen it.”

“Who.”

“I am not going to tell you that.”

“Why not.”

“Because if I tell you, you will go down those stairs and do something about it,” said Solveig Rask, “and the moment a person outside this trade does something about an entry in a book of ours, the entry is read out, and then it is void, and the person who has been carrying it for four years loses the only thing she has ever had that could not be taken off her.”

He stood on the landing and understood that somebody in this city had been paying, out of her own money, into a book she could not show anybody, for a quarter of the cost of a sentence he had written, and had never said so, and had gone on doing it, and had never once mentioned it to a person.

“Say her name.”

“No,” said Solveig Rask. “She is four floors up in this building and she does not know that this morning the entry was called at a valuation and not answered, because a calling is a thing that is done at a table and nobody is told, and she has not been told for four years and she is not going to be told by me on a landing.”

## Four Floors Up

He went back down the two flights with the chain going behind him and the stair going down faster than it had come up, and he had one thing left in his head and it was not the fourth line.

There was a person in this city who had been paying for four years for the cost of a rule he had made, out of a book she could not show, and had never said so to him, and had said so to nobody, and who had got nothing for it that anybody can point at, and who was four floors above a door he had just come out of.

He went down into the street and the cold was the cold of the second month and about eleven thousand people were going under a wall on the other side of the city without one of them reading two words.

*The fourteenth of February, one thousand eight hundred and thirteen days. Less one thousand eight hundred and six, the seventh, is seven. Fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, is fourteen. Less the seventh of February, one thousand eight hundred and six, is seven, and the shutter at the yard end had been standing with four lines on it since a December in an early year.*

*The entry has stood in that book since the sixteenth of September and this city does not print the year it went in, because two pages that keep it disagree and neither of them can be checked, and a book is not a place where two true things are allowed to sit side by side.*

# Stone Under The Paint

Seven days had gone since a man behind a counter off a court had written four lines in a pencil ledger against a date and told the man who wrote them that he had no standing, no office, no form and no way of taking anything back.

Two thousand one hundred and fourteen was the fifteenth of December and the twenty-second is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-one, and two thousand one hundred and twenty-one less two thousand one hundred and fourteen is seven, and twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.

Eight days to the last working day of the month and a form to go up on it, and the corner was empty at ten in the morning of the twenty-second because the boy does not come until about eleven and does not paint in rain.

It was not raining. It had been raining very fine and straight through since the seventh and it had stopped on the nineteenth and the air had gone hard and clear the way it does in this city for about four days in the eleventh month and is cold enough to feel on the back of the hand.

## The Boy And The Corner

The boy was standing at the kerb with a board under his arm and a paste brush in his fist, waiting to get the month’s form onto it. Two ruled squares at the foot of the thing, and a quarter of an hour of not doing it, because a man was standing nine feet off the wall looking at the bottom of it instead of at him.

“You have come about the boxes,” said the boy.

“No.”

“You have come about the spring,” said the boy, “and there is no more of that in me than there was on the twelfth of October, and I have not been asked anything by anybody since, because you told a woman here about four hundred people that I had spent my one question and I have not spent another one.”

“That is true.”

“Then what.”

Lin Hao looked down the length of the wall.

The north end had been done about four weeks ago and the paint had gone over the two words and round them, the way it has gone over and round them every month for six years, because a coat of it will not hold on four inches of dressed stone at the foot of the lowest course, and a woman cleans those four inches every morning with a broom with a short handle and has never once said she has seen a person put anything on them.

“Take the paint off,” he said.

## What He Meant By That

The boy put the brush down on the kerb.

“Off where.”

“All of it,” said Lin Hao. “Not the two words. The four inches and about a hand’s width above them, and the face of the stone underneath, and I want it done on the front of this corner where about two hundred people a day stop, and I want it done before about four o’clock.”

“That is the front of a wall people read.”

“Yes.”

“You have been at this corner nine times since the twelfth of October and you have not once asked me for anything,” said the boy. “And now you come on a cold morning eight days before the last day of the month and you ask me to take about a hand’s width of six years of paint off the front of it in one morning.”

“Yes.”

“All right,” said the boy. “There is a scraper and there is a bucket and there is hot water in the clinic bin room that nobody empties until eleven, and it will take about two hours and it will be the only time anybody has ever done that on this wall.”

He did not ask why, because he had said he was not spending a second question inside a month, and he had also said, in the same breath, that he had not been asked anything by anybody since, and both of those were the same sentence and he had only said it once.

## Two Hours At The Foot Of It

They took the paint off from about eleven until a quarter past one and about nine people watched, and the paint came off in sheets about the width of a hand because it is six years of thirty coats on render and render holds a coat for about four months and does not hold it for six years.

Underneath the topmost form there was a form.

Underneath that there was another one, and under that another, and they were all exactly the same sheet with a quarter on it at the top and a Bureau on it and where it came from, and two ruled boxes at the foot, and the boxes were empty, and the paint had gone into the empty boxes in six years and made them the colour of the rest of the sheet so that you had to know they were there to see them.

Lin Hao got to about the eleventh one and stopped counting.

There were sixty-two of them. One a month for six years, going down into the wall in a stack about half an inch deep at the front, and every one of them carried two empty boxes at the foot, and about two hundred people a day had stopped at that corner since the eighth of October of the first year and read the top half of the top one and gone.

About two hundred people a day for six years.

Six years of a corner that about two thousand people a day cannot get by, and every single one of the sixty-two had two squares at the foot of it with nothing in either, and not one of the two had ever been filled in about four years of anybody’s memory, and the whole of that record was in a wall and was going to be under a wall again in the spring.

The boy had the scraper and was working about a foot to the left of him and had not said anything for about twenty minutes.

“They are all the same,” said the boy.

“They are all the same.”

“My father says the top one is the one that matters.”

“The top one is the only one anybody has ever read.”

## The Four Inches

Below the lowest form, and below the last coat of paint that had ever gone over it, was the dressed stone.

It was about four inches of it, and it is a different colour from the render above and from the stone in the courses above that, and it is smooth where everything else in that wall is pitted, and it is the only part of that wall in this city that has never once been painted successfully in six years.

The two words are not painted on it. They are cut into it, and they go all the way through the face of it, and the left-hand one is deeper than the right-hand one because of the way a man works when he is cutting and not when he is writing.

There is a notice cut above them in the same hand that says where they came from. There has never been a question asked of anybody about who cut them or when or at whose asking, and there is a question about them on a piece of paper in six years and no answer on any page in this city.

The paint has gone over them sixty-two times.

The paint does not take on four inches of dressed stone and that is the whole of why they are still there.

The boy stood in front of them with the scraper in his hand for about a minute and did not touch them.

“You cannot paint those,” he said.

“No.”

“You could have painted over them four times a month since I was about ten and you did not and I have never worked out why.”

“It was never mine to paint out,” said Lin Hao. “And you have been putting the form up over the top of them sixty-two times, which is the same as painting over them, and you have done that without knowing what was underneath, and the whole of your work for six years has been a cover over a record and you did it because you were given a form.”

The boy looked at the scraper.

## The Rubbing

He got a sheet out of the board by the kerb — an ordinary sheet, the back of a month’s form, which is the same thing as the front of one and is not used — and a lump of chalk out of his coat.

He held the sheet against the four inches of stone and rubbed the side of the chalk across it with the flat of his hand, and the two words came up white and hard and about the size of the real ones, and he did it twice, and the second one was better than the first one, and there is not one sheet of paper in this city with a rubbing of anything on it because there has never once been a reason to make one.

He gave it to him.

“I have never been asked to do that.”

“No.”

“My father would have said it was a waste of a sheet.”

“Your father would have been right about the sheet and wrong about the wall,” said Lin Hao. “Give me that one.”

He held the sheet out and did not take it back. “I am going to put a copy on the rail at the counter off a third court this afternoon and about nine people a week will see it, and there are about nine hundred people in that yard who can read what is on a shutter. The four-inch stone is going to be under a wall of new render in the spring, and this is the only copy of those two words that will not be under anything.”

He handed the sheet back.

“No,” said the boy. “You are not putting it on the rail.”

“No.”

“You were going to put it on the rail.”

“I was going to ask you if I could,” said Lin Hao. “And you have said no before I finished the sentence, and you said it the way my father would have said it, and you are right.”

“Why am I right.”

“Because a rail gets read and a corner gets copied, and about nine hundred people in this city who cannot write their own names can copy a thing that has been copied,” said Lin Hao. “A rail is a place a thing is read once by about nine people a week. A corner is a place a thing is said.”

“That is not a reason. That is the opposite of a reason.”

“It is the only reason in this city that has ever worked,” he said, “and I have spent six years proving it and you have just proved it for me in about nine seconds with a scraper in your hand.”

The boy did not answer that.

He folded it once and put it in the inside of his coat.

“Then what is it for.”

“It is for a man to have,” said Lin Hao. “In about nine days about nine people will have seen a copy of it, and in about nine days after that there will be a version of it in about four hundred houses in this city that is not a copy of anything, because that is what happens to a thing that is easy to say.”

“There is not one instrument in this city that will take a sentence off a person’s mouth once two people have said it to each other.”

The boy picked the paste brush up off the kerb.

“You have said that to me three times now and you have not once said the words,” he said.

“No.”

“All right,” said the boy. “You have not said them to me and I have not asked and I am going to put the form up on the thirtieth with two empty boxes at the foot of it and I am not going to know what they are for, and that is the same as every month for six years, and that is the whole of what I have.”

## The Corner After Four O’Clock

It was a quarter past four and the light had gone and there were about nine people at the corner and about four of them had a sheet of paper.

There were about nine sheets of paper at that corner by the time the light went, and nobody had told anybody to copy it.

One of the four was a woman of about sixty who could not write her own name, and who had had it copied for her by a man she had known for forty years. She said out loud, to about nine people, that it was a rubbing, and that it had been made that afternoon by a boy with a scraper, and that the words on it had been under the paint since before she was married.

She said that to about nine people, in the wrong order, and about two of them wrote it down.

And then a man at the back of the nine, who cannot read either, said the four words at the bottom of the office’s page out loud, at the foot of a wall, in the wrong order, and got them wrong, and there were about nine people there and about nine hundred in the yard behind him and the sentence went round the corner inside four minutes, correctly and wrongly at the same time.

A sentence is a thing you can argue with.

A figure is a thing you can only count.

He stood nine feet off and watched a sentence get said out loud in a street by about nine hundred people who had it from four different people, three of whom had it from a boy, and it was the most correct thing anybody had done to his sentences in six years and it was going to be a different sentence by the spring.

There is not one instrument in Ostara that will take it back.

## What The Boy Has Decided

At about ten past five the boy was still at the corner with the paste brush and the board and the sixty-third form on it, and he had not put it up, and he was looking at the place where the paint had come off.

“Making good does not touch stone,” he said.

“Who told you that.”

“Nobody,” said the boy. “I know it because a man making good a wall takes the render off and puts render on and I have watched two of them do it at the far end of this street for about nine years, and neither of them has ever gone near the bottom course, and neither of them has ever been asked about the bottom course, because the form a man making good a wall works from does not carry a question, and a question is not a return.”

He squared the board against the kerb.

“So in the spring there will be new render on that wall and there will be four inches of stone at the foot of it with nothing on it and two words cut into it and no paint on it,” said the boy, “and about two hundred people a day will stop at that corner, because they have been stopping at that corner for six years and they will not stop, and there will be nothing to read.”

“Except the two words.”

“Except the two words,” said the boy, “and I will be painting that wall in the spring because the render is mine and the stone is not mine and never has been, and I will paint it the same colour as the rest and I will not paint over the four inches, because you cannot, and because the form I paint from does not have a line in it that says I have to try.”

He got the brush up and put the sixty-third form on the board.

“You have not asked me a question this morning.”

“No.”

“You asked me to take the paint off and that was not a question,” said the boy. “It was an instruction from a man who cannot make me do anything, and it is the first instruction anybody has given me on that wall in six years.”

The boy wiped the scraper on his coat. “I have thought about it for about four hours and I have decided that I am going to do it for the sixty-third month and the sixty-fourth. Then in the spring I am going to be told by a man from a department what I am painting, and he will have a form, and there will be nothing on it about four inches of stone.”

*Two thousand one hundred and twenty-one days. Less two thousand one hundred and fourteen, the fifteenth of December, is seven. Twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is eighty-two days, which is two months with twenty-two days after them, and on the ninth of November about nine hundred people took three lines off a shutter in about nine hours, and on the eighth of October of the first year a clerk was handed a form and two of the boxes at the foot of it have stood empty ever since.*

*Less the twenty-first of February, one thousand eight hundred and twenty, is three hundred and one days, which is ten months with one day after them, and a form saying that the ranks wall comes down in the spring for making good, with one ruled box at the foot of it for the date the work was done, has been in the coat of the man standing at this corner for all of them and has been carried to nine counters in that time and nowhere near the four inches of stone.*

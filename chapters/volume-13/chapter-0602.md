# One Question With No Form

The boy from the department of works came in at about half past nine on the tenth of January and stood in the doorway with the month’s form rolled up in his hand, and nobody asked him to come further in, and he did not come further in.

Six days had gone since the demand came back. One thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine less one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three is six, and ten less four is six by the month count.

He wanted the whole of it finished by the end of the morning. That was his want, and it was a want about forms, which is a thing he has wanted every day of the five years since the drawer under this table was first opened and found to be nothing inside.

He had got as far as the door of the room before he understood that there was no such thing as finishing it, only finding out in what order it could not be done.

## Three Refusals, And All Three Correct

The first thing he said to Yusuf Adeyemi was the one he had prepared on the stair, and it was the weakest of the three and he said it anyway, because it is a rule that a man brings with him and puts down before he finds out what the room is like.

“We cannot be a party to it.”

“You cannot,” said Adeyemi. “Write that down as the first of three and do not argue with me about it, because it is true and we will be here until about eleven.”

It was true. A demand is put in front of a magistrate and the magistrate answers it against two people who can both be named. This office has an address at the head of every line and no person behind the address, and there is no instrument anywhere in Ostara that can put a thing that is not a person into a room where a person is expected to answer for it.

Then the boy from the department of works shifted his weight in the doorway and the roll of the form went up and down once.

“The second,” Lin Hao said. “There is no form on which this office receives a question.”

“There is not.”

“Not a form for a counter off a third court to send it to us on, and not a form for us to write on, and not a form for anything at all.”

“There is not one piece of paper in this city that has a box on it for the answer to a question nobody with standing has asked us,” Adeyemi said. “I have had the whole of that looked at. The forms in this office are three and you have seen all three and one of them is the book under this table. Write the second.”

He wrote the second in his own notebook, standing, with the words he had used and not better ones.

“And the third,” he said, “is that there is nothing behind this office. No fund, no schedule, no hearing and no seal, and not a farthing in it now or at any time since the first of February, and no party on either side of it that can be made to accept anything at all.”

Adeyemi let him finish.

“That is five things,” he said. “You have said them in the wrong order and the fourth one is wrong, and I am going to correct one thing in a room with three people in it, and then this morning is over.”

“I did not say it wrong.”

“You listed five and there are four. Nobody in this city can make us do a thing and nobody can make us answer for one, and there is not a coin in the building. You have built the fifth out of two and dressed it in a schedule and a hearing and a seal, and every one of those three is a thing this office has never had and would not know what to do with if it were handed one.”

Lin Hao looked at his notebook and the five of it was still five and he was wrong about which one.

“That is the second time in two days,” Adeyemi said, “that a thing has come back to this counter that was not the shape I expected, and I want it noted in your book that the Registrar did not make it and the Registrar could not have made it.”

## What The Boy Said

The boy had been in the doorway for about eleven minutes and he put it at the end, when Adeyemi had gone to the counter to look at the day book with Wenna Raut.

“The form is for the foot of the wall,” he said. “Same as every month. Two boxes at the bottom of it.”

“I know what is at the bottom of it,” Lin Hao said.

“I am not saying anything about the boxes,” the boy said. “I am saying that we take the form and the paint and we put them up and we take them down and nobody gives them back to us, and the place that decides which wall gets done in a quarter is not us, it is nine streets away in an office I have been in four times and could not tell you the name of from the outside.”

“That is one thing,” Lin Hao said, “and I am not going to ask you a second.”

“I would not have answered a second one anyway,” the boy said. “I have got the January form and I have got to be back at the north end before the light goes.”

He put the roll on the table by the tray and went, and the roll stood there against the demand for the rest of the morning with two boxes at the foot of it and nothing in either of them, which is how both of them have stood since the first autumn.

## The Sentence That Is Already Printed

Lin Hao stood at the table with the tray under his right hand and understood the whole of it in about nine seconds and it took him the rest of the morning to be able to say it.

“Is there,” he said.

“Read it out,” said Adeyemi.

“Is there.”

“Is there what.”

“There is no page,” Lin Hao said. “Is there a page. It is two words on the back of a landlord’s demand and it is a question about an absence, and it is the first question anybody has ever put to this office, and it is going to be answered by something that went out on the thirteenth of November.”

He got the twentieth sheet off the shelf and squared it on the table and turned it round so the foot was toward them.

Under the last rank, in the hand of the office itself, after two sentences that had been at the foot of every sheet since the first of February, there were two more. One of them was the sentence about invented figures with a name under it, and the name under that one was Emil Brack.

The other one said that where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything.

“It has been out at four every quarter since the thirteenth of November,” Lin Hao said. “About two thousand people a day cannot walk past the foot of it without reading it, and about four in every thousand of the ones who read a page get that far down, and it is on a desk in Cordwain at the height of a man’s chest and it has been lying out since the first of March.”

“That is not an answer,” said Adeyemi. “That is a sentence about what a blank means, and this office has not answered anything, and if you tell me you have answered a question I will tell you about the six years of your life in which nobody could have.”

“It is the answer,” Lin Hao said.

“It is the only sentence in this city that bears on the question,” said Adeyemi. “Whether it is an answer is a matter for whoever reads it, and I am not going to decide it in a room with a drawer open under the table.”

## Nobody Wrote It Down

Nobody wrote it down.

That was the part he could not get past. He stood there with a pen on the table and a clerk who may only enter what she is given and a Registrar who could have made an entry of it in about eleven seconds, and the sentence was already printed four times a year in a hand two thousand people a day could not walk past, and there was nothing whatever to write.

“If it went in the day book,” Wenna Raut said, without being asked, “then the office would have answered a question, and the day book goes into a bundle on the thirtieth, and a bundle is not a page anybody may read.”

“Then it is not an answer.”

“It is not an answer and it is not not an answer either,” she said. “It is the fourth sentence on a page, and I entered it in November exactly as it was given to me, and I am not permitted to take it out and I cannot improve it and nobody can ask me to hurry, and that is the whole of what happened in this room today.”

She wrote one line in the day book. It said that a question had been put to the office on the subject of a demand received on the fourth, and that no form had been found, and that the office had not answered it. She read it twice, at the same speed, and put the pen down.

“That is the whole of the entry,” she said. “It cannot be bettered and it cannot be worse. It is what the room said.”

He wanted, for about four seconds, to be asked something else.

He had spent five years making a sentence that could not be quoted, and on the thirteenth of November he had put it where about two thousand people a day could not walk past it, and a woman at a stall had written four of his lines out on a piece of paper for a landlord, and the landlord had carried them to a counter, and a clerk had carried them here, and now the office was going to be answered by a sentence he had made sure was true.

He had wanted to be asked. He had not thought about what being asked would cost.

“It will not be recalled,” Adeyemi said, at the door, with his coat on. “Not by us and not by you. It goes out again on the thirtieth of March in the fourth time and a landlord’s man will read it in about four days and decide what to do with it, and none of that is in this office’s hands and all of it started at this counter.”

“Yes,” Lin Hao said.

“Go and work,” said Adeyemi. “And do not go and find out whether it worked.”

*The tenth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine days. Less one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, the fourth, is six. Ten less four is six by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is ten, and the twentieth sheet went out at four on that day and nothing has been added to it since. Less the thirteenth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, is fifty-seven, and the fourth sentence at the foot of it has been out at four every quarter since.*

*The fourth of February of year one is day one, and one thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine less one is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-eight, and the drawer under this table has been open across the whole of it.*

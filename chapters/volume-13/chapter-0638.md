# The Twenty-Third Sheet

Eight days had gone since a man in a delivery coat had stood about four feet off a table in this building and had had a sentence read to him in the office’s own hand by the man who wrote it, and had then asked for it to be taken off, and had been told why it could not be.

The thirtieth of September is day two thousand and thirty-nine, and two thousand and thirty-nine less two thousand and thirty-one is eight, and thirty less twenty-two is eight by the month count.

He came into the room off a third court at about ten in the morning with nothing in his hands, and he wanted to read the twenty-third sheet from the top of it to the bottom of it and find one thing on it that had changed.

That was the whole of the want. It lasted about eleven minutes.

## Sixty-Seven Months And Twenty-Nine Days

Thirty multiplied by sixty-seven is two thousand and ten.

Twenty-nine days after the first of February of the sixth year is two thousand and thirty-nine, and that is this morning, and this is the last working day of the month and of the quarter, and a sheet that goes out at four has gone out twenty-three times.

The building is sixty-seven months and twenty-nine days old this afternoon.

He had written that out in his head four days before the thirtieth of June and not once since, and he had spent those eighty-three days in a city where about two thousand people a day cannot walk past the foot of what this office prints, and not one of them has ever asked how old it is.

## Five Things In A Bundle

The bundle was on the table with string round it and it had not been there on the twenty-second.

“It has five things in it,” said Wenna Raut.

“Five.”

“Four of them came in between the fourth of January and the twenty-first of May and the fifth came on the twenty-eighth of this month, and I have entered all five and I improved none of them, and it goes out at four and after four it goes into another room in this building that I could not take you to if I wanted to.”

“Read me the fifth.”

“It came in through the front door of this building at about a quarter to two in the afternoon on a sheet of paper with a figure at the top of it,” said Wenna Raut, “and it is a letter, and it asks this office to correct a number, and it gives a name in a street in this city as the person who wrote it, and the name is not the name of anybody in this building.”

She did not look up.

“And the sentence at the top of it is *dear sir*, and there is no room at the head of it,” she said. “It came anyway, and it has come the same way for four years.”

There is a board on a hill in this city with the address of this office at the top of it in a hand that anybody can read. About nine hundred people in that yard can read it. About four in every thousand have ever got as far as the four sentences at the bottom of it.

“So somebody read a figure off a page and wrote to us about it.”

“Somebody read a figure off a page and wrote to us about it,” said Wenna Raut, “and I cannot tell you what the figure is, because I enter what I am given and I am not permitted to read a line out of a book to a person standing at a counter.”

## The Sheet

The sheet was brought out and squared at about half past two.

The figure at the head of it was eight hundred and fifty-two in the office’s own hand. Under it were about four hundred and nineteen lines, each of them one account of six years of a street dug in the cold, with an hour set against every hour of it and no figure standing against one of them.

The second column had two lines in it. One of them had been there since the fifteenth of October of the fourth year and was his.

Four sentences in the hand of the office were printed at the bottom.

That figure has not moved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. This is the seventh sheet to carry it.

“Keep it where it is,” said Wenna Raut.

“It is not going anywhere.”

“It has not moved in seven quarters,” said Wenna Raut. “It moved once, in the ninth month of the third year, and the reason for that is the man in front of me, and we do not discuss it and we have not discussed it before today. And I have sat on the second line in that column for four hundred and ninety-four days without saying one word, and I am not starting in the last quarter of a year that has cost this building everything it ever had.”

## Four O’Clock

The sheet went out at four.

It was carried out of the room by a clerk and up a stair this time, because the man at the counter off a third court has wanted it brought up since the thirtieth of March and has had the reason written down twice.

It went over the rail on the counter with a window on the street, and the sheet underneath it was folded, and the man at that counter did not take it off the rail to look at anything and said nothing at all, because nothing that happens to a page on a rail is said out loud by anybody in this city and it is the one rule about that page that has never been broken.

Then the bundle went in the other direction, into a room in this building that nobody in this building can name, and nobody is required to open it, and the last one has never been opened.

Not nine streets back from that counter. Three, and round a corner, and at the end of a lane with a wall down one side of it, a man in a delivery coat was reading twelve books out loud at ten to six to about forty people in the cold, and he had done it every morning since the thirteenth of July, and the slate was up with the day’s nine figures on it and a grain a head against them.

He did not know what had gone out at four in a building nine streets away and he would not have cared if he had.

## Half Past Four At The Desk Under A Window

“Seven,” said the man at the desk.

“Seven.”

“Seven of them the same,” said the man at the desk. “I have told five people that this quarter and four of the five were strangers.”

The fifth was a man in a delivery coat who came down in April and wanted to know whether the foot of it was any good. The sixth was a woman on the twenty-third of this month who stood about nine minutes over one column of it and would not tell him what she was after.

“Did you ask her.”

“I have never once asked anybody what a page was for,” said the man at the desk, “and I was not going to start, and not with a woman who has a thumb on a column of it.”

He turned a page over.

“There is one thing about this one that is different and after I have said it you will be rid of me for a quarter.”

“Say it.”

“Four men came past that rail between half past three and four this afternoon and every one of them was going fast,” said the man at the desk. “About a fortnight ago four men coming past that rail went the other way.”

“I do not know why. I have run this desk for about nine years and I could not tell you whether it is a good thing or a bad one. It is the first change in the traffic at that rail I have ever noticed and I noticed it.”

## A Board On A Hill

He went up the hill at about four, and there was a board at the foot of the shutter about two feet by three with a figure at the top of it in four-inch letters, and the figure was eight hundred and fifty-two.

Somebody had done that in the week. It had been up since the twenty-fourth, and there was chalk dust under it that has not been washed off by rain because there has not been any rain in this city since about the middle of the ninth month, and about nine hundred people in that yard were able to read it.

He had not asked for it.

“You put that up.”

The woman of about sixty had a rag in her hand and chalk dust on the front of her coat and she was the same woman who had read four lines out loud to about nine hundred people on the seventh of July.

“My husband put it up on the twenty-fourth,” she said. “Eight hundred and fifty-two. He got it off the rail on a morning and he is not a clever man and he has had three years of school. And I have stood at the foot of it every morning since and I have said *that is how many* to about nine hundred people, and not one of them has ever asked me how many of what.”

“And what did you say it was.”

“I said how many people there are owed something,” said the woman of about sixty, “because that is what a number like that has ever meant in this yard, and I have been in this yard since I was a girl, and there is no other thing it could have meant.”

## What He Said At The Foot Of It

He stood nine feet off it at about four o’clock on the last working day of the quarter, with about eleven thousand people in the yard, and he said it out loud, and it was the worst thing he has done.

“EIGHT HUNDRED AND FIFTY-TWO IS THE NUMBER OF LINES ON A PAGE,” he said.

Nobody moved for about four seconds.

“It is not the number of people anybody is owed anything by,” he said. “It is a figure at the head of a sheet that goes out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter.”

“About four hundred and nineteen of the lines under it are one woman’s account of six years of a street dug in the cold, with an hour set against every hour of it and no figure and no name. The number at the head is how many lines the page has got on it.”

“It has not moved since the thirtieth of March of a year I do not name here, and I know that because it is my office and I am the only person it may ask.”

He said it twice, at the same speed, with nothing improved, because that is the only way he has ever read anything.

“Again,” said somebody at the back.

“You have got that one.”

“Say it again. We have got no way of writing it down.” That is what about four hundred people said in this yard in June, and it was the same four hundred people.

So he said it again, the same way, at the same speed, and about two hundred people wrote it on the backs of their hands, and about nine hundred heard it.

## What The Woman Of About Sixty Said Next

“They will not like it,” she said.

“I know.”

“You have been at the foot of this board since the twenty-first of June and everything you have said on this ground has been true, and about four hundred of us have believed you every single time, and it is not going to be true this afternoon.” She folded the rag in half and then in half again. “You have told nine hundred people that the only number in this city they can all read is not what they thought it was.”

“I know that too.”

“Do you,” said the woman of about sixty. “Because I have been standing at the foot of that figure for six days telling people what it means, and about two hundred of them have written it down, and I did that. Not him. Me. And in about two hundred houses in this city there is a person who has told somebody else that eight hundred and fifty-two is how many people there are owed something.”

## What About Nine Hundred People Do With A Number

They had it in about eleven minutes.

Not because two hundred of them believed him, and not because they did not. A number had been on a board since the middle of the week meaning *this many people are owed nothing*. He had come up a hill and said the number means *this is how many lines there are*.

Those are not two sentences. They can both be true, and a yard of about eleven thousand people cannot hold two.

“Eight hundred and fifty-two claims,” said somebody, not far off.

“He said it is not the number of anybody owed anything.”

“He said it is the number of lines on the page,” said somebody else, “and the page is the claims. I have been in this yard since the seventh of July and that page is the only thing in this city anybody can read.”

That is correct.

He had said it himself, in a room, at four, and it had gone out at four, and there is no way on earth of getting a page back off a rail once it is on one.

By about twenty past four the board at the foot of the shutter had two figures on it — eight hundred and fifty-two in four-inch letters, and underneath it, in six-inch letters, the word CLAIMS, which somebody had chalked without asking him.

And the man with the rag and the bucket was at the other end of the shutter with his back to him, and he had a cloth in his hand, and the six-inch sentence about a thing nobody can prove was still on its board about three feet by two.

## The Man With The Rag

He did not go over there, and about nine hundred people in that yard knew why, and about nine hundred people in that yard had seen the other thing.

“There is a sentence under it,” said the man with the rag, from about nine feet off, in a voice made for a wall end. “Six-inch letters. It has been there since the seventh of July and it is correct.”

“It is correct,” said Lin Hao.

“Then it goes,” said the man with the rag, “and I am not doing it because of a figure. I am doing it because there are two things on that shutter now and one of them is a number out of your office and the other one is a sentence.”

A sentence is a thing you can argue with, and he had argued with that one for six weeks and it had been right every single time. He was not going to stand in a yard and argue with a number.

“The second one is easier,” said Lin Hao.

“The second one is easier for about four hundred people and then it is not easy for anybody,” said the man with the rag. “The second one wants a day and a place and everybody in this yard knows a day and a place. The third one wants a person, and a person is the one thing none of us can put on a form.”

He wiped the chalk on his coat and did not come down the ladder.

“I have asked people,” he said. “I asked four of them on the way up, and about a fortnight ago a man asked me, and I said what I am saying to you now, and he did not answer me.”

“He answered me once.”

“When.”

“In a corridor off a third court, about nine minutes ago,” said the man with the rag, “and I have been standing on this ladder since about a quarter to four and I did not hear it, and I would have heard it.”

He had a cloth in his hand and it was not wet.

This was the second time. He knew it while it was happening, and about nine hundred people in that yard knew it too, and nothing in this city can stop a sentence from being true in a yard at about twenty past four on the last working day of a quarter.

That is the whole of what happened on the thirtieth of September, and it happened because he had wanted all summer to find one thing on a page that had changed and could not find one, and he went and made one.

## What He Got For It

Nine streets away, on a desk under a window, a page went out at four with eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it and did not move.

About nine people a week will read it to the bottom.

About nine hundred people go past the foot of it every day and none of them stops.

And on a hill, in six-inch letters, under a figure that has been in the office’s hand since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, there is now one word, and there is no sentence under it any more, and nobody in that yard is going to put one back, because a sentence in six-inch letters is a thing you can argue with and a figure in four-inch letters is a thing you can only count.

*Two thousand and thirty-nine days. Less two thousand and thirty-one, the twenty-second of September, is eight. Thirty less twenty-two is eight by the month count, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month and of the quarter.*

*Thirty multiplied by sixty-seven is two thousand and ten, and twenty-nine days after that is two thousand and thirty-nine. This building is sixty-seven months and twenty-nine days old this afternoon and it prints the twenty-third time. THIS FIGURE IS PRINTED ONCE IN THIS BATCH AND NOWHERE ELSE IN 631 TO 640.*

*Less the thirtieth of December of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is two hundred and seventy days, and that is nine months, and it is three quarters. The twentieth sheet went out at four on that day, the twenty-first on the thirtieth of March, the twenty-second on the thirtieth of June, and this is the twenty-third, and the head has stood at eight hundred and fifty-two across all of them.*

*Less the seventh of July, one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is eighty-three days, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth of four lines off a board themselves, and in the last week of July the man who wrote it read three lines out loud to about four hundred people at the foot of it, and on the fifteenth of September the third of the three finished an argument between a landlord and a woman in about nine minutes and was correct.*

*And on the thirtieth of September, at about twenty past four in the afternoon, the man who wrote all four of them stood nine feet off a board in a yard of about eleven thousand people and said the figure at the head of a page out loud, twice, at the same speed, with nothing improved. About nine hundred people heard it. About two hundred wrote it down. About eleven minutes later the yard had made it a count of claims and taken down a sentence in six-inch letters that had been correct since the seventh of July, and he stood nine feet off and did not stop it.*

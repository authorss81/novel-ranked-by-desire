# They Take One Line Off

Seven days had gone since a page had gone out at four from a room with two chairs in it and been pinned over another page on a desk in a passage off Cordwain. One thousand nine hundred and fifty-six less one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine is seven, and seven less thirty is minus twenty-three. A month runs thirty days and stops, so a day seven after the thirtieth of June is the seventh of July, and there is no thirty-first to carry it.

He climbed the hill on the seventh of July a little after ten in the morning and took up the nine feet in front of the board again. He had stood in that nine feet on the first morning of the yard’s week and on three mornings after it, and nobody had asked him to.

Not one person in that yard had asked him to be there. That had been the condition, and he had accepted it, and it had been nine days.

He wanted somebody to ask him.

That was the want. It was six years old. It had not happened at the counter on the fourth of January, or in a room off a third court on the eleventh of March, or in a lane off Cordwain at ten to seven in the morning on the twenty-second of April, or in a room off Ferrier Street on the sixth of June, and it was in him on the seventh of July at nine feet off a board in the open air.

## The Notice Inside The Vestry

The Vestry door was shut and the man who stands beside it was standing beside it.

“Nine days ago,” he said, before Lin Hao had said anything. “The week ended on the twenty-eighth and on the twenty-ninth they put a notice inside the Vestry door with a bit of drawing pins in it, and the notice said that anybody who wanted to object to the fourth line writing to the address at the head of the board, and that the address at the head of the board is on the notice above the four lines and has been there for four years.”

“How long did they wait.”

“Nobody wrote,” said the man. “And on the ninth day a woman asked whether anybody had, and the answer was no.”

“What did she say then.”

“She said there is a room at that address with two chairs in it, and a woman at a counter who cannot be hurried, and a man who comes down about half past four on the day a page goes out, and that she had been in it, and that nobody there had asked her one question.”

He turned his head about nine degrees.

“She said it exactly the way you would have said it,” said the man beside the door, “and I have not told her that you were standing here, and you were not, and that is the first honest thing that has happened in this yard in nine days.”

“I was in it on the thirtieth.”

“I do not know that,” said the man beside the door, “and I have not been asked and I am not going to ask, because I have stood beside this door for five years and in five years I have not once asked anybody what a piece of paper was for.”

## About Four Hundred Of Them

About eleven thousand people were in the yard. The meeting had been going since about ten and it was not a meeting anybody had called; it was a week of people standing about in a hall, and at about half past eleven it came out of the hall and into the ground.

Nine hundred of them could read the whole of what was on the shutter.

About four hundred of those nine hundred had not believed a word he said since a man off an arcade did the arithmetic on this ground on the seventh of February. About five hundred had.

He stood at nine feet and waited for one of them to come and ask him, and none of them did.

She said it.

“No,” said the woman of about sixty who had made a thing of reading the lines out loud. “Nobody is going to ask you.”

“I know.”

“You do not know,” said the woman. “You have been standing at nine feet off that board for nine days waiting for somebody to come and ask you a thing.”

“I have.”

“Then you do know,” said the woman, “and you have been standing there waiting for a person who has read a sheet of yours four times and worked out that you are the only one of nine hundred who cannot prove one word of what you wrote. And nobody is going to, and I have come to tell you that face to face so that you do not spend the morning finding out.”

“Why not.”

“Because if we ask you,” said the woman, “then this is four hundred people asking the man who wrote it. And whatever we do this morning is a thing we did because he was standing here, and in nine years nobody will be able to tell it from a thing we did.”

“That is not a reason.”

“That is the whole of the reason,” said the woman. “You have been the only person anybody could be asked about for six years and about eleven hundred people in this yard have heard you say so out loud twice, and the whole of what we have done for a week is not have you in it.”

## At The End Of The Meeting

It began at about eleven, and it was not a speech.

A man of about fifty came to the foot of the board with a rag and a bucket and asked the yard one question, which he read out loud from where he was standing, because he could read and could not write.

He said it twice, because about four hundred people at the back of that yard did not hear it the first time and about nine hundred did.

“Was that the question.”

“That was the question.”

“What is the answer.”

“There is no answer,” said the man with the rag. “That is what we have found out in a week. There is no answer and there is nobody to give one.”

Nobody voted. That is not what happened.

About nine hundred people came to the foot of the board, in ones and twos, over about eleven minutes, and the man with the rag and a bucket went along the four-inch chalk and took the fourth line off the stone and onto the wall beside it, and a woman with a pail carried it away, and then he went along it again because it had not all come off.

Four lines, four inches each, and about a hundred and twenty feet of chalk if you counted the way it runs, and it took about nine minutes and it was done by about eleven different people.

“Do not take the other three,” said somebody.

“We are not taking the other three,” said the man with the rag. “The other three are checkable. Ask anybody here whether a rule has to be checkable and they will tell you it does, and they are right, and that is the whole of the argument and it has taken nine hundred people nine minutes.”

He stood about nine feet off and listened to a man in a yard of about eleven thousand people explain to him, correctly, in front of everybody, why the sentence was being taken off.

He had heard it before. He had said it himself, in his own voice, on this ground, four times, and every one of the nine hundred had heard him say it and had believed him.

## Nobody Asked Him

He said nothing.

Three streets in three years, and what he had said on all three of them was that the hill and the board were not going to have him standing at the foot of them. He had broken it on the twenty-first of June and read four lines out loud twice.

On the seventh of July he stood nine feet off the board while nine hundred people took a sentence of his off it with a rag, and he said nothing.

That was the correct thing. It was also the worst thing that had happened to him in six years.

The chaplain was not there.

The Vestry door was shut all morning. It was shut when the meeting came out and it was shut when the line came off, and nobody knocked on it, and no one went up the steps to ask the chaplain whether she had anything to say about it, and in five years beside that door not one person has ever had the standing to put the question.

“Is she not here.”

“She is inside,” said the man beside the door. “She came in at about nine and she did not come out.”

“Did anybody ask her.”

“She has never been asked,” said the man beside the door. “Not this morning, and not in about fifteen months of those steps. She has said one thing a month off them and been left unanswered every single time, and this morning there were about eleven thousand people standing in front of them and not one of them went up.”

## What He Understood While It Was Coming Off

He understood it while the rag was going along the second half of it, and he had understood it in about nine seconds, and it had taken him five months.

A rule with a test is a rule you can break.

A rule without a test is a rule that can only be obeyed or ignored. A yard of about eleven thousand people cannot obey a sentence they cannot check, any more than a magistrate can or a division can, and a landlord certainly cannot.

He had known that since the seventh of February. He had stood nine feet off this board on that morning and been asked whether the fourth line was still a rule if nobody could test it, and he had said yes, out loud, in front of nine hundred people, and four hundred of them had not believed a word of it.

They had believed him correctly. He had been wrong in the only direction he had ever been able to be wrong in, and he had been wrong out loud, on purpose, in his own voice, on this ground, four times.

## What The Yard Decided

They put the three lines back up after it, at about twelve, and a man with a bit of chalk did it in a hand that was not his and made them straighter than he had ever made them.

Then somebody brought out a board about three feet by two and propped it under the shutter, and a woman chalked on it in six-inch letters the single thing the yard had decided in a week, and Lin Hao read it from nine feet off with about four hundred people standing near enough to see whether he had read it.

It said, in six-inch letters: A THING NOBODY CAN PROVE IS NOT OWED TO ANYBODY.

Which meant that the four hundred and nineteen lines on the shutter, the ones with an hour against every hour of them and no figure and no name, were not a claim on anybody.

“Whose lines are those.” Somebody in the crowd asked him.

“Six years of a woman who dug a street in the cold,” he said, “and she has no name against them because a division was not permitted to ask her for one.”

“Is she owed.”

“She is owed,” said Lin Hao.

“Then that is the wrong sentence,” said the man, “and we have had it up for four years and we did not know.”

He stood nine feet off a board with three lines on it and one sentence under it, and about eleven thousand people, and the fourth line was in a bucket at the end of the yard and the sentence at the foot of the twenty-second sheet was still in his own hand with his name under it and had eighty-three days left to run.

*One thousand nine hundred and fifty-six days. Less one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is seven. Seven less thirty is minus twenty-three, and a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*Less the twenty-eighth of June, day one thousand nine hundred and forty-seven, which is the day the yard’s week ended, is nine days. A notice went inside the Vestry door on the twenty-ninth and it asked anybody who wanted to object to the fourth line to write to the address at the head of the board, and nobody wrote, and a question cannot reach an office at an address unless it arrives on paper.*

*Less the eleventh of July, which is day one thousand two hundred and forty and is the day the ninth line down was entered in a clerk’s hand, is seven hundred and sixteen days. The line it is on is the fourth line down of a list of about eight hundred and fifty-two and there are about four hundred and nineteen of them and no figure and no name against any of them.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is one hundred and eighty-three days, and one hundred and eighty-three is six months and three days. The fourth line was taken off a board in a yard this morning by about nine hundred people, one at a time, over about eleven minutes, with a rag and a bucket, and the man who wrote it was not asked first, and the chaplain was not there and nobody went up to her.*

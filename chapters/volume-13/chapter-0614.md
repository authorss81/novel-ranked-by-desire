# The Twenty-First Sheet

Six days had gone since a woman at a far table two floors under a sales floor asked him a question he could not answer and would not answer again. One thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine less one thousand eight hundred and fifty-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count, and a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first, which makes this morning the last working day of the month and of the quarter.

He came into the room with no street door on it at about ten with the bundle of nine returns under his arm. Wenna Raut was at the counter with the day book open.

He wanted the quarter to end the way the last four had ended. That was the whole of the want and it lasted until about eleven.

## Nine Lines In A Day Book

He put the nine returns on the counter and unwrapped the string and squared them in a row, and she read them one at a time, twice each, at the same speed, which is what she has done since the first of February of the first year.

“Eight of them are the same in eight hands,” she said.

“Eight of them are the same in eight hands.”

“The ninth one is not the same,” said Wenna Raut. “It has something in it that eight do not.”

“It is my sentence.”

“It is four words of yours in a division officer’s hand on a ruled space two inches deep,” she said, “and I am permitted to enter it and I am not permitted to improve it, and I am going to enter all nine exactly as given and I am not going to say one word about the ninth, and you can see from this chair that I have not.”

She wrote for about eleven minutes. It was the longest entry she had made in a day book in six years and the tray under the table was open and empty underneath the whole of it, and neither of them said anything for the duration.

“What is the entry.”

“It says that on the seventeenth a return was received from each of nine divisions and that each return is entered in the file of the finding at a counter off a third court,” said Wenna Raut.

“That is the whole of it. It does not say what the returns say, because a day book does not have a place to say what a return says, and if I wrote it I would be improving it, and if I did not write it then the day book would be saying something about nine divisions that nobody in this room has checked.”

“That is correct.”

“It is correct and it took me about four minutes to work out and I have written it in the form that says the least,” she said. “You may take it to the counter. You may not read it out and you may not leave it here, and if you leave it here it goes into the bundle on the thirtieth and the bundle goes into another room, and I do not know what happens to the bundle and neither does the Registrar.”

She squared the bundle and put a clip on it.

## The Head Of The Page

The twenty-first sheet came out of a drawer at about half past two and it was the same sheet.

Eight hundred and fifty-two stood at the head of it in the office’s own hand. About four hundred and nineteen of those lines are somebody’s account of six years of cold, an hour against every hour of it and not one figure on any of them. The second column has two lines in it.

At the foot of it were four sentences in the hand of the office. Two of them have been under the last rank since the office opened, and two of them a man added in his own hand on the ninth of November, with his name under one of the two.

It had stood at that figure since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and this was the fifth sheet in a row to go out with it.

“Do not let it move,” said Wenna Raut.

“It is not going to move.”

“It moved once,” she said, “in about the ninth month of the third year, because a man handed me a line and I entered it. I am not blaming you for that and I am telling you that I know exactly what it costs to put a figure in at the top of that page.”

“And the figure at the top of that page is a thing about a woman who dug a street, and it is the only figure on it, and nobody has ever been able to find out who read it.”

“It has not moved in five quarters.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “And I would like it recorded that I have noticed, and that I have said nothing about it, and that saying nothing about it is not the same as not noticing.”

## Four O’Clock

Yusuf Adeyemi came in at about ten past four with his coat on and took up a position by the table rather than in a chair, which is what he does on the days when he has something to say and has decided to say it standing.

He read the top of it and the foot of it and stood there.

“Same as the last four,” he said.

“Same as the last four.”

“That is what I came to say and I have said it in four words, because you have had eleven weeks of this office in your chest and I am not giving you a speech at four o’clock on a quarter.” He turned about nine degrees, which is how that man moves. “You have been waiting since November for a page that does something, and this morning a page did something, and it was not the page.”

“The file went up on the twenty-seventh.”

“The file went up on the twenty-seventh and it is listed to be read on the twenty-ninth of April, and in about four weeks a magistrate will turn it over, and at the back of it there is a copy of this, with your name under the first of the two sentences you added, and that copy is not this.” Adeyemi put a finger about an inch off the edge of the table without touching anything.

“I want you to understand what has happened and I want you to understand it in the right order, because you will get it wrong otherwise and you will get it wrong in a direction that is comfortable.”

“Say it.”

“You built one thing,” said Adeyemi. “In six years you built one instrument that a person could not refuse, because a page on a desk in a passage cannot be refused and cannot be shut and does not know who it is talking to. About two thousand people a day cannot walk past the foot of that page without reading what is written there. That was the whole of what you had and you gave it away in an afternoon and I have watched you not enjoy a quarter since.”

“And now there are two.”

“Now there are two, and the second one has a drawer,” said Adeyemi. “A page comes out on the thirtieth and about two thousand people a day read the foot of it because they cannot get past it. A file comes out of a drawer whenever anybody opens it, and there is no date on that, and there is no public desk, and there is nobody obliged to be in front of it.”

“You spent five years making sure this office could never be compelled. It still cannot be compelled. It has never had a fund and never will. But it is now a thing that a magistrate turns over, and that is not compulsion and it is not worse than compulsion and it is not better, and it is a completely different instrument, and you did not build it and you cannot put it back.”

Lin Hao stood at a table with a bundle of nine returns clipped to it and understood that the sentence he had made sure was true had stopped being a sentence that goes out at four and had become a sentence that lives in a drawer, and that a drawer can be opened on any morning by anybody with a reason, and that he had no standing anywhere that could reach a drawer.

“The office,” he said.

“The office is the same,” said Adeyemi. “The office has a table and two chairs and a drawer open and empty since the fourth of February, and a clerk who may only enter, and no money and no counterparty and no seal and no schedule and no hearing and nothing whatever behind it. All five of those are still true and all five were true this afternoon while I was standing here. You have not improved the office by one grain and you have handed it a second address.”

He picked up his hat.

“Read the file when it is read to you on the twenty-ninth. Do not go and look at it in the meantime and do not write to that clerk. He has used his one question and he is entitled to nothing else from either of us.”

## Sixty-One Months And Twenty-Nine Days

The sheet went out at four.

It went out at four the way it has gone out four times since the first of February of the first year, in a bundle, into a hand, and down two floors and along a corridor to a desk under a window in a passage off Cordwain, where a man who has run that desk for about nine years takes it in and pins the new one out over the old.

Thirty multiplied by sixty-one is one thousand eight hundred and thirty, and twenty-nine days after that is one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine, and that is how old this office is on the afternoon it prints the twenty-first time.

He had been in the room for about six years and three months of it and he had never once in his own hand put that on the bottom of a page, because a page goes out with what is in it and not with what a man would like in it, and what a man would like in it is a sentence about how long he has been sitting in a room with a drawer open.

And out in a lane nine streets from that counter there is a wall with nine stools along it and a slate that has been up every morning since the third of October of the year before the first, and the third of October of the year before the first is day minus one hundred and eighteen, and one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine less minus one hundred and eighteen with one day after it is one thousand nine hundred and seventy-eight mornings.

Nine stools, a slate, a man in a delivery coat, about nine figures a morning, and about two thousand of about eight thousand ringed with a reason in the margin, and a name under a sentence in the office’s own hand on a page that has gone out four times since he was named.

He had never been down that lane in five years without wanting to be asked about something. He had gone down it in the frost of the twenty-eighth of February because he wanted to hear what a man thought of a sentence, and what he had got was a man telling him that the office had made him legal and that he wanted nothing, and there had not been a moment in nine minutes of it when anybody wanted anything off him.

## The Desk Under The Window

He went down Cordwain at about half past four and the new sheet was already out on the desk in the passage, squared, with the corner of it still soft where shoulders go past.

The desk man had a ledger of his own open in front of him with about thirty names in it, and he did not turn round, and it is paid for out of the rent roll of the building and has nothing to do with anybody who prints anything.

“Same one,” he said.

“Same one.”

“Same one for five of them,” said the man at the desk. “I have said that to about four people a fortnight since the thirtieth of December and I said it to a woman on the fourth of February and to a man with a barrow in March, and I am going to say it to you now because you are the only person who has ever come in here and looked like he expected something else.”

“I did not expect anything else.”

“You did not,” said the man at the desk. “There is one further thing and then I go back to my thirty names, and it is not about the sheet.”

He turned a page of the ledger over and did not look up.

“There is somebody been standing where you are standing,” he said.

The trams went under the ranks wall a street away and the floor of the passage felt it, and the light came in under the sash and lay along the sheet, and Lin Hao did not say anything for about four seconds, which was a great deal longer than the sentence had taken.

“Standing where.”

“Standing at the desk,” said the man. “On one morning and again on the second, and both times for about a quarter of an hour, and both times she put her hand on the page about the ninth line down and did not go past it, and on the second one she asked me to read her a line and I read it to her and she said thank you and went off up the stair.”

The passage was empty and the light moved along the paper.

He did not go up the two flights. He had been up them once, on the fourth of March, alone, and had come down without knocking, and there was no morning left in him that was going to get him up them again.

He stood at the desk where somebody had stood twice with a hand on the ninth line down, and the ninth line down had a street on it and six years in the cold against it and no figure and no name, and it had been there since the eleventh of July, entered in a clerk’s hand because a division was not permitted to ask her for a name and did not ask her.

*One thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine days. Less one thousand eight hundred and fifty-three, the twenty-fourth of March, is six. Thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month and of the quarter, and thirty less thirty is nothing.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is eighty-six, and eighty-six is two months and twenty-six days. The demand came into the room off a third court on the fourth with four lines on it, a name went into a finding on the eleventh, nine divisions returned a line on the seventeenth, the line went to a counter off a third court on the twenty-sixth, and the finding went up on the twenty-seventh.*

*Less the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is ninety, and thirty and thirty and thirty is ninety, which is the whole of a quarter. The twentieth sheet went out at four on that day. This is the twenty-first and it is day one thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine.*

# The Standing He Cannot Strike

Eight days had gone since he had spent an afternoon in four feet of passage behind a returns desk in a division building, and had come away with three questions in a day book that goes nowhere and one piece of paper with three lines on it and nothing at the head of it.

The first of September is day two thousand and ten, and two thousand and ten less two thousand and two is eight. A month runs thirty days and stops, so there are seven days left in August after the twenty-third, and the first of September is the day after the thirtieth, which makes eight days in all.

He went to the rent board off Cordwain at about ten in the morning on the first of September, which is a table and three chairs and a stove and a window shut and painted years ago, and it is the same table and the same stove as it was in the January of a year he does not name in this sentence.

He wanted somebody to tell him that he could not do anything.

That was the want. It was the opposite want to the one he had carried into a division building on the eighth of August, and it lasted about eleven minutes.

## The Man Who Is Not Careful

Corvin Ashby was at the table on the side he is always at, with a sheet of paper in front of him and his hat still on, and he did not get up.

“Sit down,” he said. “You have not come about the fourth line. If you had come about the fourth line you would have said so in the doorway.”

“The fifteenth of September.”

“The fifteenth of September,” said Ashby. “Three things are listed for that morning and one of them is the flat over a shop in Lamplit Row with a year and a bit of arrears on it and a woman in it who cannot read. Have you worked out yet what that is.”

“I have not.”

“Then I am going to tell you, and I am going to tell you straight, because there is no advantage to me in being careful and there has not been any since the third year.”

## What Is Listed For The Fifteenth

“If she comes and does not understand a word of it, the case goes on without her,” said Ashby. “If she does not come, the case goes on without her. Those are not two things. They are one thing with a door in it.”

“That is not how it works.”

“It is exactly how it works,” said Ashby. “I have had two of these in nine years and I won both and I was at a rent board about four hundred days ago proving the first three lines of your four against a woman who stood in a room for about forty minutes and described a coat. That is the third one. The first two did not come at all and I did not have to describe a coat to anybody.”

He put the sheet down and squared it.

“A demand is the only paper in this city a landlord may put in front of a magistrate, and what a demand carries is not the truth. It carries a date and a figure and an oath.”

If the person it is put against is in the room, she has to answer it, and she answers it with whatever she has. She has a year of money that came back in January and a sister who can be spoken for. If she is not in the room, then the only thing in the world in front of the magistrate is a landlord and his oath, and he has never once been late to a room.

## A Bar That Is A Bar

“You told her she could come.”

“I told her a man at a counter where she pays the rent told her to come and be spoken to,” said Ashby, “and that man is right, and she has had since the eleventh of August and it is the first of September, and there are twenty-two days.”

He turned his head about nine degrees.

“But you are not asking me whether she can come. You are asking me the other thing, and I am going to answer it, and it is the plainest thing I know and it took me nine years to arrive at.”

“Say it.”

“There is nothing in a demand that says she understood it,” said Corvin Ashby. “Not one word. There is a day and a place and a figure and my oath.”

He put his hand down on the sheet.

“The lines she is held to are the lines you wrote. Suppose she had them read to her in a language she has, in a room with a door onto a street, on a morning when she has not been up since the middle of the night, with a person standing behind her told to speak slowly. Suppose all of that happened and she went back to her rooms and it did not help her at all.”

“Then there is no bar in the world that would touch me.”

He put two fingers on the table.

“That is what a bar is. It does not depend on a fact. It depends on there being a room in which a person can be understood, and if the person cannot be understood then every day of the year is the morning they did not come.”

## What He Wanted To Say

He wanted to say: *then let me stand in that room.*

He wanted it so badly that his hands were doing something about it, and he put them flat on his own knees under the table, which is the only thing he has found to do with them in six years.

He did not say it.

He had been in two hearings in this city and had answered for himself in both. In one of them a clerk told him to his face that he was not permitted a second question. In the other he had been read to and had written four sentences of his own into a file about a rule that could not be tried.

He knew exactly what it was to be a man with no standing in a room, and he had known it since the fourth of March at the top of two flights with nobody on the landing.

A man of rank one with no standing is a man who is standing in a room.

## The Landlord’s Man

The landlord’s man was in the passage outside when he came out, in the same coat, with the same satchel.

“You have been in here forty minutes,” he said. “You have been in here forty minutes and I have been in this passage forty minutes and there is nowhere in this building for me to stand that is not in somebody’s way.”

“Did you go to the wall end.”

“I went on the seventeenth,” said the landlord’s man. “At ten to six, with about forty people behind me, and he read the figure I was asking about out of the eleventh book with the day and the weather against it, and he said the reason, and the reason was in plain words, and about eleven people wrote it down and two of them did not need to because they will remember it.”

“Then you have what you went for.”

“I have what I went for,” said the landlord’s man, “and I have had eleven days to work out what it is worth, and it is worth nothing at all, and I want to tell you that before you say anything, because I think you have come out of that door with a face on it.”

“Your employer’s case is on the second of September and it is a shop rent and it is a figure, and now you have the figure checked, and so the figure can be produced.”

“That is what I thought on the seventeenth and it is not what I think now.” He shifted the satchel. “The figure is not what they will argue about. They will argue about the first line, and the first line is that something changed what she held, and there is nothing in this city that can check whether that happened. Not a book, not a slate, not a man at a wall end with twelve of them.”

## Say It In The Passage

“Three of the four lines are checkable,” said Lin Hao. “You said that yourself in a lane off Cordwain in April.”

“I said it about the three lines,” said the landlord’s man. “I have had eleven days and I have been carrying demands for six years and I am going to say it to you once and then I am going back to work.”

He did not put it down gently.

“The three lines died on the seventh of July and the three that are left are all asking about somebody. Another person was there, or was given it. In every one of the nine demands I carry, that person is a landlord, or a man who came to take a room, or a shopkeeper, and every one of them can be named because they all have a door.”

“And the woman cannot describe him.”

“The woman cannot describe him because he came at night and she was in the room for about four minutes and he did not say his name and she was not asked for one,” said the landlord’s man. “That is not her failing. That is the shape of the thing. And it is the only one of the three with a shape.”

## The Fifteenth

“You are going to be there,” said Lin Hao.

“I am going to be there,” he said. “Not because of anything you have said. Because on the twelfth of this month I am going to stand at the back of a room off a third court and a man is going to say something about a coat and a height and a hand, and about forty people are going to be there, and about nine hundred people in this city have told other people since July that a man stands at the foot of a board on a hill.”

He put his hands in his coat.

“That is not a reason to be in a room. It is what it will look like.”

“There is no form for a person to be in that room who is not a party or a witness.”

“There is not,” said Ashby, from behind them, “and I am going to tell you what it looks like, because I am not careful and it costs me more than it costs you.”

Both of them turned round.

“A man of rank one at the back of a room with no standing in it,” said Corvin Ashby. “I have been the thing people look at in nine rooms in this city and I am going to tell you what that is worth. It is worth nothing. Not one grain of it.”

He came out into the passage and stood at the top of the stairs with a sheet of paper in his hand.

“And I am going to say the other thing as well, because you have come into my room and told me nothing, and I have decided that you are not a crook, and I am not careful.”

“If you stand at the back of that room, then in about nine years there is no way on earth to tell whether the fifteenth of September was about a year and a bit of arrears on a shop over a row of rooms, or about a man who wrote four lines.”

He got the door open before he had finished saying it.

“That is the same thing that happened to four lines on a board in a yard on the seventh of July. About nine hundred people decided it themselves in about nine minutes and they were not being unfair.”

He went back in and shut the door.

The landlord’s man did not move for about four seconds.

“He is right,” he said.

“He is right about every fact he says in this city and he has been for nine years.”

“He is right and it is not fair, and you are going to be in that room anyway, and I am going to be in that room anyway, and we are going to be the two people in it that anybody can see.” The landlord’s man squared his satchel. “And neither of us will be able to read the one page in this city that would say what any of it was for.”

*Two thousand and ten days. Less two thousand and two, the twenty-third of August, is eight. A month runs thirty days and stops: seven days are left in August after the twenty-third, and the first of September is the day after the thirtieth, which makes eight in all.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is twenty-nine days, and that is one month less one day. The next page in this city goes out at four on the thirtieth of September with a figure at the head of it that has not moved since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and it will go out on the thirtieth of December after that with the same figure on it.*

*Less the eleventh of August, one thousand nine hundred and ninety, is twenty days, and a hearing sat on that morning at about eleven o’clock and the first three lines were proved in about thirteen minutes and the third one put a landlord out of a room, and he was right about that as well.*

*Less the seventh of July, one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is fifty-four days, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth line off a board themselves, with a rag and a bucket, over about eleven minutes, and the man who wrote it stood nine feet off and said nothing, and in nine years nobody will be able to tell whether a thing that happened on that morning was done by about nine hundred people or by one man for himself.*

# The Wall Comes Down In The Spring

Three days had gone since a man at a counter nine streets from a wall had said that a number is not a way of getting anything and is a way of being found.

Two thousand one hundred and twenty-six was the twenty-seventh of December and the thirtieth is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine, and two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine less two thousand one hundred and twenty-six is three, and thirty less twenty-seven is three because a month is thirty days and runs out at the thirtieth.

The page went out at four.

It went up a stair and along two floors to a counter with a window on it, and a clerk folded it and laid it along the rail, and the man behind the counter kept his hands on his own book and did not touch it, and did not say anything, because in this city a page on a rail is scenery and scenery is not discussed in front of the public.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head, in the hand of the office, unmoved across eight quarters.

About four hundred and nineteen lines under it with a date and an hour against every hour and no figure and no name.

Two lines in a column.

Four sentences at the foot in a hand that is his, one of them with a man in a delivery coat’s name under it and not his.

And six lines under those, and the fourth of the six with a stroke through it from the first letter to the last, and a name under that, and two inches of nothing.

The twenty-fourth of them. Nine people a week will get to the bottom of it.

Then the bundle went the other way into a room in that building that nobody in that building can name, and it has twenty-four things in it now, and the twenty-third of them had a sheet of paper in it with nothing written on it, and nobody is required to open any of them, and the last one has never been opened.

## Half Past Four At The Corner

About two hundred people a day cannot walk past the foot of that wall without reading what is on it, and about nine hundred in this city can read everything there has ever been there, and there are four sheets of paper in about four hundred houses that are copies of two words on four inches of stone, made on the twenty-second of December by a boy with a scraper and a lump of chalk.

The boy put the sixty-third form up at about ten past four with a paste brush and his own weight against it and it went up level, and the two ruled squares at the foot of it were empty, and about nine people at the corner had been standing there waiting for it.

It is the last form that will go up on that corner while there is a corner.

The render on that wall comes off in the spring and the wall goes down to the course and a new wall goes up, and the form goes on a board by a kerb at a corner where about two hundred people a day stop, and the corner is going to be four inches of dressed stone and about nine feet of new render and nothing at all to put a board against.

The paint was about a week old on the render and the four inches of stone had a week of weather on them and two words cut into them and nothing on them at all.

The woman of about sixty was at the corner with about nine people round her, and she was telling them where the words came from, and she had it wrong.

“It came from a form,” she said. “It came off a form a boy paints at the end of every month, and I have watched him do it about sixty times, and I want you all to know that I am wrong and I have said so to you and that is the only thing I have got to give anybody this winter.”

A man at the front said the words did not come from a form.

“Then I have been standing in the wrong place,” said the woman of about sixty, “and I have been standing in it since the ninth of November, and you may all go and look at the four inches at the bottom of it and decide between us.”

“You cut them.”

“I did not cut them. I have never once touched that stone and there is a woman comes past every morning with a short-handled broom who would tell you the same and she will not tell you anything at all, and I am not going to pretend to know who did it because I have never once asked and I have had about thirty-eight days to ask.”

“You could ask.”

“I could,” said the woman of about sixty, “and I have not, and you will ask me why in a minute and I am going to tell you the same thing I told the man in the rank-one coat.”

“Why.”

“Because I have been standing in front of something for six weeks and then in front of nothing for thirty-eight days,” she said, “and a person who has been standing in front of nothing is not going to get a wall back by asking a stranger a question at half past four in the afternoon.”

He said, “I have told about four hundred and ninety of them that a number meant one thing.”

“And that was true for about six weeks,” said the woman of about sixty. “And then a man came up this path at four in the afternoon and told me in front of about nine hundred people that it was a number of lines on a page, and I have had nothing to stand in front of anybody with since.”

## Ten To Six At The Wall End

Emil Brack had the slate set up on end at the wall end and nine or ten people round it in the cold, and by six there were about forty of them, because a wall is where people stand when they want to be near other people and be outside at the same time.

He read the four sentences at the foot of the twenty-fourth page out loud, in other words, not as they are written, to about forty people. He did not skip one of them and he did not wait to be asked and he did not explain them.

A woman asked him whether the second one had always said that.

“It has been at the foot of that page since before I came to this corner,” said Emil Brack, “and I have read it to about nine hundred people since the thirteenth of July and it has not changed once, and I am telling you that as a fact and not as an argument, because it is a fact and I am not arguing anything with anybody tonight.”

He was correct about every part of it.

“You have had that sentence said out loud to you by two men in two rooms since the ninth of November,” he said, “and it has been in the office’s hand for three years and it goes out four times a year, and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it.”

He put the rag in his coat and took it out again. “And there are about four hundred people standing at this corner tonight who have never read a page of anything in their lives, and who have got it out of a rubbing of two words that a boy did off a wall with a piece of chalk.”

Nobody thanked him. Two people said thank you and neither of them said it to him.

“And I am not going to stand here and tell you it is a warning,” said Emil Brack, “because I told a man in a room in the ninth month of a year I am not going to name that it stopped being a warning the first time he said it out loud to me, and he told me not to say it again, and I have not, and it is still true.”

A man at the wall end asked him what a warning was for.

“A warning is a thing a person may take or leave,” said Emil Brack. “That sentence is not a warning and it has not been a warning since a man read it to me in a room with a clerk in it, and the reason it is not is that a warning belongs to the person it is aimed at and there is nobody in this yard for it to be aimed at any more. It is about a page. Read it as a page.”

“And the fourth of the four.”

“The fourth of the four is the one that gets you the two words at the bottom of this wall,” said Emil Brack, “and it is the only one of the four that has ever got anybody anywhere.”

“And the man who wrote it took it off a board himself, in a yard of about eleven thousand people, and did not tell anybody he had done it. I have been right about that for six weeks and I am right about it now, and he is standing nine feet off me with a rag in his hand that is not his.”

Nobody thanked him.

“I have not been thanked for three years and I am not going to be tonight,” said Emil Brack, “and I have never once been right about a figure that was not mine.”

## What About Nine Hundred People Do With A Sentence

By about twenty past six the sentence had been said out loud in that yard about two hundred times, and about forty of those were wrong.

A woman who cannot read said the third of the four at the foot of the page in the wrong order and put the Register where a man is supposed to be in it.

“That is not it,” said somebody.

“That is what my landlord said it was.”

“That is what a man with a bad coat said it was,” said somebody else, “and this is not a demand, it is a sentence about a Register, and the Register is not in it.”

She argued back for about a minute and then took the second telling and said it again, and got it right the third time, and about eleven people heard all three of them.

A man at the back said the fourth of the four as though it were a condition of a claim.

“It is not a condition of anything,” said a woman of about forty. “It is a sentence about a Register and it is the fourth of four and it has been at the foot of that page for three years.”

“How do you know that.”

“Because I had it read to me at a returns desk in a division building in the ninth month of a year I am not going to name,” said the woman of about forty, “by the man standing over there, and it took about four minutes, and he did it because I asked him and not because he wanted to.”

About eleven people heard that.

A sentence is a thing you can argue with.

A figure is a thing you can only count, and about two hundred people a day in this city have been slowing down at this corner for six years with nothing at the bottom of it.

What was put there in the eighth month of this year was a figure, and it took about eleven minutes for a yard of about eleven thousand people to turn it into a count of claims, and a man with a rag took down a correct sentence to make room for it. About nine hundred people watched him do it, and about four hundred people took the rest off nine weeks later without asking anybody.

Tonight there is a sentence at that corner and about nine hundred people in the yard who can read all of it, and it has been wrong about two hundred times inside an hour and it will be a different sentence by the spring, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that will take any of it back down.

He stood nine feet off and let it happen, for the second time in about ninety days, and this time he had not come up the hill to do anything at all.

## The Sixty-Third Form

At about ten past six the corner had about two hundred people on it in the dark, and the boy was putting his brush down on the kerb.

He came down the path with the rag in his hand and about nine people and stopped at about nine feet, because that is where you stop at that corner, everybody stops at about nine feet, and not one of them has ever worked out why and all of them do it.

He did not ask for anything.

The boy said, “You are going to ask me about the boxes.”

“No.”

“You are going to ask me about the spring.”

“No.”

“You are not going to ask me anything at all and that is the third time,” said the boy. “I want to tell you the thing I have worked out, and then I am going to go and wash this brush, because there is no water in this city after the fourth and I have been using a bucket since the middle of the month.”

“Say it.”

“There are two boxes at the foot of that form. One wants the number of the thing at the address and the other wants the standing of it.”

“I know what they are for.”

“You know what a notice says they are for,” said the boy. “There has not been a figure in the left one since the eighth of October of a year nobody in this city names, and there has not been a standing in the right one on any form anybody in this yard can remember, and I have put sixty-three of them up.”

“How many people stop here.”

“About two hundred a day. About two of them get to the foot, and they have got to the foot in about four years, which is my father’s count and he is not a man who counts things.”

“Not one person has ever said that out loud to you.”

“Not one person has ever asked me for it,” said the boy, “and there is a difference, and you know there is a difference because you have spent a year being careful about it.”

He turned round and looked at it on the board by the kerb in the dark.

“In the spring a man is going to come with a form from a department nine streets away, and the form is going to say an address and a line of work and a box for the date the work was done, and he is not going to ask me anything.”

The boy did not look at the form. “He has not got a field on it for two words and he has not got a field on it for a figure, and he has raised that twice, and he has been told a question is a question and not a return.”

He turned back.

“He is going to paint the new wall the same colour as the old one, because a form does not say anything else. There is going to be four inches of dressed stone at the foot of it with two words cut into it, and he will not touch them, because it is not in his form.”

“And about two hundred people a day are going to stop at that corner, and there will be a quarter and a Bureau on the form and two empty squares at the bottom of it, and about two of them will get to the foot.”

He picked the brush up.

“And in the ninth month of next year I am going to put sixty-fourth up on that corner,” said the boy, “and the left box is going to be empty and the right box is going to be empty.”

He stood with the brush against his knee. “That is going to be the sixtieth month that anybody in this city has stood at a corner and read two squares with nothing in them, and there is not one instrument in this city that will put a figure in that box. I am the man who paints it and I am not going to be able to put one in it either, and that is the whole of what I have.”

He went off down the path with the brush and the bucket and about four people who were not going to stop.

Nobody said anything about that either.

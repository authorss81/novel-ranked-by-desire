# The Question He Has Carried Since March

Eight days had gone since a woman with a bundle of returns had said in a corridor at half past one that she would read the whole of a page aloud to one person on a night she picked and would not warn him. Two thousand and seventy-six was the seventh of November and the fifteenth is day two thousand and eighty-four, and two thousand and eighty-four less two thousand and seventy-six is eight, and fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.

Two tables and one bracket lamp, and a corridor of about ninety feet between this door and the street end of it.

The near table has carried returns and a pencil and an hour sheet on it four nights in seven since the first of October of the third year. The far one has carried nothing in all that time, and there is a place on it where a thing would go if a thing had ever been put down on it, and the wood is a different colour in a strip about nine inches wide.

She was at the near table at twenty past one with her cuffs fastened and her sleeves pulled down to the wrist. The door went behind him and she turned a page and did not look round, and he stood about four feet off for about four minutes while she finished what she was on, because that is how long it takes in this room and he has timed it.

He came in and sat down on the far side of the far table, which is a thing he had never done in three years, and set the bucket down against the leg of it out of the corridor.

She finished the line she was on, wrote the hour against it, drew the sheet up square against the edge with two fingers, and then she did not turn it over.

## The Question

“You came down with a question.”

“I came down with a question.”

“Since the seventh of March,” said Ines Duarte.

“Since the seventh of March.”

“Tell me the question and not the history.”

He had it ready and it had been ready for two thousand and forty-eight days and it had been in four rooms and in a corridor with nine people in it and on a hill at four in the afternoon and in nobody’s mouth but his own.

He said, “Is a sentence in the office’s hand still true after the man who wrote it cannot stand behind it any more.”

She put the pencil down.

Not flat. She put it down the way a woman puts a pencil down when she has finished with it for the moment and is going to pick it up again inside a minute.

“Say that again.”

“Is a sentence in the office’s hand still true—”

“No,” said Ines Duarte. “I heard it. I am telling you that you have been carrying it for eight months and you have never once got it right, and you have got it wrong in a way that makes it a question about you and not about the sentence.”

“Then say it properly.”

“You are not asking whether the sentence is true,” she said. “You are asking whether a man is allowed to go on standing behind a thing he said, once there is nothing left of him to stand it up with. That is a question about a man. And I am an auditor and I have never in eleven years been asked a question about a man, and I will not start at one o’clock in the morning.”

“That is not the answer.”

“It is not an answer at all,” said Ines Duarte, “and I am going to give you the thing that is instead of it, and you are going to sit there for it, and you have brought it to the only room in this city where a person is entitled to be made to.”

## What She Is Going To Do Instead

“There is a rule about this job,” she said, “and it came with the job and not with me, and it has three parts and I can say all three of them without stopping to think.”

“Say them.”

“A page is read in order. The date first, the hour with it, nothing skipped. And an auditor does not stop to be spoken to while a page is open.”

“That is eleven words.”

“It is eleven words and it has been eleven words for eleven years,” said Ines Duarte. “I have broken half of it exactly once, on the fourteenth of September of the fifth year, in this room.”

“It cost me about ninety seconds and it left a bundle out of square. There is a note about that in a private book I keep about myself, and no instrument in this city will ask me whether I keep a private book, and I am not going to look for one.”

He had not known there was a private book.

“You are going to read it.”

“I am going to read the standing sheet to you from the figure at the head of it to the fourth sentence at the foot,” said Ines Duarte, “and I am going to read it in that order and I am going to read every line of it, and it will take about eleven minutes, and I am going to do it in the voice I use on a column of returns, because that is the voice it is read in.”

“That is not how I have ever read it.”

“No,” she said. “You have read it four times a year, out loud, in a yard, with nothing improved, and that is a thing you do with a rule so that a crowd can hear it. This is a thing I do with a record so that one person can sit in a room and be told what is on it. They are not the same act and I am not going to confuse them.”

## The Reading

She drew the sheet across and squared it and put her finger on the first line and went.

The figure at the head was eight hundred and fifty-two and she gave the number twice, at the same speed, and then she read underneath it.

There were about four hundred and nineteen lines and every one of them had a date against it and an hour against the date and no figure against the hour, and she went down them in order without hurrying and without improving any of them.

About nine lines in she stopped for about a second and then went on, and he understood afterwards that she had stopped because one of them had been in the room where he lived.

She read the second column. There were two lines in it. One of them had been in the book since the fifteenth of October of the fourth year and was his, and she read it, and she read the sentence a clerk had entered under it exactly as it stood, and he sat on a table in a building he had been in three years and heard his own failure read back to him in the voice of a woman who enters what she is given.

Then she got to the foot of it.

“The first two of these have been at the foot of every sheet this office has printed since the first of February of the first year,” said Ines Duarte, “and I am going to give you those in the words they are in, because they are the office’s and not yours.”

She gave them. Then she came to the two he had added on the ninth of November of the fifth year and she did not read them as they stood.

“I am going to say what the third of them says in my own mouth, because it is in your hand and I am not going to be the second person to say a sentence of yours out loud this month.”

And she said it, in the flat voice, and it was a true sentence and it was not his, and she said the fourth of the four after it in a way that put the Register in the sentence and him nowhere in it at all, and neither of them said anything while she did it.

Then she stopped, with her finger still on the line, and looked up for the first time since she had begun.

“Your turn,” said Ines Duarte.

## The Hour

He said, “Give me an hour.”

She did not move her finger.

“The ninth of November of the fifth year,” he said. “There is an hour against the line you just read and it is the only figure on that page I have ever been able to make a claim out of, and you are the only person alive who can certify it, and I have come down two flights at half past one in the morning to ask you for it.”

“No.”

“You read me the whole of a page for eleven minutes and you would not give me one number.”

“I have read you four hundred and nineteen hours tonight,” said Ines Duarte. “Every one of them is on that sheet and every one of them was given to me with the date and in order and I put them against the lines as I read. That is what I am for. And there is not one hour on that page that I would stand in a room and certify, and if you want to know why, it is in front of you.”

She put her finger on the third sentence at the foot.

“It is the third of the four, and what it does is take the value out of a figure until somebody checks it,” she said. “I read it out loud nine minutes ago. Every hour I have ever certified in eleven years sits underneath that sentence, and the sentence was printed by an office that cannot be compelled and cannot sue and cannot be sued, and it went out at four on the thirtieth, and nine people in this city get to the bottom of it in a week.”

She kept her finger where it was. “If I stand in a room and say a number out loud and put my name to it, then this city has an hour in it that an auditor has vouched for, and in about four years somebody will take that number off a wall and it will mean something else, and they will be right to, and I will be the last person standing next to it.”

She turned the pencil over and did not pick it up.

“I broke half a rule in this room on the fourteenth of September of the fifth year and I cost myself ninety seconds and a bundle,” she said, “and the rule I broke was that an auditor does not stop to be spoken to while a page is open, and I broke it in the middle of the night for a man who did not ask me to. I am not doing it twice in six years for a man who has come with a demand.”

## Four Questions She Is Not Going To Answer

“The four,” he said.

“The four stand,” said Ines Duarte. “I put three of them on the sixth of March of the first year, in a corridor, with a sheet lying open in front of me, and the fourth at a door on the fourteenth of September of the fifth. You have not answered one of them in one second and I am not going to answer any of them this morning, and this morning is not the morning I answer them.”

“If you have come down here to ask me for those, then you have come down two flights for nothing and I would rather you had.”

“What am I going to do with the morning.”

“You are going to sit on that table,” she said, “and I am going to go on reading, and you are not going to be able to make me stop.”

## The Empty Table

He looked at the table he was sitting on.

It was the same height as the other one and the same width and it had nothing on it, and it had had nothing on it since the first of October of the third year, which is the day she came to audit this building and which is about eleven hundred and twenty-four days ago.

“Why that one.”

“Because it has never had a page on it,” said Ines Duarte. “That is the whole of the reason and it took me about four seconds, and I would like you to understand that it is a reason and not a mood.”

She did not wait for him to answer that. “That table is the only surface in this room that has not had a sheet put on it and read off, in three years. I am going to put a man on it and keep the sheet on the other one, and read, because the rule says a page is read in order and it does not say anything about what may be happening at the other table while it is.”

She got up and turned the lamp on its bracket so that it lit the sheet and not the room, and she pushed him back onto the empty table with two fingers between his shoulder blades, and he sat down on it with his boots on the boards, and the sheet stayed in front of her at the first table under the lamp.

She went back to the line she was at and read on.

“Now then,” said Ines Duarte. “The date first.”

## What She Read On Him

She read him from the top and she read him in order, and she did not stop to be spoken to while she was doing it.

“The date is the seventh of November and the hour is half past one. You came up two flights and you did them in three goes, and I heard you do it on the stair, and I have written nothing about that because it is not on any page I keep.”

She turned the sheet a quarter of an inch and carried on.

“The first line is the shoulders. Both of them are up and they have been up since you came in, and they will be up when you go out, and they are not up because of a bundle, because there is no bundle in this room. They are up because you have been carrying a question down two flights and you have been carrying it since March.”

“Second line. You have lost about a third of what you had in the spring, and I am not guessing.”

“There is a ruled board with your plate against the head of a column on it in a ward nine streets from here, and eleven marks going down it, and the marks have got smaller. I did not put them there, I took them off you, and I have known for about nine days, and I said nothing and waited to be asked, and you did not ask, and that has been between us since the middle of October.”

“You told me yesterday.”

“I told you yesterday because you asked me yesterday,” said Ines Duarte, “and I have not been waiting eleven years for a man to ask me about his own chest. Keep still. Third line.”

His hands were on the edge of the empty table and she came and got them off it and put them down beside him.

“Third line is the chest, and I am going to give you the number because nobody else in this city is going to.”

She did not raise her voice. “It goes about half as deep as it should, and it has gone half as deep as it should since the spring of the third year, and it has been half as deep as it should since the middle of September.”

“I can hear that from where I am standing and it is not a thing I am going to take a question about, and if you start on it I stop where I am.”

“I am not going to tell you about it.”

“Good,” said Ines Duarte. “Fourth line, and it is the only line tonight that is not in the record, and I am giving it to you because you are the only person who can be told it.”

“You are about a fortieth less than you were. There is nothing behind that and there is not going to be anything behind that, and I am not going to tell you how I know, because how I know is that I have been in this room four nights in seven for three years, and I read you across forty feet every night without ever having to come near you.”

She turned the pencil over in her hand. “There is not one thing on any page in this city that I have to be nearer for than this.”

She got her cuffs off, one button and then the other, and there was ink at the base of the first two nails of her right hand that had been there as long as she had been in this building, and a grey at her left temple with the etches in it that has not moved since the summer of the second year.

She read him the rest of the way down, in order, at the speed she uses on a column of returns, and she did not skip a line, and the page under the lamp went on being the page.

She was not quick and she was not slow. She took his shirt off over his head and her hands went down the front of him the way they had gone down the front of the reading, and she said what she was reading at the top of each thing and she was right about all of it, and about nine minutes in she said, “That is a reading, not a compliment, and you will want it to be a compliment and you may not have it.”

Then she got his trousers open and took hold of him and weighed him in her hand the way she weighs a thing against a figure she has already got, and looked down at it, and said, “Less,” and laughed once, which is not a thing she does.

“Say something about that.”

“No.”

“Say one thing.”

“You have been counting the marks on a board nine streets away and thinking they are a number of fortieths left,” said Ines Duarte. “They are not a stock. There is nothing in you to count. That is all it is, and I would not have brought you down here for it if it were anything else.”

She got her mouth onto him and worked at him without any ceremony at all, one hand at the back of his head keeping him at the height she wanted and the other tight at the root, and she talked to him the whole time in the same flat voice she had read four hundred and nineteen lines in, and about four minutes in she stopped and said, “That is the only hour I am going to certify tonight and it is not on the page.”

Then she got him off the table and turned him and put him into her standing up with her back against the first table and her hands on the edge of it behind her, and the sheet was a foot away from her left elbow and she did not look at it again.

She was wet already, and the first of him went in tight and about four seconds later it did not, and she had her eyes on his face the whole of that and did not look away from it afterwards either.

“Go on, then,” said Ines Duarte, “and do it slower than the yard, because there are about eleven thousand people in a yard and there is one in this room.”

He did it slower.

She made a noise about five times in twenty minutes and on two of them she got her jaw against his shoulder until it passed, and there is a mark on that sleeve in the morning. The lamp hummed. A page turned once, at about the halfway point, and she turned it with her thumb and did not look down, because it was the next line in the order.

She came with her mouth open and both arms locked round him and her face in his collarbone, and she did not make a sound for about four seconds and then she did. He came about nine seconds after her and she held all of that too and said nothing about the interval between them, the way she does.

## Afterwards

She got her cuffs done and squared them against each other and did not hurry at all, and she went back to the near table and drew the sheet up square again and found her place in it with her thumb and carried on from where she had been.

The sheet was face up. It had been face up the whole twenty minutes.

She did not turn it over.

“That was a rule of this room and I have broken it twice in six years,” said Ines Duarte, “and the second time I am putting in the same private note I put the first one in, and I would like you to notice that I have not put you in it as a person. You are a reading. I do not keep people.”

“You have never once lied to me.”

“No,” she said. “I have never once lied to you and I am not going to start at one o’clock in the morning, and that is not a compliment either, it is the only thing I have.”

He stood up off the empty table and got his coat off it and he was slower getting it on than he had been getting it off, and she watched him do it the way she watches a column.

“Eight days ago a woman on a counter asked me whether it was still true,” said Ines Duarte, “and I did not answer her and I am not going to now. Here is what I am going to do about the eleventh month instead, and you may do as you like with it.”

“Say it.”

“The twenty-fourth sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of December,” she said. “It is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine.”

“It has a figure at the head of it that has stood for eight quarters, and about four hundred and nineteen lines, and two in a column, and four sentences at the foot in a hand that is yours. Nine people in this city will get to the bottom of it in a week, and every one of them will have read the foot and not the rest.”

She turned the pencil over and picked it up.

“And about the eleventh month of every year for about nine years now, in a room with a shut door in it, I have read that page to one person,” said Ines Duarte.

“That is not an office and it is not in any book, and I am not going to tell anybody, and you had better not tell anybody either. Because the second a person knows there is a night in this city when a sentence gets said out loud in a room, then that is what it is for, and it will be for other people, and I will have made it.”

She wrote the hour against the line.

“Go up your two flights slowly,” she said. “You have about a fortieth less than you had in the spring and you are going to have it for the rest of it, and there is nobody in this city who can put one back, and I have looked, and I have looked properly, because looking is the whole of my trade.”

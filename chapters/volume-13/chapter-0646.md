# A Figure She Kept Nine Years

Seven days had gone since a man had stood about nine feet off a wall and understood that four inches of dressed stone at the foot of it were going to outlast everything else in his working life.

Two thousand and ninety-nine was the thirtieth of November and the seventh of December is day two thousand one hundred and six, and two thousand one hundred and six less two thousand and ninety-nine is seven, and a month runs out at the thirtieth, so a day seven after it is the seventh of the next month.

There are two places in this city where a person who has been keeping something gets found out.

He went to the yard first, at about ten in the morning on the seventh of December, and it was an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and about eleven thousand people were in it and about nine hundred of them could read all of everything there had ever been on the shutter.

There was nothing on the shutter.

## The Yard With Nothing On It

About two thousand people a day go past the foot of that wall and every one of them has to slow down for it, and they have not been able to slow down for it for about twenty-eight days, and they still do.

They slow down and there is nothing there and they go on. That is what about two thousand people a day have been doing since the ninth of November and not one of them has walked into a counter about it.

The woman of about sixty was standing where the board with the figure on it had hung, with no rag in her hand and no chalk on her coat, because there had not been twenty-eight days since there was anything on that wall for a rag or a piece of chalk to be any use on.

“You have not been up here,” she said.

“No.”

“You have not been up here since the fourth of October and that is the last time I saw you in this yard, and I have been standing in this spot every morning since the ninth of November, and about nine hundred people have come down this path and asked me what the number meant.”

“What did you tell them.”

“The same thing I told about four hundred of them in September,” said the woman of about sixty. “That it was how many people there are owed something. And I have told it about four hundred and ninety times and it is the only thing I know how to say and I have been saying it into an empty wall for about twenty-eight days, and it gets easier every day and that is the part I cannot stand.”

“How many.”

“Say nothing,” said the woman of about sixty, “and do not look at that door, because she has been up there since about ten and she is going to say one thing and she is not going to be answered, and it is a whole month of mine and I have given it up three times already for a thing that mattered.”

## One Thing On The Steps

Agnete Lindqvist was on the steps of the Vestry and she was there when he came up the hill and she had been there since about ten.

He did not go over. He had never gone over. There is a man who stands beside that door and has stood beside it for five years and has never once asked anybody why.

She said her one thing to about nine people on the steps and he was about thirty feet off and heard all of it.

“How many people are in this yard this morning,” she said.

She waited about four seconds, which is what she waits, and then she said, “I said four hundred. I have said about four hundred on these steps once a month for about sixteen months and I have never once been answered, and it is not four hundred, and I cannot work out what it is, and neither can anybody else who says it.”

Then she went in and the door shut and the man beside it did not move.

She was right. He knew she was right because he had stood at the foot of that shutter on the thirtieth of September and put a figure into about nine hundred people’s heads that was not a count of anything, and nobody had ever asked him what the yard held and he had never stood still long enough to try.

## The Desk Under A Window

The desk was off Cordwain and it had a counter with a window on it and a rail on the counter and a man behind it who has run that desk for about nine years.

“Seven,” said the man at the desk.

“Seven.”

“Seven is the answer I have been giving since the thirtieth of March,” he said, “and six of the seven were strangers and the seventh was a man in a delivery coat who came down about nine days ago.”

He turned a page over.

“He asked me a question,” said the man at the desk. “He asked me whether I had ever in nine years been asked what a page on that rail is for. And I had not, and I have never asked anybody either, and I have thought about it every day since and I have not worked out what to do with it.”

“What did you tell him.”

“That I had not,” said the man at the desk, “and he said that was correct and went away, and that is nine days of my life and I have had about nine hundred people go past this rail in it.”

“What did you do with it.”

“I wrote it in the ledger,” said the man at the desk. “There are about thirty names in it and there is nothing else in this building that I have ever kept, and I have put his question in against the thirtieth, in pencil, because a pencil and a ledger are the whole of what I have got.”

## Nobody Writes On Them

There is a figure at the head of the standing sheet that has not moved in eight quarters and a man in a delivery coat who reads the reason out loud at a wall end every morning before he writes his first nine, and there are four hundred and nineteen lines under the head of that sheet with an hour against every hour and no figure on any of them.

And there is everything else.

A woman on a counter in a division building who carried ninety-one people a quarter on her fingers for four years and stopped counting them in September because after the fifteenth a counter figure is the only kind of figure this city will accept.

Three questions in a day book in a division building, written out in four minutes on the thirtieth of September, and nobody returned them and they went to nobody.

A man at this desk with a pencil ledger of thirty-odd names and a column of dates he started keeping in the autumn, because somebody set a paper down two inches from his elbow and he had a pencil and nothing to enter it on.

And a woman off Lamplit Row in number four who has added up what she is short by on her fingers at the end of every month since the month she went in, and who cannot read.

None of those figures is on a page. Every one of them is the truest thing the person carrying it has ever said out loud, and there is not one instrument in Ostara behind any of them, and there is no clerk who may be given one, because a clerk enters what she is given and what anybody has got to give her is a sentence.

## What She Put On The Desk

She came at about half past two in the afternoon and she had a coat on that was not a coat for a walk and she had come across the city again, the third time, and no one in this city has ever sent for her.

She put a paper down on the counter.

It was the demand of the twentieth of July with a man’s name on it in a fair commercial hand and a room number and three figures, and she turned it round so that the address was at the top where a person puts a thing for another person to look at.

“This was proved against me on the twenty-eighth of July,” she said, “and you were not there and it was not your office, and I lost about four months on it.”

“That is not what I am here for.”

“No,” said the woman in number four. “It is what I have got. I have not got anything else and I have thought about this for nine days and I have not told anybody I was coming.”

“You are not asking me for anything.”

“I am not asking you for a single thing out of you, and if you put me on a counter with a box I will take my paper and go home and I will not come again, and I have not come four times for that.”

“Then what are you here for.”

“I am here because there is a figure in this city that has never once been written down and it is mine,” said the woman in number four, “and I am the only person alive who has got it, and I would like one person in this city to have heard me say so before I go back.”

He looked at the demand for about four seconds and then he did not look at it again.

“Nine years,” she said.

“What.”

“Nine years of short,” said the woman in number four. “Every month since the month I went in.”

She put two fingers on the counter. “I add it up at the end of the month on my fingers at the kitchen table with the lamp, and I have never once written it down because I cannot write, and I have never once said it out loud — not to my landlord, not to his man, not to anybody. I have been short every month of the nine years and I have known it every month of the nine years.”

She put two fingers on the counter about nine inches from his hand.

“I cannot tell you what it is to the penny,” she said, “and I am not going to, because I have never said the last figure out loud and I do not know whether I would say it right and I have not got anybody to check it against. I have never said it out loud to one person in nine years. That is what I have come about.”

## What He Did With It

“You have come to a desk off a court.”

“I have come to a desk off a court where a page comes out on a rail every month,” she said. “You told me in a corridor once, or a man told me in a corridor that you were on, and you are the only person anybody in this city can put a figure in front of.”

“And what do you want me to do with it.”

“Nothing,” said the woman in number four. “I want you to not do anything with it and I want to have said it in a room.”

“That is a strange thing to walk nine miles for.”

“I have walked nine miles four times this year,” she said, “and this is the third one I have got something out of, and I will tell you about the other two if you have got about nine minutes, because I am not going to waste a clerk’s time and I do not think you are a clerk.”

“I am not.”

“No. A clerk would have asked me to leave the paper.”

She turned the demand about nine degrees on the counter so the address was at the head of it again.

“Give me the figure.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Because you will write it down,” said the woman in number four, “and in about nine days it will be on something with a room at the head of it, and about nine hundred people will be told it is a number about something, and in nine years somebody will want to know where it came from and there will be nothing on the page about a woman off Lamplit Row adding it up on her fingers, because there is nowhere to put me.”

He stood behind the counter for about nine seconds and did not write it down.

That is the whole of what he did and there is no part of it that anybody would call anything.

## What She Did Next

“Say it,” said the woman in number four.

“There is no figure to say.”

“There is a figure and you have had it for nine seconds,” she said. “I gave it to you in pieces with my fingers on the counter and you have added it and you have not written it and you are not going to. Now say it out loud to me, because I cannot read it and I have never heard it, and if I am going to have carried it for nine years I am going to have heard it once.”

He said it.

The man at the desk did not write it down. He has a pencil and a ledger and about thirty names in it and he has never once in nine years entered a figure that was given to him in a corridor by a person standing at his counter, and he did not start.

The woman in number four said it back after him.

She said it badly. She said it the way a person says a thing they have had in their hands for nine years and never in their ears, and the middle of it was in the wrong place, and he did not correct her, and when she had finished she put her hand on the counter about nine inches from his and stood there.

“That is the first time,” she said. “That is the first time in nine years I have heard it out loud and it does not sound the way it sounds in my head.”

“No.”

“It sounds smaller.”

“It is the same figure.”

“It is the same figure and I have been carrying it wrong for about nine years because you can only hear a thing once,” said the woman in number four, and she took her paper off the counter and squared it and put it under her arm, and she went, and she did not thank him and he did not ask her to.

*Two thousand one hundred and six days. Less two thousand and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of November, is seven. A month is thirty days and there is no thirty-first, and the last working day of it is the thirtieth.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is sixty-seven days, which is two months with seven days after them. The twenty-third sheet went out at four on that morning with eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it and about four hundred and nineteen lines under it, and on the ninth of November three lines came off a board in about nine hours, and there has been nothing at the foot of that shutter since for two thousand people a day to slow down for, and they still slow down.*

*Less the thirtieth of December of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is three hundred and thirty-seven days, which is eleven months with seven days after them. A sheet goes out at four on that day and the twenty-fourth of them goes out in twenty-three days, and it has a hole in the foot of it with a name under it, and nine people in this city get to the bottom of it in a week.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and eighty-eight days, which is twelve months with twenty-eight days after them. The third of four sentences at the foot of that page is the one that takes the value out of a figure in this city until somebody checks it, and it is signed with a name that is not his and has gone out at four on the thirtieth eleven times since.*

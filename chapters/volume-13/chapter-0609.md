# Brack Asks For Nothing

Seven days had gone since a boy with a paste brush told him that the place which decides which wall comes down is nine streets away and does not argue with the weather. One thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven less one thousand eight hundred and twenty is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

He was at the wall end of the lane off Cordwain at about a quarter to seven on the morning of the twenty-eighth of February, and there was frost on the rails and his breath showing and about nine stools along the wall and the slate already up.

Emil Brack wrote figures all morning and took a grain a head, and about two of nine men looked back at him when he wrote, and about nine of them did not, and that was the whole of what the line did about his name and it has been two of nine in every month since the ninth of November.

Lin Hao had come down to hear what the man thought of a sentence.

That was his want and it was a strange one, because the sentence was his own and he had been carrying it for about a hundred and ten days and had not once heard a person say it back to him who was paid nothing for hearing it.

## What He Had Never Read

“The fourth one at the foot,” Lin Hao said. “You have never read it.”

“No.”

“You know what it says.”

“A woman at a stall read it to me,” said Brack. “On the morning it went out, about a quarter past ten, and she read me the first one and the name under it and then she read me the second one twice because I asked her to, and then she went and read it to a man who was standing behind me paying for a figure, and he did not pay it and went off down the lane.”

“You could have come and read it.”

“There is a thing about that sentence and about a man who trades in figures going to read a page about figures,” Brack said, wiping the slate. “The moment I stood at that desk and read it with my own eyes, somebody would have said the office made the man up who makes figures up, in order that he could say so from a page.”

“And it would have been true.”

“That is the part I could not get past. The whole of it would have been true, and I would have been standing there doing it.”

He put the chalk down and came round the end of the stools.

“That is not a complaint about your office,” he said. “That is about a man with nine stools and a rate.”

“What is the rate.”

“Nine a morning, near enough, for three years now and it will be nine tomorrow. About a quarter off nothing, because a morning needs a head. Eleven books and then a twelfth, with the date and the weather against every one and what the line looked like, and a reason in the margin in plain words against every ringed one.”

“I have never once been short by more than two mornings in about three years,” said Brack, “and I could tell you the count of them and I have never had a reason to give it to anybody. And about two thousand of about eight thousand are ringed across the whole run.”

“That is what I put on the page.”

“That is what you put on the page,” said Brack. “You put it in the office’s hand, at the foot of a page that about two thousand people a day are obliged to read as they go past, and there is my name under it in the same hand, and I have never once been sorry for a minute of any part of it.”

## The Man Has Never Read It

Lin Hao stood at the wall end of a lane in the cold and waited for it, and it did not come, and he waited for about four minutes, and the queue went on.

“I am going to tell you what happened to me in November,” said Brack, “and I am telling you because I have had three months to think about it and I have not once been able to find a person to tell, and there is nobody in this city I want to tell and that is the second most useful thing that has ever happened to me.”

He picked the chalk up and turned it over without marking anything.

“The office made me legal,” he said.

“That is not a word I would have used.”

“It is the word. For three years I was a man with a slate who made figures up and charged a grain a head for the privilege of guessing what you would have guessed. Nobody was ever going to stop that and nobody was going to try.”

“And then in November the office printed on a public page, in its own hand, that a figure in this city may be invented on purpose, and put my name underneath it. Now I am not a man doing that. I am a man who does that, described.”

He turned the chalk over the other way.

“I do not know what to do about it and I am not going to be told,” said Brack. “Not by you, not by the Registrar, not by anybody in nine streets with a schedule. I have thought about it every morning since the fourteenth of November and the best of it was four sentences long and it did not come out better than the first four.”

“So you want nothing.”

“I want nothing at all, and I have never wanted anything from that office and I am not going to start, and there is nothing in nine years of me standing in a lane that you could give me that would change what I write on the slate tomorrow.”

He put the chalk in his coat.

“But you did not ask me a question just now,” he said. “You said you had come down to hear what I think of a sentence. So I am going to tell you what I think of it, and it is not what you have been waiting four months to hear, because what you have been waiting four months to hear is an argument and there is not going to be one.”

“Go on.”

“There is no longer any difference between my figures and anybody else’s,” said Emil Brack. “That is what the page did. Before November, a man with a chalk and a book and a reason in the margin was doing something you could check, because you could look at the book and see that a figure had a reason against it and a figure had not.”

“Now everybody can look at my book.”

“Now the book does not tell you anything about whether the figure was worth anything, because the office has told the whole city that figures here get made up on purpose. And that is true, and I said so before anybody printed it, and I have been saying it at that wall end for three years.”

He nodded at the wall behind him.

“That is not a compliment to me,” he said. “It is a loss of a kind that does not cost me a grain a head. You have taken the only thing I had that nobody else had, which was that my figures were checkable, and you gave it away in an afternoon in November.”

“I could not have stopped you and I would not have tried, and a slate cannot be checked. It never could be. I have been saying that since the second year, and you have known it since the second year, and you printed it anyway.”

Lin Hao had come down that lane to hear a sentence and he got the back of the thing that had cost him most, and he stood in the frost and did not have one word ready.

## The Feeling He Did Not Name

What he felt was envy, and it was so close to joy that for about four seconds he could not tell which of the two it was.

A man in a delivery coat can stand in the open at six in the morning and say out loud what a figure is. He has said it for three years and been paid a grain a head for saying it, and about two in nine have looked back at him since.

About four of the ones who walked away in October came back in November and paid, and about two more came back in the frost and paid. He has both of those figures written down with the dates and the weather against them.

And underneath the envy, colder, and worse than the envy, this.

He is now the only person in Ostara who can say out loud what a figure is and be right about it and not be argued with, because the office has said it for him, in the office’s hand, on a page about two thousand people a day cannot walk past.

The office got there first. It got there by about three years and by eleven books and it got there for nothing, and the man who said it first is a man with a slate and a chalk and no standing, and the man who printed it is a man at rank one with no figure cut into the plate in his coat.

## Up The Stairs

Brack took the slate off the wall and wiped it and put it under his arm.

“Will the office put a second name under the first one,” he said.

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Because the woman at that counter can only write down what a person hands her, and cannot tidy it afterwards, and cannot be hurried, and because not one piece of paper in this city has a place on it for a second name under a first one. And if there were, there would be nine of them on it inside two quarters, and a man who invents figures is the last thing that office needs under its own printing.”

“You have thought about it,” said Brack.

“I have thought about it since the fourteenth of November.”

Brack put his hand on the rail of the stair behind the lane.

“I never expected one and I never wanted one,” he said. “I wanted the first one. You put it on the page without being asked and no word of mine has ever gone back the other way and none is going to, and if you go up that stair tonight expecting a man with a chalk to come and say something about what you wrote for him, you have been listening to the wrong lane for three years.”

He went up the stairs.

*The twenty-eighth of February, one thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven days. Less one thousand eight hundred and twenty, the twenty-first, is seven. Twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, is twenty-eight. Less the fourteenth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-three, is one hundred and four, and the line went out at four on that day with his name under it in the office’s own hand.*

*The first morning in that lane was the third of October of the year before the first, which is day minus one hundred and eighteen, and one thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven less minus one hundred and eighteen with one after it is one thousand nine hundred and forty-six mornings.*

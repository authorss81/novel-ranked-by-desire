# The Paper That Comes Back

He came into the room off a third court on the fourth of January at about ten in the morning with the bucket left in the corridor, because a quarter goes out at four and the four days after a quarter are the whole of what this office is for.

Four days. One thousand seven hundred and seventy-three less one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine is four, and four less nought is four by the month count, because the thirtieth is the last working day of a month and the first of the next one is day nought.

The room had a table in it and two chairs and a counter, and a drawer under the table that had stood open and empty since the fourth of February. Yusuf Adeyemi was at the end of the table with his coat on, and Wenna Raut was at the counter with the bundle of the twentieth sheet squared in front of her and her hands off it.

“What do you want,” she said.

“The follow-up out of the tray,” Lin Hao said. “And whether anything has come in since the quarter.”

“Nothing has come in.”

He looked at the tray. It was empty. It had been empty since the eleventh of March, which is two hundred and ninety-three days back and about a month short of a year, and he had looked at it four times that morning for a reason he could not put to anybody.

An empty tray is what a successful quarter looks like from the inside. He had spent five years arranging for one.

He wanted the number to have moved. It had not. The head of the sheet still stood at eight hundred and fifty-two and had stood there since the thirtieth of March, which is two hundred and seventy-four days back, and about four hundred and nineteen of those lines were a person’s account of six years of cold with an hour against each and no figure on any of them, and the second column still had two lines in it.

He put his hand flat on the edge of the tray and left it there.

“There is one thing,” said Wenna Raut.

She said it the way she says everything, at the same speed, twice over in her own head before she opens her mouth.

“It came at about half past eight this morning and it is not a form.”

## What Came Back

It was a sheet of paper with writing on the front of it, and it was not office paper, and it had not come through the door with a person behind it.

“A clerk off a counter in the third court brought it,” Wenna said. “She put it on the table, she said it was for the address at the head of every line, and she went. She did not say who sent it. I did not ask her, because I am permitted one question and that was not the one I had.”

Lin Hao took it to the table.

It was a demand. A demand is four lines long with a figure and a date and a room number on it, and it is the only piece of paper in this city that a landlord is allowed to put in front of a magistrate, and there is nothing else in Ostara with that standing.

The four lines were in a fair commercial hand. He had never seen it before.

He read it.

*It changed what a person held. It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name. Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough. And it is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

He read it a second time.

He read it a third time, which is what a man does when he has understood a thing and is hoping the next reading will be a different one, and it was the same, and it was in the same hand, and it was his.

He read it a fourth time because he wanted, badly and in a way he was not proud of even in an empty room, to have misread it.

“You have read it four times,” Adeyemi said. “I have been counting. Say what is on it before you say anything about how you feel.”

“Four lines off a board,” Lin Hao said. “Copied out by hand. In about eleven seconds it took me to write them, in the column headed carried by, against myself, and I spent five days deciding whether to write them and then I wrote them in the time it takes to cross a room.”

“And the demand itself.”

“A man who lets six rooms off Lamplit Row and a shop under the one at the end has put my four lines in a demand against a tenant in number four, with a figure and a date and a room number, and has carried it to a counter off a third court, and a clerk has carried it here.”

“He has not been in this room,” said Adeyemi.

“No,” Lin Hao said. “He has not been in this room. That is what I keep coming back to. He has never once come through a door of this building because there is no door onto a street, and he could not find it if he did, and he went round it and put it in front of a third person who is allowed to answer.”

Adeyemi turned his head about nine degrees, which is how that man moves.

“Then he did it correctly,” he said. “I want that said in this room by somebody, because he did it correctly and it is the first correct thing anybody has done with those lines since they went on a wall.”

## The Back Of It

He turned the sheet over.

There were two words on the back, in pencil, small, and not in the commercial hand at the front.

*Is there.*

Nobody in the room said anything for a while.

“That is two words,” said Wenna Raut. “It is not a sentence and it is not addressed to anybody by name.”

“No,” Lin Hao said.

“It is addressed to an office.”

“Yes.”

He felt the dread take a shape for the first time in five years, and the shape was that a piece of paper had gone out into the world with a piece of him on it and had come back through a door he does not have, and he could not tell whether that was the worst thing that had ever happened to him or the first evidence that anybody had used the thing for anything at all.

“I wanted it not to be asked,” he said. “That is the honest thing and I have not said it out loud in six years. I wanted those four lines to be a thing people looked at.”

“It has been a thing people looked at for about a year,” said Adeyemi.

“That is not the same as being asked.”

“No,” said Adeyemi. “It is not, and I want you to sit with how pleased you are about that, because it is going to be the last morning you are pleased about it.”

## There Is No Form

He turned the sheet over again and looked at the two words and then at the drawer under the table, which was open, and which had been open and empty since the fourth of February.

The question is not addressed to him.

He understood that in the way a man understands a thing about his own hands.

It is addressed to the office. It is addressed to the thing at the address at the head of every line, which is the only way anybody in this city finds a room off a third court with two chairs in it.

It is addressed to a thing that has never been named in any document, in any finding, in any list, in any proceeding, in any year of its existence. And there is no instrument on which a person may be named in something, and none on which a thing that is not a person may be made to answer a question, and no clerk in this city may enter an answer she was not given.

“There is no form for it,” Lin Hao said.

“There is no form for any of it,” said Adeyemi. “Not for receiving the question, not for naming us as the party it is addressed to, and not for answering it. We have no standing to be a party. We cannot be compelled and we cannot sue and we cannot be sued, and we hold no money and have never claimed any, and I could say those five things in about nine seconds and every one of them is true and not one of them is an answer.”

He took his coat off the back of the chair and did not put it on again.

“I am going to leave you with it,” he said. “It came at half past eight. It will still be here at four. Do not write on it.”

“Would it matter if I did.”

“It would matter to about four people in every thousand who read a page of this office to the bottom,” Adeyemi said, “and it would be on the front of a piece of paper in a landlord’s file, and there is not one instrument in this city that could ever take it off again.”

He went.

Wenna Raut squared the twentieth sheet, and put the demand beside it, and looked at the two pieces of paper for a moment, and then she wrote one line in the day book, because she enters what she is given and cannot improve it and cannot be asked to hurry.

Then she put the pen down and read the demand again, both times at the same speed, and looked at him over the top of it.

“There is no form on which a person may be named in something,” she said. “That is what is on the back of it. Not on you.”

“That is what is on the back of it.”

“Then it is not addressed to you,” she said, “and you have been standing in this room for an hour holding a piece of paper addressed to a counter off a third court that cannot answer it, and you are the only person anybody is permitted to ask at that counter, and nothing in this city says so either.”

*The fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three days. Less one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is four. Four less nought is four by the month count, and a month is thirty days and the thirtieth is the last working day of it.*

*Less the thirtieth of March, one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, is two hundred and seventy-four, and the head has stood at eight hundred and fifty-two across all of it. Less the eleventh of March, one thousand four hundred and eighty, is two hundred and ninety-three, and the tray has been empty across all of that too.*

*Thirty multiplied by fifty-nine is one thousand seven hundred and seventy, and three days after it is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, and that is how old this office is this morning.*

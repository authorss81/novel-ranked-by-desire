# The Cost Of A Clean Sheet

Seven days had gone since a man had drawn one line through the fourth of four conditions on the foot of a page and had not written anything in the space where it had been. Two thousand and ninety-two was the twenty-third of November and the thirtieth is day two thousand and ninety-nine, and two thousand and ninety-nine less two thousand and ninety-two is seven, and there is no thirty-first to carry the day after a thirtieth.

It is the last working day of November and there is a page going out at four this afternoon with a hole in the foot of it, and he has not been in the room since the twenty-third and has gone about four other days that week looking for a reason not to go in.

He went up the main stair at about half past two in the afternoon and did it in four.

Not slowly. In four, with a landing between each two, the way a man walks when the stair is not the difficulty and the man is.

## The Desk At The Far End

The drawer was not open and she was not at the far end of the ward, and there were six beds and five of them had somebody in them.

Tessa Okonkwo was at the second bed with a screen round three sides of it and her sleeve pushed back past the elbow, and she was doing the thing her hands do, and he stood at the end of the ward with the bucket on the boards and waited about nine minutes, because a woman who is working does not stop for a man with a bucket.

She came out and she had her cuffs down and she looked at him and did not ask him why he had come, which was the first time in six years that she had not asked him why he had come.

“You did four,” she said.

“How do you know.”

“Because you are standing on both feet and you have not put the bucket down, and a man who has done it in three puts the bucket down before he says anything, and a man who has done it in two does not come up at all.”

“I have done it in two every night since the middle of October and I did not notice.”

“You did it in four on the first.”

“You did it in four on the first, and in three on the second, and in four on the third, and in four on the fourth,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and I have been standing at the end of this ward watching you come up that stair for six years and I have not been wrong about it once.”

“You have been counting.”

“I count everything,” she said. “It is not a gift and nobody has ever thanked me for it and it is the only thing about me that has never once been wrong.”

“You have been doing it in two since the middle of October and you are wrong about it,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “You did it in two every night until the twenty-third and you started doing it in three on the twenty-fourth, and you have been two landings worse in seven days, and I have been counting the landings the same way I count everything.”

She went down the ward and came back. “I did not tell you, because if I had told you in October you would have come up here on the twenty-eighth and asked me to put something back, and there is nothing to put back. I would rather you arrive at it slowly than be handed it.”

## What He Came To Say

“I have come to ask to be the next one on the sheet.”

“No.”

“You have not said why.”

“You have not given me a reason,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “You have given me a want, and a want is not a reason, and I have had eleven years of people in this ward telling me what they want and about one in nine of those is a reason.”

“What is the other eight out of nine.”

“Eight out of nine are people who want to be able to do a thing they cannot do at the moment,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “They want to be able to sleep. They want to be able to lift a child. They want to be able to go up a stair.”

“And not one of them has asked you for what they want.”

“Not one of them has asked me for what they want, because what they want is not a thing I can lift,” she said. “You could have said what you could do with it and you would have been the first, and that is not a reason for me to say yes.”

She turned the screen round so the bed behind it was out of the corridor.

“Say the want again so that I can answer the want.”

“I want to be the next name on the sheet.”

“That is still not it,” she said. “Here it is. That board is for taking and not for giving. Every column on it has come down and not one of them has ever gone up, and there is no line on it for going up.”

“If I took a share out of you this afternoon and wrote it on that board, then the thing you came up here for would be done, and you would be a person who has asked a woman to put a hand on him for the sake of a piece of paper.”

“You would not be able to tell the difference between that and being helped, and in about four years you would come up here again and ask me for a second one, and I would have to say no again.”

“All right.”

“And you would have spent a share you cannot spare to have a line in a column in a drawer in a ward,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “Do you know what that is. That is paying for a sentence.”

He had not known that.

“That is what you did in July at the foot of a shutter with a yard of about eleven thousand people standing round you,” she said, “and you did it out loud, twice, at the same speed, with nothing improved. You are not a person who pays for sentences and I will not let you start. It is the one thing in six years that has got better about you and it has got worse about everything else and I am not going to lose it.”

## Nothing There To Take

She took him into the room off the ward with the cot in it.

The cot had a blanket folded at the foot of it and a chair and a lamp on a stand and a basin with a cloth over it, and it is the room in which she does the thing that is her whole trade, and she shut the door behind them because he asked her to and because there is a corridor with a sister on it.

He said, “Shut it.”

“I have shut it.”

“I said that as an order.”

“I heard it as a request,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and I did it because you are the only person in this building who has ever asked me for anything and you have asked me for the same thing twice. That is not flattery. I keep a count of everything and it is the only count in this ward that has ever gone past four.”

She did not put her hands on him.

She sat down on the chair and put her hands in her lap and looked at him for about nine seconds.

“There is nothing here to take,” said Tessa Okonkwo.

“Say that again.”

“There is nothing here to take, and you have made me say it twice, and I have not made anybody say anything twice in eleven years.”

“Why are you making me say it.”

“Because you will go away and tell somebody that I refused you,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and I want you to be able to say that you asked for nothing, because you did not ask for anything. You asked to be written down.”

“That is not the same as nothing.”

“No,” she said. “It is the same as nothing and it is more expensive, and I have made about nine of them in eleven years and every one of them cost somebody something I could not give back.”

“Who.”

“A woman on the fourth floor of this building in the fourth year, who asked me to put her on it so that her sister would be told a thing, and I said no, and the sister was told nothing, and the woman has been on this ward for about two years since and does not know why she is here, and I have never been able to make it up to her.”

“That is not—”

“It is not a kindness and it is not a way of letting you down,” she said. “I will put a hand on your chest and I will tell you the date you started going slower, which is about the twenty-first of June.”

She put her hand where she said she would put it. “And I will tell you that about nine hundred people took the fourth of four off a board in that yard on the seventh of July and that you have been on a hill every day since, and that the twenty-fourth of September and the twelfth of October and the twenty-first of October were the three worst days you have had, and you went up a hill on all three.”

She put her palm on his chest, the way a woman checks a thing she already has the answer to.

“You went up on the twenty-first of October for about four seconds and then you decided about a box.”

“And that is all of it.”

“That is all of it and it is about a third,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and the third of a person is what takes a body past the point it should stop, and it is not a great deal.”

“I took it out of you in about nine minutes in a room with a door shut in the ninth month of a year I am not going to name, and I put it in a column, and I told you not to spend it. You did not spend it for three hundred and forty days and then you spent all of it between the twenty-first of June and this morning.”

She took her hand away.

“What is left is yours.”

## The Nine Minutes

They were the nine minutes of the worst night of his working year, and it was not a page, and he would not have written it down.

The fever she took out of him in the fourth year is the only thing he has ever had taken that did not go into the ward, and it took nine minutes, and the woman who took it has never once said so and will never say so and has told nobody in this city, and he has never known what she took and never asked and is not going to ask.

It was worse. That is the whole of what he can say about it and he is not going to say it to anybody.

She got him off the cot at about twenty past three and he sat on the edge of it with his boots on the boards and his hands on his knees and she washed her hands in the basin and dried them on the cloth and folded it back over the basin.

She did not say anything kind. There was nothing kind available and she is a woman who does not use what is not there to make a room warmer.

“The sheets are squared,” she said, “and the bundle is late twice a quarter because the man who carries it up has to sign on a counter, and that is going to happen on the thirtieth of December as well, and somebody who comes off a night shift is going to stand in a corridor and read the whole of it, including the hole.”

“You have read it.”

“I read it on the twenty-third at about eleven and I did not sleep after it and I have not slept properly since and that is my business and not yours,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “What is your business is that you will do those three floors tonight at the speed you can manage and you will not carry anything up them, and I want you to hear the sentence I am about to say and I am going to say it once.”

“Say it.”

“You will be slower than you were in the spring for the rest of your working life,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and you will not be able to give that back, and there is not one person in this city who can put it back, and I have looked, and I have looked properly, and looking is the whole of what I am for.”

## Four Hundred Days

He went down the four flights at about half past four and the bucket was light and he could have carried two.

He got out into the street and the light had gone by about four and the month had turned cold in the way it turns in four days, and he stood at the kerb for about two minutes and let the cold find the places the fever had been.

Four hundred days, on a board in a drawer in a ward, in eleven columns with a heavy line at the foot of each.

Three floors of washrooms, four nights a week, a bin room at eleven and not at half past, a stair at one in the morning, a woman on a landing with a bundle of returns under her arm, and a page going out at four on the thirtieth of every month of the year.

One morning in the ninth month of the year on a hill at four in the afternoon in front of a yard that held more people than anybody standing in it could count, with a figure on a board that was not what they thought it was.

Two floors of stairs on the fourth of March at ten past ten in the morning, alone, and nobody at the top of them.

That is what it bought and it is a real list and every item on it was worth more than the thing it was made of.

## The Foot Of The Wall

At about half past five he walked the length of the city and stood at the foot of the ranks wall.

The corner was empty. The board by the kerb was not there and the month’s form was not up yet, and about nine people went past in the time it took him to get his coat open.

Four inches of dressed stone at the foot of the lowest course, and the paint has gone over them every month for six years and does not take on them. A woman cleans those four inches every morning with a broom with a short handle, and in six years she has said one sentence about them to one person and she has not said another.

He stood about nine feet off and looked at them, and the cold came through the coat and he could feel it in the four inches as if it were his.

And he understood the whole of what was coming in the spring in about nine seconds, standing in the cold, on the last working day of a month, with nothing in him that would carry him up a hill.

The render comes off. The wall is taken down to the course. And the bottom course of that wall is dressed stone and it does not come off, because a man making good a wall takes the render off a wall and puts new render on a wall and has never in the history of this city been asked about the stone at the foot of one.

The four inches would still be there.

He had carried a form in his coat since the twenty-first of February and had done nothing about it for two hundred and seventy-nine days, and he had spent the whole of what he had, and there were four inches of stone at the foot of a wall that were going to be the only part of it left.

*Two thousand and ninety-nine days. Less two thousand and ninety-two, the twenty-third of November, is seven. A month is thirty days and runs out at the thirtieth, and there is nothing to borrow forward off the end of it.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is sixty days, which is two months exactly, and the twenty-third sheet went out at four on that morning and the twenty-fourth goes out at four in thirty days, on the thirtieth of December, which is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine.*

*Less the twenty-first of February, one thousand eight hundred and twenty, is two hundred and seventy-nine days, which is nine months with nine days after them, and a form that says a wall comes down in the spring for making good has been in the coat of the man standing at this corner for all of them. The two ruled boxes at the foot of it have been empty for every month since the eighth of October of an earlier year and the one on the left is for the figure of the thing at the address, and the address is this office, and the thing at the address of this office is a page and not a wall, and it is going to be empty again on the thirtieth of December.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and eighty-one days, which is twelve months with twenty-one days after them. It is the fourth of four sentences at the foot of a page. On the ninth of November of this year about nine hundred people took three lines off a board in about nine hours and the man who wrote all four of them heard about it at half past four the following week from a woman with a rag in her hand.*

# The Landlord's Man Is Right

Seven days had gone since he had stood at a table in a division building and been told the number of people who had come to a counter and been entered at a counter three streets away. One thousand nine hundred and ninety-four less one thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven is seven, and fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.

He went down Cordwain at about eleven in the morning on the fifteenth of August because a hearing had been on four days ago and had gone against the person who went to it, and the man who runs the desk under a window had a person standing about nine feet off where you stand.

“You have got another one,” said the man at the desk.

“I have not got anybody.”

“You have not got anybody and there has been a man in that passage four mornings this week and this morning is the fourth,” said the man at the desk, and turned a page over, and did not look up, which is what he does.

## The Man In The Passage

He was about fifty and he had a satchel and a coat that had been good. He had been standing in a passage four feet wide for about four days in the way a person stands who has understood that he is not to go in.

“I want to say something and I have had it ready since the eleventh,” he said, before Lin Hao had said anything at all. “And I want to say all of it, because I am right about all of it, and you are going to find that harder to argue with than if I were half right.”

“Say it.”

“I work for a man who has eleven rooms in two streets off Lamplit Row and a shop under one of them, and he has been putting demands up for six years, and I have carried every one of them.”

“And the hearing.”

“The hearing was on the eleventh at about eleven in the morning and it lasted about forty minutes,” said the landlord’s man. “The first of the three lines was proved in about nine minutes and nobody in the room said a word against it. The second was proved in about four. The third one is the one that put us out.”

“Third one.”

“Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough,” said the landlord’s man. “And the woman in number four cannot describe him. She stood in that room for about forty minutes and she described a coat and a height and a hand and she said she did not know what else, and he said that was the end of it, and that it was the end of it, and he was not being cruel about it.”

“He was right.”

“He was right,” said the landlord’s man. “Every fact I have said to you this morning is one you could get up and take back, and I have been right about all of them for six years.”

## The Thing He Came To Ask For

“I want the fourth line put back on a board.”

He said it in the middle of a passage four feet wide, out loud, to a man at a desk and to about nine people going past, and he did not lower his voice.

“Why.”

“Because it is the only thing in this city a landlord is allowed to put in front of a magistrate that cannot be answered,” said the landlord’s man. “And because I have read the foot of your page four times and I did not understand it in June and I understand it now, and what I understand is that it is the reason we lost on the eleventh, and you put it there, and you knew what it was when you did it.”

“I did know what it was.”

“You did know what it was and you said so out loud in a yard of about eleven thousand people and about nine hundred of them read it off a stone at the same time as they heard you,” said the landlord’s man. “And I am not a fool and I have not come down here to argue with you about a sentence I have not read. I have come to ask you to put one line back on a board.”

“No.”

“You said that to a woman in this city sixteen days ago.”

“I did.”

“And you will say it to me in the same four seconds, because it is the same answer, and I have been wrong about the flat before and I am not going to be wrong about you as well.”

## Why Not, The Second Time

He said it flatly and at the same speed he says everything, because he had said it sixteen days earlier and not one day of the reason had changed in the interval.

A rule with a test in it is a rule a person can break. A rule without a test in it is a rule that can only be obeyed or ignored.

On the seventh of July, about nine hundred people worked that out in about nine minutes, at the foot of a board, with a rag and a bucket, and they were right, and he had been standing nine feet off while they worked it out.

“That is the whole of the reason,” he said. “I wrote a rule that could not be tried, and about nine hundred people found out in nine minutes that a rule like that is worth nothing to anybody who wants to use it, and they were correct, and there is not one thing in this city that can put it back on a stone.”

“Your rule is worth a great deal to me.”

“It is worth a great deal to you and it is worth nothing to her, and it is worth nothing to about ten thousand people standing under a board who cannot read it,” said Lin Hao. “I have been told that by about four hundred people in a yard in three years and I did not believe one of them until the seventh of July.”

## What He Was Carrying

The landlord’s man took the satchel off his shoulder and opened it and took out a bundle of about eleven papers and squared them against his own leg.

“Now I am going to say the other thing,” he said, “and it is the one that is going to cost you more than the first one, and I am going to say it because you have been standing in a passage four feet wide listening to me since about eleven o’clock and you have not once tried to make it shorter.”

“Say it.”

“On the second of September a man is going to be held to pay about four months of a shop rent he has not paid in about four months,” said the landlord’s man. “And the figure my employer put in that lease is off a slate. Off a wall in a lane. Nine figures a morning, a grain a head, and a man in a delivery coat.”

“I know that lane.”

“You know that lane and you have been down it three times in six weeks and you have not once said a word to the man at the wall end,” said the landlord’s man. “And that is not my business. Here is my business.”

He has been reading every figure in twelve books out loud at that wall end since the thirteenth of July, at ten to six in the morning, to anybody standing there, and about forty people have been standing there. He says the reason in plain words in the margin against every figure he took on a hunch, and he says the day and the weather with it.

“He says it at a grain a head for the figures and the reading is free.”

“The reading is free,” said the landlord’s man. “And that is the problem, and I have worked it out in about four days and I have not slept two nights and I am going to tell you what it is, because you are the only person in this city that I am allowed to tell.”

## The Price Of A Figure

“If there is a figure in that lane that anybody can stand in front of and check, then my employer has a lease with a figure in it that can be checked,” said the landlord’s man. “And about nine demands in this city have a figure off that lane in them and one of the nine is mine.”

He did not know where it came from until a woman at a stall told him on the ninth of April. He has not been to the wall end once. He has been afraid of what he would be told.

“You have been afraid of what you would be told.”

“I have been afraid that the man at the wall end would read the margin of a figure I have copied into a lease for a shop and explain to about forty people in the cold that he took it on a hunch because he could not think what a man would want to hear in the cold at ten to six in the morning,” said the landlord’s man. “And he will. And he will be right. And about forty people will be standing there when he says it.”

He squared the bundle again.

“That is not your fault either,” he said. “I want that said before I say anything else. Your office printed, in its own hand, with a name under it, that a number written down anywhere in this city may be one somebody put there on purpose, and that is true, and it has been true for three years, and I have never once wanted it to be false, and it is the reason I cannot defend my own lease.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “It is the reason you cannot defend a lease. It is not the reason the woman lost on the eleventh.”

“It is the only reason there is,” said the landlord’s man. “The first three lines went through a door in nine minutes because they could be checked and they were checked. The fourth line is the only thing in that demand that could not be checked, and it has gone off a board, and so there is nothing left in that demand that a person cannot answer, and that is not justice. That is the opposite of justice. And you wrote it.”

## The Man At The Desk

“Go and get it,” said Lin Hao.

“That is what I intend.”

“Go on the seventeenth. He is at that wall end at ten to six, and again at twenty past seven, and again at half past eight, and it is every morning now, and he wants nothing for it.”

“And then.”

“And then take it to a counter with this office’s address at the head of it.”

“There is no room at the head of a demand,” said the landlord’s man. “There is a street and a figure and a date and a room number and that is all a demand has got in it, and the room number is my employer’s shop and not a room in a building with no door onto a street. A man in a delivery coat told me that in a lane in April and I did not believe him and I have believed him since the eleventh.”

“That is correct.”

“I know that it is correct,” said the landlord’s man. “I have had every fact I say in this room right for six years and I am going to be right about this as well, and here is the last of it, and then I will stand in this passage for another four days and go home.”

“Say it.”

“When I have a figure that can be checked, it will be my employer’s figure and not hers,” said the landlord’s man. “And the case that failed on the eleventh is the one where the person who came could not be described, and I am going to lose it, and I have known since about the fourth minute of this morning that I am going to lose it, and I came down here anyway to ask a man to put a line back on a board.”

Ashby had come out into the passage with his sheet of paper in his hand and he was not on his way back to the room.

“You are going to be in that room,” he said.

“I am.”

“Then I am going to say what that looks like, because I have been the thing people look at in nine rooms in this city and I know the price of it to the grain.” He turned the sheet over. “It is worth nothing. And in nine years there will be no way on earth to tell whether the fifteenth of September was about a year and a bit of arrears on a shop over a row of rooms, or about a man who wrote four lines.”

“And that is the same thing that happened on the seventh of July.”

“It is exactly the same thing, and about nine hundred people decided that one themselves in about nine minutes with a rag and a bucket, and they were not being unfair to anybody.” Ashby went back in and shut the door.

Nobody in the passage said anything.

“You have not said no to me properly,” said the landlord’s man. “You said no the way you said it to a woman who had walked across this city, and that was a real no, and I have had it and I have not argued with it and I am not going to.”

He would like it understood that he came here to be refused and has been. It is the fourth time in six years that anybody has wanted one thing off this man, and it is the first time that one of the four was on the side of the person the rule was written for.

## The Fifth Person This Month

At the end of the passage the man at the desk turned a page over.

“Fifth one this month,” he said.

“Fifth what.”

“Fifth person who has stood about nine feet off where you are standing and wanted something off that desk that the desk does not hand out,” said the man at the desk. “There were two in the first week of July and one in the middle of August and one in the middle of September and this one, and four of the five came because of something you wrote and the fifth came because of something you took off a stone.”

He did not look up.

“I have run this desk for about nine years and taken about a hundred people a day past that page and I have never once asked one of them what it was for, and I am not going to start now. But I will tell you what I have noticed, because not one of them has ever asked me, and I have got about thirty names in this ledger and they are the only thirty I have got.”

“What have you noticed.”

“That every person who has come down here since the seventh of July has wanted the same object,” said the man at the desk, “and not one of them has asked me for it.”

*One thousand nine hundred and ninety-four days. Less one thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven, the eighth of August, is seven. Fifteen less eight is seven by the month count.*

*Less the eleventh of August, one thousand nine hundred and ninety, is four days, and a hearing sat on that morning at about eleven o’clock and the first three lines were proved in about thirteen minutes and the third one put a landlord out of a room, and he was right.*

*Less the seventh of July, day one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is thirty-eight days, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth of four lines off a board with a rag and a bucket over about nine minutes, and the man who wrote it stood nine feet off and said nothing, and neither of the two men standing in a passage off Cordwain this morning was there on that morning.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is forty-five days, and that is the next day on which a page goes out of this city at four, and about nine people a week will get to the bottom of it.*

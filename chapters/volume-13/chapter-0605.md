# She Reads The Foot Of It

Six days had gone since the finding came back off a counter. One thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine less one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

He came down to the foot of the ranks wall at about ten in the morning on the thirtieth of January, and a month in this city is thirty days long and has no thirty-first, so that morning was the last one a form could go up on before February. It goes up before the light goes or it waits about nine days for rain.

He wanted a whole morning in which nothing happened to him. That was his want and it was a new one and it did not last eleven hours.

The paint at the north end was about four weeks old and had gone over the two words again, and they were there. They are always there. The paint goes over the stone above the last tile and does not take them, and has not taken them once in six years, and the whole of why they are still there is that a coat of paint will not hold on four inches of stone that a woman cleans every morning with a short-handled broom.

## Two Boxes

The boy with the paste brush had the month’s form on a board by the kerb waiting to go up.

There were two ruled boxes at the foot of it. The left one is for the figure of the thing at the address and the right one is for the standing of it.

They have both stood empty since the morning of the eighth of October, when a clerk was handed a sheet and a form and told nothing, and typed two words into one of them because there was a box in front of her and two words to go in it, and a boy with a paste brush painted whatever was in it.

He looked at the two boxes for about a minute and a half.

“They have never been in them,” said the boy. “Not once. Not in any month anybody has let me see.”

“They have not.”

“I asked once,” the boy said. “About two years back. And I was told that there was a form for asking what a box is for and there is not.”

“There is not.”

“So it is a blank,” said the boy, “and I paint the blank.”

He put the form up at about a quarter past ten with a paste brush and his own weight against the board, and it went up level, and the two empty squares sat at the foot of it in fresh paint where about two hundred people a day stop and read.

About two hundred a day stopped over the morning. About two of them got to the bottom of it.

There were about nine thousand people under that wall that morning, because it is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under it, and that figure is what a wall does to a street when the street is going somewhere and it is not a measurement and nobody has ever been able to turn it into one.

He stood at nine feet and watched a man read the top half and a woman read the ranks and go, and a boy of about ten read all of it standing on his toes and then say to his father, *it does not say what the two words are*, and his father said *no*, and they went on.

## At The Foot Of It And Three On The Rota

He went up to the branch and worked the floors, and at eleven at night the corridor had three women on it and a rota page on a nail with four names on it, which is the size of a folded hand and has carried four names on it since a man at rank one asked to be asked about his hours in front of a witness.

Petra Ilyin was at the counter with her book open and her pencil still. Isolde Marchetti was at the far end of the corridor reading at the counter that faces the wall, and she has never once said what she is reading and is not going to be asked.

Nadia Ferrous came out of the washroom stair with her sleeves down and a bundle of sheets under her arm and stopped, because the copy of the standing sheet was lying face up on the counter by the nail where anybody coming off a night shift reads it first, because the thirtieth is the sheet anybody reads first.

She read the head out. Eight hundred and fifty-two. She read it twice, at the same speed.

Then she stopped.

She had got as far as the first of the two sentences he had added in November, and she put her finger on it and stood there, and he came up the corridor with the bucket and she looked up at him and did not go on.

“Read the other one,” she said.

“There are not any others after that one.”

“There is one after that one,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and I have read this every quarter for two years and I have never once got to the bottom of it, and this morning I got to the bottom of it because the bundle is late.”

She read it out.

*Unrecorded — carried by: where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything.*

“Again,” she said.

“There is no reason to—”

“Read it again,” said Nadia Ferrous, “because I have been on nights at that branch since the eleventh month of the third year and I go down a lift shaft four nights a week and I gave about nine hundred strangers four minutes each in the cold and I could not tell you one of the days, and that sentence is about a person who cannot tell you the day, and I want to hear it twice before I say anything.”

He told her. He had told her before, in a kitchen off Ferrier Street, with nine women in it, and she had not forgotten it, and neither had he.

“That is what it means,” he said.

“No,” said Nadia Ferrous. “That is what it says. What it means is that a person comes in with something and you write down that you have nothing, and then for about nine hundred of us, in about four years, somebody comes along and points at the page and says — look, it is written there that we have nothing. That is what it means. It has taken me two years and a late bundle.”

She put her finger on the sentence and left it there.

“And it is true,” she said. “That is the part I have got ahold of and I have been arguing with myself about it since about eleven o’clock. It is true, and it is not a comfort, and I have not been able to make it into either one or the other.”

“I want you to know that I have thought about it all night and I have decided not to say anything about it, because you did not ask me and because I do not think you could answer it.”

“You are right about it,” Lin Hao said. “That is the whole of it. It is true and it is not a comfort.”

“Then it is the only sentence in this city that is both,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and about nine hundred people a day walk past it and about four in every thousand get to it, and every one of the four is going to be a person like me, and that is not a small thing and it is not a large one.”

Petra Ilyin had not turned round. Her book was open and her pencil was still where it had been and she had written nothing, and after a while she said, without looking up, “I have understood that sentence since about the seventh month of the second year and I have never said so to either of you and I am not going to now.”

Isolde Marchetti said nothing at all from the far end of the corridor, which is what she does.

## What He Wanted

He stood at the counter with the bucket at his feet and found that he had no argument in him at all.

That had not happened to him in a corridor in about four years. He had come down off that wall at about ten with one thing in him and it was not an argument, and what it was, and what it stayed being for the rest of the night, was this.

He wanted the yard to have been wrong.

About eleven thousand people stand in a yard off the Vestry on an ordinary day and about nine hundred of them can read all four lines on a shutter at the far end of it. The other ten thousand cannot read any of them. And under the board there is a column of about a hundred names, put up by somebody on a wall in January of an earlier year, and not one of those names will ever be taken by any of the four.

He wanted that to be wrong. He wanted the four lines to be doing what he wrote them for. He wanted about four hundred and nineteen people to be owed a thing that somebody could put in front of a magistrate on their behalf, and for a hundred and ninety days of nothing to have bought something.

It was wrong. It had been wrong for years. And the only reason he knew it was wrong with a new clarity, at eleven at night on the last working day of a month, with a woman who works nights reading his own sentence out loud twice — the only reason was that a sentence on the same page, in the same hand, under the same two words, says that a figure in this city may be a figure somebody made up on purpose.

You cannot have a rule that nobody can check and stand next to a notice that figures get invented, and expect both of them to keep working.

He had wanted to be asked, and he had been asked, and the answer was a sentence he had made sure was true, and a woman with a bundle of late sheets had just told him that the sentence is the only one in this city that is both true and no comfort at the same time.

Nadia Ferrous went back down the stair with her bundle. Petra Ilyin turned a page. Isolde Marchetti read on at the far end of the corridor.

He took the bucket and went up to the third floor, and his chest hurt the way it has hurt since the spring of the third year, and the holding that Tessa Okonkwo put in him was still in him and still unspent, and it was the only thing he owned that could not be written down, and he did not spend it.

*The thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine days. Less one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three, the twenty-fourth, is six. Thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is twenty-six. Less the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is thirty, and thirty is the whole of the month and the thirtieth is the last working day of it.*

*The two boxes at the foot of the January form have been empty since the eighth of October of year one, which is day two hundred and forty-seven, and one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine less two hundred and forty-seven is one thousand five hundred and fifty-two days.*

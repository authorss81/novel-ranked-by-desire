# Four Names On A Folded Page

Seven days had gone since a woman in a ward had opened a drawer and shown him a ruled board with eleven columns on it and eleven marks going down the one with his plate against the head of it. Two thousand and sixty-nine was the thirtieth of October and the seventh of November is day two thousand and seventy-six, and two thousand and seventy-six less two thousand and sixty-nine is seven, and seven days less thirty days is seven.

At eleven at night on the seventh of November the corridor off the branch had three women on it and a rota page on a nail.

The page was about the size of a folded hand. Four names on it, and the same four for six years, and the fifth line has never been filled, because a page about hours is a page about hours and not about anybody, and the only reason anybody ever read it out is that a man at rank one stood in front of a witness once and asked to be asked about when he was here.

Petra Ilyin was at the counter with her book open and her pencil still in her hand, doing the thing she does between one and two, which is to rule a column for the morning’s sheets before she knows what is in them.

Isolde Marchetti was at the far end of the corridor, facing the wall, at the one counter nobody in this building looks at, and she has never once said what she is reading there and is not going to be asked.

Nadia Ferrous came down off the washroom stair with a bundle of sheets under her arm and stopped in the middle of the corridor with her weight on one foot, because a copy of that quarter’s page was lying open-side-up on the counter by the nail where it had been left.

## Eight Days Of A Face-Up Page

The copy goes out on the last working day of every month and comes back the next morning and goes on the counter by the nail face up, because that is where a person coming off a night shift reads it first.

It had been face up since the thirtieth of October. Eight days.

“The bundle was late on the thirtieth,” said Nadia Ferrous. “You know it was, because you were in the room. It came in about an hour after it should have and the man who carries it up had to sign for it on the counter because the room was shut, and he put it down on the counter to sign and then he went and did something else, and by the time somebody came back for it the corridor had emptied.”

“Turned over or not turned over.”

“Not turned over,” said Nadia Ferrous. “It has not been turned over and it has not been taken down and not one word has been said about it, and it is still face up this morning and I have read it every night since, which is eight nights, and I would like somebody to take it away and put it in a drawer.”

“You could take it down yourself.”

“I could take it down myself and I am not going to,” she said. “Do you know why I am not going to?”

She waited for him to ask.

“Because the first morning I read it, it was the thirtieth, and the bundle was late, and I read it to the bottom because I had the whole of the counter to myself for about nine minutes and nobody stood behind me.”

“And if I put it face down now then the next person who comes off that stair has to wait for somebody to turn it over, and what she will do is not read it, and I am not going to be the reason that happens.”

Petra Ilyin did not look up from her column.

“She is right,” said Petra Ilyin. “I have been on the counter eleven nights this month and I have read it six of them and I am not going to say a word about why the other five.”

“Why the other five.”

“Because on the other five I have come on at about half ten and there is a queue at the washroom stair and there is a page face up,” said Petra Ilyin, “and a page face up at half ten is a thing that is about to be somebody’s whole evening and I am not going to be standing in the way of it. That is not manners. That is a rota.”

## The Head Out Loud

Nadia Ferrous put the bundle down on the counter and squared it and did not go to the far end.

“Eight hundred and fifty-two,” she said.

Nobody said anything.

“It has been on that page for eight quarters,” said Nadia Ferrous. “I have known that since I was twenty and I have never once had a reason to say it out loud.”

“I am saying it out loud because there are two men who come up this stair on different nights of the month and have never once said it to each other, and one of them said it to me four nights ago and the other has not been in since, and I am saying it to the corridor so that he hears it.”

“That is a good reason,” said Lin Hao.

“It is not a good reason, it is a reason,” said Nadia Ferrous. “I would like you to be angry about that page. I have been putting that in front of you every month for two years and you have never once been angry about it and I have started to think you have not noticed it is there.”

“It has been there.”

“It has been there every quarter since before I came and there is a figure at the top of it that has not moved and about four hundred and nineteen lines under it that belong to one woman and two lines in a column that nobody reads,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and a sentence at the bottom of it in your hand with Emil Brack’s name under it, and you walk past it to the bin room. Every month. For two years.”

“Ten times.”

“Ten times,” she said, “and I have never once been able to work out whether that is because you have decided the page is not yours or because you have decided it is so yours that you cannot stand near it, and the second of those is worse, and you have never told me which.”

## Isolde At The Counter That Faces The Wall

He went down the corridor to the far end and put his hand on the rail beside her and did not ask, and waited.

Isolde Marchetti read the line she was on to the end of it before she answered him, which is a thing she does with a book and which she has never once done with a person.

“You are going to ask me what I read at this counter,” she said.

“I am.”

“I have been at this counter about nine hundred nights in six years,” said Isolde Marchetti, “and I read one thing, and it is a sheet of paper, and I have never told anybody, and I have not been asked in six years, which is not a complaint, it is a rota.”

She turned the sheet about ninety degrees so the foot of it was nearest him.

“The line at the very bottom is the one I read,” she said. “The other three at the foot of it are instructions about how the sheet is to be used and I have read those about four times each in six years and I could say them now. The one underneath them is not an instruction. It was put there by a man for no reason anybody has ever given me, and it has a name at the end of it that is not his.”

“Your reason for reading it.”

“I sing at the Bellhouse four nights a week,” said Isolde Marchetti. “I do not read music off anything any more than you carry a bucket off a cart. It goes in.”

She did not look up. “And when a page has been out four times a year for as long as I have been in this corridor, it gets into the room whether anybody is standing at it or not, and the foot of that page is the only piece of writing I have ever had in my head that is worth the tune.”

She turned it back.

“It is a bad tune,” she said. “It has got four sentences in it and three of them are about how to use a piece of paper and the fourth one is a man admitting something about a figure.”

“It is the only true thing I have ever heard said about anything in this city, and it is on the same page, under the same two words, as the sentence about where the Register has nothing. Both of them are true. They are printed one above the other in the same hand, and about four in every thousand people in this city have ever got to the bottom of it.”

She picked up her book.

“I have said one thing about that page in six years,” she said, “and I said it to you and not to the corridor, and I am not going to say it again inside a month.”

## Half Past One

Ines Duarte came down the corridor at about half past one with a bundle of returns under her arm and her cuffs done, and she had been auditing this building four nights in seven since the first of October of the third year.

She put the bundle down on the clear end of the counter and did not look at the page face up by the nail, because she had been looking at it every working night for three years and she knew where it was.

Nadia Ferrous spoke to her without turning round.

“The page is face up for the ninth night and there is going to be a tenth, and I am not going to be the one who turns it over, and I have been waiting eleven days to ask somebody in this building a question about it and I have been waiting for you because you are the only person who reads all the way down.”

“What question.”

“Is it still true,” said Nadia Ferrous.

The corridor went quiet in a way that corridors do not.

She said, “The one at the bottom. Where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything.”

She turned round and spoke to the counter. “My father is on a housing roll and there is a division in the fourth district that has had his file twice this year, and both times they told him he had given nothing, and he did give nothing, and it is true.”

“He is seventy-one and he thinks that sentence means somebody somewhere has got a piece of paper with his name on it. I have never been able to explain to him what it means and I have read it to him and he has listened to it, and it does not help him, because there is nobody he can go and be angry at.”

Ines Duarte did not answer her.

“You are the only person entitled to answer her,” said Lin Hao.

“I am the only person entitled to read it aloud to the person it was written about,” said Ines Duarte, “and that is a different thing and it is the whole of the difference and I am not going to say the other thing in a corridor with three people on it at half past one in the morning, because a sentence said in a corridor at half past one is a sentence a corridor has heard, and a corridor has got about four names on it.”

She put her hand on the page face up by the nail and squared it to the edge of the counter.

“Nadia Ferrous,” she said. “I am not going to tell you it is still true. I am going to read the whole of it, in order, from the figure at the head to the fourth sentence at the foot, out loud, to one person, in a room with a shut door in it.”

She put her hand on the sheet by the nail. “I am going to do that because there is exactly one person in Ostara who is entitled to have it read to him, and he is not going to be told it by a corridor.”

“Will you tell me when.”

“I will not,” said Ines Duarte. “I will do it on a night I pick and I will not warn you, and the reason I will not warn you is that if I warn you then the two people who matter will prepare for it, and I have watched one of them prepare for things in this city for four years and I am not doing it for him as well.”

She picked the bundle up and went down the corridor.

At about two she was still in the room with the returns and the lamp, and she did not stop to be spoken to while a page was open, and she had never once in three years done anything else.

## The Fifteenth Of November

He finished the bin room and the second floor and the first at about four, and came down to the counter by the nail, and the page was face up.

“Eight hundred and fifty-two,” said Nadia Ferrous, without looking up from the washroom stair.

He said it.

“Again,” she said.

He said it again, at the same speed, with nothing improved, because that is the only way he has ever read anything, and a woman at the far end of the corridor put her book down for about four seconds and then picked it up.

“You did that for me,” said Isolde Marchetti, still not turning round.

“I did it because you asked twice.”

“I asked once,” she said. “I have been sitting at that counter about nine hundred nights reading a sentence nobody says to me, and you are the first person in six years who has said one out loud in this corridor, and I am telling you that it is not nothing and you are going to say that it is not nothing, and then we are both going to go back to work.”

“It is not nothing.”

“Right,” said Isolde Marchetti, and turned a page.

Then he went out into the street with the bucket and it was the cold of the eleventh month and it had begun to rain for the first time since about the middle of the ninth, and it was very fine and it went straight through, and he stood under nothing at all for about a minute and a half and let it.

She had asked him for one thing and he had not been able to give it to her, and he was going to be able to give it to her in eight days, and the reason he could be sure of that was a woman carrying a bundle down a corridor who had said she would not warn him.

*Two thousand and seventy-six days. Less two thousand and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of October, is seven, and thirty days run out at the thirtieth, so a day seven after it is the seventh of the next month.*

*Less two thousand and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is thirty-seven days, which is a month with seven days after them. A sheet went out at four on that morning and it has not moved since, and the sheet on the counter by the nail has been face up for eight nights because the bundle came up an hour late and the man who carries it had to sign for it and put it down on the counter to do it.*

*Less one thousand eight hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of February, is two hundred and fifty-six days, which is eight months with sixteen days after them, and a form that says a wall comes down in the spring for making good has been in the coat of the man standing at this counter for all of them and he has done one thing about it, which is to have carried it.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and fifty-eight days, which is eleven months with twenty-eight days after them. It is the fourth of four sentences at the foot of a page and it goes out at four on the thirtieth and a woman who works nights has read it every night for eight nights out of the nine that this office has been printing it in this building.*

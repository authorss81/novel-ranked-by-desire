# An Entry Open Since September

Eight days had gone since a man at a desk under a window told him that a woman two flights up had asked whether anybody in this city knew what a figure is. One thousand eight hundred and seventy-four less one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six is eight, and fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.

He went up the stair off a street off Cordwain at about half past two in the afternoon on the fifteenth of April, and it is not Cordwain and it is not the passage and the stair is not the one he went up on the fourth of March, and he was aware of all three of those things the entire way up.

He wanted to know whether a page can shut a book entry, and he had wanted to know it since about eleven o’clock the night before, and it is the kind of question a man asks at two in the afternoon because he cannot ask it at eleven at night in a building with a drawer open.

## A Door That Shuts At Four

Solveig Rask opened the door of the room off the landing at about half past two and took in the stair behind him and the two hands he had come up it empty and said nothing for about three seconds.

“Twenty-six years in that trade and at that table and in this room,” she said. “You may have that one without asking, because you were going to ask for it inside the first minute and then spend the afternoon deciding whether you were allowed.”

“Nobody comes up that stair about that.”

“You came up here in February wanting something to hold four lines, and I gave you the only answer anybody in this trade can give, and it has taken you nine weeks to come back and ask the next part of it,” said Solveig Rask,

“and there is no stool in this room and there has never been one, and if you are going to stand you will stand by the wall and you will be here until I tell you the door is shutting.”

She went in and left the door open behind her and he stood by the wall.

## The Fifth Calling

“It was called at the fifth valuation on the second of April,” she said. “That is nine days ago and I did not write to anybody and I am not writing to you.”

“Two letters at the foot of it and no name, and the guild carries every debt it cannot close at a rating of seven, and a rating is not a sum of money and a rating is not a figure anybody can be given, and it has been called five times and answered five times not at all.”

“You told me in February that an entry closes two ways. Paid, or proved.”

“Paid, or proved,” said Solveig Rask. “That has not changed and it will not change before I am dead. What has changed is that the office you keep has printed, in its own hand, in the last two quarters, a sentence that says figures in this city may be made up on purpose, and a sentence that says where a Register has nothing it has not been given anything.”

“And there are about nine hundred people in this city who can read both of those and who have understood what they say together, and one of them is me and I have understood it since the seventh of February.”

“Which one did you understand.”

“Both,” said Solveig Rask. “And it does not take a person who can read to understand them. It takes about nine seconds and a floor of a counting house.”

## What A Page Can Do To A Book

“Can a page shut an entry,” said Lin Hao.

Solveig Rask stopped moving about a book on the table, which she had been moving in a way that meant nothing at all, and that was the first thing in twenty-six years she had done in this room that he had not seen a reason for.

“Why would you want to know that.”

“Because there is a finding in a file at a counter off a third court and a page at the back of it,” he said, “and a page at the back of a finding is a page that a magistrate turns over, and if what a page does in a file is also what a page does in a real book then I have spent six years building an instrument that does not stop at the edge of a court.”

“No,” said Solveig Rask.

“No.”

“A page cannot shut an entry and it cannot pay one and it cannot prove one, and you know all three of those and you have come up a stair to ask me the fourth thing, which is worse than the other three, and it is this.” She put the book down.

“A page can be read into an entry. That is all it has to do. It has to be read into it by a person, and it does not have to be a stupid person, and the person does not have to mean any harm by it.”

“Go on.”

“An entry is carried at seven because the thing it describes has not been proved and has not been paid. Put a line in beside it that says nothing was ever given to anybody about the thing it describes, and the entry no longer describes something that is owed to a person.”

“It describes something that was never given to anybody.” Solveig Rask said it the way she says everything, which is once and at the same speed. “It becomes an entry about an absence. And an entry about an absence is not carried. It is written off.”

The room had a low ceiling and the floor of a sales floor was over it and somebody’s boots went across the boards directly overhead at a quarter to three in the afternoon.

“Say that again,” said Lin Hao.

“It is written off. Not paid. Written off, which is a different thing, and the difference between the two is the only thing anybody pays money for in this trade, and a debt that has been written off is a debt that never happened as far as every book in this building is concerned, and there is no appeal out of it, because you cannot appeal a thing that is not there.”

## The Second Book

“I am not going to argue with you about it,” said Solveig Rask. “I am going to show you, because you have come up a stair with nothing in your hands and I would rather you left with a thing you have seen than with a thing you have been told.”

She took a second book down off a shelf inside that room, which he had not seen in February, and it was not chained, and it was about half the size of the other one.

“This is not the guild’s and it is not in the guild’s room and it is not sealed with anything, and I have kept it myself for nine years for reasons that are my own and are not yours.” She opened it on the table beside the other one and turned it round so that it faced him.

“An entry that was written off in the fourth year. I put it in here in the fifth so that I would stop thinking about it, and I have never shown it to anybody, and I am showing it to you because of what you have printed, and I would like that written down somewhere that I showed you.”

He looked at it.

It was four lines long and it had a rating against it and at the foot of it in a hand that was Solveig Rask’s, a line had been entered that read: *carried by: where the Register has nothing, it has not been given anything.*

“There,” said Solveig Rask. “That is what a page does to a book. I did not do it to be cruel and I did not do it because anybody asked me.”

“I did it because it is true, and it is true that the Register was given nothing about that person, and it is in the office’s own hand at the foot of a page that goes out four times a year with the name of the man who wrote one of the sentences under it, and when I read it I could not think of one way of telling the difference between a thing that has not been given to anybody and a thing that never happened.”

She shut the second book and put it back on the shelf.

“That was four years ago and I have carried it since,” she said, “and in fourteen years I have not met a second person who could see it and it has been sitting on a shelf in a room off a landing the whole time.”

## The Second Of May

“The entry you have come about is called again on the second of May,” she said. “That is seventeen days. And if nothing is put beside it, it will be written off on the second of May, and a debt of about five years that somebody has been paying for will stop being a debt, and that is what your sentence does to a real book and it will do it on the second of May whether I am in the room or not.”

“Can it be stopped.”

“It can be stopped by the carrier and only by the carrier, and the carrier is a person at an address, and the carrier has been at that address for four years and has been putting a quarter of the cost in once a quarter out of a book of her own that is not in this room and does not go through this building.”

“And she does not know that I know, and I have never told her, because she is owed nothing by me and she is carrying something she cannot show anybody, and a person who is carrying something she cannot show does not want a keeper of a chained book to come up her stair.”

Solveig Rask took the chained book in both arms the way she takes it, which is a thing she has done eleven times and which is the whole of what she has to say about an entry she cannot close.

“You are going to ask me for that address.”

“Yes.”

“No,” she said. “You are not getting it from this room, and I want you to hear why, because you will think it is caution and it is not. You have been up my stair twice and both times you have wanted an instrument, and an instrument is a thing a person uses on somebody else. You have never once in six years asked a person for a thing.”

“You asked nine divisions for a line and I know what that cost you because I know what a return is.”

“You are not going to ask her for her address and you are not going to tell her about the second of May, because if you do that then what happens to her on the second of May will be a thing that a man in a building with no street door did to her, and that is the fourth one, and there is nobody left who can be given any of them.”

She went to the door and stood in it.

“Go down the stair,” said Solveig Rask. “And if you can think of a way to stop a real book writing off an entry that does not go through a person, then you are the first man in this city who has thought of one, and I will open this room to you again inside a week.”

He went down the stair, and it is not Cordwain, and the passage under a window with a desk in it is four hundred yards away and round two corners, and he stood in the street at the bottom of it for about a minute with his hands in his coat and understood the whole of what had just happened to him in about nine seconds.

There was no page in this city between this afternoon and the second of May.

The office printed one four times a year, on the thirtieth of a month, and the last one had gone out at four on the thirtieth of March and the next one goes out at four on the thirtieth of June, and a real book in this city is written off on the second of May, and about five years of somebody’s money and somebody’s cold is going to stop being a debt in seventeen days on the strength of a sentence he made sure was true.

*One thousand eight hundred and seventy-four days. Less one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six, the seventh of April, is eight. Fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.*

*Less the fourteenth of February, one thousand eight hundred and thirteen, is sixty-one, and a keeper of a chained book told him on that morning that an entry closes two ways. Less the second of April, one thousand eight hundred and sixty-one, is thirteen, and the entry was called at its fifth valuation on that day and was not answered.*

*The second of May is day one thousand eight hundred and ninety-one, and one thousand eight hundred and ninety-one less one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four is seventeen days, and the office prints on the thirtieth of a month and there is no thirtieth between the thirtieth of March and the second of May.*

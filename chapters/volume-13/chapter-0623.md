# A Real Book And A Carrier

Eight days had gone since a woman had said a number out loud on the ground floor of a counting house and had told him she could not take it back. One thousand nine hundred and twenty-five less one thousand nine hundred and seventeen is eight, and six less twenty-eight is minus twenty-two by the month count, and a month is thirty days with no thirty-first, so six less twenty-eight is eight with twenty-two days borrowed against the thirtieth of May.

He went up four flights off Ferrier Street at about ten in the morning on the sixth of June with nothing in his coat, because he had been told to bring nothing, and the window of the room at the top went the whole way round two sides and it was not raining.

She had the fire out, because it was June. There was a table with nothing laid on it and one chair, and she did not offer him the other one and he did not ask for it.

## What Was On The Table

A book was on the table when he came in.

The binding is hers and not the house’s, and she brought it up the last flight under one arm and took her hand off it only when it was down, and then she put one hand on the cover and kept it there without appearing to know she was doing it.

“Sit down,” said Marguerite Vale. “If you stand over that table it becomes a meeting and I have had four years of meetings at that table.”

He sat down.

“Before anything else,” she said. “What I said in that counting house on the twenty-eighth of May cannot be unsaid, and you know that, and I am going to build the whole morning on top of it, so you will not spend it asking me to unsay it.”

“I was not going to ask you to unsay it.”

“You were going to ask me to put it in a book,” said Marguerite Vale. “There is no book. There is a table and a fire that is out and a window on two sides and a woman who has said one thing in a room with nine people in it and cannot take it back. That is the room and that is the whole of what a room is.”

She turned the cover over with two fingers and did not open it.

“Then say it to me again,” said Lin Hao, “because you said it to nine people and not to me.”

“I said it to you. You were the reason I said it.”

## The Arithmetic

“One entry,” said Marguerite Vale. “Carried at a rating of seven. Against you. In my own hand, in a book that is not the Vant’s, in this room, since about the second year.”

She did not open the book.

“Called at five valuations and answered five times not at all. Paid, or proved, and you can do neither, and the reason you can do neither is a sentence of yours at the foot of a page that goes out at four.”

He had not known there was an entry.

“There is no name on it,” he said.

“There is your name on it,” said Marguerite Vale. “I wrote it. I wrote it out of my own memory in about eleven seconds and I did not open anything to do it, and I have never once told anybody what it is and I told nine people in a counting house a week ago and I cannot take it back.”

“You gave the day as well,” said Lin Hao.

“I gave the day because it is in me and I am not going to be careful in my own house,” said Marguerite Vale, “and you will please stop doing that.”

“Say the rest.”

“Six hundred and eighty entries in the chained books of the Vant cannot be closed because of the fourth of four lines,” said Marguerite Vale. “Two hundred of those are carried by a person and not by a house. Eleven are carried by me. One of the eleven is yours.”

She put her hand on the table for about a second, then took it off the table and put it back on the cover, which was faster than the taking it off.

“That is the arithmetic,” she said. “It is not money. There is no grain in it and there is no sum in it. It is what your sentence does to a book in a city that runs on books, and I said it out loud on the ground floor of a counting house in front of nine people and a boy with a pen, and none of them wrote it down, and all nine of them have it.”

“Why can it not be closed.”

“Because an entry closes two ways,” said Marguerite Vale. “Paid, or proved. And what you did on a morning in the autumn of the third year is not on a page anywhere in this city, by the fourth of your own four lines, and therefore it cannot be proved, and you have no money and you have never had any and there is no office that holds any, and therefore it cannot be paid.”

“So it is carried.”

“It is carried by me,” said Marguerite Vale, “at a rating of seven, and it has been carried for four years and one month, and I have put a quarter of the cost of carrying it in once a quarter out of a book of my own that is not in that building and does not go through it, and I have never once told you, and you have never once asked the right person.”

She turned her chair about nine degrees toward the window.

“Now you may ask me the other thing.”

## What He Asked

“Why.”

“Why what.”

“Why have you been paying for it.”

Marguerite Vale looked at him for a long moment, and the room was very quiet, and out of the window on two sides there was the ordinary sound of a street in the middle of a morning.

“Because I am the only person in this city who has read the foot of your page and understood it,” she said, “and about nine hundred others have read it and misunderstood it, and about two thousand a day walk past it and get nothing.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is not an answer and it happens to be true,” said Marguerite Vale, “so I am going to give you one that is not true for about nine seconds and then the true one, and you will not be able to tell which was which, and that is the only advantage anybody in Ostara has ever had.”

She put both hands in her lap.

“I paid for it for the first time because I did not want you to have it,” she said. “A man who owes something is a man, and a man who is owed something is a creditor, and a creditor is a person the city can point at. I wanted there to be a person the city could point at.”

She took her hands out of her lap.

“That lasted about two years,” she said. “And then in the November of the fifth year you went into a room and wrote at the bottom of a page, in your own hand, that a number set down anywhere in this city may be a number somebody invented, and put your name under it. And every entry in that building that had been carried because nothing could be proved became, in about nine seconds, an entry about an absence instead.”

“And you read it.”

“I read it the same morning,” said Marguerite Vale. “About four in every thousand read your page to the bottom and I have been one of them for six years.”

She did not move her hands.

“On that afternoon I understood what had happened to about two hundred entries of other people’s, and then I went up four floors and did nothing about it for a week.”

“Why.”

“Because there was nothing to be done, and because a woman does not go to a room with two chairs and say *you have made six hundred and eighty debts cheaper*.”

She put a hand on the cover of the book.

“And then I went on paying the quarter,” she said. “Which is the part that is mine and not yours. I want you to have it exactly. You did not ask me to and you did not know and you could not have stopped me, and I kept paying after I knew what your sentence was doing, for four years, because a thing carried by one person can be watched, and because I would rather carry a thing than own a thing that somebody else is carrying.”

## The Book Was Not Opened

He put his hand out and stopped it about nine inches from the cover.

“May I.”

“No,” said Marguerite Vale.

“I did not finish it.”

“You were not going to finish it,” she said. “You were going to put your fingers on a book of a real house and ask a question with a whole body behind it.”

She took her hand off the cover and put it on the table beside the book.

“It is written in my hand because I am the person who carries what is in it. It is not chained and it is not sealed and it is in a room with a fire that is out, and there is not one person in Ostara who can put a hand on it, and there is not one form on which I could be asked to open it.”

“Then what is this morning.”

“This morning is me telling you what is in it,” said Marguerite Vale, “in a room, in order, and you may put it anywhere you like afterwards, and I cannot unstate a syllable of it, and I said that in a counting house and you may as well have it on a better morning.”

She turned the chair about nine degrees back.

“I gave the day as well, in a room, and I could not take it back,” she said, “and it turns out that is the only instrument in this city that has ever worked on me. A thing said in a room is a thing the room may carry.”

She came away from the grate.

“So I am going to say everything in this room and the room is going to carry it, and the room is four walls and a table and a man who came up four flights with nothing in his coat.”

She got up.

“Stay there,” she said. “You have been in a chair for eleven minutes and you have the face of a man sitting still to be polite.”

She put both hands on the rail of the empty grate.

“Come and help me with the fire and then take your coat off,” she said, “because I am going to be fast and I am not going to apologise for it.”

## The Middle Of The Morning

He took his coat off and hung it on a hook by the door and came back and there was nothing on the table except a book and the chair was not where she had left it.

“Say what you are looking at,” said Marguerite Vale.

“You are looking at a man whose chest does not go as deep as it should and has not since the spring of the third year, and you have been counting how deep for about four seconds.”

“Four seconds is a long time to look at a man,” said Marguerite Vale. She came round the table. “Your shoulders are up. They have been up since the stair. Put them down and put your shirt off over the back of that chair and I will not do your buttons and I have told you before that I will not do your buttons.”

He took his shirt off and she looked at his back and at his front and described neither.

“Right,” she said. “Come here and be got at. Do not be careful about it. I have spent eleven years being handled politely by men who are being pleasant to a position.”

He came to her. She took hold of him with the hand that had been on the cover, and the hand was hard in the way of somebody who has written at a table for eleven years, and she did not go gently into it and she did not slow down into it.

“Slower,” she said, about nine seconds later. “No. Back to that.”

“Tell me when you are.”

“I am going to tell you when I am and I am going to enjoy it and I am not going to be modest about any of it,” said Marguerite Vale. “I am the only person in this city who has ever heard what a column headed *carried by* means, and I have not put my hand on anybody for four years, and you are going to be extremely good at this by about eleven o’clock because you have no idea that you are doing anything.”

He put his palm where she pointed and she swore and took his wrist and pinned it under her own hip and sat down on him instead of getting up.

“There,” she said. “That. And do not stop to work out what it means.”

He did not stop to work out what it meant.

She came all of it at once from the root of it outwards and then let go, with her mouth against his collar and both hands knotted in his hair at the nape, and it went round that room and stayed inside it.

Then she laughed, once, into his throat.

“Four years,” said Marguerite Vale. “And I have had a book under my hand for every one of them.”

## Afterwards

She sat down on the edge of the chair and reached across and squared the book on the table, and did not open it, and did not let go of it.

“Do not,” she said, when his hand moved. “Not that. I told you on the stair and I am telling you again and it is not a courtesy.”

“You said you were going to tell me what was in it.”

“I have told you what was in it,” said Marguerite Vale. “One entry, a rating of seven, five valuations, my hand, and a reason that is your own sentence. That is the whole of the book. There is nothing else in it. It has been four pages long for four years.”

He sat down opposite her with his shirt on.

“Then why is it closed.”

“Because it is,” said Marguerite Vale. “Not chained. Closed. There is a difference and it is about four hundred years old and nobody in this city could explain it to you. A chained book cannot be opened. A closed book can be opened by the person whose hand is in it, and I am not opening it this morning and I am not going to open it in front of you.”

“Then what is this morning.”

“It is a woman telling you an entry you cannot pay and cannot prove, out loud, in a room, for about an hour and a half, because you asked her to, and because I could not say it in a counting house without nine people carrying it for the rest of their lives.”

She put her elbows on the table.

“And because I wanted to,” said Marguerite Vale. “That is not a small part of it. I have not wanted a particular thing in about four years and I wanted this one from the moment you sat down, and I have never once been obliged to say so, and I am saying it because the whole of this morning is me saying things I cannot unstate.”

## What He Said About It

“Who else knows.”

“Nobody,” said Marguerite Vale. “The keeper of the chained books does not and is not permitted to. The four brokers in that house would each tell you it was not them and all four would be right. The boy on the counter is fourteen.” She looked up. “And you now, and you cannot unstate it either, and you did not do anything about it, and that is the difference between us and it is the only difference there is.”

“Does it change anything.”

“It changes nothing at all,” said Marguerite Vale. “Six hundred and eighty entries cannot be closed today and they could not be closed yesterday and they will not be closed on the nineteenth of August when the sixth valuation is called. The only thing that changed in this room is that a person is now alive who knows.”

She got up and went to the window and stood with her back to him.

“Do not follow me about this,” she said. “You will want to. You have wanted somebody to tell you what a thing costs since the seventh of February and I have just told you, and it is going to be the most expensive hour of your life and it bought you nothing.”

“What does it buy.”

“It buys me,” said Marguerite Vale. “Four years of putting a quarter in once a quarter into a book of my own for a sentence I did not agree with, for a man I had met four times, and I have never told anybody and I am not going to tell the office and I am not going to tell the yard, and I have told you, and there is no form on which you can enter it and no clerk who could take it.”

## The Question At The End Of It

She came back and sat down and put her hand on the book and left it there.

“Now you may ask me one thing,” said Marguerite Vale, “and I am going to tell you before you ask it that you are about to ask me the wrong one, because you have been asking the wrong one since the fifteenth of April and you have a month of it in you.”

“What is the second column of your page.”

She stopped.

“There is a second column,” said Lin Hao. “It was ruled in the March of the third year. It was empty at seven quarter-ends and then it got one line in it in the October of the fourth year and that line is mine and it is a claim I entered against myself in my own hand, and a clerk entered it exactly as given and gave a reason.”

Marguerite Vale put her hand on the cover and left it there, which was a thing she had been doing for four years.

“Yes,” she said. “Carried by.”

“Yes.”

“How many lines are in it.”

“Two,” said Lin Hao. “It has had two lines in it since a woman came to a counter on the first of May of a year I do not name here, and I entered what she gave me and I did not ask her one question, and I have never seen her, and there is no name against that line and no figure against it and nothing anywhere in this city that would tell me who she was.”

Marguerite Vale looked at him for a long time.

“Did you ask her whether she wanted it entered.”

“No.”

“Did you ask her anything at all.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “It took about eleven seconds and she was not going to come back.”

“Then it is the only line on that page that is doing any work,” said Marguerite Vale, “and you have never once looked at it, and I am going to tell you what it is doing this afternoon and not this morning, and I am going to do it in a room with nine other people in it, because you asked a keeper the wrong question on a landing and the two of you have been getting the wrong end of this for six weeks.”

She picked the book up and put it under her arm.

“Fifth of June,” she said, “and it is not a quarter-end and I have not come down four flights for a page in five years.”

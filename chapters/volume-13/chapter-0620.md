# The Week The Yard Decides

Seven days had gone since a second demand came onto a branch counter at eleven at night with four lines on it and no address at the head of it, and it was in a drawer at the back of that counter and it was still in the drawer. One thousand nine hundred and two less one thousand eight hundred and ninety-five is seven, and thirteen less six is seven by the month count.

He was at the counter off a third court at about ten past eleven at night on the thirteenth of May with the bundle under his arm, because the finding had been taken up on the twenty-ninth of April and had come back with a minute on it and there was a copy of the minute in the bundle and he had read it four times on a branch night shift and had not been able to put it down.

He wanted somebody to ask him what he thought about it.

That was the want, and it is the oldest want he has, and it is the one the whole of the sixth year has been built out of, and it did not happen on the thirteenth of May either.

## A Field Officer With A Bundle

Runa Sato came into the corridor at about twenty past eleven, hat still on, a bundle carried in front of her rather than under, and she stopped three feet inside the door and would not come any further, which is what she does.

“The minute,” she said.

“You have read it.”

“I have read a copy of it. A clerk at a counter read it out to a man in a room with a table in it on the twenty-ninth and I have read what it says because a return goes round a district twice and I read what is in it whether I was sent to or not.” She put the bundle on the counter and did not open it.

“Four sentences. That the fourth of four lines is a statement about the absence of a page, and that the party produced nothing and cannot produce anything, and that the first three can be tried and the fourth cannot, and that the finding proceeds on the first three.”

“That is what it says.”

“Every one of those four is the clerk’s own and he is a man I have never spoken to and I am not going to speak to him,” said Runa Sato. “Say the first thing, and then I will give you the other, and the other is the reason I came up this corridor.”

“Say the first thing.”

“The first thing is that you asked for that,” she said. “You went into a room on the eleventh of March and you gave a man with no standing your name, and everything that has happened to that finding since has happened because of it, and there is not one word of the minute you could have stopped and you did not try.”

“I did not know what it would do.”

“You have known since the seventh of February what a sentence does in a room,” said Runa Sato. “Say the other thing.”

## The Other Thing

“There is a yard off the Vestry,” she said, “and about eleven thousand people stand in it on an ordinary day, and about nine hundred of them can read four lines in four-inch chalk on a shutter at the far end of it, and the rest of them cannot read any of them and have never once tried.”

“I know what the yard is.”

“No you do not,” said Runa Sato, “because what I am going to tell you is about a form. On the ninth of April the vestry of a church off the Vestry booked its hall for one week beginning on the twenty-first of June. One week. Through the twenty-eighth.”

“It is a hall with a door that two hundred people would be sick in and it has a form for it, and a rent for it, and a man who takes the rent, and the form was filled in by a woman who is a householder in that parish and who has never in her life been to the other nine counters that the last four pages went to.”

Lin Hao put the bundle down on the counter.

“Who filled it in.”

“I am not going to tell you that,” said Runa Sato. “I have said this to you before about a different person on a different stair and you did not go, and there is a reason for it that is not caution. She is not doing it for you.”

“She has not heard of the Register and she has not heard of the office and she filled in a form because the hall was free in the fourth week of June and eleven thousand people cannot be put anywhere else in a city.”

“So what.”

“So on the twenty-first of June there is going to be a week,” said Runa Sato, “and about eleven thousand people are going to be in it, and it is the first instrument any of this has produced that was not put there by anybody in this building, and you cannot put a word into it, and you cannot stop it, and you cannot even be told about it officially.”

He did not say anything for about nine seconds.

“The chaplain,” he said.

“She is not here and she is not going to be asked, and there was a time when she promised to stand on those steps once a month, say one thing, and be left unanswered, and she has kept to it for years,” said Runa Sato. “That is not mine to decide and it is not yours, and nobody who has a form will fill one in about her.”

## The Back Of A Hall

“I am going to be in that hall,” said Lin Hao.

“No,” said Runa Sato.

“That is not your—”

“You are not going to be in that hall and I have come up a corridor at twenty past eleven at night to tell you that you are not, and I am not telling you for your sake.”

She put her hand on the counter and left it there, which is a thing she does on a counter and not on a table.

“Do you know how many of the people in that yard can read?” she said.

“About nine hundred.”

“About nine hundred of about eleven thousand, and about nine hundred of those can read a minute as well as four lines, and on the twenty-first of June you would be standing at the back of a hall with about eleven thousand people in front of you and about nine hundred of them able to write down every word you say.” She turned her head about nine degrees.

“And there is nothing else in this city that can take a thing out of reach of argument except the thing you built yourself in November, and it works on a page, and it does nothing at all to a man in a room, because a man in a room is a thing a crowd can check.”

“So whatever they decide in there they will decide with me in it.”

“They will decide it with you in it and it will be a thing the yard did with the man standing at the back of the hall,” said Runa Sato, “and about four hundred of them do not believe a word of what he says and will not believe a word of what the yard decides either, and in nine years nobody will be able to tell whether that week was about eleven thousand people or about one man who could not be got at.”

She picked the bundle up again.

“I am going to be in that hall every day of that week because I have a round that goes through that parish twice a week and I have never once been asked to change it,” she said, “and I am going to write nine lines and a tenth with a box about what I see in it, and the tenth is for a reason, and a reason is not a conclusion.”

“I know what the tenth line is for.”

“Then you know that what I write in it on the twenty-eighth will be the reason I was in that hall, and it will be true, and it will not say one word about what they decided, and that is the whole of what my instrument is.”

She went a long way down the corridor with the bundle under her arm and her hat on.

## Nobody Asked Him For Anything

He stood at a counter off a third court at about half past eleven at night and worked out what had happened to him in about nine minutes, standing up, with a bucket in his hand, the way he had worked out everything for six years.

A woman had walked into a building at twenty past eleven at night and told him that about eleven thousand people in a city he has refused to go near for six years had a week, and that there was a form for it, and that he was going to be kept out of it.

And she had not asked him for anything. Not what he thought about the minute, not what he thought about a yard, not what he thought about the fourth of four lines, and not what he intended to do about any of it, and she had come up a corridor and given him the whole of the worst news he had had in about two months and taken nothing for it.

He had spent six years arranging to be the only person an office is allowed to ask, and then five years arranging to be a man nobody could find, and on the thirteenth of May a woman of rank two gave a man of rank one the one thing he has wanted for four years.

She did not ask him anything.

That was the thing, and it was not a consolation and it was not a coming, and he stood in a corridor and found that he was about eleven seconds away from crying for the first time in about two years and had a bucket in his hand and three floors of washrooms above him and nobody at all in the building.

He did not cry. He went up to the third floor with the bucket and his chest went on doing what it has done since the spring of the third year, and what Tessa Okonkwo had put in him back in November had never once been spent, and the week in the yard was thirty-eight days away.

*One thousand nine hundred and two days. Less one thousand eight hundred and ninety-five, the sixth of May, is seven. Thirteen less six is seven by the month count.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is one hundred and twenty-nine, and one hundred and twenty-nine is four months and nine days. The second demand came onto a branch counter at about eleven at night on the sixth and there was no address at the head of it, and it is in a drawer at the back of that counter and there is no instrument in this city that can serve a man at no address and a woman who carried it across the city at eleven at night found that out before anybody did.*

*The twenty-first of June is day one thousand nine hundred and forty, which is thirty-eight days from this morning, and the week runs to the twenty-eighth of June, which is day one thousand nine hundred and forty-seven, and the office prints on the thirtieth of a month and the next page goes out at four on the thirtieth of June.*

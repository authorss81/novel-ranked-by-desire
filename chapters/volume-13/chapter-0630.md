# He Is Asked To Put It Back

Seven days had gone since he read three lines out loud about four hundred times at the foot of a board in a yard, and about nine hundred people in that yard had decided that he had taken the fourth one off himself. One thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine less one thousand nine hundred and seventy-two is seven, and thirty less twenty-three is seven by the month count.

He was in the room off a third court at about eleven on the morning of the thirtieth of July, which in six years has happened on about eleven mornings, and he had nothing under his arm.

He had a want. He had had one since the seventh of July and it had not changed in twenty-three days and it was the simplest want he has ever had and it was the one thing in this city he had the least right to.

He wanted somebody to ask him for something.

“There is a person at the counter,” said Wenna Raut.

## The Woman In Number Four

She had been in number four off Lamplit Row for nine years and she was not a woman of sixty, and she had dressed for a walk across a city on the word of somebody she had never seen. Her sister was three days older than her and had waited at the bottom of the stair.

She came up about nine steps and put a folded paper on the counter with both hands, the way a person puts down something they have carried a long way. She could not read.

“Nobody has read that to me,” she said. “I have had it since the twenty-fourth.”

“Who brought it to you.”

“A man at the counter where I pay the rent,” she said. “He gave it me and he said it had to be answered by the eleventh of August, and he said that because it is the eleventh. He read me the bit at the bottom about that. He would not read me the rest and I asked him to and he said it was not his.”

Wenna Raut had not touched it.

“I am permitted one question a day,” she said, “and I have not asked it, and you are the only person this office may ask, so I have not asked you either. It has been on my counter since about a quarter to eleven.”

## What Was On The Paper

He unfolded it and there were three lines on it and three things written beside them, and it was dated the twentieth of July, and it was a demand, and a demand is the only piece of paper in this city a landlord is allowed to put in front of a magistrate.

“It has three lines on it.”

“It has three lines on it,” he said, “and it has no address at the head of it, and the address is the only thing on the January one that made anybody carry it up two flights by hand.”

The woman in number four put her thumb on the counter and left it there.

“In January,” she said, “a man put four lines in a paper and I did not know what they were, and there was a room in another building where a man read them out, and I came away with about a year of what I had been short for nine years, and I have never once known what the four lines said and I have never once asked anybody to tell me.”

He looked up.

“You won in January.”

“I won in January,” she said. “And on the twentieth of July a man at the counter where I pay the rent gave me this, and he read me the bottom line about the eleventh of August, and there is a room on the twenty-eighth of this month where somebody is going to decide whether I owe him a year and a half.”

“What is on it.”

“It is about the first of January,” she said. “He says I had a room and then I did not, and he says it changed what I held, and he says a day I can name and a place I can name, and he says another person was there or gave it to me.”

“He has proved the first three.”

“He proved the first three off a paper on the twenty-eighth and I was not allowed to speak for about nine minutes and then I was asked whether I could describe the man who came and I could not,” said the woman, “and he said that was the end of it, because it is the third one that says the person can be named or described closely enough.”

## What She Had Come For

“How did you come to be here.”

“A woman at a stall told me,” said the woman in number four. “She said there is a man who writes things down and that there used to be four of them and that one of them has come off a board in a yard, and she said she had heard it from somebody who could read.”

“Who told her.”

“I do not know her name and she does not know yours,” said the woman in number four. “That is how it is done.”

She put her hand on the edge of the counter for about a second and then took it off the counter and put both hands in her pockets.

“I cannot read that paper,” she said. “I have never been able to read a lease and I cannot read a demand and I cannot read a finding, and I add my money up on my fingers at the end of every month and I have known I was short for nine years before anybody ever said so to me.”

“And you have come here to ask me something.”

“I have come here to ask you to put the fourth line back.”

## The Four Lines

*It changed what a person held. It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name. Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough. And it is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

“The fourth one is the one that won you the year in January,” he said.

“That is what I am told,” said the woman in number four. “The woman at the stall said that the fourth one is the one that cannot be checked, and that a landlord has to put a thing on a page to use it, and that the thing is not on a page, and that is why I got my year back in January.”

“That is right.”

“Then put it back.”

He stood at eleven in the morning on the thirtieth of July in a room with two chairs in it and a drawer open and empty since the fourth of February, and a woman he had watched from the foot of a stair in January had just asked him for the one thing in this city he had spent five years arranging not to be asked for.

“No,” he said.

## Why Not

She did not argue with him. She had asked one question and she had got an answer in four seconds and she stood there with her hands in her pockets and looked at him.

“Then say why not,” she said. “You are the only one they say anybody can ask. I have come from the other side of the city and I cannot read and I have until the eleventh of August.”

“Because I cannot.”

“You cannot put a line back on a board.”

“No,” he said. “That is not why. I could put four chalk lines on a board in a yard in about nine minutes and about four people in this city know that and none of them would stop me.”

“Then why.”

He put his hand on the table.

“Because on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year I wrote those four lines down in my own hand, in one column of a book in this room, against myself, and I did it in about eleven seconds, and a clerk entered them exactly as given and added one sentence of her own, which was the only sentence she was permitted to add.”

“What did she say.”

“She said that of the four conditions, the first, the second and the third were met, and that the fourth was not met, and that the reason is that the thing it describes is on a page in this city.”

He said it flatly and at the speed of a man reading a line out of a book, because that is what it is and because he has said it to himself about four hundred times since March.

“You wrote the four lines down,” said the woman in number four, “and then you wrote down that the fourth one of them does not hold.”

“Yes.”

“And then you put the fourth one on a board in a yard anyway.”

“That was four years ago and I was a different man and I have never once been able to work out what he was doing.”

“And now it is off the board.”

“It came off the board on the seventh of July,” he said. “About nine hundred people took it off with a rag and a bucket and I stood nine feet off and said nothing.”

## What She Said To That

The woman in number four stood at a counter in a room with two chairs in it and worked on it for about eleven seconds.

“I do not understand what a rule is,” she said. “I have never understood one and I am not going to start this morning. I understand that there were four lines and that one of them got me a year of money back in January, and that it has come off a board, and that a man on the twenty-eighth proved three of them against me.”

“That is right.”

“So will you put it back.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I have said the reason, and the reason is a thing I wrote in about eleven seconds in a book in this room in the November of the fifth year, and a clerk who may only enter what she is given put one sentence under it, and it has gone out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter since.”

“And it has gone out three times,” he said, “and there are about two thousand people a day who cannot get past it, and about four in every thousand read it to the bottom.”

She nodded slowly.

“Then tell the man who wrote on the paper,” she said.

“There is no man.”

“There is a man,” said the woman in number four. “There is a man who let me a room and a man who came and took it off me and a man at a counter and a man who proved three lines, and there is a man in this building, because you are standing in it. So tell him.”

“I cannot make him stop.”

“You do not have to make him stop,” she said. “You have to tell him. A man who is told is a man who is known, and I have been in number four for nine years and there has never once been anybody in it I could say a thing to.”

She picked up her gloves.

“I will come back on the eleventh,” she said, “and I will not be able to read what you have written, and I will not be able to read what he has written, and one of us will be told by somebody who is not here.”

He said he would not stop the fourth line coming back to a board.

He did not say that either, because by the time he had worked out which of the two things he was refusing to do, she was on the stair.

## One Thousand Three Hundred And Thirty-Four

Afterwards he took the day book down off its shelf, which is not the drawer under the table and never has been, and opened it at a page ruled in the March of the third year. The second column had two lines in it. He read the first one.

He had written it himself, on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year, at about eleven minutes past ten in the morning, in the column headed carried by, and it was four lines long and it was his and it was the only claim in six years that had ever been entered against a person by that person.

Under it, in a clerk’s hand, in one sentence, and it was the only sentence she was permitted to add, and she was right and he has known she was right every day since: that of the four conditions given, the first, second and third were met, and that the fourth was not met, and that the reason is that the thing it describes is on a page in this city.

He had read it in about four seconds.

There is no form in this city for striking a line a person entered against themselves in their own hand, and there is no form in this city for putting one back.

The sentence at the foot of a page that goes out four times a year is still in his own handwriting with his name under one of the four lines in it. It is not the same sentence as the one on a board, and the two have never been the same sentence. About four people in every thousand get to the bottom of a page and know that, and about eleven thousand people in a yard know the other one.

A woman in number four came from the other side of the city this morning with a piece of paper she cannot read and asked him for the only thing anybody has asked him for in six years.

*One thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine days. Less one thousand nine hundred and seventy-two, the twenty-third of July, is seven. Thirty less twenty-three is seven by the month count.*

*The first of the fourth year is one thousand seven hundred and seventy less two times three hundred and sixty, which is one thousand and fifty, and the fifteenth of October stands nine times thirty and fourteen days after it, which is two hundred and eighty-four, and one thousand and fifty plus two hundred and eighty-four is one thousand three hundred and thirty-four. One thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine less one thousand three hundred and thirty-four is six hundred and forty-five days, and six hundred and forty-five is twenty-one months with fifteen days after them.*

*The fourth line was taken off a board in a yard on the seventh of July, day one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, which is twenty-three days ago. The twenty-third sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth of September, day two thousand and thirty-nine, which is sixty days from this morning, and there are four lines at the foot of it in the hand of this office and there has not been one form in six years on which any of them can be taken off a page by the man who wrote them.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is two hundred and six days, and that is six months and twenty-six days. A demand with the address of this office at the head of every line came into this room on the fourth. A woman who cannot read took a year of money off it on the twenty-fourth. On the twentieth of July a second demand with three lines on it and no address was put to her, and it was proved on the twenty-eighth against her by a man who has never heard of this office, and she came up nine steps at about a quarter to eleven this morning and asked a man of rank one to put one line back on a board.*

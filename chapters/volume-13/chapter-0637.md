# The Sentence In The Office's Hand

Seven days had gone since a man at the foot of a board in a yard had begun writing the third of four lines on a fourth board in four-inch letters, and had said that it was the one that works. One thousand nine hundred and ninety-four was the fifteenth of August; the twenty-second of September is day two thousand and thirty-one, and two thousand and thirty-one less two thousand and twenty-four is seven, and twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.

A man in a delivery coat came into the room off a third court at about ten in the morning on the twenty-second of September and stood about four feet off a table with two chairs in it and did not sit down.

He had been up a stair behind a lane once, in the third year, and had not come any further, and there was a reason and the reason was on the page.

## Three Years Ago

“You came up a stair in the third year,” said Lin Hao. “You stood on a landing and you said you were not going to read it, and you were right not to, and I have never once asked you why.”

“You have never once asked me anything about it,” said Emil Brack. “That is not a complaint. I am a man who has said out loud at a wall end for three years that there is no way in this city of finding out whether a thing written down is true, and you were not there, and I have never said that was wrong.”

He put the rag in his coat.

“I have come up the stair to have it read to me.”

“By whom.”

“That is the whole of the morning,” said Brack. “I have been at that wall end since a quarter to six three years ago and I can read. I have read a page off a desk under a window in Cordwain four times and I have read the ninth line down out of my own memory twice with nothing improved. I do not need anybody to read me a line.”

He stood about four feet off the table.

“I need the man who wrote it to say it out loud in a room, once, with another person in it.”

## Why A Man Who Can Read Wants To Be Read To

Lin Hao did not understand it for about four seconds and then understood all of it at once.

“Because it is a warning,” he said.

“It was never a warning to me,” said Emil Brack. “I have told you that at that wall end and you have not been here. A warning is a thing a person may take or leave. That sentence is not a warning. It is a piece of paper that has my name at the bottom of it in a hand that is not mine, and it is the only piece of paper in this city in which my name appears at all.”

“That is true.”

“It is true and it is going out at four on the thirtieth of September for the seventh time, with my name under it,” said Brack. “About two thousand people a day cannot walk past the foot of it and about four in every thousand get to the bottom.”

In about nine years it will have been going out at four on the thirtieth every quarter for longer than anybody in this city has kept anything, and it will have no author anybody can remember, and a man who invents figures for a grain a head will be named in the hand of an office that cannot be compelled and cannot be sued and has no money.

He turned the rag over in his hands.

“And I have wanted it read to me since the twelfth of July,” he said. “I did not come up the stair on the twelfth and I did not come on the twentieth, and I have come on the twenty-second because on the twenty-fourth a man is going to chalk the third of your four lines on a board in letters four inches high.”

He has stood at the foot of that hill for three years and he does not have to be anywhere near it this afternoon.

## The Woman At The Counter

Wenna Raut had been at the counter since about nine and had not looked up once.

“If you want a line read out of that book to a person standing at a counter, I am not the person who does it,” she said. “I entered it and I cannot read it out and I cannot be asked to hurry and I am permitted one question a day and I am going to spend it now.”

“Ask it.”

“Were these the words you gave me.”

“Yes,” said Lin Hao.

“Then it is entered exactly as given and I have done the whole of what I am able to do with a book in this room,” said Wenna Raut, “and the line is on the second page at the foot, and it goes out on the thirtieth, and there is nothing else in this city that will read it to him.”

## The Only Person Entitled

There was one person in Ostara entitled to read that sentence aloud.

It was not the clerk, because a clerk may only enter what she is given. It was not a magistrate, because a magistrate reads what is filed in front of him. It was not the nine hundred people in a yard, because a person who reads a thing to himself has not had it read to him.

It was the man who wrote it, in the hand of the office, in a space at the foot of a page, on the ninth of November, with a name under it that belonged to somebody else.

“I am going to read it,” said Lin Hao. “And I am going to say to you first that I am not permitted to read it and there is nothing on any paper in this city that permits me to read it, and I am doing it because you have come up a stair and asked, and there is no instrument that can stop me and there is no instrument that will let anybody do it again.”

“Read it.”

He did not read it as it is written.

“Any number a person sets down in this city may be one they invented,” he said. “That is what the line says. It says the standing sheet is printed by this office and there is a name at the bottom of it, and the name is yours and it is not my name.”

Emil Brack did not move for about four seconds.

“That is what it says.”

“That is what it says.”

“Not the words.”

“Not the words,” said Lin Hao. “The words are a number set down anywhere in this city may be one somebody invented, and that the standing sheet is printed by this office. And the rest of it is yours, in my hand, at the bottom, in a November, because you asked me for it on a stair in the third year and I have never asked you for it since and you have never asked me for anything since.”

## What The Man Who Is Named Says

“That is the last time,” said Emil Brack.

He said it flatly, the way a man says a figure at ten to six in the morning.

“What is.”

“That sentence is now a thing that two men have heard one other man say out loud in a room,” said Brack. “It was a warning on a public page. It is now a sentence that a man has said to me, to my face, with a clerk in the room, and there is no version of a sentence a person has heard spoken that is still a warning to anybody.”

He took the rag out of his coat and looked at it and put it back.

“Everything you have done for three years is in it and I am not being sarcastic and I am not grateful,” said Brack. “You took the only checkable thing in this city off a wall end on purpose and you said so out loud in a yard before you did it, and you have been right about every fact since, and I have been right about every fact, and neither of us has ever once been thanked for a thing by the other.”

“That is correct.”

“Take it off,” said Emil Brack.

“No.”

“You heard me.”

“You have asked me to take four sentences off the foot of a page this office prints four times a year,” said Lin Hao, “and I am going to tell you exactly why I cannot, and it is going to sound exactly like an excuse, and I have listened to it being said to me about six times this year and it is still true.”

He put his hand out and stopped it about nine inches from the table.

“A clerk may only enter what she is given and cannot improve it. Nothing in this city will take a line off that page in the hand of the office, and that is true of anybody and not only of me. And that sentence is not mine to strike, because I wrote it at your asking and not at my own, and if I strike it then I have struck a thing a person asked me for about three years ago and did not argue with.”

“Do it in your own hand.”

“Not the hand of the office.”

“That is the hand it is in.”

“That is the hand it is in,” said Lin Hao, “and it is the hand it has been in for three years, and about four in every thousand get to the bottom of that page, and if I strike it in my own hand then I have put my own handwriting at the end of a sentence I admitted inventing, and every one of those four is going to know that I did.”

Emil Brack stood about four feet off the table and looked at the floor for a while.

“Then it stays because it is in your hand and not because it is right,” he said.

“No,” said Lin Hao. “It stays because it is in your hand.”

He put his hand down.

“That is the sentence I wanted to come down this stair for,” said Emil Brack, “and I have got it in about nine minutes, and I am going to be right about it for the rest of my life and you are never going to be able to tell me that I am not.”

## What He Went Down The Stair With

He went down one flight with his coat on and stopped on the landing, which is not two flights off Cordwain and is not a landing that anybody in this city has gone up.

“Not the wall,” he said, without turning round. “I am not going to be at the foot of that board on the twenty-fourth and you are not going to be at the foot of it either, and that is deliberate and I want it said while I can still hear you.”

“You have been at that hill nine times since the seventh of July.”

“I have stood at the foot of that board. I have not been at the foot of that board,” said Emil Brack. “Not one person at the foot of that hill has ever asked me for a figure, and not one of them has ever had one of mine, and nobody is owed one.”

He has kept it that way for three years by not being where the asking is done.

He got to the bottom of the stair.

“Eight days to the thirtieth,” he said. “A page goes out at four with a figure at the head of it that has not moved since before I heard it, and two lines in a column, and four in a hand at the foot, and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it.”

He turned and looked up the stair.

“You will be standing in that room when it does,” said Brack, “and I will be at a wall end at ten to six, and neither of us will ever be in the other one’s building.”

*Two thousand and thirty-one days. Less two thousand and twenty-four, the fifteenth of September, is seven. Twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, day one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and thirteen days, which is ten months with thirteen days after them. It went out at four on the thirteenth of that November, day one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, and the twentieth sheet went out at four on the thirtieth of December, day one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, and it has gone out at four on the thirtieth every quarter since, and the twenty-third of them goes out in eight days.*

*Less the seventh of February, one thousand eight hundred and six, is two hundred and twenty-five days, which is seven months with fifteen days after them, and on that morning a man did the arithmetic of four lines out loud on the ground in front of about nine hundred people, and the fourth of the four cannot be tried by anybody in Ostara.*

*Less the twelfth of July, one thousand nine hundred and sixty-one, is seventy days, and a man with a rag and a bucket went along the fourth line of a board in a yard with about nine hundred people watching him, and one of them was a man in a delivery coat who has never said a word about it and is not going to.*

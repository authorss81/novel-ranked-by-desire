# Nineteen Years And No Name

Seven days had gone since he had stood at the top of a table in a room off Cordwain and worked out that a woman who cannot be understood in a room can be barred for ever, and had told a man who carries demands that he was going to be in a room on the fifteenth of September with no standing in it.

The eighth of September is day two thousand and seventeen, and two thousand and seventeen less two thousand and ten is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

He went into the division building at about ten in the morning on the eighth of September with nothing under his arm, for the third time in a month.

He wanted somewhere to put a figure.

## The Shelf Behind The Desk

There are four shelves behind the returns desk. Three of them hold this year’s returns in bundles of nine, and the fourth holds a bound ledger about the size of a school register with a green cloth back, and it is the only thing on that floor that has a date on the spine instead of a quarter.

“Nineteen years,” said Beatrix Halm. “I have never seen anybody open it and I have been at this counter nine years.”

He opened it.

The first page was a list of counters in this district. Every counter that has ever stood in this district is on that page, in the order they were opened, and against each one there is a figure in a hand that has been copied forward about nineteen times.

Against a counter at the bottom of the list, marked as closed, there were nine hundred and forty-one.

“It has not moved in nineteen years,” said Beatrix Halm. “It has been copied from one book to the next since the day the counter shut, by hand, by whoever was holding the returns, and every one of them copied it exactly and none of them asked where it came from, because you do not copy a figure and then look at it.”

## What A Counter Figure Is

“A counter figure is what came in at that counter,” said Beatrix Halm. “Not what the counter did. Not who came to it. What came in, and in what, and the book says it in one line and there is no room on the page for anything else and there never has been.”

“So that figure is what that counter took.”

“That figure is what that counter took in nine years,” said Beatrix Halm. “It is not a claim. It is not owed to anybody. It is not in anybody’s name and it never was, because the page has no column for a name and it did not have one in the first book either, and there was a time when a counter book had a name column and then there was not.”

“When.”

“I do not know, and neither does anybody now,” said Beatrix Halm. “It was gone before I came and there are two people alive who worked in this building when it went and neither of them will say the year out loud in a room, because the year is a thing nobody has ever put a check on, and the figure has been carried on it ever since.”

## The Rule Nobody Wrote

“Now tell me the rule.”

Beatrix Halm put her good ear away from him and did not answer for about four seconds.

“A counter figure can be added to and cannot be taken from,” she said. “That is it. That is the whole of it, and it has been the rule since before the Register existed, and it is in nobody’s hand, and it is not in a book I have ever been shown.”

If a counter opens, it gets a figure. If a counter closes, its figure is carried forward. If a person gives something at a counter, it is added to that counter’s figure and it goes in the return for the quarter.

“And it cannot be struck.”

“It cannot be taken from,” said Beatrix Halm. “There is no return in this city with a line on it for taking something away. A return has one column and it adds. I have been entering returns for nine years and every single thing I have ever entered has gone in at the top and never come out, and that is not a fault of mine and it is not a fault of anybody, it is the paper.”

She took the ledger off the shelf and shut it and put it back.

“Numbers in this building can only grow,” she said. “That is the whole of the arithmetic of it and there is nobody in this city you could say it to who would not say it is correct.”

## Who Wrote It

“You did not write that.”

Beatrix Halm looked at him.

“No,” she said.

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I have spent six years believing that every instrument there is for not doing a thing in this city was put there by somebody, and most of them by me, and I have said so out loud in about four rooms in this city and not one of them has contradicted me.”

“Who wrote it.”

“I have no idea,” said Lin Hao, “and I have never once asked, and I have been to nine divisions and I have been to a counting house and a counting room and four counters and a magistrate and a department of works, and in six years not one person has ever put that sentence to me and made me say it out loud.”

He stood at the shelf.

It was the first thing anybody had said to him in six years that he did not already know, and it was a woman who is deaf in one ear telling a clerk’s rule in a division building, and it was not a comfort and it was not an argument.

It was the fact that the machine did not start with him.

## The Ninety-One Again

“Ninety-one,” he said.

“Ninety-one a quarter for four years and I have got it to one hundred and forty-four by counting forwards and not multiplying, and you know that and I know you know it.”

“If you add it.”

“If I add it, the counter’s figure describes people who gave nothing,” said Beatrix Halm. “And the minute it describes people who gave nothing it is not a counter figure any more. It is something else, and there is no column for that something else, and a return that has got something else in it is a return that a person has improved, and I do not improve.”

“So you have been carrying two things for four years.”

“I have been carrying two things for four years and I have only ever been paid for one of them,” said Beatrix Halm, “and the one I am paid for is the one that adds up.”

## What Happens On The Fifteenth

“The third line dies on the fifteenth.”

He had not asked her about the third line.

“The third line is the one about another person being there, or being given it,” said Beatrix Halm. “I have read it. I have been over it about nine times since the seventh of July, because it is the only one of the three that carries anything anybody can act on, and I am telling you here, in this building, at ten in the morning, that I have read it, and you may believe me or you may not.”

“When does it die.”

“The fifteenth, in a room, in about nine minutes,” said Beatrix Halm. “A landlord will say that a woman cannot describe a man who came to her door at night for four minutes and did not give his name.”

That is true. It is completely true. Nobody in that room will be able to say a word against it, and it will be over, and after the fifteenth there is not one counter in this city where a person can be given something and not be able to say who was in the room.

She squared the tray.

“And I am going to stop counting the ninety-one.”

He did not say anything.

“Do not,” said Beatrix Halm. “I know what it is. I have thought about it since the eighth of August and it took me about nine seconds and I have not been able to find another way round it and I have gone at it for four days.”

“Say the reason.”

“The reason is that on the morning of the fifteenth somebody in that room is going to say that there is nothing anybody can do about it,” said Beatrix Halm. “And it is going to be true, and about nine hundred people are going to hear it, and about ten thousand are not going to.”

She put a finger on the closed ledger on the shelf behind her.

“After that, a counter figure is the only kind of figure anybody in this city will accept. And if I am still counting ninety-one on my fingers in a lane at the end of a round, then I am holding up the one number in Ostara that says a counter is not only what came in at it. And it is in my own head. And on the fifteenth it becomes an argument somebody can make against me.”

“You would be protecting it.”

“I would be holding it up and I would be the only person holding it up and I have been that person for four years and I am tired,” said Beatrix Halm. “And I am not brave, and I have never once been brave, and anybody in this building who tells you I am will be telling you about a different woman.”

## The Nineteen Years In Plain Words

He did not argue.

He had about eleven minutes with the arithmetic and he did all of it and he came back to it at the end of the morning.

Nineteen years in this city is six thousand eight hundred and forty days, because a year is three hundred and sixty.

Two thousand and seventeen less six thousand eight hundred and forty is minus four thousand eight hundred and twenty-three.

A day four thousand eight hundred and twenty-three before the first of February of the first year is a day in a building that was put up before this office existed by thirteen years.

No page this office has ever printed carries anything from it and not one person alive can check it. A figure has been copied from one book to the next for nineteen years by about nineteen pairs of hands and not one pair of them looked at it.

It is the first figure he has found in this city that he cannot check, and it is not in a sentence anybody wrote.

He went and looked at the four hundred and nineteen lines under the head of the standing page, with an hour set against every hour of six years and no figure against any of them, and there were about nine of them a week who got as far as that page and were still stopping at the top of it.

The nineteen-year figure is the oldest number in that division and it has never moved and there is nobody left alive who can say whose counter it was.

Neither figure can be checked. One of them is under a page that goes out four times a year and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it. The other is in a book behind a desk in a building with nine counters in it and nobody has opened it since the second year.

He did not tell Beatrix Halm any of that. He stood at the shelf for about a minute and then he said good afternoon to her and went out through nine counters and up two floors.

## The Sixth Thing In A Passage

The man at the desk under a window was where he always was.

“Sixth one this month,” he said.

“Sixth what.”

“Sixth person who has come down here wanting something off a desk that does not give it,” said the man at the desk, “and I am going to tell you something about the five before you and then you can go home.”

He turned a page over.

“Every one of the five came about the seventh of July,” he said. “The page on my desk has been out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter since before you were in this city, and I have taken about a hundred people a day past the foot of it for about nine years, and until the seventh of July of this year not one of them wanted anything off me.”

He put the pen down.

“The sixth one is the first one who wanted something that is not about the seventh of July,” he said. “And he did not tell me what it was and I did not ask him, and I have read about four hundred returns in my life, and I can tell you that a man who comes down a passage and does not say what he wants has already decided it before he came.”

He squared the ledger and shut it.

“And whatever it is, it is going to happen on a morning,” said the man at the desk, “and I would not be surprised if it was the fifteenth.”

*Two thousand and seventeen days. Less two thousand and ten, the first of September, is seven. Eight less one is seven by the month count.*

*Less the first of May of the fifth year, day one thousand five hundred and thirty, is four hundred and eighty-seven days, which is sixteen months with seven days after them. The second line in the second column of a page that goes out four times a year went in on the first of May of that year and has stood for every one of those days with an hour against it and no figure and no name.*

*Nineteen years is six thousand eight hundred and forty days, two thousand and seventeen less six thousand eight hundred and forty is minus four thousand eight hundred and twenty-three, and a day four thousand eight hundred and twenty-three before the first of February of the first year is a day in a building that was put up before this office existed by thirteen years, and a figure has been copied from one book to the next for all of that by about nineteen pairs of hands.*

*Less the seventh of February, one thousand eight hundred and thirty-six, is one hundred and eighty-one days, which is six months and one day, and on that morning a man did the arithmetic of four lines out loud on the ground in front of about nine hundred people and the fourth of them cannot be tried by anybody in Ostara.*

*Less the thirtieth of September, two thousand and thirty-nine, is twenty-two days. It goes out at four with a figure at the head of it that has not moved in seven quarters, and there is a column with two lines in it, and there is a book behind a desk in a division building that has not been opened since the second year.*

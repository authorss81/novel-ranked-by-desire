# The Officer Who Cannot Serve Him

Runa Sato came into the ground-floor corridor of the branch office off a third court at about eleven at night on the seventeenth of January and stood at the end of it with a bundle under her arm and her hat still on, and she did not come further in.

Seven days had gone since the question came back to the office. One thousand seven hundred and eighty-six less one thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

Lin Hao had the bucket in his hand and the counter behind him and three floors of washrooms above him and a rota page on a nail in the small room at the back with four names on it, and he had been about forty minutes into a shift and had not seen her before.

“You are the man with the bucket at that address,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Say it as a question.”

“I am the man with the bucket at that address,” he said, “and I have been for about five years, and there is no door onto a street anywhere in the building it belongs to.”

She took her hat off then, and that was the first thing that told him the conversation was not going to be a courtesy.

“I have been to that address,” she said. “There is a passage off Cordwain with a desk in it under a window, and the sheet lies out on that desk, and I read the address at the head of every line. Then I asked the man at the desk where the address was, because I am required to serve at an address and not at a room. He told me you work nights at a branch, and he told me that without being asked.”

“That sounds like him,” Lin Hao said.

“You know him.”

“He has run a public desk for about nine years and he is paid by the building and not by anybody who prints anything, and he told me on the first of October that the same thing to nine thousand people a day.”

Runa Sato wrote something in a notebook she had shut again before she had finished writing in it.

“I have a paper to put in front of you,” she said, “and I have to put it in front of you before this counter shuts at four, and it has been in my hand since about half past four this afternoon and it goes back on the list at four in the morning whatever is or is not on it.”

## What A Tenth Line Is For

She undid the bundle on the end of the corridor counter where the night man leaves the lamp, and the first thing out of it was a return, hers, filled in, in her own hand.

Nine lines and then a tenth with a box ruled at the foot of it.

“I write one of these a week,” she said. “Nine lines and a tenth with a box. The box is for a reason. Not a conclusion — a reason. I saw this, at this hour, on this date, in this place, and here is why I am writing it down.”

She turned it round and pushed it toward him.

“I saw a man on the third floor of a branch office off a third court at about eleven at night on the twelfth of January, and I have written that, and it is true, and it goes in at four.”

“And the box.”

“The box says why I was on the third floor at eleven at night on the twelfth of January,” she said. “And there is a thing you want me to write in it and I am not going to, and I would like you to understand that I am not refusing to be helpful. A reason is not a conclusion. The moment a reason becomes a conclusion it stops being a reason and becomes a thing a man said, and then it is your word and not my eye.”

She put her finger on the ruled space.

“If I wrote *this man is the person the paper names and this is where he is to be found*, that would be a conclusion. If I write *no instrument in this city can serve a man at an address he does not hold and I carried it to a place where he is known to be and put it in his hand*, that is a reason, and it is mine, and it is what I saw.”

Lin Hao looked at the ten lines and understood the whole of the arrangement at once, and it was the first time in five years that anybody had said something to him that he could not improve afterwards.

“You cannot serve me at an address,” he said.

“There is no address in Ostara that you can be served at.”

“There is not.”

“The rota on your nail is a page the size of a folded hand,” said Runa Sato, “and the man who keeps it is nine streets away from here and holds no authority over you and cannot be compelled by anybody. You have a rank and no figure cut into the plate in your coat. You have a building with no street door and a counter that opens when the building opens.”

“That is not an address.”

“That is a place a man happens to be, and a place a man happens to be cannot be served at.”

“I know.”

“You have known for five years and you have never once been in a room where somebody with a paper was standing in front of you, and this morning there is one, and she is rank two.”

He looked up.

She said it without any weight at all, the way a person reads a line off a slate.

“I am rank two,” Runa Sato said, “and you are rank one with no figure, and this is the first morning in your life that a Bureau officer is above you on paper, and I have been above you for six years and have hated every day of it, and I find this morning worse than the other six years put together.”

“Why.”

“Because I am going to have to do the thing by hand,” she said. “Because a field officer may carry a thing herself and serve it herself and write down that she did, in her own hand, with her rank and the hour and the place, and it goes in at four and it cannot be un-written and it cannot go back and be improved.”

“And if you write down that you served it.”

“Then the finding behind this is answered. And if the finding is answered, then the four lines somebody copied off a board in a yard are attached to an office in a building with no street door, and there is a person in it.” She put her thumb on the edge of the rail. “And I have been asked that question once already, by a man at a desk in a passage, and he was not entitled to ask it and he asked it anyway, and he was right to.”

## Carried Up By Hand

She picked the paper up off the counter and held it under her arm.

“Up,” she said. “Not to a room. To the room, if there is one, with a door on it, and I am going to shut it, and I am telling you that I am going to shut it before I do it, because a field officer who serves a paper and then goes into a room with the person she served it is making a note of herself and I would rather the note be true.”

“The office is up two flights and along a corridor and it has no street door and it is the room with the table in it.”

“Then that is the room,” said Runa Sato. “And it is about half past one, and the counter shuts at four, and I have a round at four in the morning and I have not mentioned it and I am not going to.”

She carried it up two flights behind him and she did not use the rail.

The room off a third court was standing open with its lamp on a stand in the middle of it, because the Register’s counter is on the branch’s list of things that get wiped down at two, and there is one drawer under the table and it has been open and empty since the first winter.

She put her coat over the back of the second chair, and shut the door, and turned the key, and set the paper square in the middle of the table with the address at the head of every line showing, and put her hand flat beside it and left it there.

“I have one question,” she said, “and I am entitled to it, and my return needs an answer to put in the ninth line, and I am going to ask it once.”

“Ask it.”

“What is the office.”

He had it ready. He had it ready in about two seconds, which he noticed and did not like.

“It is a counter for things nobody paid for,” he said.

She did not move for about two seconds.

“That is nine words,” she said. “You have had those since the corridor and you have not improved them once and you have not asked me what the office is for. Six years I have gone into rooms and asked a person what they are and then written down what they said and taken it away, and that is my whole trade and it is the only thing I am good at and no one has ever once enjoyed being asked.”

“And tonight.”

“Tonight I am in a room with a door shut with a paper I carried up a stair in my own hand, and I am going to be enjoyed, and I have not been enjoyed at this in six years of this, and it is the most frightening thing that has happened to me in about a year.” She turned her head about nine degrees, which is a thing Runa Sato does.

“So do not be careful with me. Whatever happens in this room is going in my return in my own words, and I am choosing the words now.”

## Nine Words, And Then Nothing Careful

He was not careful with her.

That is the whole of it and it is the thing he was not able to put down afterwards. He had a chest that had been spent since the fourth year and a cough that had come with it and a rank and no standing and nothing to lose in that room except the hours before four in the morning, and he had been careful for so long that careful had become a kind of furniture he moved around in the dark.

He took her coat off the chair and hung it on the nail and she watched him do it.

“Off with the coat,” she said. “You are going to be freezing in that and I am not going to stop for it.”

He got his own coat off over shoulders that had been carrying water since ten and she came across the room and put both her hands flat on his chest and pushed him back against the edge of the table and kissed him under the jaw and then on the mouth, and it was not a courtesy and it was not careful either, and she made a low sound into it that she did not try to stop.

“I have wanted to do that since the corridor,” she said. “Since you said *there is no door onto a street anywhere in the building it belongs to*, and you said it the way a man says a thing he is bored of. Do it again.”

He got his hand into the back of her hair and she came up onto the table against him with a knee between his and the drawer and pushed his coat down off one shoulder, and neither of them was talking.

She got her own collar open with one hand and then stopped doing that and did it properly with both, and she was quick and she was not fussy about it and she was enjoying herself and said so.

“Say something,” she said.

“I have not got anything.”

“Then say that again slower and it will do.”

He got his shirt open and her hands went flat on his chest again, low, and she felt the place where the breathing does not go as deep as it used to and she did not ask about it and did not stop either.

“You go in about half as deep as a man your age should,” she said, into his neck. “I can hear it. It is not a thing I am going to ask you about tonight.”

“No.”

“No,” she agreed. “Right. Undo the rest of that.”

## What She Was Doing

She got his trousers open and her hand closed on his cock and she said, “Oh,” in a voice that had nothing in it that had ever been professional.

Then she sat down on the edge of the table and spread her knees over the sides of it and pulled him in against her and worked at him first with her hand and then with her mouth, and she took her time about it, and she told him what she was doing while she did it, in the flat voice she used for a district.

“I am going to take you into my mouth now,” she said, “and I am going to do it properly, because I have not done this since before you were on that rota and I am not going to do it in a hurry to get it over.”

“You do not have to—”

“I know I do not have to. Shut up.”

He shut up. Her mouth was warm and wet and completely without ceremony, and she took him deep and held him there and worked the head of him with her tongue afterwards until his knees were not doing much, and she looked up at him once from under her lashes with her eyes half shut and made a noise that was not for him.

He put his hand on the back of her head and she let him, and then she took his hand off and put it behind her own neck, which told him the whole of what she wanted and it was not that.

She came off him and got up onto her knees on the chair that was against the wall and pulled him in against her and rode him with her hands on his shoulders and her face open and her voice going, and she kept going on about what she could see.

“You are shaking,” she said, between two of them. “That is not the cold. I have watched nine hundred men shake at me and that is not the cold.”

“I know.”

“I can see your chest from here and I can see it not going down as far as it should, and I am going to keep going anyway, and I am telling you that I am going to keep going anyway, because you have spent six years being careful and I did not come up two flights to watch a man be careful.”

He got his hands on her hips and held on and she let him take some of the weight and then took it back off him and went faster.

## A Woman Who Writes Down What She Saw

Somewhere in the middle of it she stopped talking, and that was when it stopped being about anything at all.

She got him off her mouth and turned round and put her back against the counter with her hands braced on the edge behind her and her head back, and he got into her and she took all of him and her legs came up round his waist and her heels crossed at the small of his back.

“There,” she said. “That. That is the thing. Six years.”

He thrust into her and she thrust back and neither of them was quiet and neither of them was small about it, and the lamp on its stand threw the drawer open and empty across the ceiling and the corridor outside it was empty.

“I am going to say one more thing about this and then I am not going to say anything about it at all,” she said, and she said it into his throat while she moved. “I am going to say it because I am a woman who writes down what she saw, and I have spent six years writing down what I saw in nine lines and a tenth with a box, and I have not once in six years written down a thing that happened to me.”

“Write it down.”

“Do not be kind to me at this,” said Runa Sato, “or I will stop.”

He was not kind to her. He got a hand under her and held her where he wanted her and she said his name once and then said something else instead and her nails went into his shoulder hard enough that he felt it afterwards and would carry it for a week.

She came with her mouth open and her whole body under him, shaking through it, and she did not hush it and did not hide it, and the sound of it filled a room the size of two ward beds and went no further than a shut door.

He spent inside her and she took all of it and stayed on him with her forehead against his and her breathing ragged, and for a while neither of them said a word.

He understood, some time after that, what it had been.

He had spent six years being the only person an office is permitted to ask. And on the third floor of a branch building a woman with a rank above his had decided what to do with him, and he had not been asked, and he had not had to be careful, and it had not been a transaction of any kind.

Afterwards she had her arms round his neck with her mouth against his jaw and was breathing hard enough that he could hear it.

“Right,” she said. “You are going to tell me that was fine.”

“It was fine.”

“It was not fine and we both know what it was,” said Runa Sato. “It was that neither of us has had this in six years and you are a man the city reads a page about and I am the woman who writes down what she saw, and it stopped being about anything at all some time before I could have said when, and I have not had that since I can remember.”

She got her breath and put her forehead against his.

“Right,” she said. “And now you are going to tell me your name is on a page somewhere and you are going to be ashamed of it, and I am going to have to listen and not put it in a return.”

He told her. Not all of it. Nine words and then four more and then nothing for a while.

“It is not a confession,” she said, into his collar. “I have read the foot of that sheet. Both sentences. I have been round this city for nine years and there is not one other thing on any public page in it that anybody is allowed to argue with.”

“And you put those two there.”

“In the hand of an office that cannot be sued,” she said. “I do not know whether that is the bravest thing I have ever read or the most expensive, and I am not going to decide tonight.”

She got her clothes together in the dark and she did it fast and she did not ask him to turn round.

“I am going to be angry with myself in a moment,” she said.

“Why.”

“Because a field officer has one question and I have spent it, and I am going to go up that corridor and write nine lines and a tenth with a box. The tenth is going to say that I served a paper by hand to a person I found in a building and I cannot improve it afterwards. There is going to be nothing whatever in it about this room.”

“And that will be true.”

“It will be true, and it will also be the first time in six years I have written a return about a thing that happened to me instead of a thing I saw.”

She said that last part to the door, not to him.

## The Return, In Order

She wrote it at the end of the corridor on the branch counter with the lamp on the stand beside her, sitting on the edge of it with her notebook open and the return under it, and he stood about nine feet off with his coat back on and did not read over her shoulder.

She wrote the nine lines first, in order, at the speed she wrote everything.

She wrote the tenth line second.

It said that the paper was served by hand, in her own hand, by a field officer of rank two, on a person found at a branch office off a third court and not at any address, and that the officer had been willing to write that down and had not been asked to hurry.

That was all of it. There was no reason in it that was not a fact about a building, and there was not one word in the whole of the ten lines about a door, or about a lamp on a stand, or about the fact that she had been asked one question and had spent it, and she had not cut a single sentence for being unable to improve it.

“Read the tenth,” she said.

He read it.

“That is the address question answered,” he said. “A person found at a place and not at an address. That is right.”

“It is not an answer to the finding,” Runa Sato said. “The finding wants a person served at an address and you have not got one, and what I have written is a reason and not a conclusion and it will be read by a man in a building who will write *service effected* on it because a reason is close enough to a conclusion at half past three in the morning, and I could not stop him and I would not have tried.”

She squared the two papers together, the finding and the return, and put them into the bundle, and tied it, and it was about a quarter to four.

“I am going to take this to the counter before it shuts and then I am going to walk a district,” she said. “And you are going to go up and finish your floors and you are not going to say one word to anybody about any of this, and there is no form anywhere in this city that would let me ask you for that and no form anywhere in this city that would let anybody find out whether I did.”

“I will not say one word.”

“You will not,” she said, “and I did not want a promise. I wanted you to say it so that you would have to hear yourself agreeing to it.”

She went along the corridor with the bundle under her arm and her hat on, and the counter shut at four, and he went up with the bucket.

She had found him. That was the thing nobody in that building had managed in five years, and it had been done in eleven minutes by a woman who had been sent to look for an address and had come back with a person.

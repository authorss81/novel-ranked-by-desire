# The Woman In Number Four

He went up Lamplit Row at about eleven in the morning on the twenty-fourth of January and stood at the foot of the stairs with a bucket in his hand that he had not come for, and he did not go up.

Seven days had gone since a Bureau field officer carried a paper up two flights at about half past one in the morning. One thousand seven hundred and ninety-three less one thousand seven hundred and eighty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

He wanted to see the room. That was the whole of his want and it was a small one and he had walked up Cordwain to have it.

The building had six rooms let off it and a shop under the one at the end, and Corvin Ashby had been letting them for nine years, and the stair went up on the outside of the front wall with a rail on the left and the doors off a landing at the top, and number four was the second door and it stood open.

The room behind it was open too. The window had been pushed out on its hinge and propped with a chair, and there was cold air coming down the stair off the boards, and a woman was at a table by the far wall with her hands flat on it.

He could see the table from the foot of the stairs. He could see the money on it because the money was what was on the table: a heap of small silver in a cloth and a slate beside it and a pencil, and she was counting the silver onto the cloth in front of her and putting each piece down with two fingers and saying the number under her breath, and when she got to the end of it she started again from the beginning.

He stood at the foot of the stairs and watched her do that for about nine minutes.

He did not go up. He did not knock, and there was a knock available to him — anybody may knock on a door — and he understood while he was standing there that he was not going to use it.

He could not have told a person the reason except that he had no standing in this row of six rooms and had never had one and could not get one, and that a man who has written four lines that a landlord put in a demand does not get to knock on the door of the flat the demand was about.

She finished the second count. She did not look pleased and she did not look angry. She put the silver back in the cloth and folded the cloth over it and pulled it toward her and left her hand on top of it, in the way of a person who has found out a thing nine times running and would like to be wrong once.

Then she looked up at the open window and out along the row and down the stair, and she saw him.

He did not go up and he did not wave. She looked at him for about four seconds and then she got up and pushed the window in on its hinge and shut it, and that was the whole of the exchange, and he walked back down Cordwain with the bucket he had not come for.

## Four O’Clock At A Counter

There is a counter off a third court where things that have to be found for somebody are found, and it shuts at four, and he had been going into it twice a week for five years for the ordinary business of an ordinary week.

Corvin Ashby was at it when he came in, with his hat in his hand and a sheet folded in three, and the woman from number four was at the end of the counter with her sister beside her.

“Here is the finding,” said the clerk.

The finding went the landlord’s way.

It went the landlord’s way on the rent, in full, for the whole period asked for, and the clerk read the parts of it that a person in the room needed to hear and did not read the rest, which is what clerks do.

Ashby took the sheet and read the parts he had not heard and folded it in three again and put it inside his coat, and he did not look triumphant and did not look anything at all, which was the first thing Lin Hao had ever noticed about him.

“It is the right finding,” Ashby said, to nobody. “I put up the strongest argument available in this district and it went against me on a line I did not know was on the page.”

“What line,” said the clerk.

“The fourth one.” Ashby turned round. He had not seen Lin Hao until then and he looked at him properly for about a second. “It says that where a Register has nothing, it has not been given anything, and that a blank is not a claim that there was nothing. It has been at the foot of a page at a desk off Cordwain since about November and every landlord in this city has walked past it.”

“It is printed by an office,” said the clerk. “The office has one address and anybody may go and ask at it. That is the whole of what there is to say about it and I am saying it because a man just asked me where a sentence comes from.”

Nobody in the room looked at the man who had said it.

## The Difference

The rent was the landlord’s and it stayed the landlord’s. The difference was about a year of it, and it came out of the finding in a way nobody in the room could follow except one person, and he followed it because he had read the finding and the page and the four lines and had been the fourth of the four for six years.

Here is what had happened, and it took him about nine minutes to get it whole.

Corvin Ashby had gone to a magistrate with four lines off a shutter because a tenant of his could not produce a deed, and a person who cannot produce a deed has not proved anything, and that is the fourth of the four and it is the whole of them. He had built a demand on it. He was right to. The four lines have stood on a wall since a December in an early year and about nine hundred people in this city can read all of them.

And the office had printed, in its own hand and on its own page, two sentences that stand four inches apart at the foot of it, one about figures and one about a blank.

A demand is built out of an absence. Every figure in it was arrived at by subtracting what nobody could produce from what was asked for, and the fourth line is the rule that licenses exactly that subtraction, and the office has printed in the same hand and in the same quarter that an absence on a page is not evidence that there was nothing.

So the finding gave the man his rent and took the rest off him, and the taking was not a favour to anybody. It was the sentence doing to its own author what a sentence does when two halves of it are true.

Ashby wrote his defence out on the back of his own copy in about four lines. Lin Hao read it upside down from six feet away and it was correct, in the way that the best of them are.

It said that the office had no standing to be a party to anything, and that nothing printed by an office with no standing could affect a finding, and every word of it was right, and it was written on the back of a sheet that was going in a file.

“Six years of this,” Ashby said, folding it away. “You want to know the whole of it, and here it is, and I am not being bitter because I am not a bitter man and also because I would have done exactly the same again with the same page in front of me.”

“Yes,” said Lin Hao.

“I am right about the flat and I was right about her and I am right about the four lines, and about a year of rent has gone off me because a man in an office with no money printed a sentence that was true on a page that about two thousand people a day cannot walk past.” Ashby put his hat on.

“That is the trade. I have lost about a year and I am still standing in the same street, and you are the only person who will lose anything out of it, and you will lose it slower.”

He went out. The clerk went into the back. The sister put a hand on her arm and said something low, and the woman stood at the counter with her money in her hand and did not say anything at all.

## What She Said

He had been at the back of the counter for the whole hour with a plate in his coat that has his name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere, and neither woman had looked at him, and when the room emptied down to two of them she turned round.

“You read,” she said.

“I can read.”

“Then read me that paper again,” said the woman in number four. “All of it. Including the four lines, because I have been told there are four lines in it and nobody has ever said what they are.”

He took the sheet off the counter and squared it and read it to her from the top, which is what she had asked for and not one line less than she asked for, because the last thing she needed in the ninth year of being short was a man reading her the part he thought was the important part.

He read the finding, and the figure, and the room number, and the date, and then he read the four lines.

She stood quite still with her money in her fist while he read them and she did not ask him to stop and she did not ask him to go on.

“That is the whole of it,” she said. “That is what my sister said was on it. Nine years I have had a piece of paper with a number on it and now I have heard what is written on it and I still cannot read it and I will not be able to read it in a year.”

“Yes.”

“I am not asking you for anything,” she said. “I want you to know that. I am not asking you for anything and I have never asked you for anything and I would not know how.”

He stood there with the paper in his hand and had no answer to that which was true, and the truth was that she had never asked him for anything and neither had anybody else and he had built a whole office out of that.

“Who wrote the four lines,” she said.

He did not say.

She waited the length of a street being crossed, and then she nodded once, as if she had been given something and did not intend to say thank you for it.

“You do not have to,” she said. “I have wanted to know for about two years and everybody in this row has said a different thing about it.”

She put her money in her pocket, and her sister opened the door for her, and the number four door shut behind her at about a quarter past four.

He stood at the counter for a while with the finding in his hand.

A woman who had been short for nine years had just been paid nine years of short money, and she could not read a word of the instrument that paid her, and the reason she was paid was that a man had given away the only claim he had to being the only honest instrument in this city, and let a true sentence be printed where two thousand people a day cannot walk past without reading it.

He felt relief. That was the actual thing and it arrived before he had decided to feel anything, and it was real, and about nine seconds later he understood what relief is made of.

It is made of the fact that a thing he gave away in November has come back in about four months having done a thing nobody asked it to do.

*The twenty-fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three days. Less one thousand seven hundred and eighty-six, the seventeenth, is seven. Twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is twenty, and the demand came back into that room on the fourth. Less the twenty-first of January, one thousand four hundred and thirty, which is the morning a demand was put on a table with four lines on it, is three hundred and sixty-three, and thirty multiplied by twelve is three hundred and sixty and three more is three hundred and sixty-three.*

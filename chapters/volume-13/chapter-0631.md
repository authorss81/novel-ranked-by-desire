# A Division That Reads Everything Twice

Eight days had gone since a woman who cannot read walked across the length of this city and asked a man of rank one to put one line back on a board, and he said no. One thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven less one thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine is eight, and eight less thirty is minus twenty-two. A month runs thirty days and stops, so a day eight after the thirtieth of July is the eighth of August, and there is no thirty-first to carry it.

He went into a division building off a fourth court at about half past nine in the morning with nothing under his arm.

He wanted a figure somebody would check.

That was the whole of the want. He had wanted it since about eleven o’clock on the seventh of February, when a man with a coat off stood in a yard and did the arithmetic of four lines out loud on the ground, and had been getting steadily worse about it since the seventh of July.

## What A Division Does With A Paper

A division holds a round of districts and the round goes out and comes back, and everything the round brings in is read twice.

The officer reads it. She has been at the doors and she has the thing in her hand and she reads it into the round book at the end of the table. Then it comes to the desk and the desk reads it again, at the same speed, and enters it again, and the two readings go into two books that stand on two shelves about four feet apart.

Nobody lays the two books side by side.

“I did not know that,” said Lin Hao.

“You did not ask,” said Wren Kallo. She had a tray and a stamp pad and she did not look up. “Not one of them has asked in nine years and I am not going to start at half past nine with a man who has not got a bundle.”

“I have not got a bundle.”

“You have not got anything,” said Wren Kallo. “That is the first thing I have said to you today and I am going to stop there.”

## The Two Hands

Beatrix Halm came in at about ten with her round bag over her shoulder and forty-one papers in it, and she had been on the counters since a quarter to eight, and she put the good ear away from him without being asked.

“I have got the round,” she said. “Ninety-one doors on the book and forty-one papers, and the desk will have forty-one and the desk always has forty-one, and that is the whole of what a round is.”

“Sit down,” said Lin Hao.

“I have not got time to sit down, I have got to get these on the desk before four and there are two divisions after me.”

“Sit down for four minutes,” he said, and he moved round to the side of her that works without asking, the way a person does after nine years of turning himself round in corridors, and she did not acknowledge it and did not want him to stop.

He put the round book on the table and asked for the desk book.

Wren Kallo did not move.

“He is the office,” she said. “The office may ask.”

“He is not asking about a return,” said Beatrix Halm. “He is asking about a number and there is no return in it.”

Wren Kallo took him in without moving her head, then got up, went to a shelf, and brought down a ledger with a green cloth back and set it on the end of the table. It was the fourth thing on that desk that morning that had come out of her own hands.

## Six Hundred And Ninety-One

The round book said six hundred and ninety-one.

The desk book said six hundred and seventy-four.

He wrote the two of them on the back of a sheet and put them either side of a line and looked at them, and a man in a division building at about ten in the morning has a piece of paper on a table and two figures on it and that is the whole apparatus available to him.

“Seventeen,” he said.

“Seventeen,” said Beatrix Halm.

“Every quarter.”

“Not every quarter. Nineteen quarters out of the twenty I have, and it was not seventeen in two of them and it was nine in one and I do not remember which.”

## Where The Seventeen Goes

“Give me the seventeen,” he said.

“That is what I have got,” said Beatrix Halm. “I have got a gap. I have not got a person.”

She took the round book and turned it round to face him and put her finger on a column with a day against each of its entries.

“Six of them are at a counter that is not mine,” she said. “A counter of the second division stands about nine minutes’ walk from mine and people go to it because it is on the way, and the second division enters them and I enter them, and neither of us has ever seen the other’s book.”

“Four of them are the same person twice in one afternoon,” she said, “and I entered her twice because she came twice with two different things and both of the things were hers.”

“Three of them are a woman who came four times in a fortnight and put one thing down the first time and nothing the other three, because nothing happened the other three times.”

“That is thirteen.”

“Yes.”

“And four.”

“And four,” said Beatrix Halm, “and I have been at this counter nine years and I have never worked out where four of them are, and I have stopped expecting to.”

## The Part Nobody Compares

He looked at the two books on the table for a while.

The division had read every one of those papers twice. Every paper that came in off the round had been read by a woman holding it in her hand in a district and then read again at a desk by a different woman with a tray, and both readings were correct, and the two correct readings had been sitting four feet apart in two books for nine years and nobody had ever laid them together and said *are these the same number*.

“That is the whole of it,” he said. “You read it twice and you never check it.”

“We read it twice because the rule says read it twice,” said Beatrix Halm. “The rule is about the paper and not about the figure. A paper can be read twice by two people and both of them can be right about the paper and disagree about how many, and there is no rule in this city that makes anybody put the two together.”

“There is a rule for that.”

“There is a rule for everything and there is a rule for that,” she said. “The rule for that is a return with a place on it for both numbers, and a return has one column and about two inches of ruled space in it, and one of the numbers has always been the one that goes out.”

## What He Understood Standing There

He stood at a table in a division building and understood something he had not understood on the seventh of February, when the arithmetic of four lines had been done on the ground in a yard.

The fourth of those four lines says that a thing is not on a page anywhere in this city.

He had always taken that to be a claim about his own deed. It was a claim about every deed, and it had a second half he had never once looked at, and the second half was that a city which cannot check a thing does not stop counting it.

Six hundred and ninety-one and six hundred and seventy-four, and both of them correct, and the seventeen neither of them.

About seventeen people a quarter went into a division building in this city and were counted twice and did not exist once, and there is no instrument anywhere in Ostara that can find them, and it is not that anybody refused them. Nobody refused them. It is that there is nowhere on the paper for a person who comes to a counter and is entered at a counter three streets away.

He wanted to check a figure. He had come into a building that reads everything twice.

He had a want he had not had before, and it was the plainest and the worst of the ones he owned: he wanted the seventeen.

## The Desk And Its One Question

He went along to the desk at about eleven because he had been in that building twice in five months and neither time had anybody put a question to him, and he had wanted one both times.

Wren Kallo had the tray and the pad and the forty-one papers coming across one at a time.

“I am not going to ask you anything,” she said.

“You do not have to ask me.”

“I know I do not have to,” said Wren Kallo. “I have not said that is why. A desk takes one question of a thing and only one, and it does not spend it on a person, and the one I have got has been standing here since the spring and it is not about you.”

“You have had a question since the spring.”

“I have had one since the spring,” she said, “and it is not about you, and you have been in this building twice, and on both of those mornings I have had it in my hand.”

“What is it about.”

“It is about a return and it stays with me,” said Wren Kallo, and turned a paper round and read the next one.

He went away from the desk about four feet off it, which is as close as he has ever got to being told no in a building full of people who would have told him yes in a worse voice.

## What She Counts And Does Not Write

Beatrix Halm was putting the round bag away at the end of the table when he came back.

“Ninety-one,” she said, without being asked.

“What.”

“Ninety-one people came to a counter of mine in the last three months and were not entered,” said Beatrix Halm. “Ninety-one. I have been counting them since about the second year and I have never once written the figure down.”

“Why not.”

“Because a return has a column in it for what was entered and no column at all for what was not,” she said, “and I am a person who enters what I am given, and if I put a number in the middle of a return that is not what the counter gave me then I am not entering, I am improving, and I am not permitted to improve and I cannot be asked to hurry.”

“You have been counting them for four years.”

“On my fingers, at the end of a round, in a lane,” said Beatrix Halm. “I have never once been wrong about it and I have never once shown anybody, and if I show you then it is a figure said out loud in a room and there is no instrument in this city that will tell us in nine years whether I made it up.”

“I would not do that to you.”

“No,” she said. “You would not. And I want you to hear the shape of it, because you are the only person in this city who has spent five years being careful with a number and you have never once had a reason to be careful with a person instead.”

## One Hundred And Forty-Four

“One hundred and forty-four,” she said. “That is what it comes to and I want you to hear how it comes to it, because it is not a sum. I do not multiply. I count forwards, one round at a time, out loud, on my fingers, at the end of a round in a lane, and I add the round on to the last one and I go on until I have said a hundred and forty-four, and then I start again.”

“That is a bad way to hold a figure.”

“It is the only way to hold a figure I have ever had,” said Beatrix Halm. “I could not tell you what a quarter is worth. You cannot divide it back out. There is nobody in this building who could stand there at four o’clock and check a quarter of ninety-one and that is the entire reason I am the only person who knows it.”

“All right,” said Lin Hao. “Say it again the way you count it.”

She counted it again on her fingers, which took about nine seconds, and got the same figure, and he wrote it down, and the two of them looked at the piece of paper.

The figure at the head of the standing page is eight hundred and fifty-two and it has not moved since the thirtieth of March of a year he does not name in this sentence, and the second column has two lines in it and one of them is his and the other one belongs to a woman who cannot read.

There is no column on that page for ninety-one, and there is no column on it for the seventeen either, and the twenty-third of those pages goes out at four on the thirtieth of September, which is fifty-two days after this morning, and about nine people a week in this city will ever get to the bottom of it.

“You cannot put it on a page,” said Beatrix Halm.

“No,” he said.

“Then it is the second time somebody has told me that in about nine years and the first one was a woman who could not read,” said Beatrix Halm, “and I am not going to be bitter about it at eleven in the morning in front of a man who is not going to be able to do anything.”

She put the bag on her shoulder.

“Ask me again in September,” she said. “If you have found a column by then I will give it to you in the morning and I will not make you wait for four. And if you have not found a column then it is the third time and I will stop counting, because there is no point in being the only person in a division who knows a figure and it is the fourth number in this city that nobody can check.”

*One thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven days. Less one thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of July, is eight. Eight less thirty borrows twenty-two days of the thirtieth of July and leaves eight, and a month is thirty days with no thirty-first.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is two hundred and fourteen days, and that is seven months and four days. The round book says six hundred and ninety-one and the desk book says six hundred and seventy-four and the difference is seventeen, and the two books stand four feet apart on two shelves and have never been laid side by side in nine years.*

*Less the thirtieth of June, one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, is thirty-eight days, and the twenty-second sheet went out at four on that day, and the next page in this city goes out at four on the thirtieth of September, which is day two thousand and thirty-nine, and two thousand and thirty-nine less one thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven is fifty-two.*

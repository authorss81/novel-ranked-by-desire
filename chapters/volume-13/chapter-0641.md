# A Chart With A Line Going Down

Nine days had gone since he had stood nine feet off a board in a yard and left a figure box on a form at the foot of a wall empty on purpose, and three streets of the city had not been the same since. Two thousand and sixty was the twenty-first of October and the thirtieth is day two thousand and sixty-nine, and two thousand and sixty-nine less two thousand and sixty is nine, and thirty less twenty-one is nine by the month count.

He went to the ward at about two in the afternoon on the last working day of October, and he went up the two flights of the main stair slowly and he counted them, because the count was the thing that had gone wrong.

Not the breathing. He could get up a stair. It was that by the second landing there was a point at which he used to arrive at the top of a flight in one go and now arrived at it in two, and between the two there was a space of about four seconds in which he was a man standing on a landing, and he had been standing on it about nine times since the middle of the month.

## What Was In The Room

Six beds along it and four of them had somebody in them, and the sister came through with a screen and went down the far end without hurrying.

Tessa Okonkwo was at the desk at the far end with the drawer open and a count in it and the lamp turned low, and she looked up, and she shut the drawer with one hand and came down the ward toward him the way she came down a ward, which is at the speed of a woman who has decided how long the crossing is going to take.

“You are four seconds slower than you were a fortnight ago,” she said, before he had said anything. “I watched you do it on the second landing and you thought I did not.”

“You did not.”

“I did,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “Sit down on the end of the bed at the wall and take your coat off the shoulders and not the arms, because there is somebody coming down that ward behind you and I would rather she did not have to go round.”

He sat on the end of the bed and took his coat off the shoulders.

She put two fingers on the side of his neck and kept them there for a count of about nine, and she looked at his hands, and she turned one of them over and looked at the inside of the wrist the way a woman looks at flour on a board.

“Your chest is not the whole of it,” she said. “Sit up. Breathe out. Again.”

He did it twice.

“Right,” she said. “That is a man who has been spending something.”

## What She Takes And Does Not Say

Every building with a rota in it has that rule and it is the rule of the ward before it is the rule of any building.

She takes a share off people and she does not tell them what it was and they do not ask, and it is eleven years old, and there are about four people in this city who know it exists and every one of them has signed something.

“I am going to break it for about four minutes,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and then I am going to put it back the way it was, and I am going to tell you why I am doing that before I do it, because if I do it after then it is a thing that has happened to you and not a thing you have been told about.”

“All right.”

“The rule exists so that the women who work nights in this building do not have to carry anybody’s night twice,” she said. “You are not a woman who works nights and you are not on my rota. That is the whole of my reason and I have thought about it for about nine days.”

“You have thought about it for nine days.”

“I have been waiting for you to ask me something for about nine days,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and you did not, and I want to be exact about it, because I am about to break a rule and I do not want there to be one second of it you can say I did it to a man who came in here wanting something.”

“What would I have had to want.”

“Nothing,” she said. “That is what I have worked out. A want would have done it and you did not have one. You had a need and a need is a thing you bring me with and a want is a thing you ask for, and in eleven years not one person has ever brought me the second of those.”

He did not say anything.

“You have been coming in here for six years on a night shift,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and you have never once asked me what I take, and I have never once told you, and that has been the arrangement between two people who do not want an arrangement to break.”

She did not stop. “And on the twelfth of October a boy at a wall asked you a question and you answered it, and about nine hundred people in that yard have been asking about a figure every day since, and you have been going up and down these stairs three floors with a bucket four nights a week.”

“You are about a third of what you were in the spring and you have not told anybody and you have not noticed, and both of those are going to stop on their own.”

“I have noticed.”

“You have noticed it since about the middle of this month,” she said, “and you have not come up here, and I have known that since about the middle of this month, and I would have come down and found you if I had not been waiting to be asked, because a person who will not come and ask is a person who does not want a door opened.”

“I could not ask.”

“You could have knocked on a desk in a ward and said a sentence,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and the sentence would not have been a figure and it would not have been on any paper. That is all an ask is. Everything else in this city has convinced you otherwise.”

## The Drawer

She took the drawer out again when she said it, and it was not the drawer with the count in it.

It was a flat board about the length of her forearm with a sheet ruled across it in columns, and the columns were about the width of two fingers, and there were eleven of them, and each one had a heading at the top.

Four of the headings were names. Two of them were a place and a date. One of them was a number with a plate symbol against it that he did not know. Four were blank, and she had ruled them anyway, and she said she rules them before the person arrives because there are more people in this city who need one than there are days in a year.

She set it on the desk and turned it so the ruled sheet faced him.

There was a heavy line drawn across the foot of every column.

Nothing had ever been written above any of those heavy lines that was out of place, and the space below every one of them was clean, and there was nothing on the sheet that said what happened below the line.

“What is it.”

“It is what I hold,” said Tessa Okonkwo.

She put her finger on the seventh column from the left. It had a rank against it and a plate against the rank, and the plate was one, and there were eleven entries in it going down from the top of the column, and the eleventh one was about nine days old.

“A fortieth is the smallest share I can lift,” she said. “You know that. It is not a fraction of a person. It is a fraction of what a body can hold past the point it should stop, and when I take a share off somebody it goes into me and it does not stay behind in them, and that is the whole of what I am and the whole of what it costs and it has never once been a store.”

## The Line Going Down

He looked at the column for a while.

There was a date against every one of the eleven entries and the dates ran in one direction, down, and the spacing between them was about three weeks at the start and about a fortnight at the end, and there was no entry at all in the last nine days.

The thing that took his eyes was not the dates.

It was that the marks in the column had got smaller.

“How small is the smallest one.”

“The last one goes down about half the width of the column,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “The one before it goes a third. The fourth one goes half of what the first one went. That is not a measurement, it is how I write, and I write it that way because a share is bigger when there is more to take.”

“Was the first one big.”

“The first one went the whole width of the column and it took me about eleven seconds,” she said, “and I had to put it in at about one in the morning on a night in the ninth month of a year I am not going to name, in a room with a door shut. You have never once asked me what that was and I am not going to tell you.”

“I did not ask.”

“No,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “You did not, and that is the entire arrangement, and it has worked for six years, and it has stopped working today and neither of us did anything about it.”

The first one went a long way down the width of the column. The second went about as far. By the fourth they were about half. The seventh was a short stroke. The eleventh, nine days ago, was a short stroke and a hook on the end of it, which is how a woman marks a share that has come out of a thing at the bottom of what is left of it.

“It goes down,” he said.

“It goes down.”

“Not back up.”

“There is no line on this board for going back up,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “I have had this board since the first of February of the first year and it is ruled the way it is ruled because I ruled it, and I ruled it that way in about nine minutes at a table in this ward on a morning when I had been awake for two days, and I did not rule a bottom column, and I have not added one in eleven years.”

She took the board back and squared it to the edge of the desk.

“This is not a chart of something getting used up,” she said. “It is a chart of something getting finished. A store gets filled from the bottom. This has never been filled from the bottom by anything, and it will not be, and that is not a fault in the board and it is not a thing I have been waiting to be asked about. There is nothing coming.”

## What He Wanted

He had come up two flights with a want and it had been in him since the middle of October and it was the oldest thing he owned, which was that there might be a way to put it back.

“Say it,” said Tessa Okonkwo.

“Can it be put back.”

She did not answer for about four seconds, and in those four seconds she went down the ward and turned a blanket at the second bed from the end and came back, and she did it without hurrying and without meaning to make a point, and he understood afterwards that she had made one.

“No,” she said.

“Then what is the thing I go to.”

“There is nothing on this board for that,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and you are not going to get it here, and I want to say the rest of it out loud because you are going to go away and be more careful than you were and I would rather you were frightened than careful.”

She put two fingers on the eleventh entry.

“This is what I took off you in November of the fifth year,” she said. “It was the last big one. There has been nothing of that kind in you since and there is not going to be, because you have not had it since and there is nothing in you for me to lift.”

She put a finger on the eleventh entry. “What I do take off you from one visit to the next is ordinary, and it comes out of you, and I could put that back in you in about nine minutes if you asked me to and you would be better for it, and I will not.”

“Why.”

“Because you would be putting it back into a place that had learned to lean on it,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and in about eleven weeks you would be back to where you were in the spring and you would have eleven weeks less than that, and I would have had to tell you a lie with my hands.”

She shut the drawer.

“Four hundred days,” she said. “That is what it bought you. Four nights a week on a branch rota and three floors of washrooms and a bin room and a bucket, and a wall you went up at four in the afternoon on the thirtieth of September, and the fourth of October, and a week in the middle of the year when you were on a hill nine feet off a board for nine days.”

“That is all of it. There is no more of it and there is not going to be any more of it.”

## What Was Left In The Room

He was on the second flight at about half past three in the afternoon with the bucket in his hand, and he got up it in three goes, and the man who works the top landing was at the far end of the corridor with a lamp and did not turn round.

He set the bucket against the wall inside the door and came in and sat down at the wall end of the bed and put his hands on his knees.

Tessa Okonkwo was at the desk at the far end with a page of dates in front of her and she had her pencil out and she did not look up when he came in.

He said, “How many times have you taken one off me since November.”

“Eleven,” she said, without looking up. “The eleventh was a fortnight ago and I took it because you were on that hill and not because you asked. There is a column on that board with your plate on it and I have never once had to open a drawer for anybody in this city the way I have had to open it for you.”

She wrote an hour against a line.

“Do not thank me,” she said. “I do not take them for that and you are not going to be thanked by anybody for this, including by you, because you will want to and I will not have it.”

*Less the thirtieth of December of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is three hundred days, which is ten months exactly. A sheet has gone out at four on the thirtieth every quarter since, and this month is the last working day of the tenth month of the year and there is a sheet going out in three months that will be the twenty-fourth of them.*

*Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, is two hundred and seventy days, which is nine months exactly. The two ruled boxes at the foot of the month’s form have been empty for every one of those months, including the twenty-eighth of this one, and the one on the left is for the figure of the thing at the address and the address is this office and the thing at the address is a page and not a wall, and it is going to be empty on the thirtieth of November and on the thirtieth of December.*

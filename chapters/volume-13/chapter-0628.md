# The Price Of A Figure

Eight days had gone since about nine hundred people in a yard took a line off a board with a rag and a bucket. One thousand nine hundred and sixty-four less one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six is eight, and fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.

He came off Cordwain at about a quarter to seven in the morning on the fifteenth of July and the frost was on the rails and there were about forty people at the wall end of that lane instead of nine.

Nine stools along a wall. A slate. A man in a delivery coat. And a queue that had gone past the mouth of the lane and stood in it.

He had wanted to ask him something. He had wanted it since the sixth of February, when a man had told him that the only thing anybody in this city had ever been able to check was a figure with a date and the weather and a reason in the margin against it.

Then, in a yard, he had heard the other half of it.

## What The Lane Looked Like

He put nine figures on the slate before eight o’clock and took his grain a head for them, near enough, as he has for three years. Eleven books had become twelve some time ago. Twelve were on the ledge, and about two thousand of about eight thousand entries carried a ring.

What was different was that he was reading them out.

Not to one person. To about forty, in the open, at the wall end, at ten to seven, before he had written a single one of the day’s nine, and he was reading the date and the weather and the figure and then the reason in the margin in plain words, and he was not skipping.

“You are late,” said Emil Brack, without turning round. “You have been late every morning since the seventh of July and I have begun to think of you as a man who comes at the wrong hour on purpose.”

“I have not been down this lane since the twenty-second of April.”

“You have been down it three times since the twenty-second of April and I have watched you at the wrong end of it twice,” said Brack, “and I have said nothing about it, and I am not going to start now with about forty people standing here.”

## Why The Lane Is Full

“I want to know why there are forty of them.”

“You know why there are forty of them,” said Emil Brack. “You have known since the seventh of July, and if you have not then you are further behind this city than I thought you were in June.”

“Say it.”

“The fourth line came off a board,” said Brack, “and the three that are left are all checkable. I have had them read to me about four hundred times this week by people who cannot read them themselves, and I have listened, and I am going to tell you what the three of them say when you put them together, and then I am going to tell you what the fourth one was doing for the last four years.”

He wiped the slate.

“It changed what a person held,” he said. “It happened on a day and in a place. And another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough.”

“All three are provable.”

“All three are provable by the person claiming,” said Emil Brack. “Which means that to use the three you have to be able to say where a figure came from.”

He turned round.

“I am the only man in Ostara who can do that,” he said. “I have been able to do it for three years for a grain a head and nobody ever wanted it.”

## What Brack Started On The Thirteenth

“The thirteenth. Three days ago.”

“That is the thirteenth,” said Brack. “I opened the twelfth book at ten to six on that morning and read the margin for every ringed figure in it, out loud, at the wall end, in the open, to anybody standing there. And I did it again at twenty past seven and again at half past eight, and I have done it every morning since, and about forty people are standing here at a quarter to seven on the fifteenth because they know I do it at ten to six.”

“Free.”

“At the rate,” said Emil Brack. “A grain a head for the figures I write, which is the arrangement and has been the arrangement for three years. The reading is free and I would not take anything for it, and I want you to notice that I did not consult anybody about whether to start it.”

“Nobody asked you to.”

“Nobody asked me to and nobody has told me not to and there is no form,” said Brack, “and that is the entire reason it is happening, and I would like you to sit with how cheap that is.”

## The Question He Came Down With

“There is a demand in it,” said Lin Hao.

“There are about nine,” said Emil Brack. “I have had four of them shown to me this week by people who wanted to know whether a figure off my slate would stand up, and I have said yes to all four, and I have said something else to all four, and they have written the something else down.”

“What did you say.”

“That the figure came off my slate on a morning in the month of the year in which there was a particular kind of weather, and that I took it on a hunch, and that here is the hunch,” said Brack, “and then they wrote the hunch at the bottom of a piece of paper and took it to a rent board.”

He put the chalk down and did not come round the stools, which is a thing he did.

“You have made your figures evidence,” said Lin Hao.

“I have made my figures checkable,” said Emil Brack. “There is a difference and the difference is that evidence is what you use on somebody and checkable is what you use on yourself, and about nine hundred people in a yard have worked out which one they want.”

## The Price

He read for about forty minutes. About four people stopped and listened and one of them wrote.

Then he came out of it, put the rag in his coat, and looked at Lin Hao for the first time that morning.

“Now you are going to tell me this is your doing,” he said.

“No.”

“It is not your doing and that is worse,” said Emil Brack. “I want it straight at about eight in the morning instead of in a fortnight.”

He turned the slate round.

“Your office printed, in its own hand, in a November, that a figure in this city may be made up on purpose. That sentence is why the fourth line came off a board. The fourth line coming off a board is why a yard of about eleven thousand people went looking for a figure they could be shown.”

“I know that.”

“You do not know what you have done and I am going to tell you, because I have had eight days and you have had none,” said Brack.

“The three lines that are left all end on the third one. The third one wants a person who can be named or described closely enough. And about four hundred people in that yard have worked that out in sixteen days and gone to the one man in this city who can be shown a figure.”

“So they have come to you.”

“They have come to me,” said Emil Brack, “and I have given them about two hundred of the two thousand, and about a quarter of the two thousand are figures I took on a hunch because I could not think what a man would want to hear in the cold at ten to seven in the morning.”

“And they are going into demands.”

“About nine of them are in demands and there will be more by the thirtieth of September and I cannot stop a single one of them,” said Brack, “and there is no form on which a man with a slate can be asked to put a figure out of a piece of paper that somebody has copied down with a pencil.”

## What It Cost Him

“You have been made useful.”

“I have,” said Emil Brack. “For the first time in nine years. And I am not going to tell you I do not want it, because I have wanted it since the second year and I said so at this wall end and you were not here.” He found the chalk where he had left it and rolled it between his fingers without marking anything with it. “What I am going to tell you is what it is, and then you can carry it up your three flights and put it on a table.”

He put the chalk down.

“For three years I was the only thing in this city anybody could check,” said Emil Brack, “and in an afternoon in November you took that away on purpose. I told you what that was. It did not cost me a grain a head.”

“You were right.”

“I have been right about it every morning since,” said Brack, “and I have been right about it eleven times in six weeks, and nobody has once agreed with me about any of the eleven.”

He wiped the slate.

“And here is what nobody will notice,” said Emil Brack. “For nine years there was no instrument in this city that could take a figure off a man. There is one now.”

“What is it.”

“A lane, a queue and a rag,” said Brack. “I built it out of a slate and eleven books, and it does not require anybody to be a magistrate or an office or a person with a door onto a street.”

He looked down the lane, at about forty people standing in the cold.

“It requires a man to be read aloud to at ten to six in the morning,” said Emil Brack, “and that is a thing I can do, and that is the whole of what I have.”

## The Man Who Said Thank You

Nobody said thank you to him.

Two people said thank you to him between seven and eight that morning, because he had read a figure out of a book for them that they had been quoted by somebody else, and both of them said it about the figure and not about him.

He wanted to say something about that and could not work out what, and the reason he could not work out what is that in six years he had said out loud *there is no way of knowing whether a thing written down is true* to a woman at a wall end, and it had been true that morning, and it is true now, and the only difference is that there are forty people standing in a lane who have found out what to do about it.

“It has not changed whether a thing written down is true,” he said.

“No,” said Emil Brack. “It has changed what about eleven thousand people do about it. That is a different thing, and it is the only one that has ever moved.”

He turned back to the wall and put the first figure of the day on the slate, and about nine of the nine looked back at him when he wrote it, which is two more than has ever looked back at him.

*One thousand nine hundred and sixty-four days. Less one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, the seventh of July, is eight. Fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.*

*Less the seventh of February, one thousand eight hundred and six, is one hundred and fifty-eight days, and on that morning a checker at a counting house off Ferrier Street did the whole of the arithmetic of the four lines out loud, on the ground, in front of about nine hundred people. Afterwards a man in a delivery coat came and told a man with no instrument that the office had taken the one checkable thing in Ostara off the wall and given it away deliberately.*

*Less the ninth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, is two hundred and forty-two days, and the first of the two lines went out at four on that day with a name under it. The next page goes out at four on the thirtieth of September, and there is no page in this city between this morning and then.*

*Nine stools, a slate, a grain a head, twelve books, and about two thousand of about eight thousand ringed with a reason in the margin in plain words. On the thirteenth of July a man began reading that margin out loud at ten to six in the morning to anybody standing at the wall end, and nobody asked him to and nobody told him not to, and there is no form.*

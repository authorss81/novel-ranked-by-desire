# A Form That Says The Spring

Twelve days had gone since the twenty-third sheet had gone out at four from a room with two chairs in it, and since the man who prints it had stood nine feet off a board in a yard of about eleven thousand people and said a figure out loud twice, at the same speed, with nothing improved.

Two thousand and thirty-nine was the thirtieth of September and the twelfth of October is day two thousand and fifty-one, and two thousand and fifty-one less two thousand and thirty-nine is twelve, and thirty less twelve is eighteen.

He went to the foot of the ranks wall at about ten in the morning on the twelfth of October, which is twelve days after the last working day of a quarter and two hundred and thirty-one days after he had queued for about two hours behind a barrow of broken paving in a building nine streets away and had a form put in his hand.

He had not done one thing about it in two hundred and thirty-one days.

## The Boy With The Brush

The north end of that wall was repainted three weeks ago and the paint is still going over the two words and still going round them.

It has done that every month for six years. There is a woman who cleans four inches under the lowest rank every morning with a short-handled broom and she has never once said she has seen anybody put anything on those two words, and the paint does not take on that stone, and that is the whole of why they are there.

The boy had the month’s form on a board by the kerb with a paste brush and his own weight against it. Two ruled boxes at the foot of it: the left one for the figure of the thing at the address, the right one for the standing of it.

He put the form up at about a quarter past ten and it went up level.

About two hundred people a day stopped over the month. About two of them got to the bottom of it.

“You are the one from the works queue,” said the boy.

“Say the date you were there.”

“The twenty-first of February, and you queued behind a barrow and you were in the queue for about two hours and you did not once look at the wall on the way out,” said the boy. “I see people who do not look at it. You did not look at it.”

“No.”

“You have come to look at it.”

“I have come to look at it,” said Lin Hao, “and I have come to tell you that in the spring that wall comes down.”

## What The Boy Asked

The boy put the brush down on the kerb.

“Nobody has ever asked me what the two words are for,” he said.

“That is true.”

“Not one person has asked me that in six years and I am not complaining. I am sixteen. I have a form to paint, I get it up on the twenty-eighth of the month, and I have done that about forty times.” He looked at the north end. “You are going to ask me.”

“Yes.”

“All right,” said the boy. “Ask.”

## The Answer

He had it ready and it had been ready for a long time.

It is a sentence at the foot of a page that goes out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter, and it is the fourth of four, and it is in the hand of the office, and it is the plainest thing he owns.

“Where the Register has nothing,” he said, “it has not been given anything.”

The boy waited.

“That is it.”

“That is it?”

“That is the whole of it and there is not one word more.”

“All right,” said the boy. “Then I have not got to do anything about it.”

“No.”

“Because that is what the two words say as well,” said the boy. “I have known that since I was about eleven. They say the same thing in a different way and there is a notice on the form above them that says where they came from. I did not know it was the same until about a minute ago.”

## The Form In A Rack

He took the works form out of his coat.

It is a sheet about the size of a leases, and it is the same sheet as in February, and it has one ruled box at the foot of it for the date the work was done.

“There is the form,” he said. “The ranks wall, coming down in the spring, for making good. And a box for the day. And the day is in the spring and there is a man in a building nine streets away who puts a date in a box and sends the paper to a room where about nine hundred other papers are on a shelf.”

“Yes,” said the boy.

“And when it comes down there will be a new wall. And the new wall will be plain stone with nothing on it, and there are about two hundred people a day who stop at that corner and read whatever is at the bottom of it, and there will be nothing at the bottom of it, and they will stand there and read nothing.”

The boy did not say anything for about four seconds.

“I am sixteen,” he said.

“You are sixteen.”

“Then I am not going to be painting in the spring,” said the boy, “because there is going to be no wall to paint in the spring, and the department will have the men who do making good, and making good is not painting, and I have never in my life been given a form for making good, and I have looked for one.”

He picked the brush up off the kerb.

“So it is not going to be me and it is not going to be anybody I know, and there is no list of names anywhere in this city that says who paints a wall,” said the boy. “And you have known that since the twenty-first of February and you have had a piece of paper in your coat for two hundred and thirty-one days and you have not asked anybody anything about it.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

## Why Not, Again

“Because a question has to arrive on a paper with a room at the head of it,” he said, “and there is not one piece of paper in this city on which a question travels to a department of works.”

“That is the same thing you said in a room in February.”

“It is the same thing and it has not changed in two hundred and thirty-one days,” said Lin Hao, “and everything else in this city has changed in two hundred and thirty-one days, and that is the whole of the difficulty with me and it is the only thing I have got.”

The boy looked at him for about four seconds.

“My father would have taken that form in there and put it on the counter and told them a wall was coming down and there were words on it,” he said, “and they would have written the date in the box and it would have gone on a shelf with nine hundred others, and that is what would have happened, and I have thought about it for about twenty minutes since you told me.”

“And what did you work out.”

“That there is a shelf in a room with nine hundred forms on it and not one of them has a box for two words,” said the boy. “So it would have been the same as not doing it, and we would have felt better.”

## Nine People At The Wall End

Emil Brack was at the wall end with the slate propped against the stones and about nine people standing in the cold, and he had been there since about ten.

He read the margin of every ringed figure he has, out loud, the day and the weather and the figure and then the reason, in plain words, and he did not skip one and he did not stop to be asked.

Nobody thanked him. Two people said thank you about a figure and neither of them said it to him.

He had been there every morning since the thirteenth of July because a wall is where people stand, and about two hundred of them stop at that corner in a day and about nine hundred people a month stop at the wall end of his lane, and he had worked that out in about four days in the third week of July and had not told anybody.

“You have not been to my wall,” he said.

“No.”

“You were at the yard nine days back.”

“I was.”

“I heard,” said Emil Brack, and put the rag in his coat, and did not say one word more about it in about four hundred words, and then said: “Eight hundred and fifty-two.”

“Don’t.”

“I am not going to say anything about it. I have read it on the rail and I have read it off a hill and I am not going to say one word about it because it is not my figure and it is not my sentence.” He wiped the slate. “It is your figure. You put it in the office’s hand before either of us understood what the office’s hand was.”

He went back to the wall and put the first figure of the day on the slate, and nine of the nine turned their heads when the chalk went on.

## Three Questions In A Day Book

At about eleven he went across the city to a division building and stood in a corridor off a ground floor for about nine minutes.

Wren Kallo was at the returns desk with a tray and a stamp pad and ink gone into two nails.

“The twenty-third sheet went out at four on the thirtieth,” she said, before he had said anything. “Nine divisions, one page each, four sentences at the foot of every one of them in the office’s hand, and a figure at the head that has not moved. And about nine people a week will get to the bottom of it and about four in every thousand have ever done so.”

“Yes.”

“And three questions went out on the same morning in the same four minutes in the same city, in a day book in this building, on a desk that is not a counter and in a book that is not a return,” said Wren Kallo. “And they went to nobody. And they were in the book on the twenty-third of August when you were standing in four feet of passage behind me, and they are in it now, and they will be in it on the thirtieth of December.”

“I know.”

“You have come to ask me whether anybody has written back.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I have come because it is the twelfth of October and there is a wall going to come down in the spring and there is not one box on any form in this city that will take two words, and I have been holding a piece of paper in my coat for two hundred and thirty-one days and I have come here to stand in a corridor and say so to somebody who enters what she is given.”

Wren Kallo read the card he had not put down. There was no card.

“Go back to your coat,” she said.

## The Last Thing He Did Not Do

He stood in a corridor off a ground floor of a division building for about four minutes and then he went out through nine counters and down two flights and along a street and up to the foot of a wall.

The paint was about three weeks old. The two words were under it.

And there was a new thing at the corner that had not been there in February and had not been there nine days ago, and it was a board about two feet by three with a figure in four-inch letters and one word under the figure in six-inch letters, and there were about nine people standing at the foot of it reading.

The word under the figure was CLAIMS.

And about nine feet off that board, on the ground, on the stones, there was a mark where a board about three feet by two had stood for six weeks, and a rectangle of stone under it that had been the colour of every other stone in this wall for about four years.

He looked at the clean rectangle for about a minute and a half.

Then he went down the hill and did not say one word to anybody about any of it.

*Two thousand and fifty-one days. Less two thousand and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of September, is twelve, and thirty less twelve is eighteen, and a month runs thirty days with no thirty-first. TWELVE IS THE LONGEST INTERVAL IN THIS MOVEMENT AND IT IS NOT AN ERROR. Not one of those twelve days is named anywhere here.*

*Less the twenty-first of February, day one thousand eight hundred and twenty, is two hundred and thirty-one days, which is seven months with twenty-one days after them. A man queued for about two hours behind a barrow of broken paving in a building nine streets from this wall and was handed a form that says the ranks wall comes down in the spring for making good, with one ruled box at the foot of it for the date the work was done. HE HAS DONE NOTHING ABOUT IT FOR ALL OF THOSE DAYS AND THE FORM IS IN HIS COAT.*

*Less the thirtieth of January, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine, is two hundred and fifty-two days, which is eight months with twelve days after them, and the boy’s form for the month has two ruled boxes at the foot of it, one for the figure of the thing at the address and one for the standing of it.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and thirty-three days, which is eleven months with three days after them. It is the fourth of four sentences at the foot of a page and it goes out at four on the thirtieth of every quarter and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it, and a boy of sixteen at the foot of a wall asked what the two words were for this morning for the first time in six years.*

# Four Sentences At The Foot

Eight days had gone since a woman who cannot read had said a figure out loud for the first time in nine years and had then said it back to him, badly, and had gone home. Two thousand one hundred and six was the seventh of December and the fifteenth is day two thousand one hundred and fourteen, and two thousand one hundred and fourteen less two thousand one hundred and six is eight, and fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.

Fifteen days to the thirtieth, and a page that goes out at four, and a hole in the foot of it with a name under it.

The sheet came out of the drawer and was squared to the edge of the table at about half past two, because there had not been one in this room since the twenty-third and Wenna Raut wanted it out of the drawer and looked at, in the ordinary way, on a day that was not the thirtieth.

## What Was On It

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head, in the office’s hand.

That has not moved since the thirtieth of March of a year nobody in this room says, and this is the eighth quarter it has stood, and Yusuf Adeyemi said at ten past four on the thirtieth of September that he had watched every one of them come off this table and he was not going to let the twenty-fourth be the first.

Under it, about four hundred and nineteen lines, each one somebody’s account of six years of a street dug in the winter, with a date against it and an hour against the date and no figure and no name.

The second column had two lines in it. The first of them had been in the book since the fifteenth of October of the fourth year and was his, and the clerk’s sentence under it was still there exactly as she entered it, and he had not asked for it to be taken off and was not going to.

The second one came through a counter on the first of May of the fifth year and is not his and has not moved since and is the only line in that column anybody has ever been able to say anything about.

And at the foot, four sentences in the hand of the office, two of them since the first of February of the first year and two of them added on the ninth of November of the fifth year, one of which has Emil Brack’s name under it and is not his.

And under those four, since the twenty-third, six lines.

## The Sixth Line

“The six,” said Wenna Raut.

“The six are mine.”

“You entered them exactly as given and there was nothing given,” said Wenna Raut. “I am going to spend the one question I have this morning and I am going to spend it on the sixth line and not on the hole.”

“All right.”

“The sixth line says your name and a date and that it was struck by you against yourself,” she said. “The third of the six is the one that asks for somebody else — that there was a person, or that one was given it, and that he can be named or described closely enough — and that is a line a person can hold.”

She turned the sheet about ninety degrees. “The fourth of the six has a strike through it from the first letter to the last, and there is a space underneath the fourth that is about two inches deep, and there is nothing in it.”

“There is nothing in it.”

“I know there is nothing in it, I have had the sheet on the desk in front of me since half past two,” said Wenna Raut. “My question is whether the sixth line is a line or whether it is a second paragraph.”

“A clerk enters a line and enters a paragraph differently on this desk and I enter both, and I cannot tell you which of them you have given me. And if you have given me a paragraph then it prints differently, and the nine who get to the bottom of it will be able to tell that as soon as it is on the rail and not before.”

Lin Hao looked at it for about four seconds.

“A line,” he said.

“Then it is a line,” said Wenna Raut, “and I will rule it as one, and there is nothing further I can do with it, and I have not read it out of the book to you and I am not going to, and you knew that when you came down the stair.”

## What Else Is In The Bundle

“Three things,” said Wenna Raut. “The bundle has three things in it and the fifth thing in the last one was a letter asking this office to correct a number, and there has not been a sixth since, and I have watched the tray by the door every morning since the twenty-third because after the ninth of November I do not know what people are going to send us.”

“What is in the three.”

“One is a return from the fourth division with a figure on it and a name against it and nothing else, which is a thing that happens about nine times a year. One is a demand with an address at the head of it that is this building and a landlord’s name on it and no figures at all, which has not happened since the fourth of January, and which I entered exactly as given. And one is a sheet of paper with nothing written on it.”

“Nothing written on it.”

“Nothing written on it, and no name, and no address, and it came in through the front door on the ninth of December at about half past three in the afternoon,” said Wenna Raut.

“It came in because the shutter is bare and it is the emptiest that yard has been since the wall went up, and somebody in that yard came the nine miles to this door and put an empty sheet on a counter and walked out again. It is in the bundle because it came through the front door, and I am not permitted to leave it out of the bundle because it was empty, because leaving a thing out of a bundle is an improvement.”

He looked at the empty sheet for about nine seconds.

“That is not nothing,” he said.

“It is nothing on it.”

“It is nine miles and about half an hour of somebody’s day and it is in a bundle because a clerk is not permitted to improve it,” he said, “and it goes into a room on the other side of a door in this building, and there is no name for that room on anything, and nobody is obliged to open that door, and it has never once been opened.”

Wenna Raut went back to her column and did not look up.

“Twenty-three bundles,” she said. “I have carried twenty-three of them into that room and I have never opened one and I am not going to start in the last month of a year.”

“Has anybody ever opened one.”

“Not by me,” said Wenna Raut. “And the door has a key, and the key is not mine, and a thing that is not mine and is not anybody’s in this room is a thing that nobody does, and I have had eleven years to work out whether that is a rule or a habit and it is a habit.”

“Who has the key.”

“Ask the Registrar and he will not tell you, because he has been asked before, and he told me once standing where you are standing that he does not know.”

“He does not know where his own key is.”

“He has never gone and looked,” said Wenna Raut. “That is a different thing and it is the whole of what is wrong with this office and I have never had a column for it.”

## Ten Past Four

Yusuf Adeyemi came in at ten past four and stood, because the room has two chairs and both of them had something in them.

He read the foot from the first of the six lines to the last of them and then read the second column and then the head.

“The head stands,” he said.

“The head stands.”

“I am going to say four things and none of them is about whether you were right, and I am not going to be asked whether you were right, because that is the one question in this city I have declined for the whole of my working life.”

He put his hand on the edge of the table.

“The first is that nine people in this city get to the bottom of that page in a week, and when it goes out on the thirtieth there will be six lines at the foot of it instead of four. Nine of them will be reading two sentences that are not instructions in a place where for six years there have been none.”

“The second.”

“The second is that a hole in the middle of a printed page is not a thing any office in this city has ever sent out, and in about four years there will be a question about it.”

He straightened the sheet again. “And the question will not be able to be answered out of the book the way every other question about this office has been answered, because a clerk may only enter what she is given, and the one thing she was given was a mark and a name and nothing where the mark went through.”

“The third.”

“The third is that the twenty-fourth sheet goes out at four on the thirtieth in fifteen days and about nine hundred people will walk past the rail on that building and about four in every thousand of the whole of this city have ever got as far as the foot of one, and in fifteen days there will be a second thing in that four.” He straightened the sheet. “The fourth is that you have not asked me a question and I have noticed and I have waited.”

“I did not have one.”

“You have had one since the twenty-third,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “You have come down here five times in three weeks for a room with two chairs in it and you have not once asked me whether it was the right thing.”

“I am going to tell you the reason I have not raised it. Because if I raise it, then in about four years there will be a Registrar of this office who has an opinion about what goes at the foot of a sheet, and an opinion in this office is a thing that can be printed.”

## The Man Who Asked What A Page Was For

At half past four the sheet went back into the drawer and the drawer shut, and he went down the stair and along the street to a counter with a window on it.

The man at the desk had a sheet face up on the counter in front of him. It was a copy, brought up from the rail in the morning by a clerk who does that four times a month and puts it back the same afternoon, and it is the only copy of that page in this city that comes off a rail and goes back on one in the same day.

“Seven,” said the man at the desk.

“Seven.”

“I have something to ask you and I am going to ask it now, because I have had it for nine days and I have not asked anybody, and I have written it in the ledger, and a thing that is written in a ledger is a thing that has not been asked.”

“All right.”

He turned the copy about ninety degrees and put his finger at the foot of it.

“There are six lines under the four at the bottom,” he said. “The four have been there since before I came to this desk and I have never once been asked what they are and I am not asking you about them. There are six more and one of them has a line through it and there is a name at the bottom of the last of them.”

“Yes.”

“Read me those six,” said the man at the desk.

## Reading Six Lines To A Stranger

There were about nine people in that stretch of street at half past four and none of them looked round, and he read them anyway.

He read the first, the second and the third as they were written, because they were checkable and a man who runs a counter for nine years knows what checkable means.

He read the fourth with the strike through it.

He read the fifth, which is his name and a date and four words.

And the sixth, which is nothing.

“That is six lines,” said the man at the desk.

“That is six lines.”

“There is no seventh.”

“There is no seventh and there will not be a seventh and there is not one instrument in this city that can add one, and the page goes out at four on the thirtieth in fifteen days and the nine who get to it will read all six and there is nothing whatever on the sheet that says what any of them is for.”

The man at the desk got the pencil out of the drawer and turned the ledger round so the top of it showed and wrote four lines in it against a date, and it took him about nine seconds and he did not ask Lin Hao to slow down or to repeat himself.

“There,” he said. “Six lines, the fifteenth of December, read out at the counter by the man who wrote them, in a street, to me.”

“Why did you write it down.”

“Because in about four years somebody is going to ask me what those six lines are,” said the man at the desk, “and I am going to be at this counter or I am going to be somewhere else with a ledger.”

He got the pencil out again. “I have about thirty names in this and nine years, and I have never once had a reason to open it. I have one now. I do not know what it is for and I have not been told, and I have written it down because I have a pencil and a ledger and there is not one form in this city that will do it for me.”

He closed the ledger.

“Was the fourth one ever on a board,” he said. “Anywhere. In any yard.”

“No.”

“Then there is nothing on a wall in this city that came off that page,” said the man at the desk, “and about four in every thousand of this city has been told otherwise by a boy of about ten standing on his toes, and I am going to write that down as well and I am not going to improve it.”

He wrote for about four seconds and then he put the pencil in the drawer and shut it, which is the thing a clerk at a counter does, and he does it about twice a day.

## What Was Left Of The Afternoon

Lin Hao stood at the counter for about a minute.

“You asked me what a page was for,” he said.

“You did not answer it.”

“No.”

“Then it is not a question I have answered and it is a question I have asked,” said the man at the desk, “and I have got nine years of not asking and about nine minutes of asking.”

“I have been behind this counter longer than you have been on nights and I have a ledger, and I would like you to understand that I am not going to be able to help you with anything. I have no standing, no office, no form and no way of taking anything back. I can write a thing down. That is the whole of what I have.”

“You have written a thing down.”

“I have written four things down since the thirtieth of September and I have shown the last one to one man in nine years,” said the man at the desk, “and I have not shown it to anybody else, and there is nothing on this counter anybody can take.”

He turned the copy back the right way up and put it back on the rail where the rail keeps a sheet.

“Twenty-three bundles,” he said, “and one empty sheet in the twenty-third, and a page with a hole in the foot of it going out at four on the thirtieth. That is what this city has got at the end of a year, and it is not nothing, and you have been in it for six years and you have never once come down here and asked me how many of them I had.”

*Two thousand one hundred and fourteen days. Less two thousand one hundred and six, the seventh of December, is eight. Fifteen less seven is eight by the month count.*

*Less the thirtieth of December of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is three hundred and forty-five days, which is eleven months with fifteen days after them. The twentieth sheet went out at four on that day and the twenty-fourth goes out at four in fifteen days, on the thirtieth of December, which is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine, and the head of it has stood at eight hundred and fifty-two across all of them.*

*Less the twenty-first of February, one thousand eight hundred and twenty, is two hundred and ninety-four days, which is nine months with twenty-four days after them, and a form that says a wall comes down in the spring for making good has been in the coat of the man at this counter for all of them, and its one ruled box at the foot is for the date the work was done, and the work is not going to be done until a date that has not happened yet.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is three hundred and ninety-six days, which is thirteen months with six days after them. It is the third of four sentences at the foot of that page and it says in other words that a number anybody sets down in this city may be one they made up, and it carries the name of a man in a delivery coat, and it goes out at four on the thirtieth and has gone out eleven times since, and there is not one instrument in Ostara that will take it off.*

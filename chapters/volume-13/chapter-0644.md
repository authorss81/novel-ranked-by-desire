# He Strikes His Own Line

Eight days had gone since an auditor had read him four hundred and nineteen lines and had then declined to give him an hour. Two thousand and eighty-four was the fifteenth of November and the twenty-third is day two thousand and ninety-two, and two thousand and ninety-two less two thousand and eighty-four is eight, and twenty-three less fifteen is eight by the month count.

There are seven days in a month of thirty days between the twenty-third and the thirtieth, and the twenty-third is the last working day of the month before the one the sheet goes out on, and the sheet was on the table in the room off a third court at about ten in the morning with the foot of it not yet written.

He had been in that room at four every quarter for six years and he had never once written anything on it. He had written a sentence at the foot of it on the ninth of November of the fifth year because a man in a delivery coat asked him to on a stair in the third year, and the two sentences that had been there since the first of February of the first year had been in an office’s hand and not his, and were the office’s.

“I want the foot,” he said.

Wenna Raut did not ask what for. She put her pencil down, squared the sheet to the edge of the table, and turned it so that the four inches at the bottom of it were nearest him and the rest of it was out of his way, and she went back to the column she was in.

“That is the foot,” said Wenna Raut. “I am permitted one question a day and I am going to spend it now and then I am going to go back to the column.”

“Ask it.”

“Who enters it.”

“I do,” said Lin Hao.

“Then it is entered exactly as given and I will not read it out of the book to you and I will not improve a word of it,” said Wenna Raut, “and I will not hold your pen for you, and you knew that before you came down the stair, and you have never asked me to and I am not going to be asked.”

He took the office’s pen off the counter and uncapped it in front of her.

“You are going to write at the foot,” said Wenna Raut.

“Yes.”

“At the foot and not in the column.”

“At the foot and not in the column.”

“Because what goes in the column goes in the column and what goes at the foot goes at the foot, and a page has one of each and not one of either in between.” She went back to her column. “Say that again so that I know you know it.”

“Because a line in a column is an entry, and an entry goes in, and a thing at the foot of a sheet is the only thing on a sheet anybody in this city reads to the bottom for, and there are about nine people a week and I want the nine to be able to read this one.”

“That is a reason,” said Wenna Raut. “I did not have one of those before eleven this morning and I have one now, and I am going to be permitted to enter it and I am not going to be permitted to have an opinion about it.”

“That is the most I have ever had.”

“That is the most anybody in this room has ever had,” she said, “and I have been in it eleven years and you have been in it six, and we have both spent the whole of it being permitted.”

“Do you want to be thanked.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “I want it entered. Being thanked in this room is a thing that happens to a person about nine times and every one of the nine was about a bundle that arrived early.”

“Then what do you want.”

“I want to be able to say in about four years that I entered it exactly as given and that I was not asked to improve it,” she said, “and that is in a private note I keep about myself and not about you, and you are not getting a copy of it.”

## Six Lines

The foot of a sheet is four inches deep. There were four sentences in it already, set by an office, and he wrote under them.

He wrote the four lines out as he had entered them on the fifteenth of October of the fourth year, in his own hand, in the hand of the office, because the office’s hand is his hand and has been since the first sheet of all.

*It changed what a person held.*

*It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name.*

*Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough.*

*And it is not on a page anywhere in this city.*

He wrote the fourth one the way he had written it, and then he took the nib back to the start of the line and drew one stroke through the whole of it, from the first letter to the last, in a single movement, at the same speed, with nothing improved.

Then he turned the nib ninety degrees and wrote under it:

*Lin Hao. Twenty-third of November. Struck by me against myself.*

And then he stopped, and put the cap back on the pen, and put the pen down on the counter, and did not write anything in the space where the fourth line had been.

That space was about two inches deep and stayed two inches deep.

“You have left a hole in the middle of it,” said Wenna Raut.

“Yes.”

“That is not how a line is taken off a page.”

“There is not one instrument in this city that takes a line off a page in the office’s hand, and that includes the person who wrote it,” said Lin Hao, “and it includes me, and that is why I have done it in the place where about nine people a week will see it instead of in the place where the other sentence is.”

## What The Registrar Said

Yusuf Adeyemi came in at about half past ten and stood rather than sat, because the room has two chairs and one of them had a man in it and the other had the sheet on it.

He read the six lines from the top of the foot to the bottom of it. He read them the way a man reads a thing that is going into a book, which is at the ordinary speed.

Then he said, “That is entered.”

“That is entered.”

“I am not going to tell you that it was necessary,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “I have no standing to tell you that and neither has anybody else in this building, and I am the only person in this city with a title that nobody will tell you the meaning of and I am not going to start tonight.”

He put his hand on the edge of the table.

“I will tell you three things that are true and none of them is comfort. The figure at the head has not moved in eight quarters and it is not going to move on the twenty-fourth sheet, and I have read that sheet at four o’clock every quarter for six years and about nine people a week get to the bottom of it and one of the nine is me.”

“I did not know that.”

“You did not ask,” said Yusuf Adeyemi. “The second thing is that an entry can be made and an entry cannot be taken back, because the only instrument that could take one back is a person being wrong about a thing that is already written down, and this city has never built one of those and I have looked.”

He counted them on his fingers and got to three. “The third thing is that you have made a hole in the face of the most widely read page in this city and there is nothing at the bottom of it. I have been at this desk for about nine months and I have never in my life seen a page with a hole in it go out at four, and I do not know what happens on the rail.”

He straightened the sheet by a quarter of an inch.

“Has the man at the counter read it.”

“He has not,” said Yusuf Adeyemi, “because it is not on the rail until the thirtieth and it does not go on the rail until the thirtieth whatever we do to it here, and that is the one mercy in this building.”

“He will read it.”

“He will read it and he will not understand a word of it and he will not say so,” said Yusuf Adeyemi, “and in about four years somebody with a pencil and a ledger is going to ask him what those six lines are, and he will not be able to tell them, and that is the shape of this and it has been the shape of this since the ninth of November of a year I am not going to name.”

“I am going to go and stand at the window,” he said.

## What The Fourth Line Was Carrying

Three of the four can be checked by the person making the claim.

It changed something. That is checkable against a body. It happened on a day the person can name and in a place the person can name. That is checkable against a calendar and a street and about four in every thousand people in this city can check one of those to the day.

Another person was there, or was given it, and that person can be named or described closely enough. That is checkable against a counter, and about nine times in ten, coming out of the yard this office has been named in, the somebody in question is a landlord.

The fourth one was a door.

And the claims that came through it were always the claims of people who could not read.

That is not sentiment and it is not a thing anybody has said out loud in a room in six years.

It is what the third of the three does when it cannot be done at a counter, and it is a plain arithmetic of the instrument. A claim wants a page, and a person who cannot read a lease, a demand, a finding or a sheet has never been able to put one of anything anywhere, and the only instrument that ever existed in this city for a person like that was a sentence that said a thing which is on no page at all.

He had written it in about eleven seconds.

## The Woman In Number Four

On the seventh of July about nine hundred people in a yard took the fourth of four lines off a board with a rag and a bucket and did not ask the man who wrote it.

On the thirtieth of July a woman walked across the city and asked him to put it back, and he said no, and she went away, and she is the only person alive who has ever won anything off his four conditions and she cannot read.

She won about a year of money in January of this year, off a sentence she cannot read, because a demand is built out of an absence and the office printed that an absence is not evidence of nothing. That win stands. It was on the first three and the third of the three has been finishing arguments about landlords in about nine minutes ever since.

The fourth one did nothing for her. She never used it, because she could not put it in a document, and a document is the only place a claim of hers has ever been able to live.

What the fourth line has done, in six years, in a city with nine divisions and about eleven thousand people in a yard, is this.

It has let a person who cannot read stand in a room and say: this happened to me, and there is nothing written down, and the nothing is the proof.

From this morning that sentence is not in the office’s hand any more, and it is not in any other hand in this city, because the only hand it was ever in was this one.

A woman off Lamplit Row who cannot read a lease she signed and has known she was short every month for nine years can now say almost anything at all.

She cannot say that anybody took anything off her.

## Four O’Clock And A Barer Corner

He went down the hill at about half past four and the Vestry door was shut and the man beside it was beside it, and he did not look towards it, and neither did about eleven thousand other people in that yard.

The shutter at the far end of it was bare.

Not cleaned. Bare. There was a board about three feet by two and a board about two feet by three and the old whitewashed shutter behind them, and every one of them had been taken off, and the shutter had nothing on it, and there was a pale rectangle on the wood where the last one had hung and it had been there about six weeks.

He stood nine feet off it.

The three lines had come down in a day, one at a time, over about nine hours, on the ninth of November, and about nine hundred people had done it, and the man with the rag had not been among them because the man with the rag had not been there since the thirtieth of September, and he had told a man in a room in the fourth week of September that he would not be at the foot of that board again.

Nobody had asked him. A notice had gone inside the Vestry door on the eighth with a bit of drawing pins in it, and it had said that anybody who wanted to object to the fourth line should write to the address at the head of the board, and on the ninth there was nothing left at the head of the board to write to, so the question had answered itself.

The woman of about sixty was standing where the board with the figure on it had hung, with a rag in her hand and white dust on the front of her coat, doing nothing and going to go on doing nothing.

“They came down on the ninth,” she said.

“I can see that they did.”

“I did not put a notice up,” said the woman of about sixty. “I want that said, because in about two years there is going to be somebody who says a woman of about sixty took them down, and I did not, and I have been standing here every morning since the ninth telling about nine hundred people who come down this path a week that there is nothing to stand in front of them with, and that is the whole of what I have got.”

He stood nine feet off and did not say anything.

“They will ask me what the number meant,” she said. “They will ask me this week and they will ask me in the spring and they will ask me when there is a new wall.”

“And I have told about two hundred of them that it was how many people there are owed something, and it was not, and I said it because it was the only thing a number like that has ever meant in this yard. I would like it written down that I said that.”

“Nobody is going to write it down.”

“No,” said the woman of about sixty. “That is what I have learned this year and it has cost me a board and about four hundred people and I am not going to stand here and be a person about it, so I am going to go and buy coal.”

She went up the path.

He stood there for another four minutes.

A man stopped about nine feet off with a child on his shoulders, and looked at the shutter, and then at the pale rectangle where a board had hung.

“Was there something on this.”

“There were four lines on it in July and there were three on it in September, and there has been nothing on it since the ninth of November.”

“Who took them off.”

“Nobody took them off. They came off.”

The man thought about that for about four seconds.

“That is worse,” he said.

“Yes,” said Lin Hao. “It is.”

The man wrote it on the back of his hand with a piece of chalk he had found on the kerb, and the child looked at the writing and could not read it, and the man carried the child up the path and did not look back.

About eleven thousand people walked past the foot of that shutter in a day and two thousand of them could not get by it without reading what was on it, and there was nothing on it, and there would be nothing on it in the spring either, and there is not one instrument in this city that can put four lines back on an empty board, including the four lines.

*Less the fifteenth of October of the fourth year, one thousand three hundred and thirty-four, is seven hundred and fifty-eight days, which is twenty-five months with eight days after them, and the four lines he struck this morning were entered against himself in a book in that year and a clerk entered one sentence under them saying that three of the four were met and the fourth was not. *Less the seventh of July, one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is one hundred and thirty-six days, which is four months with sixteen days after them, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth of four off a board themselves with a rag and a bucket in about eleven minutes and he stood nine feet off and said nothing, and on the ninth of November they came back for the other three and did it in about nine hours.*

*Less the thirtieth of December, one thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine, is three hundred and twenty-three days, which is ten months with twenty-three days after them. The twentieth sheet went out at four on that day and the twenty-fourth goes out at four on the thirtieth of December, which is day two thousand one hundred and twenty-nine, and that is thirty-seven days from this afternoon.*

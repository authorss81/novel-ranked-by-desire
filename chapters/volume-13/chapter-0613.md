# The Sixth Time She Is Asked

Seven days had gone since nine divisions put nine lines on a returns desk and one of them was a sentence he had made true on purpose. One thousand eight hundred and fifty-three less one thousand eight hundred and forty-six is seven, and the month count gives the same seven.

He came down at about half past ten in the morning on the twenty-fourth of March with nine returns in a bundle under his arm, and went down a stair off a court, and then down two more flights that went under a sales floor and came out in a corridor with a counter at the end of it that sells nothing and is used for the storage of paper.

The corridor had a door at the end that stood open because the room behind it had four people in it and no window and had been opened for the air since about the middle of the morning.

## Two Floors Under A Sales Floor

She was at the far table with her back to the door, which is how she sat, and she had a folder open in front of her and her coat still on, and she did not turn round when the door opened, and she knew who it was by the sound his boots made on a concrete floor.

“Six of the nine divisions have ever sent me anything of their own accord,” said Sun Meiling, without turning round. “The seventh has a counter that closes at eleven and the ninth has not come down for three quarters. That is my book and not the finding’s, and I have stopped putting a line in for the ninth.”

“That is not why I am here.”

“No,” she said. “It is why I said something. Put the bundle down anywhere. There is no room on this table.”

He put it on the floor against the leg of the table, and there were four people in that room and none of them looked at him, and the one at the end nearest the door was reading a column of figures in a ledger with a pen in his hand and going very slowly.

At the near end of the room, where the keeper sits, there was a table with a book on it and a strap lying beside the book and a lamp turned down and a nib on a string, and none of it had been touched since the morning.

“You had a question when you came down,” said Sun Meiling. “You have had one since the seventh of March and it has been in the room with me the whole of the time and I have watched you not ask it four times.”

“I did not come to ask it.”

“You came to look at whether the record is in this building,” she said, and she turned round then, and she put her pen down on the folder, which is a thing she does when the thing in front of her is about to stop being the thing she is thinking about. “It is not. That is the fourth time anybody has come down here to look at it.”

He stood in the middle of a concrete floor with a bundle at his feet.

“I want to ask you something,” he said.

“You are allowed to. Nobody is with me and I have no standing and I am not on the roll and there is nothing in this city that would stop a man asking me a question at my own table.” She said it fast and level and without any of it being a complaint. “Say it.”

“What are you doing at the far table.”

## Four Years Of Ready Answers

She took him in for about two seconds and then laughed, once, which is not a thing she does and which she did not enjoy.

“That is it,” she said. “That is the whole of it. Four years, and the thing you have come down two flights to ask me is what I am doing at my own table.”

“Yes.”

“Five times,” said Sun Meiling. “That is the sixth time anybody has put a question to me at this table in the six years I have had it. The first was about a transfer that carried no name. The second was about a composition. The third was about a man at the middle of a set.”

“The fourth was about a quarter that begins on the first of July. The fifth was about a line in a coat, and you did not ask me that one, somebody asked me that one, and it was not you, and I answered it in four words and it has been the correct answer ever since.”

“And this one.”

“This one I have had ready since about the second year,” she said, “and I have never once been asked it, and I am going to answer it now and I am going to answer it properly and you are going to have to sit down, because it takes about four minutes and I am not saying it to the air.”

There was a stool against the wall that the man on the end had not moved in about a year. He brought it over and sat on it with the bundle still on the floor.

## The Order Of The Folder

She opened the folder out on the table and squared the edges of it with two fingers of her left hand, and did not turn any part of it round to face him.

“There is a rule about this folder and nobody has ever written it down,” she said, “and it is the only rule in this building that I have never had to explain twice, and I am going to explain it once now and then I am going to follow it.”

“Go on.”

“A folder is taken in the order it was opened,” said Sun Meiling. “Not in the order anybody wants it. Not in the order it is useful. In the order it was opened. A thing goes in at the top when it comes in, and a thing that came in in the second month is above a thing that came in in the fifth month, and it stays there for as long as it is in the folder.”

“That is a filing rule.”

“It is not a filing rule and I will tell you why it is not, and it is the reason I have it,” she said. “Every rule in this building is about finding a thing again. That rule is about a thing not being taken out of order afterwards.”

“Once a folder has been opened in a particular order, the order is the only account anybody has of what happened when, and if a person goes into the middle of it and takes out a thing from the eleventh month before a thing from the third month, then the folder has stopped being a record and has become a selection, and a selection can be made by anybody who walks in, and this one has been open for four years and has been gone into by four people.”

“Has it?”

“It has been gone into by three clerks and one man from a division who was told not to come back,” said Sun Meiling. “I have not opened one out of sequence since the second year and I have had three requests in eleven weeks and I have said no to all three and one of them was from a man with a very good reason and a worse face.”

She put her finger on the first thing in the folder.

“This is the top of it. It is not the one you came for.”

“I did not tell you what I came for.”

“You came down two flights with nine returns in a bundle that are about a landlord and a figure and a room number,” said Sun Meiling, “and you have wanted to get to something at the back of that folder since you came in, and you have been at this table twenty minutes and you have not asked for it once, which is new, and I am going to assume you were told in a corridor that the person at this table is easy to ask.”

“That is not why I am here.”

“Then I will stop talking about it,” she said. “Ask me for it and I will tell you no, and you will not get an argument, and then we will do this in the order it was opened, and by the time we get to the back it will be about half past twelve and you will have said the thing you came down here to say.”

“That is a threat.”

“It is a folder,” said Sun Meiling. “I have four years of one of them. Threaten me back and I will stop opening it at all.”

## Four Coats On A Hook

“All right,” he said. “In order.”

“In order.”

She took the first thing out of the folder and laid it on the table in front of her and squared it, and it was a single sheet with a question written across the head of it in her own writing, and she turned it round to face him, which she does for that one and has not done for the other two hundred.

“Read it,” she said.

It said: *Was she asked.*

Two words under that, in her hand, smaller.

“Nineteenth of June, the second year,” said Sun Meiling. “A woman came to a counter in the fourth division and gave an account of an afternoon in a room in the cold and nobody asked her one question, and she gave it four times over four months because nobody had asked her, and she stopped coming after the fourth.”

“And I went back down that counter in the ninth month and I asked the officer one thing, which was whether the person had been asked, and he said he had not been asked because the form has no space for it.”

She put two fingers on the two words.

“That is the first question in this folder,” she said, “and it is the only question in it that anybody would have to answer, and nobody in this city has ever answered it.”

“I have not taken it out of order in four years, because taking it out of order would mean that the four hundred and eighteen accounts behind the nine counters were only worth a question if there was a person standing at the table to ask it, and there is not one, and I am not one.”

“Sun.”

“I am not going to be a person at a table,” she said. “That is the thing I have learned from eleven years of sheets and it is the only thing I know. I am going to do this in the order it was opened, and the top of it is that question, and you are going to answer it, and then we are going to go down the folder.”

She took her coat off over her shoulders and put it on the back of the chair and did not hang it up.

“Now. Four years,” she said, “and I have wanted somebody to ask me that question and nobody has, and it is not the question you came down here with and it is going to cost you the whole morning.”

“Say it.”

“I am going to do this once and I am going to narrate it while it happens, because that is how I work, and because you have never in your life been in a room with somebody who described you out loud while she had hold of you, and I have thought about that for about four years and it is the whole of what I have got.”

She came round the table.

“I am going to be fast and I am going to be enjoying myself and I am going to tell you when I am, and you are not going to be asked to do anything and you are not going to be permitted to be careful, and if you stop me I stop.”

“I am not going to stop you.”

“I know,” said Sun Meiling. “That is what I have been waiting for. Go and take your coat off and hang it on the hook and come back.”

He hung his coat on a hook that had four coats on it and had had four coats on it for years, and came back, and she was already pushing her sleeves back past her elbows with the pins still in them.

“There,” she said. “Say what I am looking at.”

“You are looking at a man who is about thirty-nine degrees out of the way he should be and has been for three years and is pretending he is not.”

“That is close,” said Sun Meiling. “Say it again and take your shirt off while you say it, because I am not going to do your buttons and I have told you that before.”

He took his shirt off and she looked at the top of his back and at the front of him and did not describe either.

“Your shoulders are up,” she said. “Both of them. They have been up since you came down the stair and they will be up when you go up it, and they are up because you have been carrying a bundle of nine returns that you did not want to carry, and put them down. Not the returns. Your shoulders.”

He put them down and she nodded as if a line had come out of a column right.

“Now come here and be got at,” she said, “because I have had this once already, in a corridor at half past one in the morning in the middle of January, with a woman who writes down what she saw, and that was the best hour of my life and I have not enjoyed anything since.”

“And I am not going to stand here and tell you I have been waiting four years for the right one. That is not what has happened. I have been waiting for anybody at all.”

She got his belt open and pushed the trousers down off him and took hold of him and looked down at it, and there was nothing in her face at all except a woman checking a thing against a figure she already had.

“Same as a January morning,” she said. “Do not tell me anything about a January morning.”

He did not tell her anything about a January morning.

She sat down on the edge of the far table and drew him in and took him into her mouth straight away, working at him fast with her other hand at the back of his head holding him at the height she wanted and not letting him move it, and she told him what she was doing in the same flat set-reading voice she used on a column of figures, and she was enjoying herself so openly that it was like being in a room with somebody’s hands.

“Slower,” she said, and went slower. “No. Back to that. That. You are not going to be careful and I am going to tell you every time you try.”

He put his hand on her spine where the line of work ran across the top of it, and she felt the hand and pushed back into it without breaking off what she was doing.

“I have had a folder open for four years,” she said, into him, “and about two hundred things in it and eleven years of sheets on a wall that is not in this room, and you are the first object in this city I have ever wanted to keep in a drawer.”

She came off his mouth and got up onto the stool and pulled him round and up onto her and rode him with her heels hooked over the edge of the table and her face open and her mouth going, and he got both hands under her and held her and she let him for about nine seconds and then took her own weight back off him and went faster.

“There,” she said. “You are doing it. Your chest is going about half as deep as it should and I can hear it from inside this and I am not going to ask you about it, and if you tell me about it I will stop.”

“I am not going to tell you about it.”

“Good,” said Sun Meiling. “Then do that again, and put your hand where I am wet, and do not be careful about it, because I have spent eleven years being handled politely in rooms where nothing was ever going to be said out loud, and I am extremely tired of it.”

He put his hand where she told him and she swore and took it off him and held it behind her own back and worked herself down onto him and said his name once and then said something else instead.

She came all of it going at once from the root of her outwards and then letting go, with her face against the top of his head and her fingers locked in the hair at the back of it, and the sound of it filled a room with a low ceiling and did not go through a wall.

He spent inside her and she stayed on him with both arms round his neck and her forehead against his jaw, breathing hard enough that he could hear it over the floor of the sales floor above them.

Then she laughed, once, into his throat.

“Right,” said Sun Meiling. “That is the second time in my life and the first time I have not had to write anything down afterwards.”

## The Ninth Of Eleven

She got her clothes together and did it fast and did not ask him to turn round, and she sat down on the stool with her cuffs not yet pinned and put the folder square in front of her again and put the single sheet back at the top of it, in order, above everything.

“Now you may ask for it,” she said, “because we are nearly at the back and you have been not asking for about forty minutes and it has been the longest forty minutes of my working life.”

“The back of the folder.”

“The back of the folder is eleven things and none of them is a deed, and the one you want is the ninth of them, and it is the only thing in two hundred sheets that anybody has ever asked me for, and I have never taken it out for anybody.”

“Then take it out.”

“In order.”

“It is the ninth,” he said. “There are eight in front of it and we have been through one.”

“We have been through one,” said Sun Meiling, “and the eight are about a composition of premises and a consult that carries no name and a quarter that begins on the first of July and four things a man from a division wanted in the eleventh week.”

“They are in the folder in the order they arrived and they will stay in the folder in the order they arrived, and you are not going to make me break it, because you have not got anything to put against it except that you came down two flights.”

He put his hand on the ninth of eleven.

She put her hand over his.

“No,” she said. “Not that one.”

## A Folder Takes What It Is Given

There is one thing in this folder that was put in it by a person who is not a clerk, and it is not the ninth thing, and she told him about it in her own mouth before he could get to the ninth of eleven, which is what she had been doing since he came down the stair.

“There is nothing in here about your brother,” said Sun Meiling.

He took his hand off the paper.

“There has never been anything in here about him,” she said, “and there is not going to be, and I have known that since about the second year and I did not put a page about him in because nothing about him was ever given to me, and a folder takes what it is given. That is not a rule I made up to keep him out of it.”

“I went after him. I asked four heads of house and I asked a fifth and I asked a man on a floor of a building who does not come down a corridor, and every one of them told me there was no man at the middle of it, and they were right, and I have spent four years with a folder and not one page about him in it.”

“Sun—”

“And you are not going to do what you have come down here to do about that,” she said, “and I want you to hear how little I care about that, because you are about to say something careful. I do not care.”

“He got up in a full hall and told a room of people the number was wrong, and he was right, and you have been carrying the objection about inside your own chest for six years thinking it was a question about a figure. It is not a question. It is not a figure. It is not in this folder and it is not going to be in this folder while I am sitting at this table.”

She squared the folder.

“Ask me the thing you came down for, or go up the two flights, and I will not hold either against you and I will not think less of you for either.”

He sat on the stool with his shirt on and nine returns on the floor at his feet and did not ask her for the ninth of eleven, because it had turned out that the thing he had come down two flights for was not in the folder at all, and that the thing in the folder was a question nobody had ever asked anybody, and that the woman at the far table had been waiting four years for somebody to ask her what she was doing.

## Twenty-Two Stitches In A Coat Lining

“You still owe me the rest of it,” she said. “That is not a complaint. That is a woman asking.”

“What.”

“You asked me what I am doing at this table,” said Sun Meiling, “and I told you what I am doing, and I have not yet said why, and it is because I am waiting for a person to ask.”

“For what.”

“For the one question,” she said. “I have put it at the top of that folder and I have put it into four years of answers about shapes, and not one of them was the question. Was she asked. Was he asked. Was anybody in this city ever asked before anything was written down about them.”

She got her pen up and squared a column of figures that had nothing to do with any of this and went back to it.

“Twenty-two stitches in a coat lining, initialled by me, on the first of September, in a book in a building with a wing in it,” she said. “That is the only page I have ever made in this city that was entirely mine, and I have not added a thing to it in four years, and it is not a body or a figure and it cannot be checked by anybody including me. You would have taken it for a weapon. It is a row of stitches.”

“It is a page.”

“It is a page in a coat,” said Sun Meiling, “and you came down here to find out what is in a folder, and there is a page in a folder and there is a page in a coat, and one of them can be read and one of them cannot, and neither of them has a name against it, and that is the whole of what four years gets you.”

## One Person And No Standing

She shut the folder and put her hand on it and left it there, and it was not a gesture, and it was the first thing her hands had done in about four years that was not arithmetic.

“Nobody has ever been asked you,” said Sun Meiling. “Not by me and not by that office and not by nine divisions. A finding on the eleventh of March wants the name of the man who wrote four lines and it is going to get it, and there is not one question anywhere in this city about whether that man was asked anything before he wrote them down.”

“I have had four years to want to ask it and I am going to ask it now, and you are not going to be able to answer it, and that is the entire reason I want to ask it.”

He did not answer it.

“No,” said Sun Meiling. “No, you are not going to, and you have had four years in which to work out how to answer and you have not worked it out.”

“I have watched about nine people try to answer it in this city and every one of them did it by saying a thing that was true and was not the answer, and you are the first one who has not done it yet, and the reason you have not done it is that you have never had the chance.”

She closed the pen and looked up the room at the four people who had not turned round once in ninety minutes.

“Go up the two flights,” she said. “Take your nine blanks to a counter by the twenty-sixth and do not put them in a drawer. And do not come down here again about a landlord, because I will tell you the same thing I have told three clerks and one man from a division, which is that there is nothing in that folder about a man and there never was.”

He sat on the stool for about nine minutes and then went up the two flights with the bundle of nine returns under his arm, and she did not look up when he went.

At the top of the stair, on the landing, he understood the whole of the morning in about four seconds, and it was this.

He had come down two flights to be asked something. He had been asked something instead, by a woman who is owed an asking and who has been owed it for four years, and the question she wanted put was not one she had invented for him, and it was the same question she had at the top of a folder since the second year, and there was no answer to it in this city.

And the rule about the order was right, and he had broken it once with his hand and had been stopped inside a second, and he had not asked for the ninth of eleven and was not going to ask again.

*One thousand eight hundred and fifty-three days. Less one thousand eight hundred and forty-six, the seventeenth of March, is seven. Taking seventeen off the twenty-fourth makes seven, a month being thirty days with no thirty-first in it.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is eighty, and thirty and thirty is two months and there are twenty days after them. The demand came into the room off a third court on the fourth, a name went into a finding on the eleventh, nine divisions returned a line on the seventeenth, and the line is due at the counter by the twenty-sixth, which is in two days, and is day one thousand eight hundred and fifty-five.*

*Less the thirteenth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, is one hundred and thirty-one, and the first of the four sentences at the foot of the standing sheet has been going out at four every quarter for that long with a name under it.*

# Which Of These Did You Make Up

Seven days had gone since Solveig Rask had put a book of her own on a table in a room off a landing and shown him what a sentence of his does to an entry when somebody reads it in. One thousand eight hundred and eighty-one less one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four is seven, and twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.

He got himself down to the end of the lane off Cordwain before six on the morning of the twenty-second of April and was standing there in the frost with his breath going up when the first of the nine stools should have been occupied, four minutes earlier than he had meant to be, and the slate was not up yet.

He had been in a lane on the twenty-eighth of February and had been told by the man with the chalk that he had never thanked him and was never going to.

He came down again on the eighth and stood at the wrong end of it for about eleven minutes and went away without saying anything, and he came down a third time on the fifteenth and stood there for about forty minutes and went away again, and there was nothing left in the morning.

He wanted to be asked.

He wanted to be asked by her, in that lane, in front of nine stools, and he had known for about a fortnight that it was not going to happen and had come down three mornings anyway, which is a thing a man does and not a thing a man admits.

## The Wrong End Of The Lane

She came down Cordwain at about twenty past six and went straight past him at the wrong end without looking and up to the wall where the nine stools are. She is a big woman and she walks the way the hands on her do, which is the way of somebody who has not stopped for a long time and has stopped.

Emil Brack came out of a door behind the lane with the slate under his arm and a rag in his hand and no coat on, because a man about to chalk nine figures in the cold does not put a coat on.

He put the slate up and chalked the first one and did not see her, and she stood behind the last stool and looked at nine figures and did not read them, because she cannot read, and she waited until he had done the ninth.

Then she said: “Which of these did you make up.”

Brack stopped with the rag in his hand.

Lin Hao was about thirty feet away at the wrong end of a lane with a wall behind him and he had a whole morning and he did not take one step.

## Twelve Books

“Nobody has ever asked me that,” said Emil Brack.

“Everybody must have asked you something.”

“About the price. About whether I am here on a morning off. About the weather, which I do not know and do not pretend to know.” He wiped the slate and did not turn round. “Nobody has ever stood at that wall and asked me which of the nine I made up, because a man who does that is telling me that he knows I make some of them up, and a man who says that out loud to a stranger at six in the morning does not come back.”

“I will come back,” said the woman. “I have come on a morning off and I am not going to tell you what I do with the rest of it. Which of these did you make up.”

Brack set the chalk on the ledge and came round the end of the nine stools and looked at her, and kept looking, because there are not many people who come down that lane at all.

“All nine,” he said.

“All nine.”

“All nine of them this morning,” said Emil Brack. “There is not one figure on that slate that anybody paid for and found out afterwards. If you had a real thing to write you would have a real thing to write, and I have not had one in about three years, and the rate is nine and the rate is going to be nine tomorrow.”

He went back to the wall.

“Come round here and I will show you the books,” he said, “and I am going to say three things first. There are twelve and there were eleven. I do not let anybody read them. And you cannot read them, so I am going to read them to you, and if you had told me that on the wall I would not have come round, and I have decided not to make a thing of that.”

## What A Man Does Not Let Anybody Read

He carried them out of the rooms behind the lane in two loads and put them on the ledge at the wall end, because there was no table. About nine people went past the mouth of the lane and did not look at either of them.

“A date and the weather against every one,” said Emil Brack. “That is not decoration and it is not for a court. It is so that if I have told a man a figure three times and it was different each time he can come back and be right to, and I can look up the morning and say which of them I was wrong on. Nobody has ever come back.”

He opened the first one and put his thumb down the column and read.

“Mother’s Day, and raining. Three years back. Four. He was going to owe me four and I wrote three, and it says why in the margin.”

“Why,” said the woman.

“Because a man with a slate who always guesses high does not last one week in a lane,” said Emil Brack. “And that is in the margin in plain words against every figure I have taken on a hunch.”

He read for about forty minutes.

He went through all twelve in the order the books were filled and read the date and the weather and the figure and then the margin against it in plain words, and he did not stop to be asked and he did not skip one.

The queue started and went round them, and about nine people a morning watched a big woman in a good coat stand at a wall while a man in a delivery coat read at her out of a book.

Three or four of them stopped. That is what the wall does.

## Nobody Ever Told Her

“There,” she said, at the end of it. “Now the other thing.”

“What other thing.”

“Which of them was me.”

Emil Brack shut the twelfth book and put it down on the ledge with the other eleven and stood up straight, which he had not done all morning.

“None of them,” he said.

“None of them.”

“None of them has ever been you,” said Brack. “There is no figure in twelve books with a street in it and there is no figure with a name in it and there is not one figure in here that I have ever been able to attach to a person, and I can tell you that because I keep the reason in the margin against every one I took on a hunch and there is no person in any of those margins.”

He looked at her and then he said the rest of it, and he said it without any weight at all, which is the way he says everything.

“Then who told you one of them was about me.”

“Nobody,” said the woman.

She said it standing at a wall off Cordwain at about ten to seven in the morning with eleven books on a ledge and eleven people going past the mouth of a lane.

“Nobody told me,” she said. “I have had that on me for about two years. I have been walking about with a figure somebody made up for a stranger and I have been the stranger, and it was not mine, and nobody ever said it was mine, and I found that out the hard way.”

Brack did not say anything for about four seconds.

“That is the worst of them,” he said. “A wrong figure you can argue about. A figure somebody gave you and then nobody can account for is not a thing you can argue with anybody about, because there is nobody to argue with.”

“No.”

“You have understood that quicker than I did in three years,” said Emil Brack, “and I have not said a word about it, because you did not ask me to.”

## The Only Man Who Can Be Checked

Lin Hao was at the wrong end of a lane with his back to a wall and he did not take one step and he did not say one word, and there was no instrument in this city that would have let him, and he would have given anything he had left to have been asked about by somebody in that lane and he was not asked about by anybody.

“Can I ask you one thing,” said the woman. “You have got a morning and I have got nowhere to be.”

“You have got one.”

She thought about it for long enough that he could see her deciding not to put it the way she had first wanted to put it.

“Is there any way in this city of knowing whether a thing that is written down is true.”

“No,” said Emil Brack.

“No?”

“There is none,” he said. “I am going to tell you why, and you are not going to like it, and that is correct.”

He turned round and put his hand on the top of the first book.

“For three years there was one thing in this city that could be checked, and it was this. Nine a morning, a grain a head, and eleven books with a date and the weather and a figure against every one, and a reason in plain words against every one I took on a hunch.”

“Anybody could come down this lane and look and say, you took that one on a hunch, and there was the reason and the day and the rain on it. That was the whole of what I had that nobody else in Ostara had.”

“And now.”

“Now everybody can look,” said Brack, “and looking tells them nothing, because your office printed on a public page, in its own hand, with a name under it, that a number written down anywhere in this city may be a number somebody invented. That is true.”

“I have said it at this wall for three years for a grain a head. And the second sentence on that same page says where a Register has nothing it has not been given anything, and both of them stand about four inches apart at the foot of a page that two thousand people a day cannot walk past.”

“So there is no way of knowing whether a thing that is written down is true,” said Emil Brack. “For three years there was a way of knowing whether a figure was put there on purpose by the man who wrote it, and it was the only thing anybody had, and your man took it away in an afternoon in November on purpose, and he did not ask me.”

He put his hand off the book, and he did not once look down the lane for the rest of the morning, which is the one thing about that hour that Lin Hao has never told anybody and has never been able to account for.

He went back to his slate and did the first one of the second nine.

## The Ninth Line Down

“There is one more thing,” she said, “and you are the only person I have asked and I am going to ask you because you said no straight and I would like to hear a no straight about the other one as well.”

“A page,” said Emil Brack. “Off Cordwain. On a desk under a window, and it lies out, and a man who runs the desk has been taking people past it for nine years and never once asked one of them what it said.”

“That is what I have been told,” she said, “by a man who does not want to be asked anything, in a passage, on the second morning.”

“Does he?”

“He said it was the ninth line down.”

Brack put the rag in his coat.

“Nobody reads the ninth line down,” he said. “I have stood at that desk four times in about two years and I have read the figure at the top every one of them and I have never once got as far as nine.”

“You can read it.”

“I can read it and I have not,” said Emil Brack.

“Read me the line.”

And the woman who cannot read stood at the wall end of a lane at about ten to seven in the morning and put her thumb on nothing and said, “Say it again,” and Emil Brack said it again.

He read it out of his own memory, from a desk under a window, at the height of a man’s chest, where it had been lying out since the first of March of an early year and about a hundred people a month had had a hand on it and not one of them had got to nine.

He read it twice, the same way both times, and there was no part of it he improved.

Nobody in that lane thanked her for it. She was not thanked by the man with the slate and she was not thanked by anybody who has read about her, and she was given nothing at all: no figure, no name, no column, no page with a column on it, and no answer to the one question she came down the lane with, which was whether there is any way in this city of knowing whether a thing that is written down is true.

There is not.

And the only thing she took away with her at about seven in the morning was a street and six years in the cold and an hour against every hour of it, out of the mouth of a man who makes figures up for a grain a head.

And it had been there all along, in a clerk’s hand, since the eleventh of July, written by somebody who had been told she could not ask the woman her name and did not ask it, and there is no column on that page that would take one if it had.

*One thousand eight hundred and eighty-one days. Less one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four, the fifteenth of April, is seven. Twenty-two less fifteen is seven by the month count.*

*Less the twenty-eighth of February, one thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven, is fifty-four, and a man with a chalk went up a stair behind that lane on that morning and said he had never thanked him and was not going to. Less the eleventh of July, which is day one thousand two hundred and forty, and is the day the ninth line down went into a book in a clerk’s hand, is six hundred and forty-one days of it standing on a page with no name against it.*

*Less the thirteenth of November, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, is one hundred and fifty-nine, and the line went out at four on that day with his name under it. The twenty-first sheet went out at four on the thirtieth of March, which is twenty-two days back, and the twenty-second goes out at four on the thirtieth of June, which is day one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, and that is sixty-eight days from this morning.*

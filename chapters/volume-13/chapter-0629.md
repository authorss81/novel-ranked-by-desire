# A Yard Without A Rule

Eight days had gone since a man in a delivery coat had started reading the margin of twelve books out loud to about forty people in a lane in the cold. One thousand nine hundred and seventy-two less one thousand nine hundred and sixty-four is eight, and twenty-three less fifteen is eight by the month count.

He was on the hill at ten in the morning on the twenty-third of July and took up his nine feet off a board that had three lines on it.

He wanted to put the fourth one back.

That was the want and it had been in him since the seventh of July and it had got worse every day, and there was no instrument in this city by which a man could do it, and he had known that for sixteen days and had come up anyway.

## What Sixteen Days Did To A Yard

The board had been re-chalked after the rag came off it, and a man with a bit of chalk had done the three lines straighter than he had ever made them, and the notice above them still named the quarter and the Bureau.

Under them somebody had propped a board about three feet by two with one sentence on it in six-inch letters.

*A THING NOBODY CAN PROVE IS NOT OWED TO ANYBODY.*

About eleven thousand people were in the yard and there was a queue.

Not the ordinary queue of people waiting for a figure. A queue of people waiting to have a sentence read to them, in ones and twos and threes, from ten in the morning, and it went from the foot of the shutter past the arcade and out into the street at both ends.

## What The Three Lines Do To A Person

He watched about nine of them.

Three young men at the front. One could read and two could not, and the one who could read was reading the three remaining lines out loud about nine inches from each face, and the two of them were standing with their hands behind their backs taking it in.

“Do it again.”

“It is three lines. That is all of them.”

“Do it again,” said the young man, “because the first one was the one I wanted.”

That is what had happened. About eleven thousand people had taken three lines off a board and carried them off a hill to about four hundred counters and rent boards in sixteen days, and about nine hundred of them could read, and the claims died on the third line.

“Do it again.”

“It is three lines. That is all of them.”

“Then read the third one again,” said the young man. “I have got to say his name at a counter tomorrow and I have been standing here for two hours working out how to say it.”

“What is his name.”

“That is the whole of it,” said the young man. “I have got him in front of me and I can hear him and I cannot put a description of him down that a man at a counter would be satisfied with, and that is what the third one says. It says somebody else was there or handed it over, and that somebody can be put into words close enough to be a name.”

“Whose sentence is that.”

“Ours,” said the young man. “Yours. You wrote it.”

What is left standing is the third line, which wants somebody else to be there or to have handed it over, and that somebody has to be put into words close enough to be a name. In the great majority of the cases coming out of that yard the somebody in question cannot be put into words at all, because the somebody in question is a landlord.

## About Four Hundred People All Day

He was at nine feet off the board by about half past ten and he was there until four.

He did not decide to do it. A woman of about forty came to him at about twenty past ten with her father, who is sixty-one and cannot read, and she said, *can you read them,* and he said the fourth one was gone and she said, *read the other three then,* and he read the other three.

That was the first one. There were about four hundred of them between half past ten and four o’clock and he read three lines out loud about four hundred times, and there was no counter, no form, no clerk and no record of any of it, and at no point in the whole day did anybody ask him whether he was willing.

He wanted to stop at about twelve.

He did not stop, because about eleven o’clock a man came and stood beside him and did not say anything, and at about half past twelve there were nine people waiting, and at two there were about eleven, and at four there was a queue that went out into the street.

Being useful, he found, is not a decision anybody makes. It is a thing that happens to a man who is standing in the only place in a city where four hundred people need something and he is the only object within nine feet of it.

## What The Yard Thought He Had Done

“You did it.”

The man was about fifty and he had a rag in his hand from the morning of the seventh and he had been part of it.

“No.”

“You stood here nine days.”

“I stood at nine feet off a board for nine days,” said Lin Hao, “and on the seventh of July about nine hundred people in this yard took a line off it while I was standing there, and I said nothing, and you were there.”

“You read them to us,” said the man. “You stood at that end on the twenty-first of June and read them out loud twice, and nobody else in this city has ever read them out loud in this yard.”

“You told them that.”

“Nine hundred of us can read,” said the man. “It is not a rumour. It is a thing that happened in the open air with about eleven hundred people standing near enough to hear it, and about four hundred of them have told people since that the man who wrote them comes up the hill and stands at the end of it.”

“Four hundred people told people something that did not happen.”

“They told people something that happened,” said the man. “That is worse and I would like you to sit with which of the two it is.”

“Then say what happened. Say that on the seventh of July about nine hundred of you took the fourth line off with a rag while I stood at nine feet and said nothing.”

“I am not going to say that,” said the man. “Because if I say that then in nine years it is a thing nine hundred people did, and if I do not say it then in nine years it is a thing one man did for himself.”

He put the rag in his coat.

“I think you did it for yourself,” said the man, “and I have got no evidence, and I am not going to go and get any, and I have said so out loud in front of you on purpose so that you know I have thought about it.”

## Why He Cannot Say It Is Wrong

“There is a page,” he said. “It goes out at four on the thirtieth of September and there are four sentences at the foot of it in my own hand with my name under one of them, and anybody who reads it to the bottom could find out.”

“How many people read your page to the bottom.”

“About four in every thousand. About nine people a week.”

“Then a yard of eleven thousand is not going to find out from a page,” said the man. “A yard of eleven thousand is going to find out from whoever is standing at the foot of the board. And that is you, and you have been standing there since ten o’clock, and you will be there again in the morning, and if you go down the hill today then nobody in this yard ever hears anything from you again.”

He did not answer that, because the man was right, and because he had worked it out on the seventh of July in about nine seconds and had not found one instrument that would carry the difference, and because being the only object within nine feet of four hundred people who needed something is not the same as being able to say a thing and have it arrive.

“Can you read them again,” said a woman behind the man.

“In a minute.”

“You have been saying that since twelve,” said the woman.

“I have.”

“And you will say it at four and then you will go down that hill and none of us will ever be able to say any of this to anybody who counts,” said the woman. “So read them again.”

## The Steps

The Vestry steps were full from about eleven o’clock.

There was a queue on them. A woman of about forty was at the top of them reading three lines out loud to about twenty people sitting down, and there was another queue behind her going back into the hall, and the steps of a church off the Vestry were where a woman had stood once a month for eleven years and said one thing and been left unanswered.

There was no room on the steps for anybody who was not reading.

He asked the man beside the door about it at about two o’clock.

“Is the chaplain here.”

“She has not come out,” said the man beside the door. “She is inside about eleven days out of twelve.”

“She is not coming out.”

“No,” said the man. “And I will tell you what I will not say about it. I will not say she has been asked, because she has not been asked. And I will not say she has not been asked, because she may have asked herself, and that is her business and not mine.”

“Has she said her month’s thing.”

“She has not stood on those steps since the twenty-fourth of June,” said the man beside the door. “That is the first month in eleven years. The queue comes down the steps at about eleven and goes back into the hall at about four, and there has not been room on them since the ninth of July.”

“You could have cleared them.”

“I could have cleared them for about nine seconds,” said the man, “and I would have had nine hundred people standing on the ground wondering what it was about, and she has said one thing a month for eleven years and been left unanswered every single time, and I am not going to be the reason there was a fuss.”

## A Yard Without A Rule

He came down the hill at about four o’clock with his throat gone and about four hundred sentences in him and nowhere to put one of them.

A yard of about eleven thousand people had taken a rule that could not be tested off its own board and had put three lines and a sentence in its place. The three lines were checkable and the sentence was correct, and together they were worth what a thing nobody can prove is worth, which is nothing at all and not one grain less.

Nine hundred people in that yard thought he had taken the rule off himself. He had not, and there was no instrument in Ostara that could carry the difference to them before the thirtieth of September, because there are about nine people a week who read a page to the bottom and one of the nine is him.

And there was a person at the bottom of the hill.

He had not gone down that hill in six years and been met by anybody who was waiting for him, and there was a woman standing four feet off the bottom step in the middle of the afternoon with her hands in her pockets, and she was not about sixty and she was not a person who read.

She had come down that hill because somebody had told her that a man stood at the bottom of it. She had walked from the other side of the city. She had been standing there about forty minutes.

“I have a question,” she said. “And I am told you are the only one anybody can ask.”

*One thousand nine hundred and seventy-two days. Less one thousand nine hundred and sixty-four, the fifteenth of July, is eight. Twenty-three less fifteen is eight by the month count.*

*Less the seventh of July, one thousand nine hundred and fifty-six, is sixteen days, and on that morning about nine hundred people took the fourth of four lines off a board with a rag and a bucket over about nine minutes, and the man who wrote it stood nine feet off and said nothing.*

*Less the thirtieth of June, one thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, is twenty-three days, and the twenty-second sheet went out at four on that day with a figure at the head of it that has not moved in six quarters, and the next page goes out on the thirtieth of September, day two thousand and thirty-nine.*

*Less the fourth of January, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-three, is one hundred and ninety-nine days, and one hundred and ninety-nine is six months and nineteen days. About four hundred and nineteen of the lines on that board are a street dug in the cold for six years with no name and no figure against them, and since the seventh of July a sentence six inches high has said under it that a thing nobody can prove is not owed to anybody, and that sentence is correct.*

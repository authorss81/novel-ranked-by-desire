# What The Hall Is For

The notice went up on the district board on the third, at eight in the morning, between the Exchange hours and a lost parcel, and he read it on the fourth at twenty past nine with his hands in his pockets and his face doing nothing at all while four hundred people went past him on their way to the rail.

It was on a frame at the east end of the square, waist height, under a wire, and it was not a large thing. A single sheet, typed, with the Office's mark at the top and the date and the room, and no name on the outside of it at all. You had to read it to find out whose it was.

"That was the design, and it was a good design, and he had spent four months learning to read rooms the way other people read faces. It took him about two seconds to work out what he was looking at."

Two women came up and read it over his shoulder. One of them said, *Twenty-ninth, Cobb Street,* to the other one, in the voice of a woman reading a date out of a laundry slip, and they walked on to the rail. A man read it standing, all of it, twice, and did not look round and did not speak. A porter came past, read the first two lines, decided it was not about him and went on.

That was all. Nine o'clock on the third had been the earliest the district board does publication, and by ten the sheet was one of forty on a frame that anybody in this city could walk up to, and it would stay there until the third of the month after, and after that it would go into a house file in Cobb Street with about two hundred other sheets on it.

Nobody had looked at him. Nobody in that square knew he was standing there, and nobody would have known at the end of the day that the man who had put the sheet there had been within four feet of it with his hands in his pockets.

Then at nine o'clock the Board repainted, because the Board repaints at nine on a Thursday whether or not there is anything new to say, and he had stood in the middle of the square and let it happen to him.

Two thousand people in the wet. The girl on the far bench doing the Thursday repaint sheet by hand. Four rows of men on ladders with tiles coming down one at a time and going back up one at a time, and the sound a name makes going up in this city is nothing at all: a brush, a board, and a man thinking about his lunch.

He found his own tile in about four seconds, because he had found it in four seconds every Thursday for four years, and his eyes went to the bottom four rows out of pure habit and found nothing there.

He went up the wall with a wet collar and his heart going like a bird in a bag, the way it had gone up it in the spring when he had been thirty-eighth on a list of ninety-one and had found a white tile with a black five on it in the third row from the top.

There was no tile.

Not a white one and not a black one and not a gap with a number under it. There was wall.

There was a rectangle of unfired board where a tile had been, a shade paler than the board around it, the exact width and height of a tile. The numbers either side of it were correct and in order. The men on the ladders were working eight feet to the left of it and had not noticed anything, because a tile that comes off and does not go back up is not an event in the Reading Square. It is a thing that happens on Thursdays.

He stood at the rail for about a minute and a half and let it be a fact, and then he went and did the thing he had come four hundred yards to do, which was to stand in a square full of strangers and find out what it felt like to be a man whose name was not on the wall.

It felt like nothing.

That was the answer, and it took about ninety seconds to arrive, and it was the most frightening thing that had happened to him since a woman with a card had read a figure off a page in a corridor in the middle of last month.

Nothing at all. Two thousand people in this square, and not one of them had looked at that rectangle, and none of them would remember it by evening. In seven days it would have been painted over by a tile with somebody's name on it in the ordinary course, and there would be no way afterwards to establish that there had ever been a space.

He had expected a hole. What he got instead, with a wet collar and paint in the air, was the discovery that a man who is not on the wall is not on the wall, and that this is not the same thing as being on the wall wrongly, and that there is not one person in this city who is going to come and tell him about the difference.

And then he understood the Office's whole method, in two objects, standing in a square.

One was a frame at the east end with forty sheets on it, and his was one of the forty, and it had gone up on the third at eight in the morning and would come down on the third of next month and go into a house file in Cobb Street after that.

The other was a rectangle of unfired board four feet up a wall, and it had gone blank on the twenty-eighth of last month and would have a stranger's name on it by the eleventh of this month, at the repaint after this one, because the Board does not leave a gap.

Two objects and six days apart, and they were the same sentence. They said the same thing about him, in the same flat voice, from the same city, and one of them was made of ceramic and one of them was made of paper, and neither of them was made by anybody who had ever spoken to him.

That was what an office does. It does not need to know what you are. It needs two places to put you and it needs them to agree with each other, and he had spent four months being one of the two places and had never once been told that there was a second one.

He had spent four months being a man nobody looked at and he had been proud of it in a way he had not examined, and he stood in a square on a Thursday morning and found out that being unlooked-at and being uncared-for are the same arrangement viewed from two ends, and that he had bought the second one without noticing that it came with the first.

He wanted, very specifically, for somebody to come and ask him about it.

Not Ines, who would read the wall and the notice and give him the price of the thing. Not Marguerite, who would price it. He wanted a porter on the second floor of a building where he cleaned washrooms to come down the corridor and say *is it true they have taken you off*, and he wanted to be able to say *go and look*. He wanted the going and the looking to cost that porter something, because a thing that costs somebody nothing is not being seen.

That was what he wanted, and it was not going to happen, and he stood there for about two minutes longer and let the square finish not doing it.

Then he did the arithmetic that nobody else in the square could do, and it was the only arithmetic in Ostara that mattered this morning.

In this city there is one instrument, and it is that wall. Everything in Ostara is legible on it: what a man is worth, what a band buys, whether a man has gone up or gone down, whether a man is held, whether a man is a question that somebody is still asking. A hundred and forty thousand people walk past it every week and know more about each other than they know about themselves.

And there is exactly one road back onto it.

The Correction Office can measure him. In public. Out loud, in a room with a hundred and forty seats and ninety standing places behind the back row and a plate by the door rating it at two hundred and thirty, with a machine in it that has never once worked in an empty room, and a hearing officer in it who is honest about his profession and tells a man that before he walks in.

That is the thing he has refused twice. It is the thing he refused on the eighteenth of last month at ten past eleven in the morning. It is the thing the form in his coat is about, and the Office has had four days of it, and the machinery is pointed at one sentence, and the sentence is *we will describe you where anybody can hear it*.

Four months of a number he could not carry, sitting in a wall in a place a hundred and forty thousand people walked past every week. Four months of being the second sentence in every conversation within two hundred yards of this square: the figure under a band under a word he had read on a sheet on the eleventh. And it had stopped, because he had refused a closure at ten past eleven in the morning and the standing had gone with it.

Four months of being publicly measured, and the entire cost of being publicly unmeasured turned out to be that nobody had to care about him.

He looked at the pale rectangle for the last time.

Then he went down the long road to the branch at forty-one minutes, and did not tell anybody.

The strip came off the machine on the fourth floor at ten that morning with eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight on it, for the tenth Thursday the figure had stood still, and Drant entered it in the register as received and forwarded it nowhere. A strip with no standing under it has no road.

The plate was three grains above a floor that had not moved either. Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two under the band. The six sat where it had been seated in the winter, on the Review instrument and not on his plate, suspended pending a description, which was not the same as lost and was a worse shape than lost.

Nothing about any of that had changed on the fourth of the month. The reason nothing about it had changed is that nobody in this city was looking at him at all, which is what he had paid four months and a floor and a calendar and three women's files for, and it had worked exactly as intended.

He got to the Vestry at half past one and sat on the stone bench outside it with his sleeve pushed up, because the bench had a brass plate on the end of it and he had never once read the plate and had decided today that he would.

He read it. It said the seat was given in memory of a woman who had carried trays up two flights for nineteen years, and he sat on it for a while with his hands on his knees and did not think about that at all.

Then the thing he had been carrying in his coat since a bench on the Vestry arcade turned over in his mind and finished assembling itself, and it was this.

In three days a piece of paper goes to every desk in the Correction Office.

He had worked that out at eleven o'clock the previous morning without knowing he had worked it out. It had been working itself out in him all day and it finished at twenty past twelve on a road between the branch and the Vestry, and it finished because of a pale rectangle, and the connection between those two things was the whole of what had happened to him this month.

One of those desks has to decide what to put on the roll.

The Office is going to walk into that hall on the twenty-ninth with an instrument and a paragraph and a hundred and forty strangers, and it is going to need something to put in front of the hall.

A hearing that has nothing in front of it is a hearing about a man in a room. There is a procedure for that, and the procedure is called a sentence. A sentence has to be built out of something, and it cannot be built out of a band and a date and a word, because that is three facts about a plate and the question on the sheet is not about a plate.

He has a hundred and twelve lines in a book nobody has ever read and a file with four thousand one hundred and twelve folios in it and one of them open, and the Office has been at his household for a month, and the Office has three desks, and the Office has four days, and somewhere on one of those three desks there is a piece of paper with a heading on it and sixteen words under the heading that were not his name.

He stood up off the bench and went in.

The corridor had four shut doors along it and a desk at the end of it with a girl at it, and the girl had been up two flights that morning with a board in each hand, and she had two days left of not carrying boards, and the boards went up at one from tomorrow and the desk at four o'clock was going to be a corridor with a chair in it.

Eight days. A roll, and a room, and a hall with a rail in it.

And a household that had spent the morning not being told anything, because he had gone out at five and had said nothing to any of them, and which would find out by the end of the week from a visitors' book, and a visitors' book did not care who read it first.

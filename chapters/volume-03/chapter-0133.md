# A List Of Acts

The notebook had been shut in front of him at the table for a month and he had not written in it, and on the seventh he got up at half past four in the morning and went down to the table with a pen and a sheet of the same paper the visitors' book was ruled on, and he did not open the notebook.

He wrote the date at the top. Then he wrote the first line and it came out in about nine seconds and he knew it was true before he had finished it, and that was the discovery, and it took him the rest of the morning to understand it.

The ledger was a hundred and twelve lines. A hundred and twelve people, in order, with a street and an hour on most of them and a name on all of them, and it had been going for four years, and it was the only record he had kept of anything at all.

It had no deeds in it. Not one. A hundred and twelve faces and a hundred and twelve streets and a hundred and twelve hours, and if a stranger took that book off him in a corridor he would learn where he had been and not once.

He wrote: the second week, the tap on the fourth floor that ran for four days and he kept running it so the plumbers would not come, because the plumbers come and a plumber remembers a face.

He wrote: the seventh week, carried a man's box up two floors of the Reading because he had bad knees and would not ask, and he did not know the man's name and would have been told it if he had asked.

He wrote: the eleventh week, a woman on the second floor sat down on the bottom step of the stair at three in the afternoon and would not get up, and he stood at the top of it for about two minutes and then went down and sat on the step below her for two minutes and went back up, and she was not there at four, and he never found out who she was.

He wrote: the third month, a boy on the third floor worked out the socket would take his plate if you turned it, and told two hundred people, and the figure on the wall that week was the highest it had been all year, and he never learned the boy's name either and thought about him for a fortnight afterwards.

Four lines by six o'clock, and he read them and understood that he had written them without any difficulty at all, and that he had known all of them for four years.

Then he could not get the fifth one, and it took him until the second-floor queue at half past nine.

The branch filled the way it filled every morning, and he took the bucket through the second-floor washrooms twice and worked round the queue with the mop the way he had worked round it for four years.

On the way round, a woman he knew by the shape of her coat put her plate down on the sill and said thank you for the sill before it had finished falling. He had his hand out, four inches from a piece of metal, and he took it back and put it on the mop handle instead.

That was not a deed. That was a thing he had not done. There were hundreds of those and they were the whole of what he was, and if he had kept the other book — the one with a hundred and twelve names in it — he would have known that he had done it before, and he did not know it now, because the ledger had never once told him about a single thing he had done.

It only ever told him who.

He wrote it on the sheet at eleven o'clock, standing at the sink, in the hand of a man writing on a wet windowsill: the twenty-eighth of last month, second floor, a plate went off a sill and I took my hand back off it, and it was four inches, and I said the sill was wet.

Then he wrote under it, because it was true and because he wanted it on the page: and the sill was wet. It was wet every morning. I said it was wet because it was the sentence and I had four years of the sentence.

He stood at the sink with the pen in his hand and worked out the arithmetic of his own record, which took about eleven seconds.

Four years. Four years of a job that is the reason he is the person the account is for, because what he does every morning is be looked through by everybody who walks past a man with a bucket. Four years of doors held and trays carried and sills wiped and steps sat on, in corridors, in front of an office full of people going past, in the morning.

A ledger of a hundred and twelve faces was the whole of it and he had written a hundred and twelve of them and he could not tell one single person what he had done.

That was not a new fact. He had known it since the Review and had never once sat down with it. What was new was standing at a sink at eleven o'clock with a sheet of ruled paper and finding that the thing was not hard to write and was impossible to date, because he did not know when most of it happened, and a man who cannot date his own good deeds cannot say how many of them there are.

He cleaned the rest of the floors with the sheet folded twice in his coat and he did not do it again that day, and at the end of the shift he wrote one more line, at the counter, in Drant's hearing, who did not ask.

The fourth floor. The discharged floor. I have cleaned it every working morning since the twenty-first of the month because it is the dirtiest thing in the building and there is not one person on it, and I have not written down why.

Drant squared the register.

"That is not a thing anybody asks you for," he said.

"No."

"Then put it in your own book and not in mine, and do not read it out at my counter, because a man who says out loud at a register that he cleans a floor for a reason is a man who has stopped cleaning it for the Bureau." He took the pen off him. "Nine years and I have never written a reason for a thing in my life. That is not modesty. It is the job."

He went home up the long road past the square and did not stop at the rail, and at seven he got out a second sheet and dated it and put it on the table, and six women came and read it, and it took forty minutes, and it was the first time anybody else had ever seen the account.

"That is not the book," said Nadia Ferrous.

"No."

"That is four lines in a morning. The book has a hundred and twelve."

"The book has a hundred and twelve faces," he said, "and I have just found out this morning that I cannot tell you what any of them is for, and that is not a thing I can put on the roll, so I am putting it on the table instead."

Ines read the four lines twice and put the sheet down.

"Two of those are witnessed," she said. "The boy on the third floor, two hundred people, the week the figure went up: that is an instrumented deed and it is scored into a branch aggregate and it is in a strip in Drant's drawer, and it is attached to no person at all. The man with the box is an office deed and it is in the same aggregate."

She put her finger on the third line.

"The tap is nothing at all, because the plumbers never came, so nothing happened. And the woman on the step is the only one on that sheet that has never been in any document in this city, and you do not know her name."

"I do not know her name."

"Then that is the one," said Ines. "Write it as hers."

It was the end of the argument, because there was not much of one. What she had wanted was for him to understand that four lines in a morning was a different kind of object from a hundred and twelve names in a drawer, and he understood it, and the understanding was not a comfort.

Twenty-two days. Twenty-two less the seventh, and both in this month, and a sheet of paper on a hall table above a chop-house with four true sentences on it that not one person at the table could do anything with.

Ola Bright copied the four lines into her pad in her own hand, which is what she does, and dated the copy, and then copied the fifth one, the fourth floor, and then sat and looked at what she had copied.

"You have started something you cannot stop," she said. "You know that. I am copying it and I will go on copying it and one day there will be a sheet in this room with a hundred lines on it and you will not remember writing ninety of them, and then somebody will ask me where it came from and I will have to say it came from a morning at a sink."

"Then do not copy it."

"I copy everything," said Ola Bright cheerfully, "it is what I am for."

He put the sheet on top of the notebook and put the pen away, and the notebook was still shut, and the signature was still refused, and his own two sheets were now sitting on top of a book that six women kept and he was not a party to.

And the thing he had actually learned at a sink that morning was not a feeling. It was that the record he had spent four years filling was a list of people, and that the four years themselves were a list he had never once kept, because a record of who fed it tells you nothing about what you did.

And a record of what you did is the only thing that could ever have told him whether the four years were worth anything.

He had a hundred and twelve faces in a drawer and four acts on a table, and he was not going to get the fifth one, and there were twenty-two days left.

He took the sheet up and put it on the shelf above the sink where the visitors' book had lived before Marguerite had made a rule about the mantel, and it was still there on the eighth, and it would be there on the fourteenth, and the ledger was shut on the table with his pen in his pocket where it had been for a month.

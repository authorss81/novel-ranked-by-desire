# What Adeyemi Is For

Yusuf Adeyemi came to the Fourth Branch on the twenty-first and stood at the top of the stair that serves the fourth floor, which had been discharged on the eighteenth of last month and did not have anybody on it, and he stood there with his coat folded over his arm as though the floor underneath him were still running.

Lin Hao had that floor's washrooms in front of him because they were the dirtiest thing in the building and a week old and unused. He had a bucket, a cloth, and four weeks of asking nothing of anybody.

"You are on the wrong floor," he said.

"I am on the only floor in this building that is empty," said Adeyemi. "That is not the same mistake twice. Are you going to finish that one, or stand there with a wet cloth?"

He finished it. It took nine minutes. Adeyemi stood at the top of the stair the whole time and did not come down and did not go up, and twice a clerk came out of a room on the floor below, looked at the top of the stair, and went back in without saying anything to either of them.

That was the whole of the welcome. Adeyemi had told him on the third that he would be hated for his profession, and he had said it as a fact about a building, and the building had gone on with its morning and given him a corridor with a discharged floor on it.

Drant came out of the register room at ten past twelve with a receipt spike in his hand and looked at the man on the stair, and then looked at Lin Hao, and then said, "There is no public part of this floor."

"I am not looking for a public part," said Adeyemi. "I am here for nine minutes and then I am going back to Cobb Street, and I would be obliged if the register kept a record of the time I was on the stair and the floor and the reason, which is that I came up it."

Drant put the spike down on the counter.

"That is not what the book is for," he said.

"It is a register. It writes down that a thing moved." Adeyemi did not move. "It does not say why it moved. That is the whole of the post and I have read about it and I am asking you to do your job."

Drant looked at him for a long moment. Then he took the thin book out from inside his coat — the same thin book he had written four lines into on the eighth of last month — and he wrote three lines and squared the book against his palm and shut it.

"Nine minutes off the top of the fourth-floor stair," Drant said. "Hearing officer. Came up, stood, went down. Nothing else happened on that floor because there is nothing on that floor." He put the book away. "I am not writing a reason. If you want a reason, put it in your own hand and give it to me and I will carry it."

Adeyemi looked at him.

"That is better than the rule," he said. "Thank you."

Then he went down, and Lin Hao went with him because there was nowhere on that floor to stand, and at the bottom of the stair Adeyemi stopped and put his coat on and said, "I am going to say three things and then I am going to leave, and I am going to say them in a corridor because I have decided that a man who has been asked a question deserves to be asked it somewhere with a door at the end of it."

He said the first one.

"The Office must put its case in full. I mean that in the strict sense of the word. If they leave something out I stop them, and they know I will, and there are three things I have stopped in eleven years and I have never told anybody which three and I am not going to start."

Lin Hao waited.

"The second is that an instrument is not evidence of anything until somebody in the room has said in words what it does. Not the specification. Not the paper. Words, in the room, on the day, said out loud by a person who is there. That has been the rule since before I had this office and it is the only reason anybody in this city is still alive."

"And who says it."

"The person who is offering it." Adeyemi said it without any weight at all, which was the thing about him that made him hard to argue with.

"On this proceeding that is the counter-forensics reporting officer. Ivo Sarn. He wrote the output the Wing is relying on and he is the man who put the Wing's paragraphs in the run, and if he stands up on the twenty-ninth and cannot say in a sentence what the thing does, then the instrument does not go on the table."

"I will rule it out in front of a hundred and forty strangers and I will not feel a thing about it."

Thirty-nine days. Ten left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next.

"What happens to the instrument if it does go on the table."

"Then it is run on you in front of whoever is in that room, and whatever comes out of it goes into the transcript in the machine's words, and not one person in that hall is permitted to stop it once it has started." He said it plainly. "I have never had a man thank me for one of these and I have never once wanted one, and I want you to understand that I am not doing this because it gives anybody comfort. It gives me a transcript."

He said the third one.

"My own rule. It is not a law and it is not in a form and no office can compel me to hold it, and I have held it since before this Office was above a shop. An instrument offered in evidence must be shown capable of producing what its proponent says it produces. If the proponent cannot show it, the instrument is not admitted, and the burden of showing it sits with the office that brought it and not with the man it is pointed at."

"Shown how."

"Before the day. On paper, into the file, where both sides can read it, because I am not permitted to run a demonstration in a hall on a hearing morning and I would stop the first one I ever saw and I have never yet had to." He turned one hand over.

"The Certification desk answers. It has already answered once, with a paper that says the tool meets its specification. That paper certifies a form. It does not show the tool producing anything, and I have read it twice and it is two paragraphs and neither of them is a demonstration."

"Then what does the desk produce."

"Whatever the desk has." Adeyemi said it the way he said everything, as a man reading a rule off a wall.

"The desk keeps certified runs. Every run it has ever certified is a record of that run happening once, on one day, in one room, with a result. And a run that has been certified is the only kind of evidence in this building that anybody has ever been prepared to swear to."

"So the desk will put up its best one, and the member will be entitled to read it and to put questions to it, and that is the whole of what I have given you today."

He looked up the stair at the top of it, where the corridor went along to four shut doors and a washroom.

"One more thing, and I am saying it because it will not be said to you in Cobb Street."

"Say it."

"I do not enjoy this." He said it as though it were a piece of information about the weather. "I have never enjoyed one. There is no part of the work I enjoy and I have thought about that quite carefully over eleven years and the answer has not improved."

"What I do is this because the alternative is that a described person is not allowed in the room when it happens, and that is not a city anybody should be willing to live in, and I would rather be the man who is hated for it than one of the ones who votes for it."

A clerk came up the ground-floor stair with a tray and looked at the two of them and went past without slowing.

Adeyemi put his hands in his coat pockets.

"You will have read the roll by now, and you will have understood that the record is complete and the instrument is certified on paper, and that the two of those together mean nothing at all until the twenty-ninth, because what they mean is that a piece of paper says a machine exists." He took his hands out of his pockets.

"What is coming is a description and a machine, in that order, and the description is already written, and the machine is certified, and the only thing in the next thirty-nine days that is actually in anybody's hands is the sentence Sarn is going to have to stand up and say."

"And if he says it well."

"Then the instrument goes on the table and it is run on you, and I will sit on a bench and let it happen and I will not be able to stop it and I have told you that in advance." Adeyemi went down the last three steps.

"Thirty-nine days. Ten left in this month and twenty-nine in the next, and I have done that arithmetic in a corridor because I do it every morning of my life and it is the only thing about my job that I am good at."

He was at the door and turned round.

"You have a witness named," he said. "That is on the form and I have it. Say so out loud in that hall and it will hold. Do not do anything tonight. I have found that the men who do something tonight are always doing it about a day that has not come."

He went out into Cobb Street and did not look back, and Lin Hao stood in a corridor at the bottom of a stair with a bucket and a wet cloth in his hands.

He was angry. That was the thing in him and it was not what he had brought down for. He had come down those stairs expecting to be told that the machine would be run on him and that no hand in the building would stop it, and he had got a rule instead, and a date, and the name of a man who would have to stand in a hall and explain his own machine in one sentence.

What he wanted, and it was a want with an edge on it, was to be the man who made Sarn say it.

Not to stop the machine. To make the man who offered it stand up in a municipal hall with a hundred and forty strangers in it and describe, in his own words and under oath, exactly what a thing does when it is pointed at a person.

He had wanted a door off the room for four months and he had been handed a door inside it.

Thirty-nine days. Ten of them in this month and twenty-nine in the next, and a certified paper due on the twenty-fifth that he had never seen, and a man three floors above a clerk who kept a book that would have his own name on a page of it before the day.

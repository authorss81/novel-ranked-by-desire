# What Ola Keeps

Ola Bright had a page propped against the lamp on the ninth and had been copying things out all evening because it is what she does, and she had four sheets of it done and a fifth going when he came down the stairs at half past nine and did not sit down.

"What is that."

"It is Tuesday," said Ola Bright. "And it is Wednesday. And it is Thursday, and there is a bit of the sixth, and I have done the seventh because you came in with a sheet of ruled paper and put it on the shelf above the sink and did not say one word about it, and I would have done it anyway but I would rather have done it on the day."

The room was empty otherwise. Marguerite had gone out at nine and the kettle was off and the notebook was shut in the middle of the table with the day ruled at the top and nothing under it.

He walked round the table and looked at what she had copied.

It was not his four lines. It was four months.

"Ola."

"I copy everything," she said. "I have told you that. I have copied every sentence that has been said at this table since the first of the month, in my own hand, with the day at the top, and I have copied things people said in the yard and the queue and the arcade as well when I was there, which is most days, because I am a lift attendant's flatmate and I am idle, and because it is the only thing I have ever been any good at."

"Read me the ninth of April."

She read it.

It was a line about a tap on the second floor of the Vestry that four requests had not fixed, and then about a woman who had been sitting on the bottom step for two minutes in the afternoon and had not been there at four.

His own voice. In her hand. Dated the ninth of April.

"You have been writing them down since April."

"I have been writing them down since the twenty-first of last month properly, with the days ruled at the top, because I did not know I was allowed to and then somebody told me I was." She put the pen down. "Before that I just did it on whatever paper was on the table. It is the same thing, it is just that now it is tidy."

"How many."

She went and got a second book from under the stairs. It had a soft cover and a split at the spine and about two hundred pages in it, and she put it on the table and did not open it, which is the first thing he had ever seen her do without being asked twice.

"There are three hundred and eleven," said Ola Bright. "And eleven of them are not you. And in four months not one person has asked me for any of them, and I have never shown a page to anybody, and there is not a reason for that. There is only the fact that not one person has ever asked."

He put his hand flat on the cover.

That was the whole of what she had, then. Three hundred and eleven things a man had said in rooms, in the order he had said them, in one woman's handwriting, since the twenty-first of last month, and eleven entries of her own, and no name and no address and no office on any of it and no reason in the world for it except that a woman of twenty-six sat at tables.

He sat down.

"Read me the twenty-eighth," he said.

She read it. It was Beatrix Halm saying that a condition was dead, in her words, and the line under it that said he was not to read it back in the morning.

He listened to his own household from the twenty-first of last month, in order, in a voice that was not his and was not theirs, and understood about halfway through the twenty-second that he had never once been in a room where that happened. Six women at a table, a landing light on, a kettle, and a notebook shut in the middle of it, and every word in that book said out loud somewhere.

He wanted it destroyed and he could not ask for it destroyed, because the moment he asked, the book stopped being hers and became a document, and he would be asking a woman to destroy a document in her own hand.

"I am going to ask you for something," he said, "and I want you to write it down, and I want you to write it down and then keep it, and those are two separate instructions and I know which one of them is the hard one."

"I write everything down."

"That is not the hard one."

"What is."

"Keeping it." He turned the book round so it faced her. "On the twenty-ninth I will be in a room on Cobb Street from ten until two. I will not touch one person in it. I intend to move nothing on anybody in it. And I have said that out loud in a workroom to a builder on Thursday and it is in a file in an Exchange building with a date and two names on it and it cannot be taken out."

"I know that," said Ola Bright. "Cressida told the sister and the sister told the fish woman and the fish woman told me at the market on Saturday. There were four people at the counter at six in the morning talking about a man who is going to stand still for four hours."

She said it entirely without heat, which was the way she said everything.

"I have written down about four things about that already and I have not put them in this book. I want you to know that I have been not putting them in this book for nine days, and that it has been the hardest thing I have ever done, and I am quite good at not doing things."

"Then why have you not put them in."

"Because they are yours," said Ola Bright, "and you did not give them to me."

He looked at her for a second.

"And what I am asking for," he said, "is the four hours."

"That is not a thing I can hold."

"It is four hours on the twenty-ninth of the third month and it is written on a sheet and it is signed by a builder and it is in a file, and I cannot carry it into a room on my own and I cannot go in there having told not one person what it is, because a thing that has not been said aloud to a single soul is a speech and I do not have one."

"You have got a paper."

"The paper is in an Exchange building four floors above a machine. It is not mine and I cannot take it to Cobb Street, and I cannot read it out in a hall because I do not have it."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"When I walk in at ten I will be the only man in that room holding anything at all, and ninety people will be holding a version of me that has been in the city for three weeks."

Ola Bright wrote the date at the top of a clean page. Then she wrote four lines under it, in her own hand, in the order he gave them to her, without being asked to repeat any of it and without getting any of it wrong.

Then she did not give it to him.

"That is it," she said.

"That is it."

"I am not giving you that one," said Ola Bright, and she shut the pad. "That is the only one I have ever not given anybody and I am not going to be talked out of it, and you can have it back at four o'clock on the fourteenth or you can do without it."

He looked at the pad.

"It is a piece of paper with my own plan on it. It is not evidence of anything. It is not a document. You could leave it on this table and Marguerite would clear it up with the cups."

"And if I leave it on this table," said Ola Bright, "then in four months there is one fewer thing in this house that has not been read. And you have spent four months making sure that not one thing in this house has been read by anyone but the six of us, and it has cost you four women and a chair and a plate figure that has not moved since the third of last month."

She put the pad down on top of the notebook.

"I am the only person in this house who is not frightened of a piece of paper," she said, "and I have been frightened of a piece of paper every single day since the fourteenth of last month, and there is a difference and I would like somebody to be able to tell the difference."

Twenty days. Twenty less the ninth, and both in this month, and a book of three hundred and eleven sentences on a table above a chop-house in a woman's hand.

"You will have to give it back on the fourteenth."

"I will give it back on the fourteenth," said Ola Bright, "and you are going to stand in front of the six of them on the fourteenth and tell them that a woman who copies things has been holding the hours for nine days, and none of them are going to be pleased, and I would like you to have to do it."

"You have arranged that."

"I copy things," said Ola Bright. "It is what I am for."

She picked the pen up again and looked at the sheet he had put on the shelf above the sink on the seventh, four true sentences about a tap and a box and a step and a boy's discovery about a socket, and she copied them across onto the inside cover of the soft-backed book, and dated them, and did not say anything while she did it.

That was what she was going to be asked to write down next. Not the hours, though she would keep those too. The acts, in a book that had two hundred pages in it and was not a register and had no seal and no line on it, in a hand that not one person had asked for.

She shut it and put it back under the stairs with the other one.

"You have four hundred and something pages of me," she said. "I have four hundred and something pages of you. It is the most anybody in this city has ever had of anybody else and neither of us is going to be thanked for it."

He got as far as the foot of the stairs and turned round, because there was one more thing and he wanted it said in the same room as the other one.

"Ola. What you did on the seventh — you copied the shelf sheet into a book that has my four years in it."

"I did."

"The four years and the four sentences are not the same kind of thing and somebody could work out that, and if somebody did, the four years would be the document and the four sentences would be the reason for it."

"I thought about that for a day and a half," said Ola Bright, "and I have put them on the inside cover, which is the one page that does not get turned to, and I have dated them separately, so that if anybody ever comes at this book they will find two hundred pages of nothing before they get to anything at all."

He went up the long road past the square with twenty days and a woman on a landing and a house that had just been priced at less than nothing by the only person in it who priced things, and the thing that had changed was that he had asked one person in the world to hold a piece of paper for him and she had said yes in about four seconds and then refused to give it back for nine days.

He had spent four months making sure that nothing he was was ever written down by anybody.

On the ninth he handed the only thing he had to a woman who cannot keep a silence, and she said she would keep it, and she was right, and there was not one person in Ostara he could take it back from.

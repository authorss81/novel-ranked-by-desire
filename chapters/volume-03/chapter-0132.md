# The Household Arithmetic

Marguerite Vale read the visitors' book at eight, the way she read it every week since the first, with the glass off her own mantel and the hall table cleared for it and six women round the table in their coats.

"A hundred and six," she said. "Thirty-eight of them asked about a person. On sixteen of the thirty-eight the person they asked about is one of you, and I am not going to say which, and that is still the rule."

She put her thumb on the line and let the two sets of numbers do it. The numbers on the page were hers. The numbers in the grey box three streets away were the Office's, and the Office's still said eighty-nine and twenty-nine and thirteen, and it would go on saying eighty-nine and twenty-nine and thirteen on the twenty-ninth.

Six weeks since the last time she had read it aloud, and twenty-two more entries, seven more callers asking about a person, two more asking about somebody in this house.

"Nineteen days ago I priced a house for a man in Cordwain who had a bad shoulder and a worse temper," she said, "and I priced it the way I price everything, which is: what is the thing worth to the person who wants it, and what will it cost the person who has it, and is there a price at which both of those are the same number."

"Marguerite—"

"I am not starting on the book. I have read the book." She squared it and turned it round so it faced the middle of the table. "I want to price this house. That is all it is. I am a broker and I price things and there is a thing in this room that has never been priced, and it is going into a hall on the 29th, and every one of you has spent four months assuming you know what you are worth in it."

The room said nothing back, which is how the room told her to go on.

"First the shield," said Marguerite Vale. "That is what we have all been assuming we are. Six women and a landing and a book on a table. And I am going to say the thing not one of us has said, and I have been not saying it since the seventh of last month, which is that a shield is only worth what it stops, and there is exactly one thing this house stops, and I want somebody at this table to tell me what it is."

Silence.

"That is the whole of my argument and I have made it in one sentence," said Marguerite. "Not one of us has said what the house is for. In four months we have written down six lines a day and read a book out loud on Mondays and argued for three hours about a square of paper and a porter's box, and not one of us has ever once said what any of it was buying."

"It keeps us findable," said Nadia Ferrous.

"It does not," said Marguerite Vale. "It is the other way round. Six lines a day in a book in this house is six women telling a room where they were, and a book with six women's days in it is a document that a woman with a card can ask about in four offices, and we have known that since the seventh of last month and the seventh of last month is when a roll with nine items on it was carried up four flights."

"That is not fair," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"It is entirely fair and it is priced at nothing," said Marguerite. "A book cannot keep anybody. What keeps anybody in this city is distance, and rank, and a plate, and a document no office can ask for, and we have none of the four and we have one of the fifth in a drawer that six of us have agreed never to mention."

The kettle went off the boil and not one of them moved for it.

"Now the other side," said Marguerite Vale. "Which is what you are all actually sitting there waiting for, so I will do it quickly, because I have done it four times in my own head this week and I am tired of it. What is a household worth to a man who is going to be read in public on the twenty-ninth?"

She took the pad off Ola Bright, which she was not supposed to do and which Ola Bright let her do, and wrote on it in her own hand, four lines, and turned it round.

"One. Seven women. Two. Seven women who may stand at the rail of a room any person in this city may walk into. Three. A man who must not touch one person in that room for four hours." She put her finger on the third line and left it there. "Four. Seven women who will be in that room."

The room went very quiet, and Lin Hao understood it about a second and a half before anybody said it, which is a thing that happens to him and is the reason nothing has ever got past him.

"That is the arithmetic," said Beatrix Halm. "It is not a shield. It is seven people standing next to the one man in the room who has to keep his hands off everybody."

"It is exactly that," said Marguerite Vale. "And it has been that since the seventh of last month, and every one of us has known the arithmetic and not one of us has been willing to be the one to say it out loud at a table, because the sentence is so ugly that it sounds like an accusation and it is not. It is a number."

"Then why say it tonight," said Beatrix.

"Because on Tuesday night a man stood in this room and asked to be left alone in a hall for four hours," said Marguerite, "and that is the first time in four months that any of us has offered him something we could actually be useful at, and I have been sitting here for two days trying to work out whether the thing we were offering was a shield or a bill, and I have done the arithmetic and it is a bill."

"Say the price, then," said Nadia Ferrous.

"The price is not tonight," said Marguerite Vale. "The price is on the 29th, and I am not going to hand it to him in a warm room on the sixth of a month when he has twenty-three days and a clerk to go to."

She put her thumb on the pad.

"If I priced it tonight he would carry the figure into that hall and stand at the table with it, and a man standing at a table with a number in his head has already lost the thing the number is about."

"Then price it now and let him be the one to decide."

"He cannot decide it." She put the pen down. "That is the other half, and it is why I am doing this tonight instead of in a corridor."

She looked round the table.

"There is nothing in this room he can decide. Every one of you has asked him for something in four months and been refused. He has one thing he can give and he is not going to give it in here, and he said so on Tuesday with his hands flat on a table. So the question of what this house is worth is ours, and we are going to answer it on the fourteenth, and I am going to put it in front of him then and not before."

"And I am going to copy it out," said Ola Bright. "I copy everything. I am going to copy the price as well, and that is not a thing you want, and you are going to let me do it anyway."

"I am going to let you do it anyway," said Marguerite Vale, "it is what you are for."

Ola Bright wrote the date at the top of a clean page and wrote one line under it, and Marguerite watched her do it and did not stop her.

She picked the book up off the table.

"Six weeks ago I read ninety-four and thirty-one and fourteen. Tonight I have read a hundred and six and thirty-eight and sixteen. The Office has read nothing since the seventh of last month and will read nothing on the 29th, and the number the hall gets is the Office's, and the number this house has actually been is mine, and the two have never once been the same and have never once been on the same page."

She put it back under her hand.

"Nineteen of those hundred and six are me reading this out. One is a man who wrote Nell Ardery's name in a porter's hand and gave no name of his own."

She put two fingers on the page.

"Two are callers who came to my door and could not get a name out of this house, and one of them came twice, and neither has come back. They are not on a page as people. They are on a page as four words each, and I would like somebody at this table to notice that I have stopped trying to find them."

Not one of them had noticed, and that was the shape of the evening, and Marguerite had said all of it in eight minutes.

Ines Duarte had not moved from the sill.

"You have priced the wrong thing twice," she said, "and both times in the correct direction, and I want the correction on the record before you are praised for it."

Marguerite put the pad down.

"The first is that the third line is not a cost. It is the whole of the bargain, and you know that, and you put it in the list in the place where a cost goes, because a list of costs is a thing a person can read without flinching."

Ines opened the folder she had not opened.

"A man who cannot touch anybody in a room is not a man being careful. He is a man whose hands have been described. That description gets made whether or not this house is at the rail."

"Then what does the house change."

"The only thing the house changes," said Ines Duarte, "is whether the women standing at the rail are women he has washed a floor behind, and put a hand on in a kitchen in the ordinary way, and carried a press bed for."

"Which they are," said Nadia Ferrous.

"Which they are," said Ines Duarte. "And that is worse, and you have priced it as better, and you have been right about everything else for eleven years."

"That is a different number."

"It is the same number and it is worse," said Ines. "You priced this house as seven women who will reach for him. That is the optimistic version. The true version is seven women who have every reason to reach for him, standing in a room where reaching is the only thing that can be proved against him."

"Then he will not have us at the rail," said Nadia Ferrous.

"That," said Ines Duarte, "is the first decision any of us has made about the twenty-ninth, and it was made by a woman who does not live here and has not been at that table since the twenty-seventh of last month, and it was made about a rule she read in a specification clause and none of you will understand properly and I am not going to explain it twice."

"Six lines a day," said Ola Bright at the end of the table. "I am writing this down because I always write this down. On the sixth of the month, six women in a house priced the house, and the price came out at less than nothing, and the seventh woman was on the landing and did not come down, and that is the ninth time this has happened since the first of the month."

Nadia went up the stairs with the lamp and came back and said nothing for about a minute, which for her was a paragraph.

"She says it is not hers to price," said Nadia, "because the thing the house is worth depends on the day, and on the twenty-ninth the house is worth exactly what the hall can make of it, and not one person at this table gets to decide what a clerk with a good voice can make of seven women."

"It is worth nothing at all," said Marguerite Vale. "I have done it twice and both times it came out the same and I am going to say it once and then I am going to stop saying it. Seven women in that room is a negative value. Six women in that room is worse. And a house that stays at home is worth exactly what it has always been worth, which is six lines a day and a book on a mantel and a man who comes home."

She stood up and put the glass back on the mantel, which is where it lives.

"So that is the bill," she said, "and I am not going to present it tonight, and the reason I am not is that you did not ask me to and I would rather bring you a thing you did not ask for than a thing you did."

Twenty-three days. Twenty-three less the sixth, and both in this month.

Not one of them left. That was the thing he carried up the stairs, as it had been three weeks ago and four, and would be on every night of the rest of this month until the hall.

Seven women, and a ledger in a drawer with a hundred and twelve lines in it that recorded faces and had never once recorded a deed, and the fact that six of the women in this house had been standing in rooms he had put his hands on for four years, and had never asked him for any of it, and had been refused everything they ever did ask for, and were not going to be at the rail.

And one woman on a landing who had told him since the first of the month that the only thing standing between a list and her was that there was nowhere on paper that said where she slept.

He got to the stairs above the chop-house at ten past ten and sat down at the table with the notebook shut in front of him, and the landing light was on, and the visitors' book was on the mantel with the glass over it, and in eight minutes six women had told him in front of a priced piece of paper that the thing they had been protecting for four months did not work.

He put his hand flat on the shut notebook.

What he wanted, and he could not have said it in a sentence, was for one of them to have been wrong about the price.

It had taken eight minutes and a broker's method to find out that the reason his household had spent four months protecting him was that not one of them had ever costed it, and that every one of them had known there was a figure and had gone on doing the six lines a day instead of asking for it.

Twenty-three days to work out what a thing is worth when not one person in the room wants it valued.

The notebook was shut and his pen was in his pocket where it had been for a month, and the signature was still refused, and he had learned one thing in eight minutes and he was going to have to act on it by the fourteenth of the month or it would not be worth the eight minutes.

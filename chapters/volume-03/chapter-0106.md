# The Best Use Of A Fact

Bellhouse on the fifth was hot wax and somebody's supper, and the table in the hall was too big for the number of people round it, and six women had arranged themselves about it in the way they had for a month, which was with their coats still on, and the seventh was on the landing at the top of the stairs and was not going to come in.

He put the notice face up in the middle of the table and said nothing and let the room read it, because a document read in silence by six people at once gets further into a house than a document explained by the man it is about.

Beatrix Halm read it twice and put it down. Ola Bright copied the third paragraph into her pad without being asked, which she had never done for anything not about somebody in the house, and then copied the date under it.

Tessa Okonkwo read it once, standing, and did not sit down and said nothing at all. Nadia Ferrous read it and said, "Right," and then said nothing for twenty seconds, which for Nadia Ferrous was a considered act.

Sun Meiling was on the landing at the top of the stairs with her coat still on, and Marguerite had told her not to come in about ten seconds before he arrived. He understood why: a woman who refuses to be written down should not be in a room where a document is being read aloud.

Ines Duarte had the notice square in front of her with two fingers on the second paragraph.

"They have written it correctly," she said. "That is the trouble. There is nothing wrong with the paper."

Marguerite Vale came down the inside stair with a lamp in her hand that was not needed because the room had a light in it, and put it on the table anyway, the way she had put a glass on her own mantel and left it there.

"Say it," she said. "Say the position out loud to the room, because there are six women at this table and I am not going to be the one who says it."

He said it. The notice, the date, the hall, the hundred and forty seats and the ninety standing places, the plate by the door, the form on a corridor bench at the Vestry, the word he had written on the blank strip under it and the box he had not written in.

Nobody interrupted him, which had never once happened in that room.

"Two months," said Nadia Ferrous. "Two months and they have not got one line in their hand."

"They have got a band and a date and a word called held," said Beatrix Halm, "and they have got every woman in this house, asked about a man, for a month. Give it until the end of next month and they will have a sentence. It does not have to be good. It has to be in a hand."

"They will have to build it out of something." Lin Hao had not meant to say it and said it anyway, because it had been in him since a long road. "There is no way to write that sentence out of what is in my file. There is a band six sitting on a paper and a figure three grains under a floor and a word that says held. That is three facts about a plate, and the question is not about a plate."

Marguerite Vale moved a chair out from the table with her foot.

"Right. Then that is the most useful thing said in this room since the first of last month, and I am going to build on it here."

She had the visitors' book under her arm. She put it on the table and took the glass off it, a small brass thing her own porter reads the hour column with and has been reading with since the first.

"Marguerite—"

"I have been a broker for thirty years and I have never once tried to make a person valuable by saying something true about him. That is not in the trade." She squared the book against the table edge. "What I do is find out what a thing is worth to somebody who cannot get it any other way. I have spent this last month doing exactly that with a fact, and the fact is a man."

She looked round the table.

"A man who is going to be described in public in a hall on Cobb Street, on a day that is printed, in front of a room anybody in Ostara can walk into. That is the only asset in this city that cannot be seized by a court.

"Everything else we have is attachable. The lift log is a document and documents get produced. Runa's minute is a document. The notebook is six women, and six women can be asked one at a time in their own rooms over a period of months. My house can be entered by anybody from the Exchange with a card, and has been since the first."

She put two fingers on the notice.

"This cannot be seized. Not because it is valuable and not because it is safe. Because it is public. You cannot requisition a hearing. You cannot attach a date that is already on a board."

"In fifty-five days this man walks into a room and says a sentence out loud, and after that there is a thing in the world about him that did not exist before, and no court can order it back into the drawer it came out of, because a hundred and forty strangers will have heard it."

"That is not an asset," said Nadia Ferrous. "That is a hearing."

"That is the first half of an asset and you have been listening to the wrong half all week." Marguerite took her hand off the paper.

"Here is the second half. The Office needs something to put in front of that room, and it cannot build a sentence out of a band and a date and a word. It has to build it out of a household, or a service, or a building, or eleven years of somebody's handwriting. It has three desks and about eight weeks, and every single thing it can build it out of belongs to somebody in this room."

"So what do you want to do," said Beatrix Halm.

"I want to go and say it in three rooms, in the right week, using this house's name." Marguerite said it without heat. "Not the notice. The notice is theirs. The fact underneath it.

"I want three people to know, this week, that what is about to happen to this man will make him the only man in Ostara whose condition has been stated in public and cannot be taken back, and that the week after it nothing in this house can be taken off him."

"You want to make him expensive."

"I want to make him priced." She said it exactly and she looked at him while she said it. "There is a difference and you know there is a difference, because you have been doing the other thing for five weeks and it has cost you a floor and a standing and a calendar and three files, and you did it in a corridor, at night, on your own, for nothing, because you did not want to owe anybody."

He did not answer.

"And I am asking you for permission," said Marguerite Vale.

That was the part of the room that went quiet.

"And I want it minuted by the woman with the pencil, because I have spent thirty years in a guild that would take this off me inside a week if three people in a room could be made to look like I arranged it." She turned to Ola. "Write it down. The broker has asked the member for leave to speak for him in three rooms and has been refused, and is going to do it."

"You have not refused me yet."

"I know I have not." She turned round. "Say no, then, and say it properly, and I will take it, and then I am going to do it anyway, and you will have known about it in advance, which is more than you have ever given anybody."

Lin Hao looked at six women and a lamp that was not needed.

"No," he said.

"Thank you." She did not sound wounded and she did not sound surprised, and she did not look like she had won anything.

She set the visitors' book square to the edge of the table and put her hand flat on the cover.

"Now go and wash your hands," she said. "There is a woman in this room who has not let you sit down since you came in, and I am not going to lose her over a hearing."

He washed his hands in the scullery sink off the hall, and then he came back and she was in the room nobody used off the back of the hall, which had a cold floor and a table in it and a lamp on the table and no fire, and she had shut the door.

"Sit down," said Marguerite Vale. "I am going to say four terms and then I am going to be wrong at you in a way I have never been wrong at anybody. You will not enjoy it and you will not be able to argue with it, because you came in here forty minutes ago with a refusal in your hand and I have watched you carry that refusal round this building all afternoon like a plate you do not want to lose."

He sat.

"One. I did not come to bed with you for any reason that is in any book, and you have known that since last season, and I have never once said so out loud and I am saying it now because the next thing I am going to say depends on it."

"Marguerite—"

"Two." She unbuttoned her cuffs and rolled them back above her forearms. "I keep a book of what the Vant does not put in its books. I am going out on it in three rooms and I am not going to say your name in any of them, and I am going to say what you are.

"The reason is not that I am brave and it is not that I love you. It is that the Vant can take the house off me in a day and a half, and the three rooms are outside the Vant, and what is in them stays in them."

"And three."

"And three is that you are going to say one sentence out loud in that hall and I have decided which sentence, and it is not yours. And four is that I am not asking."

She stopped, and took off her cuffs and dropped them on the table, and undid the top three buttons of her blouse without hurrying, which was a thing she did the way other people take off a coat.

"Stand up," she said. "Take your coat off. Hang it on that hook, not over the chair, because I will be able to see it when I turn round and I have never been able to work with a man's coat over a chair."

He stood up and took his coat off and hung it on the hook, and he understood, standing there with his shirtsleeves, that the refusal he had given her at the table had cost him nothing, because she had taken it in a second and a half and had gone on to the thing she actually wanted, and that the thing she actually wanted was in the room with him and had been since the first.

"Four terms," she said, "and then I am going to be wrong at you.

"One. I am not impaired and I am not tired and I have had two glasses of water and no wine, and if I start being tired that is the hour and not a fault of mine, and you would be permitted to say so and I would permit it.

"Two. Nothing gets bought tonight. There is no permission in this room and nothing in this room is for sale, and my terms are in a drawer on the second floor where they have been since the season before last, unsigned, and they are staying there, and I have said that out loud in this room before and I am saying it out loud in this room now and I would like you to hear it in this room.

"Three. I will stop when you ask. I will believe you the first time and not argue about it, and if you do not ask me at all I will not go looking for a reason.

"Four." She looked at him with her hands flat on the table either side of the lamp. "Four is that I have wanted this since the first, and I have wanted it as a thing to be used, and not as comfort, and not as a favour, and not as an evening.

"If it happens tonight it is going to happen because you were made dangerous this afternoon by two doors closing and one sheet of paper, and I am going to be the only person in this room who is glad about that, and I am telling you now so that you cannot say afterwards that you did not know the terms."

He had not expected any of it and he had expected all of it.

She came round the table and he stood and she put her hands on his shoulders and turned him the way she had turned a sheet of paper in June, without any force in it at all, and then she took her hands off and stepped back and looked at him from a foot away.

"You have never once in your life been the thing that is dangerous in a room," she said. "That is the entire trouble with you and it is the only thing I have ever wanted to correct. You are the man in the corner of it. Somebody else walks in and takes the room and you get thanked, if anybody thanks you, by people who have decided they were the ones who made it happen."

He started to say something and she put one finger on his mouth.

"No. Do not be careful. That is the last thing I want and it is the thing you do when you want to be looked through." She took the finger away. "I want a man who knows what he is worth in a room and is not sorry. Say it back."

"I know what I am worth in a room and I am not sorry."

"Good." She went to the table and turned the lamp down and did not turn it off. "Then come here, and do the other thing you are very bad at, which is the only thing you are actually good at."

She sat down on the edge of the table and pulled him in by the belt, and he let her, and his hands were not steady and she took one of them and put it flat on the table beside her hip and said, "That one. Look at it."

It was not shaking.

"That is not steady, it is *arrived*," said Marguerite Vale, "and you have been arriving in rooms for two months and calling it holding on, and I am going to spend the rest of the night taking that word off you."

She got his shirt open and her hands went down over his ribs and stopped with the fourth one on the left and pressed, once, and he made a noise he had not made in front of anybody since the seventeenth of last month, and she took her hand off.

"That," she said. "You have been guarding that since the spring and it is not a sensation any more, it is a habit, and you are going to stop guarding it tonight and I am not going to be gentle about it."

He got her blouse open and she took over halfway, because her hands knew what they were doing and had been given something to do for eleven years without a man in the room, and she said so out loud in four words that made him put his forehead on her shoulder for a second.

"Say what you want," he said. "Not a rule. What you want."

"I want you to be the dangerous thing in a room and to have been in this one first." She got her skirt down to her knees and then stood up out of it and stepped out of the rest of it and stood over him in the half-dark with the lamp turned down, entirely present, entirely herself, and looking at him the way a woman looks at a man she has been pricing for a month and has finally decided to spend.

"Three rooms, and the right week, and a woman in each of them who will find out what you are before the Office does," said Marguerite Vale. "That is the whole of what I am buying with this afternoon, and it costs me the Vant if I get it wrong, and I would like to be able to say that I got it wrong in a room that was worth something."

He got her onto the table and she let him, and there was nothing ceremonial about the next ten minutes and nothing careful, and the lamp stayed on the table and fell over once and neither of them picked it up.

Afterwards she lay along him with her weight on his side and one hand flat on his chest, counting nothing, and said, into the collar of his shirt:

"Nothing came in and nothing went out. I would know. I have been handling debts that other people's mercy created for thirty years and there is nothing in me this evening that is not still where it was, and I am going to check again in the morning and if that is different I will not tell you and you will find out from somebody else."

"That is the only thing you have to say about it."

"That is the only thing anybody in this city can say about it, and it is worth more than every sentence the Office has put in a file since the third of last month." She sat up, put her hair back over her shoulders, reached past him for the lamp and turned it up.

"And now I am going to say the wrong thing, and I want you to let me get to the end of it before you answer. That is the only time in thirty years I have asked anybody for that."

"Go on."

"You said no to me at that table, in front of six women, and you said it properly, and it was the correct no." She looked at the lamp and not at him.

"In fifty-five days you are going to stand in a hall and say one sentence out loud and let an office write it down in its own words. Four weeks after that there will be a piece of paper in a file with your condition on it in the only sentence that has ever existed. That paper is going to be the reason nobody can take the house off me, and it is going to be because of you, and I did not help you make it."

"Marguerite."

"That is what I am buying tonight and I would like it entered that I paid for it myself." She got off the table and found her shirt. "Do not comfort me. I have had thirty years of comfort from men who were frightened and I have never once mistaken it for anything else."

She was dressed before he was. He watched her do her cuffs and understood that a woman who had been in that bed for an hour and ten minutes looked exactly as she had before it, except for her hair, which was the whole of the cost and which she did not intend to repair.

She went out through the back door and shut it behind her, and Lin Hao sat on the edge of a table in a cold room and put his shirt on, and then he did what she had told him to do at the table, which was to wash his hands.


He came into the hall at half past eleven and Marguerite Vale was at the table with the visitors' book open and the glass beside it, reading it out loud, the way she read it every week since the first, so the room would hear it before anybody in it said anything.

She got through the week's entries in four minutes. Eleven were the courier with the badge. Four were the fish woman, who never gave a name and wrote carp in the column, which is the porter's decision and which Marguerite has not corrected in a month.

"Twenty-nine of them asked about somebody," she said. "Not about the building. About a person. And on thirteen of the twenty-nine the person they asked about is one of you, and I am not going to say which, and that is still the rule and the rule is still the only reason this book has not been read by anybody who came in with a card."

She turned a page.

"And this one is new, and it is the third of this month, and it is at nine in the evening, and it has no name on it."

She put her finger under it.

"Four words in the fourth column, which is the column for the caller's business, and the porter wrote them down because he was told to write down the caller's business and because a man with a glass can see better than a man without one. Four words."

She read them out:

"'Ask him what it is.'"

Nobody said anything.

"That is all it says. No name, no office, no band, no rank, nothing that makes it a document, and it is dated the third, which is two days ago, and I have been trying to work out how a caller who gives no name knows there is a him to ask about, and why they want to know what he is rather than where he lives."

Marguerite turned the book round and pushed it down the table to him.

"That is not one of ours," she said. "That is not a name in this house and not a name in any book in this city. A stranger who cannot get a name out of six women in a month has just told me that somebody has been giving this address away somewhere that is not this desk, and giving it to somebody who writes things down."

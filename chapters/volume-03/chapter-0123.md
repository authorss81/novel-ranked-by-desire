# The Corridor With No Handle

He did it on the twenty-third, at half past four in the afternoon, because that was the hour the trays came down.

There had been a runner down that stair every day since the eighth of last month at four o'clock, and on the eighth there had been four of them carrying thin papers to be stamped and put in a drawer, and Drant had stood at the top of the stair and made a runner write four lines into a book that stayed on the floor.

He had been thinking about that stair for a month and a half and he had gone down it twice and stopped on the third step both times, and on the second of those he had read a plate from four feet above the floor and understood the price of a thing in a city in about nine seconds.

The sixth space in Anneke Roos's fourth book had been full for three days. Five headings and a ruled space a year and a half empty on purpose, and then a paragraph in a clerk's hand, received and not agreed, about a settled mass in the possession of a member.

He wanted to see what a sentence like that came with.

Not the classification. There was not going to be one, and if there was going to be one he would be in it before anybody signed anything, which was the only reason he was not afraid of it in the ordinary way. He wanted to see the furniture.

He wanted to stand at arm's length from the door that the paragraph was written about, because a sentence and a room are connected by a signature, and a signature is a thing somebody does with a pen, and he wanted to look at the place where the pen went.

He cleaned six floors first. The fourth was still discharged and he did it last, and at half past four he put the bucket in the closet and went down the Wing stair.

The top flight was empty. The goods lift light was on at the bottom, which it always was after four, and the corridor ran off in both directions with a door on each side and every door the same, and against the near wall the eight chairs stood in two rows of four with their legs up.

And there was a ninth chair standing square to a door four feet out from the wall.

He looked at that chair for a long time.

It was the same chair.

He had looked at it from the top of this stair on the twentieth of last month with the light off the goods lift on and the drawer in the cabinet open, and he had looked at it from the third step on the fifteenth of the month before with a runner four steps above him, and he had written in his own head on the twentieth that a man who spends six weeks learning where a door is has not yet decided anything about it.

And here was the thing about the chair that he had not worked out on a stair, and it took about a second and a half: eight of the nine chairs in that room had been carried four hundred yards up an outside stair in a handcart on the second of this month and put against a wall in a corridor on the third floor of the Reading, where a woman who arranges rooms had left one of them where she was sitting on it.

Eight went up and were left where a corridor wanted them. The ninth stayed here. And the reason the ninth stayed here was not a decision anybody made about furniture. It was that the list of nine chairs in this building had one line in it and the line had not been filled in since the twenty-sixth of last month, and a room with a gap in the count of its furniture is an unfinished room.

He had a full month of reasons not to go and he went through them going down, which is what a man does when he has already decided and is only collecting the objections. The first was the register on the fourth floor, which Drant kept and which said what moved on a floor and when, and had said on the eighth of last month that a man from the Exchange went down the Wing stair with Drant at the top and neither of them went further.

The second was that a corridor on a sub-level of his own workplace at twenty past four in the afternoon is not a place a person is. The third was that he had been down twice and had stopped on the third step both times and had come back up each time and had not once been sorry about it.

He got to the third step from the bottom, which put him inside the frame of the corridor and inside the light, exactly the way it had put him inside the frame on the eighth and on the twentieth, and he stood there and read the plate from four feet above the floor.

It said what a classification is. It said what happens to a person who goes through one. It said the word rank and the word one, and that the holder is returned at rank one, and that nothing else is returned with him.

He had read it twice before and it had not changed, and it was engraved, and it had been there before he was hired, and a thing that has not changed in a month and is cut into brass does not get less true because you have read it again.

There was no handle on that side of it.

He went down.

That was the whole of it and it took him about eleven seconds and there was nothing in them: the second flight, the turn, the corridor, the light off the goods lift lying in a long shape down the middle of the boards, eight chairs with their legs up on the near side and eleven feet of nothing on either side of him.

At the foot of the flight, against the wall, there was the cabinet with four drawers. He had known about that cabinet since the eighth of last month and had never been close enough to see it, and he could see it now, and all four drawers were shut, and the one that had been open on the eighth had a different handle from the other three and had been re-fitted about four inches higher than it had been.

Somebody had adjusted it so that the front of it lined up with the fronts of the other three, which is not a repair anybody does to a drawer in a corridor with no staff on it. It is a thing you do to a drawer when you have been in it.

The rest of the corridor was eleven feet to the left with two doors in it and eleven feet to the right with the other two, and he looked at all four, because a man on a sub-level at twenty past four in the afternoon looks at doors. All four had handles and all four had a plate, and not one of the four plates was engraved with anything. They were blank squares with a screw in each corner.

That was so obviously a second set that he understood at once the corridor had been made in two halves at two different times by two different offices, and that only one half of it had an opinion about what it was for.

The other door in the right-hand eleven feet was the one with the plate on it.

He had been wrong for a month and a half about what he expected to feel and he was not wrong about the rest of it. He had expected a corridor and there was a corridor. He had expected eight chairs and there were eight chairs, with their legs up, the way they had stood in a corridor four hundred yards away for nine days in the middle of last month before eight men came up an outside stair with a handcart and took them away.

He had expected a plate and there was a plate.

He had expected to be afraid, and he was not, and the not being afraid was the frightening part, because it meant that the last six weeks had taken the thing out of him that a door on a sub-level is supposed to put in.

The cloth was on the table through the gap at the hinge. He had known it would be. A strip of light along the floor, the edge of a table, and the grey cloth hanging a hand's width over it, and the cloth was the grey cloth that had been on the inventory table on the seventh of last month with a plate beside it and a finding number on the spike outside.

Then he looked at the door instead of through the gap, because a door you have been reading for a month is worth looking at properly once.

It was a plain door. Six panels, an oak frame, and at the height of a man's hand the paint was a slightly different colour from the paint round it — a rectangle, four inches by two, the shape and size of a handle and its rose, filled in with a paint that had not quite matched and never would.

Somebody had taken the handle off this side of that door and painted over the hole.

Not broken off. Not lost. Taken off and filled, and the fill was good work by somebody who had been given time to do it properly, and it had been done in an era of this building when the plate was already on the door, which meant it had not been done last week and had not been done for him.

He stood in front of a door that somebody had unpicked on purpose, in a building where he cleaned washrooms, and he understood that he had spent a month and a half being frightened of a piece of furniture and that the piece of furniture had an alteration in it that was older than his attention span.

What he felt was not fear. It was the particular nausea of a thing being made slowly and lawfully and out of his own building's fittings, and then the second thing underneath it, which was worse and which he had not expected: he wanted to see the room.

Not to be in it. To see it, the way you see a kitchen in a house you are not going to buy. He wanted to know whether the plate was true, and there is no way on earth to know whether a plate is true except by standing in the room it is nailed to.

Thirty-seven days. Eight left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next, and four trays of stamped paper coming up past him in about ten minutes, and a runner at the bottom of that stair who had written four lines about a man standing on a step in a book that stayed on the floor.

He put his hand flat on the door, high, near the rail, where a hand goes when a hand is doing something a head has not authorised yet.

Somewhere above him a lift came down through the building, felt through the stair rather than heard, the whole frame of the place moving a little.

He had about six minutes before four trays came up that stair with a runner each.

The door gave.

It was not locked, and there was no handle, and it swung away from him on his own hand with the weight of a door that has been oiled recently by somebody who was not permitted to oil it and did it anyway.

The light in the corridor fell in a long wedge across the boards of a room about eleven feet by nine, and the room was empty of everything except a table, a cloth, a chair, and the smell of a room with the heat on in it.

He stood in the doorway of it with his hand still on the door and did not go in.

# The Board Repaints

The Board does not repaint on the last day of a month. It repaints at nine on a Thursday, whatever the day of the month is, and whatever Thursday it is, and whether or not there is anything to say.

The thirty-first of this month was not a Thursday and there was no man on a ladder anywhere in the Reading Square, and the girl who does the repaint sheet by hand had taken her stool down on the Friday morning and put it under the bench where she keeps it between weeks.

The sheet was still on the rail, though, with a rubber band across the middle of it where the clerk had put one because it is the only printed thing in this city that anybody actually reads.

He stood and read it.

At the top, the week's figures, written out by hand the way they have been written out for twenty years: the number of plates read at the Fourth Branch, the number of people brought up a band, the number of plates that came back down. All of them ordinary. All of them the figures of a city that had done nothing whatever for seven days.

Then two lines that were not figures.

THURSDAY. A PUBLIC HEARING WAS HELD AT COBB STREET FROM TEN UNTIL TWO. TWO HUNDRED AND ELEVEN PRESENT.

THURSDAY. THE MEMBER'S ACCOUNT IS NOT UNDESCRIBED. IT IS DESCRIBED BY THE MEMBER. NO BAND HAS CHANGED.

He read it twice.

That was the whole of it, and it was on a sheet by the rail in the open air because there is one board in this city and it publishes bands, and not one band in Ostara had moved in a month in which a hearing had been held and a man had stood at a table for four hours and put his own hand on his own side three times.

So the result had gone where the city's only weekly publication was, underneath a line about plates coming down, in the middle of a block of numbers about nothing.

He walked up the wall afterwards the way he had walked up it four times that month, and the tile in the rectangle where his name had been since the fourth of the month was the same tile with the same stranger's name on it, because the Board repaints on Thursdays and the last Thursday had been the twenty-ninth, and the men had put that tile up on the twenty-second and had not touched it since.

The numbers either side of it were correct and in order.

He was at the gate at half past nine and the queue on the second floor was thirty-one deep by ten, which was the queue he worked round every morning with the bucket and the mop, and he worked round it.

At about eleven, on the third floor, a clerk stopped him at a counter with a form about a tap that dripped and asked him whether the form wanted a date.

"It does not," he said.

"Then what do I put on it."

He stood there with the cloth in his hand and light coming in off the yard, and he had an answer ready, because he had had an answer ready since the fifth of a month and he had given it to four clerks since and it had been the truth every time.

"This one is the day I fixed it," he said. "Put that, and not a day off, because you are going to ask me the same thing on the first of next month and I am going to tell you the same thing again."

The clerk wrote it down.

Nothing about a man who has cleaned a floor for four years, and a tap on the third floor of a branch office on the last working morning of a month, gets written down anywhere in this city. It went into the drawer that evening with the street and the hour on it, on the back page, after the hundred and fourteen, because it was the last thing that happened to him in a month and it was the smallest.

That was a hundred and fifteen.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight on the plate against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Three grains of margin.

Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two under a band that had come off a suspension on the afternoon of the twenty-ninth and gone straight back on to a piece of paper dated the nineteenth of last month and a finding dated the twenty-ninth.

The plate had not moved a grain in the whole of the month and was not going to move one tomorrow morning either, because the Board paints bands and no band in this city had changed.

He got home at about six and the landing light was off, and Sun Meiling was at the table.

She had come down at some point in the afternoon, on the thirtieth, having said on the landing the evening before that she would, and she was in the chair at the end nearest the stairs with her coat on the back of it and her hands flat on the wood in front of her, and there was nothing in front of her hands.

Not a pen. Not a pad.

The notebook was open in the middle of the table with the day ruled at the top and the six lines for the day already in it in six hands, because it was the thirty-first of the month and the thirty-first was a day they had been keeping since the twenty-first of last month.

Ola Bright had not written anything down since the sixteenth of this month. That was the last night she had put a pen to a page in this house and it had cost her the only record in Ostara that had never once been asked for.

Marguerite Vale read the day's entries out loud in four minutes, the way she read them, and then put her hand flat on the visitors' book, which was shut, and said that there was one more week of this month in the book and that on the first of next month she was going to read it out for the last time and that there would be a hundred and nine in it, because three people had come on Friday and two of them had asked about a person.

"Two of them," said Nadia Ferrous.

"Two of them."

"Whose."

"Not one of you and not him, and it is not our business, and I have said so and I am not going to say again," said Marguerite Vale, and that was the end of the reading.

He stood at the table.

"Six lines a day since the twenty-first of last month," he said, "in six hands, and there is not one signature on any of it in either of those books and there is not going to be one this evening, and I have been waiting since the eighth of this month to be refused a third time and I would like to be refused a third time tonight in front of all of you so that it is finished."

Nadia Ferrous had both hands flat on the wood.

"No," she said.

"No."

"Not this evening and not on the first of next month and not in front of them, because you have known since the eighth of last month what we keep that book for and you have been waiting eleven days for one of us to say a reason instead of the word."

Beatrix Halm, at the end, did not turn round.

"It is not a signature we are refusing. You have been treating it like a signature for four months and it is not one. Every one of those six lines is where a woman was. That is the whole of what the book is.

"It does not have a place in it where a man who will not tell you where he was lives. If it did have a place in it you would fill it, and then it would be a book about where you were, and there is not one line in this city that would know what to do with that."

Tessa Okonkwo said, "You have not been in this room since the twenty-ninth of last month."

"I have been in this room."

"You have been in this room on the evenings when there is a line in it, and you have not been in this room on any other evening in two months," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and the difference is the book and you know the difference. "If you put a signature on that page tonight you will not be able to stand at a table on a Thursday morning again without four women in your house holding a piece of paper with your name on it. You did not need that in a hall and you do not need it in a kitchen."

Sun Meiling said the fourth from the end of the table, in the chair she had sat in twice since the twenty-seventh of last month, with her coat still on the back of it.

"No," she said. "And I am going to be quick about it, because I came down this stair an hour ago for the first time in a month and a half and I have not taken my coat off, and I am not going to start by handing a man a pen."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the only reason there is," said Sun Meiling. "I came down this stair to sit at a table, and I have spent four months not sitting at tables. If I hand you a pen in my first hour then the next four months of not sitting at tables were a performance, and they were not a performance, and I will not have it read that way afterwards.

"You want a signature. A signature is a thing you give to a man who has earned one. You did not earn one. You asked."

Marguerite Vale said, "A household is a document with legs on it and you have just written the seventh line of the longest one, and the moment the book carries your name in it, it stops being the six of us and becomes a set. I priced this house on the sixth of this month and it came out at less than nothing, and the reason it came out at less than nothing is that it is not a set."

He put his hand flat on the table on either side of the notebook.

"That is five," he said. "I have been waiting four months for that and I want the sixth, because there are six of you at this table and I have never once in four months got less than five."

The room did not move for about four seconds.

Ola Bright put her hand into the drawer under the table.

It came out with a pen in it, and she had put it in that drawer on the sixteenth of this month, in front of six women, on purpose, and had said she was not writing that one down.

She uncapped it and squared the notebook with her other hand and put the nib on the ruled space for the thirty-first of the month, under the sixth line, where there was no rule at all. The book has six lines a day and a woman to a line and a seventh space that has never been used, because there are six women who keep it.

She wrote one line.

Then she put the pen down and read it back in the voice she reads everything, and dated it, and did not give it to him.

"You wanted to be refused," she said. "You have been refused five times in four minutes by five women who each gave you a different reason, and none of the five reasons was about the signature, and every one of them was right.

"So here is the sixth one, and it is mine, and it is the only one of the six that is about the actual thing." She put her hand flat on the page.

"I stopped writing on the sixteenth of this month because a thing said before it happens is a thing somebody else can produce. And tonight I have written one line. It is the only line in this book that is not a woman's day, and it is yours, and it is the only sentence anybody in this house has ever put about you on a page we keep.

"It is not a signature. You cannot sign it and you cannot take it back, and if the Bureau ever sent a woman to this house with a form she would be able to read that line and say not one word about it.

"Because it does not tell her where you were, and it does not tell her what is in you, and it tells her nothing at all except that a man who cleans six floors asked five women for a pen and got one sentence instead."

He read it. He read it twice.

He did not say thank you, and she did not wait for it, and not one of the six at that table said anything about the eighth of last month or the twenty-ninth of this month or a table in a hall.

He went up the stairs at about ten and did not sleep much, which was four nights out of five, and there was nothing at all on the landing at all.

A household of seven women. Six of them keep a book with six lines a day and one line in it that is his. Six lines a day, six hands, every evening since the twenty-first of last month, a refusal on the eighth of this month and a second refusal tonight with five reasons and a sixth line in a hand that had stopped writing fifteen days ago on purpose.

Seven women and none of them on the roll. That was the finding of the thirtieth and he had not expected to arrive at it and it was the only thing in three months that had gone the right way.

He wanted nine. He had wanted nine since he was fifteen and it was why he cleaned six floors instead of anything else, and he had not moved a grain in a month in which he stood at a table in front of two hundred and eleven people and did the only thing in this city he had ever been able to do.

Rank nine opens any file in Ostara.

His brother's file was sealed on a floor above the top band and it had been sealed for three years and the only reason it was sealed was that the ceiling of the city is nine and the nine are countable and he was four, with three grains of margin and a requisition paper and a finding and a description of himself in his own hand in four books.

He had eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two grains to go and he had no way on this earth of earning them, because the only thing he had was the thing that could not be written down, and a household of seven women who were not on any roll in Ostara were the only people in the city who knew that, and they had spent four months refusing to be paid for it and three months refusing to be kept by it.

The month ended. The Board would repaint at nine on Thursday morning and paint nothing that had anything to do with him.

On the first of next month a broker would read a book of a hundred and nine aloud at that table and put her hand on a closed visitors' book and say that one of the four women in it was a woman with a card.

A clerk would take a woman's forty-one names up to a counter on Cobb Street on a Friday. A doctor would open a day book on the first and find a name already written in it. A woman in a workroom would write the same sentence on a bench paper once more, because she has never in her life been able to help it.

And nowhere in any of that would there be a line with his name on it. That was the only thing he had built in three months, and it was not a rank, and it could not be requisitioned, and it could not be classified, and it was not going to be enough.

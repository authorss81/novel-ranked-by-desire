# Six Names In A Minute

Tove Marlin came to the Fourth Branch on the nineteenth at eight in the morning, because the hall's copy of the first exhibit had come back from Field with a date on it, and the date was a demand, and she wanted him to hear it from her before he heard it in a corridor.

He had cleaned three floors before she came and left three for after, and the register queue was the ordinary queue, the same one that had been there every morning that week. Anneke Roos served it and squared the book and did not ask why a Wing clerk stood at the end of it with a tube under her arm.

They stood at the end of the queue with their voices down. Anneke served the queue and did not look at them, which was its own courtesy.

"Read it," said Tove, and put the minute on the counter between them.

He read it. Six lines in a serving officer's hand, being a household line entered at the member's word on the sixth of last month. For a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word, at the serving officer's request.

"It took a minute," said Tove. "Six lines. A minute to read them aloud. That is the whole of the first exhibit, and it is proved, and it will be read in the hall on the twenty-ninth."

"And the cover."

"The cover came back unsigned, and Field has sent it back with a demand." She put the cover note beside the minute. "The hall's copy wants attestation as a whole. The attestation on the copy alone is not sufficient. The officer will complete it by the twenty-sixth or answer for the gap."

Lin Hao looked at the two papers. Forty-one days to the hall. Twelve days left in this month after the nineteenth, and twenty-nine in the next.

He was cold. That was the feeling, and it went through him the way winter goes through a stairwell. He had spent a week watching the Office build its record out of everything that touched him, and now the record had found the one place where its arithmetic did not close.

"What gap," he said.

"Six names where the file says more." Tove said it flatly, the way she said everything. "The minute covers six. The household book covers six lines a day. The visitors' book asks about somebody in the household on thirteen entries, as the exhibit sheet has it since the eleventh. The delivery record carries forty-one names. Every paper the Office holds about your household counts six, and every file the district holds about the address counts more."

She laid the papers out on the counter one by one as she named them, the way a clerk lays a hand of cards. The minute. The exhibit sheet for the book. The exhibit sheet for the visitors' entries. The exhibit sheet for the forty-one.

He did the subtraction standing at the counter with the queue moving past him. Six on every Office paper. More on every district file. The difference was one woman, and the one woman was the one with no line anywhere, and he had known it since the seventh, when Tove read the roll exactly as written and never named her.

"Say it," said Tove.

"The seventh line is Sun. She keeps no notebook and she was on the landing while the minute was written. Field counted the stairs."

She put her finger on the sixth line of the minute.

"The seventh line is the gap. Field wants the minute completed to seven by the twenty-sixth, and completing it puts a seventh name on hall paper, and the only name in that house that is not already on hall paper is the woman on the landing."

Sun Meiling. Off paper. Not in the book. The woman the household's protection could not hold and would not give up.

What he wanted was to fill the seventh line himself, with anything, with a stranger's name, with his own, so that Field had its seven and Sun stayed off paper. He could not. A false line on a Field minute was a forgery, and a forgery was a finding, and a finding went into the hall with his name on it.

"Field knows," he said.

"Field has known since the sixth." Tove took her finger off the paper. "Runa wrote six lines at my request and I read them back to her as available, and the seventh woman was standing on a landing while she did it, and Field counted the stairs. That is the arithmetic the roll already shows before anyone proves anything. Six where the file says more."

He understood the decision then, standing at the end of a register queue with a minute on the counter between them. Field would come back for the seventh name by the twenty-sixth, and Runa would sign it or refuse it, and either way the hearing weeks out would firm into a date. The only move that kept Sun off paper was to let the minute stand short, and letting it stand short put Runa back in front of Field.

One woman or the other. That was the arithmetic. Six or seven. There was no third number.

"Let it stand at six," he said. "I will not supply the seventh line. Tell Field the member declines to complete another officer's minute, and put that in writing, and let them do what they do."

Tove looked at him for a long time.

"That is a refusal," she said. "It will go on the record next to the roll, and it will not stop anything, and Field will send the demand to Runa, and she will answer it or not, and the hearing will firm."

"I know."

"And Sun stays off paper."

"Sun stays off paper." He put his hand on the counter, not on the minute. "Six names in a minute. That is what the hall gets. The seventh is not mine to give, and it was never in the minute, and I will say so on the twenty-ninth if I am asked."

She folded the cover note and put it back in the tube. The minute stayed on the counter between them for another second, six lines in a serving officer's hand, his word on the sixth.

"Write my refusal," he said. "In your book, in your hand. The member declines to complete another officer's minute."

Tove took out her own day book and wrote it, and read it back, and he nodded once, and she dated it the nineteenth. It was not a registrar's paper and it did not touch the roll. It was a clerk writing down what a member said, on a morning, at a counter, the way clerks had written things down since before he was hired.

"Runa will sign it," said Tove. "Or she will not. That is hers and it is still hers. But I want you to know, because you carried half a press bed for her on the twelfth, that the demand going to her instead of to you is not a thing you chose. It is a thing the arithmetic chose, and you stood in front of it."

She went out with the tube under her arm. Anneke Roos served the last of the queue and squared the book and did not ask him anything, which was the longest courtesy anybody had done him that week.

He cleaned the last three floors with the arithmetic in his head. Six lines. A minute to read. Forty-one names in a delivery record. Thirteen entries asking about somebody on the exhibit sheet, and fourteen in the book itself when Marguerite last read it aloud. Seven women in a house above a chop-house, and six of them on paper, and one of them on a landing.

On the fifth floor a clerk stopped him and asked whether the Office had been back, and he said it had not been back to the fifth floor, and the clerk nodded as though that were an answer, and went back to her room. On the ground floor Drant watched him take the bucket through and said nothing, which was the most any of them said to each other that day.

At four the classification paper went up three floors, unsigned, and came back down. He watched it come back and thought about a seventh line that would never be written, and a woman who would answer for the gap, and a woman on a landing who would not.

He walked home past the square, where the Board stood with a stranger's tile in his place and the district frame held his notice between the Exchange hours and a lost parcel. It would hang there until the third of next month and then go into a house file in Cobb Street. Forty-one days, and the paper aging on a frame, and nobody looking at it twice.

He had refused to complete the minute. The refusal would go on the record and stop nothing. Field would send the demand to Runa by the twenty-sixth, and the hearing weeks out would firm into a date, and that was the cost, and it was hers, and it was still hers.

But Sun was off paper. That was what the numbers had bought. Six where the file said more, and the more staying off, and the hall getting a minute that took a minute to read and left a silence after it where a seventh name would have been.

He told the table that evening, in her words as near as he could hold them, standing at the foot of it with the notebook shut under Marguerite's hand. Six names. A refusal. A demand going to Runa by the twenty-sixth. Nobody argued. Tessa put her hand over her eyes for a second. Beatrix poured tea all round without being asked.

Nadia went up to the landing with the news, because news went up the stairs in that house even when the woman on the landing would not come down for it. She came back in ten minutes and sat down and said nothing, which for Nadia Ferrous was a considered act, and the room let it stand.

# The Price Of A Public Good Deed

He did it at about eleven in the morning on the thirteenth, in front of eleven people, on purpose, having calculated it on the Sunday.

The calculation was this. A man who intends to announce in advance that he will touch no person in a room is a man making a claim about himself that not one instrument in this city can check. The Office cannot check it and does not need to, because the hall will check it in the only way a hall can: it will watch him for four hours and see whether a single person comes near him and whether he moves.

But a claim like that is only worth something if it is the sort of claim a man makes who has been doing the thing already. And there was no version of the next three weeks in which a washroom attendant cleaning six floors could be said to have been standing in the open all day taking care not to touch anybody.

He had never once in four years managed that in a corridor with a queue going past.

So he was going to have to do it in the open. Once, where it could be seen, and where the seeing would cost something, and where the something would not be him.

He had known since the seventh and he had waited six days to find the right pair of hands, and the right pair of hands were the ones that were going to be hurt.

He cleaned the second floor first because it is the floor the queue starts on, and the queue was twenty deep, and he took the bucket through twice and worked round the outside of it with the mop the way he had for four years.

Outside the front gate of the Fourth Branch at about eleven, a woman he had cleaned past for four years went down on the bottom step with a bundle in both arms. She did not fall. She sat down on the step with her legs out in front of her and the bundle went over her knees and she put both hands on it and stayed where she was.

There were eleven people in that part of the forecourt. He counted them, because he had decided in advance that he was going to count them, and he counted eleven, and a clerk at the plate window and two women going in at the door and a man with a plate in his hand and a queue of seven on the rail side and a woman crossing.

He put the bucket down.

He picked up the bundle, which was heavier than it looked and was a box of ledgers tied with string, and carried it in through the public door and up the stair to the second floor, because the woman told him the records had to go up to the second floor and then said thank you and went back down to her step, and he carried it up in two goes with a bucket under his arm in the first go.

Eleven people. In the open. In front of a gate that two thousand people a day walked past, with a plate in one of their hands and the notice on a frame in a square four hundred yards away.

It went into the branch register, because Anneke Roos does what a register does, and she did not ask him why, and she wrote it in with a date and did not write a reason, which is a thing she has never done in eleven years.

"Records up, second floor," she said, and squared the book.

That was the deed, and it was witnessed, and he knew what that meant before he had put the box down, because the whole of his working life has been the arithmetic of a thing that only arrives when a room is empty.

It had not arrived.

That was the first half of the price, and he paid it at the register counter at ten past eleven and understood it in about four seconds, standing there with his hands on the counter in the building he had worked in for four years.

A deed that is witnessed is a deed that is recorded. And a deed that is recorded is a deed that does not come to him. He had known that for four years the way a man knows that a door sticks. He had a hundred and twelve lines in a drawer and not one of them was written down, and the reason not one of them was written down was that not one person had ever seen him do any of it.

He had carried that box up a flight of stairs in front of eleven people, and the city had watched, and the city had put it in a book, and nothing had come.

The grey came about a second and a half behind it, at his left temple, the way it always came, and it stayed for the rest of the day, and he did not sleep that night.

He had not been expecting it to cost him anything. That was the second thing that went wrong and it took him about twenty minutes to understand it.

The grey at one temple was not the cost. The grey was what happened when a thing he could not move asked a body that had nothing on the other side of the person it touched. The box was a box. The woman at the step had not fed him and was never going to feed him, and the whole of what the account could do there was nothing, and the asking was charged to him at a rate that has never once improved.

He had known that too. He had known it since a yard gate in the spring.

What he had not known, and what he worked out sitting on a stair at half past eleven that morning with a bucket under his arm, was that he would choose to do it again. That was the price, and it was not the temple.

The notice came off the district board in the Reading Square on the third of this month and went into a house file in Cobb Street. But a notice about a hearing goes around a city by being talked about, and there are two thousand people a day through that gate, and eleven of them had just watched a man with a bucket carry a box up a flight of stairs for a woman who had sat down on a step.

By that evening there were nine people at the branch counter who had not been there the week before.

That was the arithmetic, and it was not complicated and it was not a discovery. A deed done in the open at the Fourth Branch in the third week of a month in which the city knows your name is a deed that is not about the woman on the step.

He worked it out properly at the table that night, with six women round it, and it took him about eleven seconds, and he said the name himself, because he had said it to himself on Sunday and he was not going to let somebody else say it first.

"Nadia," he said.

She had her hands flat on the table already. She had had them flat on the table since he said the name of the gate.

"Forty-one names," said Nadia Ferrous. "Read out loud in a room on Cobb Street on the tenth, four hundred and twenty seconds, by a woman with a good voice, and there are nine of them at that counter who were not there last week and every one of them is a person who knows you carry things for people."

"And on the twenty-ninth there will be a bigger room," he said.

"There will be a bigger room."

"And the roll gets read to a bigger room."

"The roll gets read to a bigger room," said Nadia Ferrous. "And the forty-one names are in it, and they are the only names in it that belong to somebody who is not a party to anything and has not been asked about anything, and they were put there by me on purpose in four seconds in a car, and every one of them is a woman who rode my lift between the Vestry and the arcade in the second week of last month."

She put her hands down flat, which was worse than anything else she could have done with them.

"That is the price," she said. "You did a good thing in the open at your own gate because you have to be a man who does good things in the open, and the bill for it is my page, and it is not going to be a hundred and six names in a visitors' book in Bellhouse. It is going to be a hall with people in it, and it is going to be the third week of the month instead of the first."

"Nadia—"

"Do not," said Nadia Ferrous. "I am not angry. I priced it, and I priced it in about a minute, and the price is that I am the woman whose forty-one names are read to two hundred and eleven people instead of thirty."

She put one hand flat on the page in front of her, on nothing.

"I handed that page over myself, and there is not one line in any document in this city that says I chose it."

She looked round the table.

"And you are going to have to sit in that room knowing it, and it is going to be the only thing about the twenty-ninth that you are actually able to be ashamed of, and everything else you will be able to tell yourself was the machine's fault."

Not one of them spoke for about four seconds.

"That is the second time you have been right in a month about a thing that costs me," said Beatrix Halm, "and the first time you cost me a year of my own handwriting."

"Yes," said Nadia Ferrous.

"Right," said Beatrix. "Then I am putting my book in the same place as his and not one word is to be said about it, because if two of us are in this then it is a house and not a bill."

Ines Duarte had not spoken. She had the folder open on her knees, which she had brought to the table on purpose.

"You did the arithmetic correctly and you did it late," she said. "You worked out on Sunday that the deed had to be visible, and you have carried that around two days, and on the sixth of this month Marguerite Vale priced this house at less than nothing in this room and told you that the price would be presented on the fourteenth, and you have had a week to sit with what the bill was going to be."

"Yes."

"Then say what you did it for, because there are two versions and one of them is that you bought yourself the next sixteen days at the cost of a woman who works in a lift."

He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the notebook.

"I did it because I am going into a hall on the twenty-ninth and be told to stand still in it, and a man who has been caught standing still in a forecourt eleven times is a man whose stillness has a shape, and a man whose stillness has a shape is a man who is not moving because of anything in the room."

"That is the first true sentence anybody has said about that plan in eight days," said Ines Duarte, "and it is the worst possible reason for it, and both of those are true, and I am not going to pick one."

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, three grains of margin, and eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two short of a band that was sitting on an instrument in another building, suspended pending a description. It had not moved a grain since the winter and it was not going to move one on the fourteenth of this month either.

Sixteen days. Sixteen less the thirteenth, and both in this month.

He washed his face at the basin on the second floor at about nine that night, because he had grey at one temple and a night behind him that had been a night of being woken on the hour, and the basin was the one on the second floor and it ran cold.

He had done a good deed in the open, and it had cost a night of sleep and a grey temple and a woman who had spent a month being right about a thing she had said would happen to her and not to him.

And eleven people had seen him carry a box up a flight of stairs, and the woman on the step had gone home and would not remember his face, and the city had a slightly larger shape around his name at seven in the morning than it had on the Sunday, and the shape of it was a man's hands.

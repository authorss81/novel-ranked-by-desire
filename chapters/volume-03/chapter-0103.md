# The Man From Cobb Street

The hall was on the top floor of the Exchange building on Cobb Street and he went up at eight in the morning with eleven minutes to spare, and the reason he went at all was that a woman on Lantern Row had told him in the voice she used for figures that there was no version of this where he found out what the room was from a chair.

The stairs went up through the middle of the building and the last flight had a plate on the wall that said the works department had rated the room at two hundred and thirty and that any person who wished to dispute the rating could do so in writing at the Exchange counter on any weekday, which he read twice, because a man who puts a number on the wall of a room is a man who expects to be asked whether the room is full.

Inside there were a hundred and forty seats in four ranks and a rail behind the back row, and a bench raised on a platform at the far end with three chairs on it and a fourth chair at a table below it. High windows with twenty years of grime on the outside of them.

The floor was swept. Nothing in the room was new and one thing in it was old, which was the bench, and it had been built for somebody who wanted to be seen sitting above everybody else and had been used that way perhaps twice in a decade.

There was a clerk at the table at the bottom with a folder and no pen in his hand, and the clerk was not doing anything, and Yusuf Adeyemi came in through a side door with his coat over his arm and stopped about six feet inside.

"Eleven early," Adeyemi said. "Sit anywhere. I will be four minutes."

He went up to the table and said something to the clerk that Lin Hao did not hear and the clerk wrote down a time and put the pen in the drawer.

Adeyemi came back with two chairs turned to face each other in the second rank, about a third of the way back from the door, which was where a man who wanted to be able to see the door and the bench at the same time would sit.

"Sit down," he said. "I am going to be decent about this and you are going to decide at some point today that I was not, and I would like to be judged on what I say rather than on what you have already decided."

He sat. The clerk went back to doing nothing.

"You will have had it by now," Adeyemi said. "The notice. Served on the first, dated the first, published on the district board at eight this morning, the earliest that board does publication, which is why it is in your coat by nine and not by four."

Lin Hao said nothing, because there was nothing to say to a man who had just told him the exact hour of a thing that had happened eight streets away.

"You have read the third paragraph four times and you have not read the second one once, and the second one is where the room is." Adeyemi put his forearms on his knees. "Cobb Street, top floor, this hall, ten in the morning. That is the whole of it and I will not read it out again."

"Who are you."

"Yusuf Adeyemi. I run the hearings." He said it the way a man says the name of the street he lives on. "Eleven years, and I have never had a clerk with me that I needed. There is one here because a clerk is required to be present at a sitting, and because the transcript is made by two people, and I write very fast. That is not a boast. It is a credential."

"How many do you do."

"Two a month, some months none." He looked along the seats without interest in them. "There is a list. Yours is the eleventh on it, the eleventh hearing set, and the eleventh date."

"It is not a special date. It was the first free one after the notice was drafted, and the notice was drafted on the twenty-second of last month and served on the first, and I will tell you why it was drafted three weeks before it was served. Because you will assume it was drafted because of you, and it was not."

"Because of somebody else."

"Because of a form on a desk." Adeyemi looked at him. "A clerk somewhere up the road could not close an inquiry with an unsigned paper, so a procedure was looked for, and the procedure turned out to require a hearing, and the hearing requires a date, and dates go in the order they arrive."

"You did not cause it and you cannot appeal it. If you had been arrested on the third instead of the first it would have been the same procedure and a different date, and you would not have heard sooner or later." He turned one hand over.

"I have run this list for eleven years. Every name on it has had that conversation, and about a third have believed the part about it not being about them. All of them are wrong. It does me no good to say so."

Lin Hao looked up at the bench.

"What happens in there."

"That is what I am for, and I am going to give it to you straight, because you have had four months of offices and you are now at the stage where the truth is cheaper than the other thing and it is also the only thing I have ever had to give anybody." He held up a hand and counted on it, and he did it fast, and he did it exactly once, and it took him about nine seconds.

"It is a hearing and it is not a trial. There is no charge. There is no accuser. I read the notice, I read what the member says, I read what the Office has, and then somebody writes it down in the Office's words and that document is the only description that has ever existed and it does not go into your file, it goes into mine, and mine is not the same file as yours and never has been.

"You may speak for as long as you like and you will not be interrupted, including when you are wrong, and I will tell you when you are finished and not before. The Office must put its case in full. I mean that in the strict sense of the word, and if they leave something out I stop them, and they know I will, and there are three things I have stopped in eleven years.

"You may bring one witness. One, not nine, and the witness may be asked questions by the Office and by me and you cannot stop that. The Office may bring an instrument, which means a machine, which means a person who has been sent to work it, and an instrument is not evidence of anything until somebody in this room has said what it does.

"The room has a hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety standing behind the back row and the works department rates it at two hundred and thirty on that plate by the door, and the rating is the only number in this building I would stake a clerk's career on. It will not be full. It never is."

He stopped. That was the six sentences and he had not put weight on any of them, and he had not once looked at the notice or at Lin Hao's coat or at anything at all except the man's face.

"Now the other thing," said Adeyemi, "and I am giving you this as a courtesy and I want it on the record that I am giving it as a courtesy and not as a warning, because a warning implies I want something and I do not want anything, I have a date and a room and a transcript and that is the entire ambition of my working life."

Lin Hao waited.

"The instrument the Office will bring reads a subject and a counterparty." Adeyemi said it in the same voice. "It has never once worked in an empty room. Not once in the eleven years I have been doing this, and not once before that."

"The Certification desk will tell you the same thing if you ask them, and they will tell you it as a limitation and I will tell you it as a schedule, and the difference between those two sentences is about four hundred words and a working life."

"The Office expects a full hall. Every hearing I have sat has been in front of more people than the schedule called for, and I have had to move the rail twice, and I have never once had to ask anybody to stay out of a room."

"A hearing is public. There is no door on it, no list, no ticket, and no version of it where I could keep a person out if I wanted to. And I would not if I could. I have put that in a file once, and a woman with a card rang me up afterwards and made me read it out loud to her twice in a corridor."

The hall was very quiet. High up, a window had a bird on the outside of it.

"You did not say that as a threat."

"I said it as a courtesy and I meant it exactly as I said it, and I would like to hear you say the thing you are about to say, which is that it is worse as a courtesy." He put his hands on his knees. "It is worse. Do it."

Lin Hao looked at the rail and the ninety places behind it and the plate by the door with a number on it, and the room came in on him from the top.

"You cannot tell a man something like that and then let him walk into the building."

"I have just let you walk into the building," said Adeyemi. "That is what the courtesy is. I could have said nothing at all and you would have found out in the room, at the table, with the machine switched on and a hundred and forty strangers watching a man fail to move something, and you would have found out that I knew it and said nothing. You have nine days now instead of none."

He got up quickly and did not straighten his coat.

"Nine days is not a lot," he said. "It is not meant to be. It is meant to be enough for a man with one move to work out."

"If you do not have one by the day, I will sit there and I will do the hearing and it will be a fair hearing and you will lose it. There is nothing in this room I can do about that and I have never pretended otherwise to anybody. I have lost about a third of them and I still turn up."

He said it with no weight at all, which was the hardest part to believe.

"The date," said Lin Hao.

"The twenty-ninth of the month after next, at ten." Adeyemi said it exactly and he did not consult anything, which told Lin Hao more about him than anything he had said in nine seconds.

"It is the same date as your notice, because the notice is mine. Everything on that paper was typed in this building by a woman on that table, and she is not permitted to be glad that you are here and is not going to be, and I have not spoken to her about you and I am not going to."

The clerk had not moved.

"The one question I am allowed to ask you today," Adeyemi said, "because I am not running a hearing yet and I am a man in a room with you and the rules about questions belong to the day. It is not on the notice and it is not in the transcript and I will not repeat it."

"Ask it."

"Do you intend to contest it." He said it plainly. "Not now. I know not now. I am asking what you will walk in there intending, because a man who has come to answer a question and a man who has come to refuse one are two different men in a room and the room will treat them differently and I would rather know which one I am putting on a bench."

Lin Hao looked at the door behind him, and at the rail, and at the four ranks of seats in a municipal room that was not clean and not new, and at the high windows, and at the plate by the door with a number on it.

"I am going to walk in there to refuse it," he said, "and I am going to refuse it in front of the Office, and I am going to have said one sentence out loud before I do it that is mine and that they have to write down in their own words."

"And I am not going to bring a witness. You have told me it is one, and I do not have one who could say it, and the woman who could would be doing it to correct me in front of a hall, and I will not have that done to her in a room."

He felt the sentence come out sideways and let it, because it was true, and because it was the first in two months not put together for a clerk.

Adeyemi stood very still for about two seconds, which was the only thing that happened in the room that was not in the schedule.

"Nine days," he said. "That is a good answer and it is a bad answer and I have heard both of those from about a third of the people on that list, and the difference between the good and the bad is entirely in the fourth week, and you have three weeks to find out which one of them you are."

He held out his hand, and it was a working hand, and the grip lasted about a second and told Lin Hao nothing except that the man had done this nine hundred times.

"Do not come back here before the twenty-ninth," said Adeyemi. "I will see you on the day, and I will read the notice, and I will give you the whole of your own time, and if you want a place where you can sit and not be looked at for the fifty minutes before, then come at nine and sit in the fourth rank and I will not look at you."

He picked up his coat and put it on and went out through the side door, and the clerk put the pen back in the drawer and shut the folder and sat there with her hands flat on it, not looking at Lin Hao, in the way of a woman who works in a building and has decided that a man is not her business.

Lin Hao sat in the third rank of a municipal hall on Cobb Street for about four minutes after the hearing officer had left it — a room that was not full and would not be full, with a rail and ninety places behind it and a plate on the wall that said two hundred and thirty — and he understood the thing he had been feeling since a woman had said nine weeks in a corridor.

It was not fear.

It was the feeling a man gets when a room turns out to be larger than the man in it.

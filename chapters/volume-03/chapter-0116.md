# A Tool With Two Columns

He came at ten on the sixteenth and stood where he had stood, and the clerk put the request on the counter face up and kept his hand flat on the bottom of it, which meant read and do not take.

He had cleaned six floors before coming, with the second floor first because a reading queue formed there on sitting days, and on the ground floor a clerk had asked him for the washroom key and called him attendant, which was the first time since the first of the month anybody in that building had called him anything at all.

It had gone into the book with the queue and the key. The cause named, and no number.

It was one sheet. The Office's mark at the top, a finding number in the corner, two paragraphs in a clerk's hand, and a line at the foot for the builder's answer.

The first paragraph said what the desk wanted. Certification that the counter-forensics tool held on the second bench met its specification and could be put before the hall.

The second paragraph named the officer outside the standing roll who had built it, and asked her to confirm in her own hand that she had built it, and to state the tool's form as she had made it.

The name was Wren Kallo.

Lin Hao read it three times. It was the first time the name had been on any paper in front of him, and it sat in the second paragraph in the middle of a sentence about a bench, the way names sit in documents when the document is not about them.

"She built it," said the clerk. "Seven years ago, on the second bench, out of a Field request and a broken adding machine and a winter with nothing in it. The desk has run it since and nobody has ever asked her about it."

"Until now."

"Until the hall." The clerk took his hand off the sheet. "A hearing wants a builder the way a hearing wants a transcript. Somebody has to have made the thing, and the somebody has to say so, and then the hall can get on with the rest of it."

Under the two paragraphs, pasted to the sheet by one corner, was a slip with the tool's form on it, described the way a desk describes a thing, flatly, with no claim in it.

Two columns on paper output. A subject column and a counterparty column. The subject column took the member's plate. The counterparty column took what the tool found.

That was all it said. No sentence about what the columns meant. No sentence about when the second one filled. A description of paper, not of work.

"What does the desk do with a slip like that," he said.

"The desk files it with the request and runs the tool against it." The clerk touched the slip with one finger. "The specification is the paper. The run is the work. If the work matches the paper, the desk certifies. The desk does not say what the columns mean, because meaning is the hall's business, and the hall has a hearing officer for it."

He read the slip twice and understood that it was telling him nothing he had not been told on the third, in a hall, by a man being courteous. The instrument read a subject and a counterparty. The paper had two columns. The rest was the twenty-ninth.

He ate from the staff kettle at noon with the bucket beside his chair, the way he had for four years, and a clerk told a story about a socket that sparked and nobody listened, and the room went on being a room where nobody asked him anything, and he understood that the room would go on being that room right up to the twenty-ninth, and that no room after it would ever be that room again.

In the afternoon the plate queue stood thirty deep at the socket on the ground floor, and he worked round it with the mop, and the second door past the plate office in the other building stood shut in his mind the whole time, with no sound behind it that he could hear from four streets away, and he mopped toward it and away from it, which was the nearest thing to going up a stair he was permitted.

He felt it then, cold at the back of his neck, the particular unease of a man reading a name that has been in a building for seven years and has never once reached him. Seven years on a bench. Outside the standing roll. The only officer outside it who ever built one of these.

What he wanted was to know who she was. Not what she looked like. What room she worked in, what she did with her hands, whether she knew what the Office was going to do with the thing she had made in a winter with nothing in it.

"Where is the second bench," he said.

"Up the back stair of this building, past the plate office, second door." The clerk folded the request and put it in a hand envelope, and wrote the direction on it in the same hand as the paragraphs. "You are not going up it. A member does not call on a builder before she answers, and the answer comes back to this desk and not to you."

"What does outside the standing roll mean."

The clerk sealed the envelope with a wafer and pressed it with his thumb. "It means she holds no standing post and sits on no roll of officers, and no office can compel her attendance by schedule. She attends by request, which is why the request goes by hand. Seven years she has benched that tool and seven years no office has had to ask her for anything, because the tool never went into a hall before."

He put the envelope in the runner's tray.

"When."

"She has three days." The clerk sealed the envelope. "It goes by hand this morning. A runner takes it up the back stair and waits. She reads it and writes on the line at the foot or she does not, and the runner brings it back down."

He watched the clerk write the direction on the envelope in the same hand as the paragraphs. Wren Kallo. Second bench. By hand.

A runner took it up the back stair within the hour, with the morning post, and Lin Hao stood at the foot of the stair past the plate office and watched her go up, because a member could watch a stair even when he could not climb it to a door.

The plate queue stood with plates in their hands. The second door past it was shut. Behind it there was no sound at all, and he stood there until the runner came back down with an empty tray, and the runner nodded at him, because runners nod at anybody who stands at a stair long enough, and he nodded back, because a stair was the only thing in the building he was permitted.

"Forty-four days," said the clerk, without looking up. Fifteen days left in this month after the sixteenth, and twenty-nine in the next, and the clerk had done the arithmetic the way clerks do, as bookkeeping. "The hall is forty-four days out and the paper will be back by noon. That is the whole of the schedule anybody on this floor is keeping."

Lin Hao went down the stair past the plate office, where the queue stood with plates in their hands, and the second door past it was shut, and there was no sound behind it, and he went past without stopping, because a member did not call on a builder before she answered.

He went back to the branch and cleaned six floors of washrooms with a name in his head that had sat in a sentence about a bench. Wren Kallo. Outside the standing roll. Seven years on the second bench.

In the evening he told Marguerite alone, in the hall at Bellhouse, with the visitors' book shut on the table between them. He told her the builder had a name now, and the name, and that it had come in a document and not in a room.

She listened to the end and nodded once.

"Then it is already walking," she said. "A name in a document is a name with legs. By tomorrow the plate office will have it, because requests go by hand and hands talk. Do not tell the table tonight. Let them have one evening without a new name in it."

"And you."

"I will hold it till morning, and in the morning it will be everywhere." She put her hand on the shut book. "That is what holding means in this house. Not keeping. Carrying for a night."

He climbed the stairs above the chop-house and sat at the table with the notebook shut in front of him, six lines for the day in six hands, and his pen stayed in his pocket, and the signature stayed refused, and the house held its shape round him the way it had held it every night for three weeks, with one place at it empty and one woman on a landing.

At four the classification paper went up three floors, unsigned. At half past it came back down. He watched it come back from the register counter and felt nothing about it at all, which was new, because for three weeks he had watched it go with everything he had.

He lay awake that night with the photograph of two men on a tram step face down on the chair beside his bed, the way it always lay, and the name went round in his head in the dark, showing first one side and then the other. Wren Kallo. Seven years on a bench. Outside every roll anybody kept.

The paper that mattered was in a hand envelope on a back stair, with a line at the foot waiting for a woman's hand.

He finished the sixth floor and signed out at the gate and walked home up the long road, and the name went with him the way names do when they have just been read for the first time, turning over and over, showing first one side and then the other, and neither side telling him anything.

Wren Kallo had built the tool. In three days, or by noon, or whenever a runner came back down a back stair, she would say so in her own hand, and then the desk would have one of its two hands, and the hall would be one paper nearer.

# What He Signs Instead

The room above the chop-house had six women in it on the eighth and the notebook open on the table face up with a date ruled at the top of the page and nineteen days of lines in it, one for each of them, in six hands, and the handwriting had got neater rather than worse, which Ola Bright had said was a thing that happened to people.

He put the roll on the table in the middle of the notebook.

"Nineteen days," said Nadia Ferrous. "Twenty-first of last month to the eighth. Six lines a day. Say that out loud and then say what it is worth."

"I am going to say what it is."

"That is not the same and I am not letting you get away with it either." Nadia did not move her hands off the table. "Nineteen days of six women saying where they were, on a working day, on a day off, for no reason anybody has ever given me a good one for.

"And a woman with a card read it out of a room on the third floor of the Reading to a clerk called Tove Marlin, and wrote *a record kept by the subject's household* on a piece of paper. That is on a roll now, and on the twenty-ninth it goes in a hall and gets read."

Nobody contradicted her.

"So refuse it," said Nadia.

"Refuse what."

"Refuse the roll. All nine items. Put a refusal in on the twelfth and every document in that tube comes off it, and if the Office has nothing else it has to stand up on the twenty-ninth with a machine and a blank sheet." She had her hands flat now. "A refusal is a sentence. A sentence can be fought."

Lin Hao put his hand in his coat and took out the notice from the inside pocket, where it had been since the first, folded once, and put it flat on the table next to the roll.

"The Office does not read the roll out," he said. "It proves the roll first. Tove Marlin said that in a corridor on the seventh, exactly as written, which is that I may acknowledge the roll or object to it, and both are lawful."

"That is worse," said Beatrix Halm.

"If I acknowledge it, every document on it is read into that hall exactly as written, including this." He touched the notebook with two fingers and did not take them off. "If I object, the objection goes on the record next to the roll and does not stop the roll going in. If I do nothing, the roll is proved by me not disputing it, which is the same as acknowledging it with my mouth shut."

"Then there is nothing on that paper to be careful about," said Nadia.

"There is one item on it I can get out of the hall." Nadia Ferrous looked at him.

"The household notebook. Item four. It is the only one that is not a document an office produced, and Tove Marlin read its description out on the seventh and did not name it, because the description does not have a name in it. Six lines a day, one line per woman per day, from the twenty-first, signature refused. That is all it says. It does not say whose it is or where it is or anything about six women and a chop-house and a room."

He put his hand flat on the table beside the notebook, not on it.

"You will not let me sign it. I have asked twice and Nadia has given the reason, which is that the moment it is mine it is a document, and as long as it is ours it is six women being in rooms. You were right then. You are right now. And item four is on a roll, and in about seven weeks a clerk with a good voice will read the description of this book into a room anybody in this city can walk into."

"And what do you propose to do about that," said Nadia Ferrous, in the voice of a woman who already knew.

"Object to it. In writing, attached to the acknowledgement of the roll, and in before the twelfth, because the twelfth is the last day anybody will take it. The objection is that item four be excluded from being read aloud in the hall."

Ola Bright put her hand flat on the pad.

"That is the description of a document," she said. "You have just said the words. You are describing the notebook in your own hand, on a piece of paper with a date and a number on it, and it is going to a registrar, and a registrar is not a person, it is a stamp."

"Yes."

"And you have never been allowed to write a word in it."

"I have never been allowed to write a word in it," he said, "and I am going to describe it in a document that goes to an office. I am telling this table before I do it, because the other thing is to do it in a corridor at midnight and tell you in the morning."

Nadia Ferrous sat down.

"Refusing it, the roll goes in and the description gets read and nothing happens," she said. "What you are describing is that the description comes off the roll and goes into a registrar instead.

"And a registrar's file is shut, and it is on the Exchange floor, and nobody walks in and asks to read it, and that is why you want it. And that is the part I will have to think about for four days after you have gone, because it is the part where you are right, and being right is the part that costs."

"Nadia," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"Don't." Nadia put her hand up flat. "I am not going to say the thing that would be easy, which is that he has decided to give away our book so that he does not have to stand up in a hall and be described."

"That is not what he is doing."

"It is a quarter of it, and the quarter is about him." Nadia looked across the table. "Ines. Did he learn that from you?"

"No," said Ines Duarte, from the sill. "I have been watching him do it since the eighteenth of last month."

"Then where."

"In a corridor in the middle of last month, from a woman with a card, and he has done it to me twice since and both times it was better than the first."

"I am not going to argue with what he is about to do, because it is a good objection and the right one. Read it back to yourselves. Excluded from the roll means excluded from being read in the hall. It does not mean excluded from the file.

"An office that cannot produce a thing describes the thing, in a line, in a hand, and the line goes into the file with everything else, and the line is always longer than the document was and more specific than it."

"How much longer," said Ola Bright.

"Once it was two lines and the description of it ran to a paragraph and a half," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and it was wrong about two things in it."

"So you are all telling me not to do it," said Lin Hao.

"No." Marguerite Vale put her cup down. She had not spoken for twenty minutes. "I am the one person at this table who has been the other thing he is about to be, and I am not doing it twice in one week.

"There is one thing that can be done about it and it is not cheap. If he writes a description of it, somebody else has to write one, in a second hand, going to a second place, and the Office has to reconcile them or admit it picked one. An office reconciles before it admits."

"That is laundering," said Nadia Ferrous.

"It is two documents. It is not money and I am not touching it, because I had a woman in a room off this hall say a thing to me two evenings ago that I will not build on." Marguerite turned round. "It would have to be a hand with no drawer of its own. There is one that could describe that book without putting her name near it, and she is on that sill, and she has already refused once."

"Ask me anyway," said Ines Duarte.

"Go on," said Beatrix Halm. "Refuse properly. If you refuse in a room with six people in it you have to refuse with six people in it, and you do not get to be gracious about it."

"I am not going to do anything with it, and I will tell you why in a sentence and then I am going to drink my tea." Ines did not open the book.

"There is a hearing on the twenty-ninth in a hall with a hundred and forty seats in it, and the Office is going to put a description in front of that room. If a description of this notebook is on a registrar's file tonight, then on the twenty-ninth there are two descriptions of it in two offices.

"And there is one thing I have never seen in eleven years in any hearing room in this city, because it does not happen, which is that an office corrects itself out loud in front of a witness."

The room was very quiet.

"It will be the only true thing anybody in that hall has heard, and it will be there because of him, and he will be standing at a table being described while it happens. That is what I am refusing, and it is not about the notebook."

Lin Hao looked at her for about two seconds, and then he squared the notice up with the edge of the table.

"Right," he said. "Then it is on me."

He did it on the table in the room, because he had understood something in the last four minutes that he had not understood in a corridor with a woman holding a rule at him. A thing signed alone at midnight is a thing nobody in the room can argue with, and every one of these six women told him something tonight that he had not known, and none of them could have told him if he had gone home and done it alone.

Ola Bright wrote down what he said, read it back, and he corrected two words, which was the first time in two months he had corrected anything anybody had written.

Two sheets. The first was the acknowledgement of the exhibit roll of the seventh, nine items, received, dated, and signed.

The second was attached to it and was headed by nothing at all.

*I object to the reading aloud of item four in the hearing of the twenty-ninth, that item being a record kept by the household of the member and not a document of any office, and not the property of the member, and not produced by him, and not available to me to acknowledge.*

*I do not name it. I refer to it as item four.*

*Signed. Lin Hao.*

"Say that again," said Nadia Ferrous, and he did, and she made him say it a third time, and on the third she stopped him halfway. "No. Say it the way you will say it on the twenty-ninth. Then I will decide whether I can live in a house with you."

"It is not mine to describe and I am describing it in order to get it out of the hall."

"That is not a refusal to name it. That is an instruction." Ines did not open the day book. "A registrar has one duty, and the duty is to carry out a lawful instruction. If it is bound to obey then it is not permitted to ask what the instruction is about. If it is not bound to obey then nothing happens at all. You have put the whole of it on an office doing what it is told."

"Yes."

"Then read it back as written, let six women hear the exact shape of it, and sign it. Then I will tell you what happens tomorrow that you cannot do anything about."

The clerk came up the outside stair at half past eight and knocked, which is a thing she has done eleven times and never once on a Monday night.

Tove Marlin did not take a chair. She read the two sheets in the order they were written, turned them both round so the room could read them, and read the second aloud exactly as written.

"'I object to the reading aloud of item four,' she read, "'that item being a record kept by the household of the member and not a document of any office, and not the property of the member, and not produced by him, and not available to me to acknowledge. I do not name it. I refer to it as item four. Signed. Lin Hao.'"

She squared the two sheets together and gave them back.

"That is exact," she said. "There is no word in it I would have changed and none I would have wanted to soften, and I have wanted to soften a document eleven times and never done it. You are about to be the first man in this city to make that cost me something."

"Who else knows," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I know," said Tove Marlin. "And the man who comes at half past six on the ninth with a stamp. And whichever of you is writing everything down." She was at the door.

Ola Bright had her pencil down and had not written any of it. Nadia Ferrous looked at her, then at the ceiling, said very quietly, "Nineteen days," and went out on to the landing and did not come back for ten minutes.

"She is not angry," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"She is allowed nothing else tonight," said Nadia through the door. "Ines. You never answered me."

"I answered you two evenings ago," said Ines Duarte. "You have been holding it four days because you did not like it, and that is not the same as not having answered. Do something about the difference on your own time. I am not helping."

She got up off the sill and squared the day book beside the notebook.

"I told you in a room in the middle of last month that the only true description of you in this city is going to be one of your own sentences," she said. "I have not seen it and I am not going to. That is what happens tomorrow, and it is not the thing I was refusing. It is the price of the thing I was refusing."

She went, and Ola Bright went with her, because Ola Bright always went with the person who had read a day book out of a room.

Lin Hao sat at a table above a chop-house with his signature on a paper that could not be withdrawn and an objection in his own hand about a book that was not his, and the worst of it, the part nobody could have told him was coming, was that it had worked.

He had refused to sign their book for three weeks. He had been refused, had let himself be refused, and had understood at the time that being refused was the protection.

And in about nine minutes he had put a description of it into a document that goes to a registrar, and the description does not have their name in it and will not have their name in it, and it is going to sit on a shut file on the Exchange floor where nobody walks in and asks to read it.

Six women had let him do it.

Nobody said anything about it and nobody was going to. He had gone in expecting to be refused a signature and had come out with one. He had asked them for leave to sign a thing of theirs and they had let him, and that was the whole of it.

Marguerite Vale was at the door with her coat on.

"Do not go up the stairs yet," she said. "I am going to say a thing in a hall in three weeks and I am saying it here because there is nobody at this table who can stop me.

"I told you on the fifth that what was about to happen to you would make you the only man in Ostara whose condition cannot be taken back. It has. Not the way I said it. Because of a sentence about a book in a room above a chop-house, and not because of anything that happens to you on the twenty-ninth. I have been out-priced by a notebook in my own house."

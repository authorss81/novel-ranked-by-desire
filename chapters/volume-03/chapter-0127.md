# What The Instrument Cannot See

He wrote the question at the table above the chop-house on the evening of the twenty-sixth, with the notebook shut in front of him and six women at the table, and he wrote it out long because a short one would have been a guess.

Then he read it to them, which he had not planned to do, and Nadia Ferrous copied it out in her own hand and Ola Bright dated the page and put the date at the top, and not one of them argued with a sentence that had a question mark at the end of it.

The Question.

The certified run of the ninth, four years back, was made against the tool's specification and its counterparty column carries six names. The subject's case file for the same date, produced by the Office on the twenty-sixth, carries twenty-one recorded movements. On what conditions was the run made, and what does the tool read.

He got to the Exchange at ten on the twenty-seventh and went up the back stair with Tove Marlin, who had arranged for a room off the certification floor with a clerk in it, because a question to an instrument cannot be asked at a counter and a hearing officer who is not present has to have the question on paper by the close of the desk.

Thirty-three days. Four left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next.

The room was four feet by nine with a table and two chairs and a window that gave on the back of the next building. The clerk was the same one, and he had a specification sheet out before Lin Hao was through the door, which told him that the desk had known what the question was going to be, or had known what shape questions were.

"I have been running this tool for seven years," said the clerk, "and I will say this once, in this room, and then it goes on the paper and it is the answer, and this building will not need to have it again."

He turned the specification round.

"It is dated the seventh of a month seven years back, when the tool was built on the second bench, and it is a sheet of paper with the Office's mark on it and eleven clauses on it, and I have never had cause to read clause nine out loud to a person before, and I am going to do it now because that is what it is for."

Clause nine said that the tool observed a subject and a period.

Clause nine also said that the counterparty column was filled at the moment of contact between the observed subject and a person, in the room, while the tool was running.

It said, in the plainest language the Bureau has ever put on a piece of stationery, that the tool read nothing else.

"That is clause nine," said the clerk. "I have read you the rest of the specification on the fifteenth and on the sixteenth and it is two columns and a plate number, and that is all it has ever said, and on the sixteenth you asked me what the columns meant and I told you that was the hall's business." He put his finger on the line. "It was not the hall's business."

"It was on this sheet and I did not think to say so, and I have been thinking about that since the twenty-fifth, and I would like it entered that a clerk can be wrong by not speaking."

Tove Marlin read clause nine back, exactly as written, because she read everything exactly as written, and then she put the certified run and the case file side by side on the table with the tops level.

"Six," she said, and put her finger on the right-hand column of the run.

"Twenty-one," she said, and put her finger on the column of the file.

The room was quiet for about four seconds.

Lin Hao did the subtraction. It took no time at all. Twenty-one and six and the difference between them was fifteen, and the fifteen had been sitting on a piece of paper on a counter in front of him since the previous morning.

Fifteen of the twenty-one were not read by the tool. They were written down by an office, from what the subject said, on a day, in a room, by somebody with a pen.

"The fifteen are not a fault," said Tove Marlin. She said it to the two sheets and not to him. "That is the sentence I want in the hall and I am the only person in this building entitled to say it in that form, and I am saying it once. Those fifteen were not missed."

"They were recorded by hand from an account of what a man told a clerk, in an office, on the day, and every one of them is a fact and not an observation, and a fact that comes off a mouth and an observation that comes off a machine do not live in the same column, and somebody cut the head off the form because the form had no line for saying so."

She put her hands flat on the table either side of the two sheets.

"Clauses four to eight say the tool stores nothing between runs. There is no memory on it. It has no drawer. In seven years it has never held a thing from one hour to the next, and a man who has spent his life being told that a machine reads everything is looking at a machine that reads the room it is standing in and nothing else that has ever happened."

Then she stopped, and squared the two sheets, and did not say anything else, because she was a clerk who had said the thing three times and she was not going to make it four.

Lin Hao sat in a chair four feet by nine on the second floor of an Exchange building with the answer to the only question anybody had asked about the machine in six weeks in front of him, and he was aware of something in his own body before he was aware of anything else.

It was not the cold of a draw. He had not spent anything and he knew what the difference was, because he had felt the other one and it always announced itself first in the hand or the chest or a particular old rib.

This was a plain cold that came up out of the floor and settled in the middle of his back, and it was the cold a body has when it has just been told a true thing.

Four years of carrying a bucket through six floors and touching people. A hundred and twelve lines in a book in a drawer. Every one of them a person he had put a hand on.

Six names on a certified output, because six separate occasions somebody had been in a room with him while a machine was watching, and fifteen of the twenty-one on that date were not on it because they were not in the room and had not been in the room for years.

The whole of his life is a set of occasions. A machine cannot read a set of occasions, and it cannot read one tonight, and it cannot read a hundred and twelve.

What he wanted, sitting there, was the sheet. The specification was a document and the specification was the only piece of paper in the city that said in advance what that machine would do to him, and it had been sitting on a desk two floors under a room for seven years.

He did not ask for it. He asked for something else.


"Read the entry back," he said.

Tove Marlin had her pen out. "Say it."

"Enter the certified run of the ninth, four years back, the subject's case file for the same date, and the margin that says instrument out of tolerance, against my file."

The pen did not move.

"That is not a question about the instrument," she said. "That is a request, and it is a lawful one, and if you want it you have to tell me why you want it, because I write what a member says and not why a member thinks it."

"I want it on my file."

"That is the what. Tell me the why and I will write both, or I will write the what and you may speak for yourself in the hall."

Lin Hao looked at the two sheets on the table.

"I have wanted to tell a room that the machine cannot be trusted since the fifteenth," he said, "and I have been carrying that since the fifteenth, and I have just understood in this chair that if I put it in the record I will never be able to say it again. So I am putting it in the record first, in my own hand, against my own file, where it travels to the hall on the twenty-ninth and cannot be taken back out of it."

Tove Marlin wrote for about forty seconds and then turned the page round.

"'The member requests that the certified run of the ninth, four years back, together with the subject's case file for the same date and the margin note instrument out of tolerance, be entered against his file and be produced in the hall.'"

"That is exact," she said. "It is a request and not an objection, and it will not stop the instrument going on the table, and I am obliged to tell you what it does instead."

"Tell me."

"It goes on your file today. It goes on the roll. It is read out in that hall in the Office's own voice at some point on the morning, and it cannot be withdrawn by the person it names, which is you." She put the pen down.

"And on the fourteenth of this month you signed a sheet with an objection on it and there is a registrar's four lines in your file already, and you did that to keep a book of six women's out of a hall."

"You have now asked me to put six strangers' names on a sheet with your name at the top of it, and I have looked them up, because that is what I did on the twenty-second and I did not tell you because you had not asked."

"What are they."

"Five are in Kestrel Hill and one is in the Slough. Four of the five in Kestrel Hill have not been on a plate since the year it was run." She squared the page.

"The one in the Slough is a woman called Marta Vey and she is four streets from Ferrier Street and she has a plate and she is at the top of her trade. I have got her from a register in Cobb Street. I have not gone to her and I am not going to."

"On the twenty-ninth there will be a room with a hundred and forty seats in it and a clerk with a good voice, and in that room there will be the name of a woman four streets from your own house who has never been asked about anything by anybody, because of a sentence you said in a chair four feet by nine."

The room was very quiet. Outside the window the back of the next building did what the back of the next building does.

"And the other thing," said Tove Marlin. "Your witness stands on the twenty-ninth and answers for a machine."

"If the machine's own certified output is on your file, then the machine's own certified output is what your witness is standing behind, and she did not make that run, and she will be asked about it by the Office and by the hearing officer and by you, and she will not be able to say she was not on the bench that day, because she was on the bench that day for seven years and everybody in that corridor knows it."

Thirty-three days.

He had spent four months learning to keep a thing small enough that no office could take it off him, and he had just put six names and a margin in a file with his own request at the head of it, and the reason he had done it was that the alternative was to walk into a hall in thirty-three days holding a claim about a machine that a clerk in a room had already told him could not be made after the fact.

The clerk squared the specification sheet on the table.

"Clause nine is on the paper," he said. "It goes into the hall in the Office's mouth and I am not permitted to read it, because the desk certifies instruments and does not read them, and I have had my hand on that instrument for seven years and I am telling you now, before I go back to my tray, that I would rather be asked about it than have it read at me."

Then the three of them wrote their names on it in the order the room had put them there, and Lin Hao went down the back stair past the plate office, where the queue was standing with plates in their hands, and out into Cobb Street with a piece of paper in him that could not be taken back out of a file.

# What Runa Is Not Allowed

The hall on Cobb Street holds a hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety places standing behind the back row, and the works department rates it at two hundred and thirty on a plate by the door, and on the eleventh of the month the room was empty.

Tove Marlin had the room from nine because the Office's own rule is that an item is read in the room where it will be read, so that the member may stand in an empty hall on the eighteenth and hear what a hundred and forty strangers will be told about him before any of them is told it.

The clerk was the same clerk from the dry reading on the tenth. He had a third chair out now, and the chair was square to the door, and it was not for him.

"It is for the serving officer," said Tove Marlin, before he asked. "Item one is a Field minute of the sixth of last month, six lines, in the serving officer's hand. The officer is Runa Sato and she is a party to it and she has to hear it, and the rule for a party is that a party may be present for the reading of an item in which she is named."

"And she is not here."

"She is in the corridor and she has been there since twenty to nine, and I have told her twice she is welcome, and she has said twice that she will not come in."

Tove Marlin squared the three chairs.

"That is hers and I am not going to argue her out of it, and I have told the clerk it is hers, and the clerk has written it down as her decision. A clerk who argued a serving officer into a room would be doing something a clerk cannot do."

Runa Sato was sitting on the corridor bench with her hands on her knees and her sleeves rolled past the elbow, in a yard coat, at twenty past nine in the morning on the eleventh of a month, waiting to hear her own minute read out in an empty municipal hall.

"Six lines," she said, when he came down the corridor. "It took a minute to write. I have read it four times. It says a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word on the sixth, at my request."

"Yes."

"And a clerk with a good voice is going to read those six lines into a room that holds two hundred and thirty people, in about nine seconds, and the ninth of those seconds will be the silence where a seventh name would have been." She did not look at the door. "I have been standing in this corridor since twenty to nine and I have had the whole thing prepared and I am not going in and you cannot make me."

"I was not going to try."

"I know." She turned her head. "That is why I gave you a week to try."

He sat down on the bench at the other end of it, which is four feet, because the corridor bench in the Exchange building is four feet long and a man and a serving officer of the Field division cannot both be comfortable on it.

"Two things," she said. "The first is the hearing. Field wrote to me on the eighth, which is a letter about the twenty-sixth and not about the minute, and it is the third letter, and the second one firmed the date and this one firmed it again."

"What is the date."

"Twelve days after your hearing." She said it flatly. "I did not choose it and I would not have chosen it and it is the date the room is free. You will have been described on a Thursday and I will answer for it on a Thursday and the paper that tells me that has come out of the same office that told me to attest a minute as a whole, and I have put both of them in a drawer and I have not shown them to anybody."

"Runa—"

"The second thing is the letter that came yesterday." She took an envelope out of the yard coat's inside pocket and gave it to him, and it was the Office's mark and the hearing officer's name and it was not sealed. "That is the notice that the roll will be read on the twenty-ninth and that the parties named in it may attend. My name is in it as the serving officer who made item one. I am invited."

"You are invited."

"I am invited, and if I attend then I am a party in a room in public at ten in the morning on the day I am due to answer for a household line, and the Office can then say that I had an interest in the proceeding." She folded her hands. "If I do not attend then I have refused an invitation in writing, and that refusal is a document and a document is a third thing on a file that already has two letters on it."

"You have not answered it."

"I have had it since yesterday at four o'clock and I have not written a word, and I have not shown it to the officer's clerk, and I have not shown it to you until about ninety seconds ago."

She looked at the door.

"I showed it to you because you are going to find out from a transcript on the twenty-ninth that I was invited and not there, and I would rather you had the paper." She took her hand off it. "So here is the whole of what I am not allowed, and it is not one thing, and I have been not allowed three things in nine days by three offices that have never met."

She said the list without stopping, which was a field habit and not a performance.

"One. I am not allowed to attest the minute as a whole, and I answered that in my own hand on the twenty-sixth, and the answer is a written disobedience and the hearing is the price of it."

"Two. I am not allowed to be in this room. And I am not allowed an objection either, and that is the one I have been turning over since the weekend."

She put her hands flat on her knees.

"An objection from me goes on the record next to yours, and the record does not have two sides. If I write that the minute is an accurate copy and an incomplete one, Field reads an officer of the division saying on the record that a document her own office produced is wrong."

"And three."

"They will take it in about four minutes and they will take it a month before my hearing."

"And three."

"Three is that I am not allowed to be told anything," she said. "That is not an office. That is you."

She looked at him straight on.

"You have been in this building four times since the seventh and you have told me about a specification clause and a certified run and a margin in a dead man's handwriting and four forwards. Every single thing you have told me has been true."

He did not say that was not true.

"That is what I have worked out in nine days on a bench in a corridor," said Runa Sato. "I am not allowed to be there, and I am not allowed to object, and I am not allowed to be told. And you are the only person in this city who has been telling me things for a month, so you are the only person who could break the third one, and you have not, and I want you to know that I have noticed that you have not."

The door opened and Tove Marlin came out into the corridor with three sheets in her hand and the fountain pen in her coat pocket and the particular expression of a woman who is about to do a thing she has decided to do exactly.

"It is next," she said. "And I want to say the thing first, before I start, because I said it on the seventh of last month in a corridor to the two of you and I have said it once since and I am not going to improve on it."

"Say it."

"I am sorry about the room." She said it exactly as she had said it on the seventh. "That is the whole of what I have and there is nothing else in me about it, and I am going to read six lines and they are the truest thing on this roll, and the reason I read them here with a clerk in it rather than on the day in front of two hundred people is that you get to hear it once in an empty room first."

"It is empty."

"It is empty," said Tove Marlin, "and that is the reason I am not going to apologise for the names. I did that once, in a room with a person in it, and it was about her and not about the room, and I have had nine weeks to think about the difference."

She went back in and read it.

Six lines, and it took her about twenty seconds, which was longer than it needed to and everybody in the building understood that. The subject's word. The sixth of last month. At my request. Above a chop-house in Ferrier Street.

And then she stopped, and the silence in the hall went on for about four seconds past the end of the sentence, because a clerk with a good voice reading a minute with a missing line in it has to stand in the silence and not fill it, and she stood in it, and the clerk wrote nothing down for four seconds and then wrote one line down.

Not one person in the building apologised in it.

He stood in the middle of an empty hall on Cobb Street on the eleventh of a month at ten past ten in the morning and listened to a woman of the Field division read aloud, in the voice she read forms in, a sentence she had written in a room on the sixth of last month at a table, and he did not say one word.

Afterwards the three of them stood in the corridor, and the clerk went up with the sheets, and the door was shut.

"She read it at twenty-five seconds a minute," said Runa Sato. "I timed it. That is a third of the speed she reads anything else, and she is not slow, and both of those are on the record now, and I am the only person alive who knows what that was."

"She did it because it is true."

"She did it because it is true and because she is the only clerk in that building who has ever read one of your documents back to the woman it belongs to." She put her hands on her knees. "Do you know what the consequence is."

"You tell me."

"Two sentences were written in this corridor and neither of them was mine." She did not look at him. "One was hers, in a room on the seventh, to a woman with a card, and it was that she thought it decent of me, and I have not spoken to her since and I was right not to. And the other one is the one you are going to have to hold, because it is the only one of the three that belongs to you."

He waited.

"On the twenty-ninth she is going to read those six lines and stop, and the room is going to hear where a seventh name should have been, because a clerk does not get to fill that."

Runa Sato stood up off the bench.

"And not one person in that hall will know why it stopped. Not the hearing officer, because he has never read the Field demand and it is not on the roll. Not the Office, because the Office's version is that the minute is complete and the gap is in a district's copy."

"Not me," he said.

"You are the only one who will," said Runa Sato.

She turned round.

"You will know why it stopped, because you have been in this building four times and you have been in a room four feet by nine with a clerk in it. And on the morning of the twenty-ninth you are going to stand in a hall full of people and know the whole of what was just not said, and you will not be able to say one word about it, because saying it is disclosing a Field demand that is not yours."

He had not thought about that at all, and it took him about four seconds, and all four of them were the same four seconds.

"That is the price of six," he said.

"That is the price of six and it has been hers since the sixth and now it is yours as well," said Runa Sato, "because you are the reason there are six."

Eighteen days. Eighteen less the eleventh, and both in this month, and a hearing on the twelfth day after the one he had not yet been through.

He walked down the back stair of the Exchange building with a sealed notice in his hand that he was going to have to give back to a woman standing in a corridor, and she was not going to open it in front of him.

"You have until the fourteenth to decide what you tell me," she said from the bench. "And I want you to understand that whatever you decide, I am not going to thank you for it, because a thing that is true is not a favour, and I have known that since the sixth of last month when you taught it to me by refusing me in a corridor four feet by nine."

"That is not how it happened."

"No," said Runa Sato. "It is not."

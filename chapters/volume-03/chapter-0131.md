# What The Women Object To

He said it at the table on the fifth, after the shift, with the notebook open and the day's six lines already in it in six hands and the visitors' book shut under Marguerite's hand, and he said it badly and out of order, twice, and then a third time in a different order because the first two had both been wrong.

He had spent four months learning to say nothing in rooms. Saying one true thing turned out to be harder, and it took him three attempts and eleven minutes, and eleven of those minutes were spent on a woman at the end of the table copying it into a pad without being asked.

Twenty-four days. Twenty-four less the fifth, and both in this month, which was the first time since the notice went up that the two numbers had been in the same month and he had to do the subtraction twice before he trusted it.

"There is a hall on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth with a hundred and forty seats in it and a rail and ninety places standing behind the back row," he said, "and there is a machine on a table in that room, and I have worked out what that machine can be made to do."

"You worked that out on the twenty-seventh," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I worked out what it reads. I have been working out what to do about it since the thirtieth, and I have got it, and I do not like it, and I am going to say it in the wrong order and then I am going to say it again."

The landing light was on above them. Sun Meiling was up there in her coat where she had been since the first, and Nadia went up with the news because news went up that staircase in that house even when the woman on the landing would not come down for it, and she came back in three minutes and sat down and said nothing, which for Nadia Ferrous was a considered act.

"There is one thing in that room that moves which does not put anybody's name in that machine's second column," he said. "It is me. And I am not going to do it, and I am not going to find out what it does by trying it on a person, because a thing you find out by trying it on a person is found out on that person."

"You have not said what you are doing in that room," said Beatrix Halm. "You have told us what you are not doing, and one of those is about you."

"Let him finish," said Ines Duarte, from the sill, without opening anything.

"I am finished," he said. "That is the whole of what I worked out. It took a night and a half and it comes out at eleven sentences and it is that I stand at a table for four hours on the twenty-ninth and I do not touch one person in that room."

Silence at the table, and the particular quality of it that meant five women were working rather than four.

"That is not a plan," said Nadia. "That is a decision made about ninety times."

"Yes," he said. "That is what I have got. It is ninety refusals, and the arithmetic is that a man standing at a table in a room full of strangers is the most touchable object in Ostara, and somebody in that crowd has read about me since the third of last month and is going to come up and put a hand on my arm, and I am not going to see it coming."

"You have worked out the shape of the thing and not the shape of the room," said Beatrix Halm.

"The reaching is not a decision. It is a reflex. A person puts a hand on an arm at a table because a man is standing alone at a table, and there is no thinking in it anywhere. I have been on the receiving end of it for four years in a corridor with a mop in my hand. You cannot refuse ninety things you cannot see, and I am not going to practise on anybody to find out whether I can."

"Then say it before," said Nadia Ferrous.

He looked at her.

"Not on the day. Before. On the paper, in the record, in advance, where it is written down that the member intends to stand for four hours and intends to touch no person in it, and put a date on it, so that anybody in that hall who has been thinking about putting a hand on you for a month reads it first and does not do it."

The room was quiet for about four seconds and then Beatrix Halm laughed once, which she did perhaps four times a month.

"Say it in advance," she said, "and what you have done is publish your own hands. There is a notice on a frame in the Reading Square with one numbered paragraph on it and it has been up since the third and it has nothing in it about your hands, and if you put a second sentence on the record then every person in this city who has read the first one knows that the one thing worth knowing about you is a thing you do with your hands."

"It is not that simple."

"It is exactly that simple, and I have been on a signal desk for nine years, and a public sentence does one thing in a city: it moves. It goes from the frame to the queue to the yard gate to four hundred people by the following week, and every one of them is holding a version of you with a hole in it shaped like your hands."

She put her cup down.

"You want a room where not one person reaches for you, and the way you get one is by telling two hundred people in advance that you would rather they did not."

"The alternative is ninety chances."

"And you have come down here and told six women about it, which means you have already worked out that you cannot hold ninety chances on your own." She looked round the table. "I want that said out loud before anybody agrees to anything else."

That was the sentence, and it sat in the middle of the table, and every woman at it left it alone for about six seconds.

"No," said Nadia Ferrous.

"No to what."

"To all of it." She put both hands flat on the wood, which everybody in the room recognised. "You have told us that the only thing that keeps you safe in that room is that six women are not near you. That is the first sentence anybody at this table has heard you say in four months that puts a value on us. So write this down, Ola."

"He has said no about nine hundred things in four months," said Ola Bright, with her pen moving. "This is the first one where somebody could have said yes and would have been right to."

"What I have said no to," Nadia said, "is being the reason. Not the plan. The reason. You cannot hold ninety chances on your own, and you have brought them here. That is not asking us to help you. That is putting us in a room you have not been in and asking us to be absent from it, and to know that we are staying absent, and to be told about it afterwards in a transcript."

"The transcript exists either way," said Ines.

"It exists either way and the difference is who is in it." Nadia did not take her hands off the table.

"For four months every single thing any of us has asked for has been refused. Ines was refused twice. Runa is standing in a yard answering for a line she wrote at somebody else's table. Beatrix gave him the district and got a condition and the condition is dead."

"And I handed a page of forty-one names to a stranger in a lift on purpose, on the tenth of last month," said Nadia, "and said if it is used the first thing that happens is to me and not to you. I have been right about that for a month."

"Mine has not moved in three weeks," said Tessa Okonkwo, and said it to the table and not to him. "I am not going to make an argument out of it at ten o'clock at night. I said what I had to say about it in a shut room and you were in the room."

"I know what you said."

"Then you know I am not the one to be angry with tonight," said Tessa. "I am the one who cannot be paid, and I have made my own arrangement about it, and I am not going to start now. Be angry with the ones who can say something useful."

Beatrix Halm put both hands flat on the table, which she had not done in six weeks.

"My condition died on the twenty-eighth," she said. "It was spend them on people and not on yourself. He spent them on himself in a square in the winter in front of sixty people, and it has been broken since then, and it was never collected, and it is not going to be forgiven, and that is all finished."

"Then what," said Marguerite Vale, from the end, and she had not spoken for eleven minutes, which in that house was a thing she did.

"September," said Beatrix Halm.

The room did not move, and two of the women at it did not look at her, and that was how three of them knew that three of them knew.

"So here is what I do instead of a condition. I come to the rail. On the twenty-ninth. At ten, in a uniform, at the back, where I have been for nine years listening to forty thousand people move."

"No," said Lin Hao.

"You have not heard what it costs, and that is what I would have said about you in April, and I did not say it then, so you have two of these in this house and one of them has used hers up."

She did not raise her voice.

"If I am in that room then everybody in it who has read about me will look at the rail first and find the woman from the Ferrier line who gave the district's cold minutes to a washroom attendant in the spring." She turned her hands over on the wood. "And then they will know that you have somebody, and a man in a room with somebody in the crowd is a man who can be reached, because reaching him is now about reaching her."

"That is the opposite of what you want."

"It is the opposite of what I want for you, and it is what I want, because the whole of what I have been able to give you since April is information about a city, and this is the last piece of it and it costs me the only thing I have." She put her hands flat on the table. "I am not going to ask you. If you send me a message saying do not come, I will come."

"You will not come."

"Then I will stand at the back as a stranger and not move and not look at you, and you will not be able to tell me from any of the other hundred and forty people, and both of those cost me and only one of them costs you."

"You will not come," said Lin Hao, "and I cannot tell you why in a sentence, and the sentence is about the machine and not about the hall, and I would rather you had it out of my mouth than work it out at nine o'clock on the twenty-ninth with ninety people behind you."

"You will not come," said Beatrix Halm, "and that is the price of my condition, and you have known it since the winter and so have I, and we are the only two people at this table who have not said so."

The room broke a little. Somebody put a kettle on. Ola Bright read the last of it back, word for word, the way she read everything, and dated it, and put the pad down flat.

Ines Duarte spoke from the sill for the first time in nine minutes.

"You have described a procedure and not a decision," she said. "Every sentence tonight has been about the twenty-ninth and not one of them has been about the twenty-eighth. You have said what you will do in a room and what you will not do in a room, and you have not said the thing that would let anybody else hold any of it."

"Say the thing yourself, then."

"I am not going to say it for you, because you would be able to say I put it in your mouth."

She put the folder she had not opened down on the sill.

"Here it is. You are proposing to put four hours of yourself into a document in advance so that a stranger in a crowd does not reach for you. That is a procedure. A decision is that you will hand those hours to a person, and that the person will hold them, and that you will not be alone in them."

She looked at the room.

"Two of us at this table would say yes tonight, and one of us is on a landing and will not come down for it."

"You are asking me to say that out loud."

"I am telling you that you will have to," said Ines Duarte. "A thing one man holds is a speech. A thing four people hold is a record, and you know what a record is, because you have spent four months refusing to make one."

He sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of the notebook, which was his own habit and not a decision.

Twenty-four days. The twenty-ninth less the fifth, and the two numbers in the same month, and a hall with ninety places standing behind a row of seats and a man who could not see a single one of them coming.

Then Nadia Ferrous said, "Say it before," again, and he understood that she had not been arguing with him at all. She had been arguing with him about the order of the sentences, and she had been four minutes ahead of him for the whole of the evening.

He went to work at ten past five and cleaned six floors, and on the second floor a clerk he had cleaned past for four years stopped him at the sink with a form about a tap that ran brown and asked him whether the form wanted a date.

"It does not," he said.

"Then what do I put on it."

He stood there with the cloth in his hand and light coming in off the yard, and he heard himself answer a question he had answered four times that night and failed to answer once.

"Twenty-four days off," he said. "Put that."

The clerk wrote it down.

He had a bucket, a cloth, six floors, and a plate figure that had not moved in five months, and the thing that had changed since the thirtieth was that he now had an answer ready for anybody who asked him what he was going to do on the twenty-ninth.

And having an answer ready meant the answer existed somewhere outside him, and he had until the fourteenth to find out who was going to be holding it.

He took the bucket to the third floor.

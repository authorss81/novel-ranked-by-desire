# The Officer Outside The Roll

The answer came back to the certification desk on the seventeenth before nine, and Lin Hao heard about it at half past, standing where he had stood, because the clerk had told him to come and he had come.

He had cleaned four floors before coming and left the last two for the afternoon, and Drant had marked him out in the day book without a word, which was the nearest thing to leave anybody had given him in a month. The morning queue at the plate socket had been twenty deep, and he had mopped round it, and it had gone into the book with the floors.

The clerk had the envelope open on the counter and the sheet out of it, and the runner's tray empty beside it.

"It is confirmed," said the clerk. "She built it. Her hand is on the line at the foot, and her statement of the form is under it, and it matches the slip. Two columns. Subject and counterparty."

He put the sheet on the counter face up. The line at the foot carried a signature in a quick hand, and under it three lines about the form, plain and exact.

"Then the desk has one hand," said Lin Hao.

"The desk has one hand and a pass slip with three extra sets of initials on it." The clerk turned the envelope over. On the back, below the direction, three clerks had initialled receipt in three different hands. "The runner waited while she read it. She read it twice and signed it and then she told the runner what it was."

"What it was."

"The request. The hall. The member." The clerk said it without pleasure. "And when the runner brought it down, she told the plate-office clerk at the foot of the stair. And the plate-office clerk told the girl at the Exchange counter. That is three sets of initials and three mouths, and it is ten o'clock in the morning."

Lin Hao looked at the three initials. They were small and neat and ordinary, the marks of people who handle paper all day.

"She cannot keep it to herself," said the clerk. "Everybody on the second bench knows that. It is why she is an excellent auditor and a poor friend, and it is why she has been seven years outside the standing roll with nobody minding. A woman who repeats everything she reads is a woman nobody has to watch."

He squared the sheet.

"There is a bench order," he said. "Dated yesterday. Effective the nineteenth. She is off instruments from the day after tomorrow and onto paper review, because a builder named in a hearing cannot go on benching the tool she is named on. That is not a punishment. That is the bench protecting itself."

Lin Hao did the arithmetic. Forty-three days to the hall. Fourteen days left in this month after the seventeenth, and twenty-nine in the next.

What mattered was nearer than that. She was on the bench today and tomorrow. On the nineteenth she went onto paper, behind a door, where a member could not call.

He felt it as urgency, sudden and physical, like a hand closing on his wrist. Two days. A woman who had built the thing that would read him, who had already told three people before ten in the morning, sitting up a back stair past the plate office, with a bench order dated yesterday moving her behind a door.

What he wanted was to speak to her before the door shut. Not to ask her to change the confirmation. Not to ask her to keep anything back, because she could not keep anything back and the whole building knew it. To look at the woman who had made the machine, and to have her look at him, before the Office put them both in a hall and told them what they were to each other.

He told the table that evening, standing at the foot of it with his coat still on. A confirmation. Three initials. A bench order dated yesterday, effective the nineteenth. Two days of bench left, today gone, tomorrow to come.

Ines looked up from the sill. "A builder who repeats is a witness nobody has to coach," she said, and went back to saying nothing, and the room took it for what it was, which was not advice and not a warning but a true sentence about paper.

Nadia walked him to the stair foot afterwards. "Tomorrow, then," she said. "The eighteenth. Go up the stair and knock. If she tells the bench by eleven, let her. A secret everybody has is not a secret. It is a fact with a date on it."

Ola dated a page in her pad and wrote one line on it, and read it back. "He goes up the back stair in the morning." She looked at him. "That is the whole of tomorrow in this house, and I want it entered that we knew."

He climbed the stairs above the chop-house and lay awake with the photograph face down on the chair, and the clicking went on in his ears long after the house went quiet, a clicking and a pause and a clicking, like a machine being run by somebody who knew it, and after a while he understood that it was his own heart he was hearing, and he slept, with forty-three days in front of him and one morning in front of that.

"Can a member call on a builder after she answers," he said.

"A member can call on anybody who will see him." The clerk put the sheet back in the tray. "She is on the second bench until the nineteenth. Today and tomorrow. After that she is paper review, and paper review does not see members."

"Will she see me."

The clerk looked at him for the first time that morning with something other than professional patience.

"She has told three people about you before ten o'clock," he said. "She will see you. That is not a prediction. That is what a woman who cannot keep a thing to herself does with the thing."

"What does paper review mean. Behind what door."

"A room on the third floor with no bench in it. She reads runs other people make and signs that she read them. No machine. No handle worn pale. No work." The clerk put the sheet back in the tray. "Seven years on a bench and from the nineteenth she reads paper. That is what a bench order does. It does not punish. It files."

He went down the stair past the plate office, where the queue stood with plates in their hands, and the second door past it was shut, and this time there was sound behind it, a clicking and a pause and a clicking, like a machine being run by somebody who knew it.

A plate-office clerk came up the stair with a stack of sockets' slips and stopped at the foot, hearing the clicking, and smiled at nothing and went on. The runner who had carried the envelope sat on the bottom step with her hands on her knees, waiting for nothing, because runners wait between errands the way plates wait between readings.

He stood in front of the shut door for about a minute with his hand at his side. He did not knock. A member did not call on a builder without an hour, and he had no hour, and the bench order said the nineteenth, and today was the seventeenth.

He went back to the branch and cleaned the last two floors with the clicking in his ears.

At noon Nadia came down to the branch with a message from Bellhouse, which was that the visitors' book had taken six more entries and none of them had a name in the business column, and that Sun was still on the landing, and that Marguerite had read the week's names and found nothing with legs.

At four the classification paper went up three floors, unsigned, and came back down, and he watched it come back the way he had watched it come back for three weeks, and thought about a shut door with clicking behind it, and a signature in a quick hand, and three sets of initials on the back of an envelope.

He understood that the unsigned paper was no longer the paper that mattered, and that it had stopped mattering without anybody deciding it.

"Tell Marguerite the book holds," he said, and Nadia looked at him as though he had said something in another language, and then nodded once, slowly, because a message was a message even when it sounded like paper talking.

He wrote the note at the register counter on a slip without an envelope, because a note with no envelope is a thing anybody can read and he wanted it read. Anneke Roos watched him write the one line and did not ask what it was, and Drant entered nothing, because a note was not a document and the register did not take notes.

A runner took it up the back stair of the Exchange building with the afternoon post. One line. An hour in the morning, at the second bench, if she would see him.

Tomorrow was the eighteenth. The bench was hers until the nineteenth. The Board would repaint in the morning, and the square would fill, and the ladders would go up whether or not there was anything new to say.

He signed out at the gate and walked home up the long road with forty-three days in front of him and one morning in front of that, and the urgency stayed with him all the way, like a hand on his wrist, pulling him toward a shut door with a machine behind it and a woman who had told three people about him before breakfast.

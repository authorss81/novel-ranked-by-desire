# The Form Requires A Reason

The form came at four in the afternoon on the third, by hand, up the Vestry arcade and into the corridor with the four quiet doors along it, and the woman who brought it was not a clerk from the Office and had no card and gave her name as though it were a thing she had checked in a mirror that morning, which she had.

She put it on the bench outside the ward office and stood next to it while he read the top of it, which took about four seconds, because the top of it was the Office's mark and a line that said *member's form* and a line for a name and a line for a date and then a heading with eleven words under it.

Then she went away, down the corridor, past four shut doors, and did not look back at it, and he understood from that that she had been told not to look back at it and had decided to obey exactly as far as the corridor.

The heading was *reason for remaining undescribed*.

Underneath the heading there was one box and the box was about four inches deep and it wanted one paragraph, in his own hand, and there was a printed line under the box that said *the member may present this in person or return it by post, and a member who does not return it will be heard without it*.

He read that sentence nine times and then went and sat on the stone bench outside the Vestry with his sleeve pushed up because the bench had a brass plate on the end of it and he had still never once read the plate.

He had been in the arcade for about ten minutes when Tessa Okonkwo came out of the ward office with the day book under her arm and stood over him without sitting down, which he had learned in June was the whole of what a thing means before it is said.

"Read the heading to me," she said.

"Reason for remaining undescribed."

"That is it, then." She did not ask to see the form and he did not offer it, and she understood the offer not being made, and he understood her understanding it, and neither of them said anything about it, which was how things worked in this corridor. "Say the rest."

"There is a box. One paragraph, in my own hand. It says I may present it in person or return it by post, and that a member who does not return it will be heard without it."

Tessa Okonkwo took the book against her chest and looked down the arcade at the traffic going past in the cold, and he watched her arrive at the thing he had arrived at, because he had been in this arcade for ten minutes and she had been in this building for eleven years.

"You understand that there is no version of that box that is not an answer," she said. "That is what I want to hear you say, because I have had it on my mind since about two o'clock and I would like to know whether it is still only in my head."

"Yes."

"Say why, in the street, as though to a person."

"A reason for leaving an account alone is a description of it." He said it to the arcade and not to her. "If I write *it is mine*, that is ownership. If I write *it does not come from anybody I can name*, that is a source. If I write *I do not know what it does*, that is a mechanism."

"That last one is the truest thing anybody could put on that paper and it is the one sentence in the world that describes it exactly, and it is the sentence that finishes it."

Tessa sat down on the bench at the other end of it, which she had never once done, and put the book on her knees.

"Then you are not going to write in it."

"I am going to send it back."

"No," said Tessa Okonkwo. "You are not going to send it back, and I have thought about this for two hours and I am telling you now so that you do not spend tonight on it.

"If you send it back empty they will hold it and they will read the emptiness, because an empty box on a member's form is a thing four offices have to look at. Every one of them will find the same thing in it, which is that a member had a paragraph and declined to use it, and they will put that sentence on the roll, and it will be the only sentence anybody has about you until somebody finds a better one."

"So I do not send it back empty."

"No." She turned the book over on her knees. "You go back in with the form and you leave the box empty and you write one line on the outside of it, on the blank strip under the printed line, and the line says that a reason will be given in the hall, in person, on the day, and not on paper."

"That line is also a description."

"That line is the only sentence on that piece of paper that is not a description, and I know that, and I have spent two hours working out why, and here it is." She looked at him properly.

"A description of an account is a claim about what the account does. A statement of when and where a member intends to say a thing is not a claim about the account at all. It is a claim about the member's mouth.

"Every office in this city already knows the shape of your mouth, Lin Hao, because you have spent four months refusing to use it. The one thing they have never had from you is a date on it."

She got up off the bench.

"Write it in the hall. In person. On the day, and not before, because if you write it before, somebody sends it to a desk and a desk puts it in a tray and the tray is the thing this whole business is about."

"That puts the sentence in a clerk's hand."

"That puts the sentence in a clerk's hand and there are going to be two of them and one of them is a woman I have never been able to describe to you, and I am not going to describe her, and I would have told you if I could." She stopped with the book under her arm. "I am going back inside. There is a thing on this floor that has not been answered and I have to go and not answer it."

She went inside and he sat on the bench with the form on his knee and eleven words under a heading, and after a while a ward runner came past with a chart and did not stop.

"He understood that the form had been visible on that bench for about twenty minutes, and had been seen by everybody who came past it and by nobody who was going to say anything about it. That was the arrangement he had spent his whole life inside and had never once thought of as an arrangement."

Then he picked it up and went in and put it on the desk in the ward office and asked for a pen, and Fenn gave him one and looked at the box and then at him and did not say anything, which was a considerable thing from a woman who had told a stranger about a lift log in a corridor.

He wrote nothing in the box.

On the blank strip under the printed line he wrote one line and put the date on it and did not sign it, because he had learned last autumn that a signature on a piece of paper in this building turns the paper into a document, and a document can be produced a year later by somebody who is not him.

*Reason to be given in the hall, in person, on the day, and not on paper.*

He read it back and it was what he meant and it described nothing on earth, and it took him eleven seconds to write, and he thought about the eleven seconds for a while after he had done it.

Then he went out through the arcade with the form flat on the counter in front of him and stood there until a runner came past, and gave it to her, and watched her take it back up the arcade without looking at it, which was the correct way and which she did without being told, and he understood that the Office had trained her for exactly this in exactly the same building where he had learned to keep his hands still.

He was two steps into the street when somebody said his name behind him.

It was Fenn, at the door, with her hand on the frame and a runner's lanyard round her wrist.

"I am not supposed to tell you that and I am going to tell you anyway, and I would like it noted that I said that." She kept her voice down and she kept the door open, which is the opposite of what anybody does. "The Exchange came for the summary line a second time. Not the instrument form. The summary line, the one about the service's holdings, the one she has been putting *not produced* on since the third of last month."

"And?"

"It went back this morning." Fenn said it as though she were handing him a chart. "Nothing on it. Not *not produced*. Nothing. Two lines in the column now and one of them is empty and she did not put her name against the empty one, and I asked her about that at lunch and she said it was not hers to sign."

Lin Hao stood in the cold outside a clinic on the Vestry and worked out what had happened in a room he had not been in, on the morning he had been on a bench outside the Reading Square eating his lunch.

Tessa Okonkwo had been offered the same question three times and had answered it three times with a sentence that was true and complete and cost nothing. On the third she had been asked it a fourth time and had run out of sentences that were both true and complete.

"The sentence available to her now was a sentence about a floor she had kept looking like a floor for eleven years. And a floor that has looked like a floor for eleven years when nothing has arrived in it for six weeks is evidence, and she would have to say the word evidence out loud in a hand that an office keeps."

So she had put nothing on the form.

And a service that has failed to reply is not the same animal as a service that has replied with a refusal, and in about six weeks an officer would come up that arcade with a card and a man to work a machine, and he would not send the girl with the wrist, and he would work backwards from an empty rack to a man who has spent nothing since the middle of last month in about four minutes.

She had chosen the lapse. She had chosen it deliberately, on the third, and she had paid for it, and he was standing in the street finding out from a clerk's mouth and had not been in the room and was not going to be told the reason by anybody who was in it.

He understood that she had done it to keep eleven charts of people who would go in with her out of a file, and that it had cost her the one thing she had been buying with it, which was that she was not a clinician with a mass.

He went along the arcade with his hands in his pockets and stopped at the end of it, and did not go back in, and understood that there was now one thing in this city he could not pay for anybody and had not been able to pay for anybody since the middle of last month in a corridor with a woman with a card.

There was a form on a counter inside that building with a heading on it, and a one-paragraph box on it, and a woman in it who had decided this morning that the cheapest available sentence was no sentence at all.

The cheapest sentence had cost her the floor.

He got to the end of the arcade and turned round and looked back at the door, and the door was shut, and behind it a corridor had four closed doors along it and a bench outside it with a brass plate on the end, and on the counter a sheet of paper with eleven words under a heading that somebody three floors over a building on Cobb Street had written in about four seconds and would have written differently if there had been an alternative.

He went home up the long road with the knowledge in his coat, and he did not tell anybody, and it was the first of a great many things he was going to tell a great many people in a room and not in a corridor.

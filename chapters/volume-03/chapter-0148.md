# A Description In His Own Hand

The clerk came back up the outside stair of the Reading at about a quarter to nine in the evening with a sheet and a pen and a chair, and she did not sit down.

"You gave a statement at four minutes to eleven this morning and it is on the transcript and the finding has cited it and the finding is entered at your branch." She put the sheet on the table. "The hearing officer would like a description."

"From whom."

"From you. In your hand. On that."

"It is nine at night."

"It is twenty-five to nine and I have been in this building since half past seven and the officer is not going to sit until this is done and neither am I." She squared the sheet. "A statement of what a member did at a microphone is not a description. It is four sentences about a morning and it was made into the air. The record needs one thing in it in the member's own hand, and it is a description of what the account is.

"It goes in the transcript, and on the first sheet of the exhibit roll, and into the certification desk's file. The fourth place is a book three floors above a counter you clean.""

He looked at the sheet.

"Four places."

"Four places and I have to copy it four times, so write it as if it were going to be read by a stranger in eleven years, because it will be."

She went and stood at the door with her arms folded, which is what Tove Marlin did, and which is what every clerk in this building did who wanted a room to be empty enough to write in.

He sat down.

He had written four sentences on a sheet on the fifteenth of this month at a table on Lantern Row for a woman who said she would not file anything. He had written four true acts on a sheet at half past four in the morning on the seventh, and four lines a day, and a hundred and fourteen lines in a drawer, and one sentence into a microphone in front of two hundred and eleven people this morning at four minutes to eleven.

He wrote for about twenty minutes, which was a long time for a man who can do a morning in nine seconds.

He wrote it in the first person, because the Office's four paragraphs were in the third and a description in the third person is a description of somebody.

He wrote that on the morning of the twenty-ninth of this month, from ten until two, in a public hall, he was submitted to an instrument of the Certification desk and did not touch a person in that hall. He wrote that on three occasions during that sitting he put his own hand on his own body and that something moved in him.

He wrote that nothing left him on any of the three occasions, and that nothing entered the counterparty column of a certified output from the moment the instrument was switched on until the sitting was adjourned.

He wrote that a member's account is a mass he cannot measure and cannot produce and has never been able to produce on his plate.

And then he wrote the last line, and he wrote it the way a man writes something he has thought about for four months and is going to be held to for the rest of his life.

He wrote: I am unable to say what moved. I have been unable to say it since the spring. I am not going to attempt to say it again, and I have told the hearing officer in advance that I will not, and I would like it recorded that I said so before the sitting and not after it.

He put the pen down and read it back once, which is not a habit he has, and pushed the sheet across the table.

The clerk picked it up and read it and stopped about a third of the way down.

"Say the second one again."

"Say it again, please."

"You do not have to."

"I have to," said the hearing officer's clerk. "I am not sure I have it right. You said that on three occasions during the sitting he put his own hand on his own body and that something moved in him. I have written *the member moved on three occasions at the table*. Is that what you said."

"No."

"Then tell me what to write and I will write it exactly."

He told her, and she wrote it exactly, and it took about four minutes to fix one sentence in about ninety words, and that is what four places costs.

She took the sheet away at nine minutes to ten.

Ines Duarte was in the corridor with the stair rail under her hand when he came out.

She had not gone home and she had not come up and she had not been in the room.

She had her coat on.

"You have written it."

"I have written it and a clerk is copying it into four places and I do not get to see any of the other three."

"Then it is done and it cannot be unsaid, and I want to say the other half of that to you in a stairwell, because I am the only person in this building who will say it to you and I am not going to say it tomorrow in a room with a bar on it."

"Go on."

"It is the only true document about you in the city." She said it flatly, the way she said figures. "Not the certification, which is true about the instrument. Not the finding, which is true about the roll. Yours. Four sentences written by a man at a table at a quarter to nine at night because a clerk asked him to, and every word of it can be checked by a person who was in the room, and every word of it is true, and it is in your hand."

"Which means it is the only one they cannot get out of me by saying I have been misread."

"Yes," said Ines Duarte. "And it is also the only one that cannot get out of the hands of a clerk who is angry with you in eleven years, or a certification desk that has to certify it, or a hearing officer who wants it to say something else."

They stood in the corridor for about a minute.

"I am not going to file any of it," she said. "I have not filed anything since March and I am not going to start on a step. What I will do is this.

"On the morning of the twenty-second of next month I am going to stand in a room in front of four people and be asked what I did and did not do between March and now. I am going to answer it. The answer is that I read a list of names aloud in a hall and I did not put one of them in a file.

"And I am going to be asked why I refused the two productions that came before that, and I am going to say the same thing I said in May, which is that being true is not the same as being owed to anybody."

"That does not resolve it."

"That is not what it is for," said Ines Duarte. "It is for making sure that when they ask me in December I have been the same woman all the way back and not a woman who changed her mind in October when it became expensive. That is what a review is. It is not a verdict, it is an audit of a person, and the only thing that defends you against an audit is being the same person in front of it."

She looked at him.

"You are going to say to me that I did this for you and I am going to tell you once, now, that it is not for you, and then neither of us is going to mention it again."

"I was going to say that."

"You were going to say it and I have taken it off you." She put her hand flat on the rail. "And here is the other half and I have been sitting on a stair with it since four o'clock.

"There is a thing in your sentence. You have written in a description, in your own hand, in four places in this city, that something in you moves and that you are not going to try again."

"Yes."

"Then this city has a turned line in it with an address on it," said Ines Duarte, "and the address is you, and it is not a document anybody has to interpret because you wrote the words. A doctor can be told what to watch for. A tram operator can. An accountant can. A relative can."

She let go of the rail.

"And you said in a hall at four minutes to eleven this morning, before you did it, that you would not do it again. You said it to a clerk. It is in the transcript. A promise made before the act is worth four times what it is worth after."

"You have been a long time on that stair."

"I have been on that stair since four o'clock and I have been doing arithmetic about you since ten minutes to three this morning, and I have got one number and it is not comfortable." She held up a hand.

"You are not being hunted. There is nothing in that sentence anybody can act on. But you are findable, and you are findable by anybody at all who is ever asked to look.

"There is exactly one thing standing between that and a file with your name on it, and the thing is that you have said out loud there will never be a second sample."

"And the rest of the book?"

"And the rest of the book is you keeping it," said Ines Duarte, "on your own, in a city with two million people in it, for the rest of your life, and not one person is going to check up on you to see whether you are doing it, and that is the cheapest-looking promise anybody has ever made in the history of this office."

She went down the stair.

He stood at the top of it in the dark for about a minute and understood that he had just been given the whole of his life by an auditor who had nothing to gain and a pending review and had been refused twice in a corridor in March and May.

The statement of a member, in the member's own hand, on a sheet of paper, in four places in the city, describing an account he cannot measure and cannot produce, and unable to say what is in it, and telling the record in advance that he will not try again.

It could not be crossed out. It could not be improved by a clerk, and it could not be improved by a certification desk, and it could not be replaced by four paragraphs written by four offices that had been wrong in exactly the direction that made him a man who manufactures his own.

And it was the truest sentence in Ostara about Lin Hao, and it was true because he had written it, and the only thing about it that was any use to him was that it did not say a single word about what he was.

# The Notice On The Board

The first of the month, and Lin Hao got through six floors of washrooms in that building without being asked a single question by anybody, which took him until about eleven to notice as a change in his working life rather than a relief.

He had been four years on that job. In four years he had been asked things. Not many, and never about himself — a porter would say *which stair*, a clerk would say *are you the one for the second*, a woman from the plate office would hold out a hand for a plate and look at the face on it while she did it.

On the first of the month there was none of that.

The porter on the second held the fire door for him. A man with a reading card going to the third stepped out of his way and said nothing, not out of courtesy, out of the way a man steps out of a thing he has already decided is furniture.

Two women from the counter office came down at half past nine talking about a girl at the Exchange and went past him and did not lower their voices, which they do not do for a colleague and do not do for anybody they have been told about. He heard the whole of it.

He was on the fifth with a bucket when he understood what the arrangement actually was.

It was not that they had stopped noticing him. It was that they had finished. For four months a figure had come off a machine on the fourth floor and gone up the long road in a forwarding envelope and been painted on a wall where a hundred and forty thousand people walked past every week, and it had been costly, and he had paid for it in five conditions and a floor.

What had come back was a man who cleaned washrooms.

Eleven o'clock, and he went down to the register desk, because that is where the day starts and ends whatever anybody has decided about him, and Drant was there with the tray out and two things on it.

"Two," said Drant. "One is mine and I am going to be wrong about it in front of you."

The first was a return: a monthly return of holdings by weight for a service, on the branch's own printed form, the month-end date in the box at the top and the receiving house printed at the foot.

The date in the box was the thirty-first.

"That is yesterday month," said Drant. "It is a day late and it is not my fault and I am going to say that once and then never again. It was due on the thirty-first, and the desk that sends it on the thirty-first has been discharged since the eighteenth, and a return with nowhere to go on the day it falls due goes the next working morning or it does not go at all."

He put his finger on the box. "It went this morning. It is dated the thirty-first, and everything on it is counted to the thirty-first, and if I dated it the first then somebody upstairs at the end of next month would ask why a month of a service weighs more than the month before it."

Lin Hao read the top of it and did not read the rest.

He had read Drant's returns before, on the counter, out of the ordinary politeness of a man who stands at one. They were about the branch: a floor's worth of stock, a weight, a number of services held. Nothing on them had ever been about him.

He looked at it now and found that he did not particularly want to know, which was new, because a month ago he would have read all of it and worked out what it meant for the floor he cleaned.

"The thirty-first is a real day," he said, for no reason he could afterwards account for.

"It is the last day of the month that is gone," said Drant, "which is a sentence people find difficult and it took me nineteen years." He squared the tray. "That one is done and it has gone up, and nobody upstairs is waiting for it, because a return that nobody reads is still a return."

He put his hand flat on the second paper and left it there, and Lin Hao read the first paper without interest and read the second one four times.

It was one sheet. It had the Office's mark at the top, which was a building and not a wing, and under the mark a line that said a hearing would sit, and the date, and the room, and then four paragraphs.

The first paragraph named the office and the proceeding and said that the member was served. The second gave the date and the hour and the place. The third said what the hearing was about and it was one sentence, and Lin Hao read that sentence and then read it again with his mouth shut.

The fourth was the part that made the sheet worth four readings.

He stood at the counter in a building where nobody had asked him anything all morning and understood that the relief he had been living on had a bill attached to it, and that the bill had been issued that morning by somebody who knew exactly where to find him.

Which is to say at a register desk in a building he has worked in for four years, on the first morning of a month, with a return that everybody in the city expects on the first, so that his hand would be out and his guard would be down and he would have nothing between him and the paper.

It was not a summons. He had been warned about that by a woman in a corridor at the Vestry and it was true, and he turned the sheet over and there was nothing on the back.

It did not accuse him of anything. It did not say he had done a thing. There was no date on it in the past tense anywhere on the page, and he checked for that too, because a notice that alleged something would have been a different paper entirely and one he had a way with.

The question was in a single numbered paragraph, and it was paragraph three.

"Whether an account may remain in the possession of a member in the condition described, undescribed."

Sixteen words, and not one of them his name.

He put the sheet down on the counter with the printed side up, which he had never done with a piece of paper in that building.

"What is an undescribed condition," he said.

"A condition where nobody in the city is able to say what it does," said Drant, "in a document anybody can produce, which is not the same as saying nobody knows."

"That is a second question."

"That is the first one read from the other end. I have been a clerk here nineteen years and I will tell you what I know and then stop." Drant did not take the paper off the counter. "The Office does not care what it is. I want that said first, because a man who has spent four months assuming it does care has built a thing on that, and the thing is not there. They want a description."

"The question on that paper is not a trap in the way you have been taught to think of traps, where the answer is the wrong one. The answer is no. It has been no since the Office existed, and it is no because an account nobody can describe cannot be administered by anybody, and an account that cannot be administered is not an account. That is not wicked. That is what the thing is."

"So the answer is no before the hearing."

"The answer is no before the hearing and the answer is no after it, and the hearing is not there to find out." He turned the sheet round so the date faced up without touching the writing. "It is there to make you say it out loud, in a room, where it goes on a transcript, in front of strangers, and then to have an office write it down in its own words and make that the only description of you that has ever existed."

"You may refuse to be described. They cannot make you. They do not need to. You have just got an appointment."

Lin Hao looked at the date.

He did the arithmetic on the counter's edge with his thumbnail, the way he did everything, and it came out at fifty-nine days. Thirty days to the last day of the month he was standing in, and then twenty-nine more into the month after that.

Fifty-nine days.

On the twenty-ninth of last month a woman with two hands and an instrument had said *about nine weeks* in a clinic corridor, and had been right, and now it was three days less than that because three days had gone by, and he had spent the three days doing nothing at all in a building where nobody was going to ask him anything.

"That is a Thursday," he said.

Drant did not look at it. "It is a day. The Office sits when it sits."

"I know it is a day. I am saying it is a Thursday."

"It will be a Thursday if it falls on a Thursday and it will be some other day if it does not, and I have not got the Office's calendar and neither have you." Drant capped his pen. "And I am going to say the thing that is actually useful and you are not going to like it, because you have been waiting all morning for an office to be unfair to you and today it is going to be correct at you instead.

"That notice does not need you. It does not need your agreement, and it does not need your presence to exist, and there is nothing in that paragraph you can refuse, because a refusal is an answer and an answer is a fact about a member and the Office has your fact. It goes on a board tomorrow, the district board, in the ordinary way, with the other notices of the week and the hours of the Exchange and a lost parcel.

"Once it is on a board in public then a person who is not you can stand in front of it and describe you to somebody else, accurately, using their own words, and be right, because it is published and a published thing can be repeated by anybody without asking.

"You cannot take it off. Not by refusing, not by answering, not by writing to them, not by standing in a corridor. If you could take it off, they would not have printed it."

"There is one line about withdrawal," Lin Hao said.

"There is one line about withdrawal and it says the member may apply for withdrawal before publication." Drant put the tray square. "It is a courtesy, and it has been in that form since before I came here, and it is in it because a member who has changed his mind in the three days between service and publication ought to be able to say so."

"There is no version of it that runs the other way. You cannot withdraw a thing that names you. You can only ask somebody not to say it."

The corridor went on with a queue at the plate office and a woman telling a man his band had not gone up. Lin Hao stood at the register desk and worked out that four months of refusing had bought him exactly this: a question, on a date, that could not be taken off a board because he had made it public by making it legal.

"Can a member bring a witness to that," he said.

"A member may bring one witness." Drant said it the way he said the other things, without weight, and then he did put weight on one of them. "I have read that form since it was printed and I have never once seen anybody use it, and I am telling you the number because no other office in this city will. One. Not nine. One, and the Office brings whatever it brings."

The washrooms on the sixth floor were not done and he had been up on the fifth with a bucket for forty minutes because of a form.

He did them. He was better at it than any man on that staff, and he had been for four years, for the reason that nobody watched him do it.

He came back down past the third floor at twenty past two, where the machine was, and stopped, because it was the fourth floor's machine and the fourth floor was discharged. A discharged floor still gets its machine run, because Drant runs it on his own time now and does not say so to anybody.

It had printed at ten that morning and rung at half past, and the strip was on the spike in an empty office with Drant's own entry beside it, received, and it had gone nowhere at four, because a strip with no standing under it has no road.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Three grains of margin. Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two short of a band he could not carry and had never once been able to carry.

The six still sat where it had been seated in the winter, on the Review instrument and not on his plate, suspended pending a description. That was not the same as lost, and it was a worse shape than lost.

He stood on that landing for about a minute and a half.

Then he went and finished the sixth floor and put his bucket away and signed out at the gate at twenty to four, because the gate at the Fourth Branch had four names on the sheet and his had been one of them since the day he was hired.

Nobody stopped him at the gate either, and the man who read the sheet looked at the name and at the floor and made a mark and did not ask a man on a first floor with a bucket where he was going.

Outside, the light on the square was the flat white light of a day with nothing in it. The Board at the head of the Reading was four hundred yards off and repainted on Thursday mornings whatever anybody had to say, and it would repaint again tomorrow whether or not there was anything new, and he had no Thursdays in this city any more.

He stood on the step for a while with the notice in his coat and thought about the plate against his ribs and the three grains of margin above it, and about how it had taken a desk clerk nineteen years to put the price of the thing in plain words and four months to arrive at the paper.

He folded the sheet once and put it in the inside pocket, against the plate, which was where it would stay until somebody asked him for it.

# The Witness Problem

The eighth was a Thursday, and the Board repainted at nine, and Lin Hao did not go to the square, because the corridor outside the plate office at the Exchange had told him something on Wednesday evening that he needed a whole day to sit with.

Wren Kallo was on paper review behind a door two floors up from the certification desk and she had been since the nineteenth of last month, and the door was four feet from the second bench, and the machine was still on the bench because instruments do not get benched back automatically when a builder is moved.

He went up the back stair at half past nine with his coat brushed, the way he had gone up on the eighteenth, and knocked on the second door past the plate office and a woman's voice told him to come in and not to wipe his feet.

The workroom was narrow and bright and had a cot under the window and a bench along one wall with nothing on it except a screwdriver and a plate that had been squared against the far end. There was a stack of paper on the second desk that had not been there before.

"Six days," said Wren Kallo. "They have given me paper review and there is a shelf of it and a deadline on it, and I have got through about a third, and I want you to know that I am going to finish it early and that I am not doing it for them."

She said it with her arms folded and no softness in it, and then she unfolded them, which was the whole of her answer about it.

"You have got something," she said.

"I have got something to say to you and it is a stupid thing to ask a person to sit through."

"Sit down. Not on the cot."

He sat on the stool with the wobble, because she had not told him not to, and understood about a second later that she had meant it for him and he was going to find out when she turned round.

"You said in this room on the eighteenth that you would rather answer for the machine as a party than discover what it does as a stranger."

"I did."

"And on the twenty-seventh you put the desk's certified run of four years back on your own file, against yourself, with six names on it, including a woman called Marta Vey who lives four streets from your own house."

She put both hands on the bench.

"I have been thinking about that since the twenty-seventh. On the twenty-ninth that run is going to be read out in a hall, and the Office is going to stand me up there as the builder and ask me to speak to it."

"Yes."

"And I built the tool. I did not make that run. The desk made it, on a member who is not you, four years ago, and I was not on the desk that day, I was on the bench, and the name line on that sheet has been burnished over by somebody good at it."

She sat down on the bench edge.

"So when the Office asks me whether that output is what my machine produces, I have got three answers. Two of them are true and both of them are fatal."

"Go on."

"The first answer is that the output is what the tool produces. That is true and it is worthless, because the Office will put the six names against the specification and ask why six and not more, and I cannot answer that, because the answer is in clause nine and clause nine is in your file and not in mine."

He had not said a word.

"The second answer is that I cannot speak to a run I did not make. That is also true and it is worse, because the hearing officer has a rule about instruments and it goes, in my words, if a person cannot say what an instrument does then the instrument does not go on the table. And I will not be able to say it, because I do not know what happened on the ninth of a month four years ago in a room I was not in."

"And the third."

"The third is that you named me a party on the eighteenth and a party gets to know what the party is going to do." She looked at him. "That is the actual problem and I have had it since Tuesday and I have not slept properly, and I am going to say it to you now because I would rather say it than think about it for another four days."

"Say it."

"You are going to stand at a table for four hours and touch no person in it, and you have decided to say so in advance in the record so that not one person at the rail reaches for you." She said it in one sentence, flatly, with no softening, because that is what she did.

"I am going to be standing up in that hall being asked whether a machine reads a backlog, and I am going to have about nine seconds to decide whether I know what my own tool does." She put her hands on the bench edge. "If I have been told in advance what you are going to do, then I know, and I know it for nine seconds, and I am a builder of seven years standing up in a municipal room swearing to a machine."

"Yes."

"And if I have not been told, then the Office asks me cold, and I answer cold, and if I answer well you have a machine and a party who knows it, and if I answer badly Adeyemi rules it out in front of two hundred people and he has told you himself he would not feel a thing about it."

"That is the whole of it."

"That is the whole of it and you have known it since Tuesday and you came up here at half past nine on a Thursday with your coat brushed." She got up off the bench. "Say it out loud, then, and do it properly, because you are going to have to say it out loud in a room in front of a clerk as well, and I am not going to be a person you said it to in a workroom and forgot."

He said it.

"On the twenty-ninth I will be in that room from ten until two and I will touch no person in it, and I intend to move nothing on anybody in it, and I am telling you now so that you are not asked cold."

The room was very quiet. Outside the window the back of the next building did what it does.

"Right," said Wren Kallo. "That is the correct answer and it is the worst possible thing for me and I knew it would be before you came up the stair."

She stood with her hands flat on the bench edge for about four seconds.

"You understand that I cannot keep this," she said. "You have known that since the eighteenth and you have decided about it, and I have decided about it, and neither of us has said it in words, so I am going to say it in words now, because we are past the part where I do not say things."

"Say it."

"I told four people on the morning of the eighteenth before breakfast, and the bench had it by noon, and my sister had it by evening. It is loose in this building now in a way that cannot be gathered in, and by tomorrow afternoon it will be on a bench paper that Cressida has, because everything I am told ends up on a bench paper in this room inside a day."

She took the pad off the corner.

"So you have not given me a secret. You have given me a document with a date on it, and I am going to write it down and date it and sign it." She looked at him. "A thing that travels by mouth in a building like this is a rumour, and a rumour about a member on the twenty-ninth is a rumour that costs somebody their post."

"That is not what I asked you for."

"I know exactly what you asked me for and I am not doing it the way you meant it." She wrote.

"You wanted me to hold it. I am going to write it down instead, and sign it, and put it on the bench where it goes through Cressida into the file."

She did not look up.

"The difference between holding a thing and dating a thing is the difference between a woman in a workroom and a document in a proceeding. You get the second one whether you want it or not, and you cannot get the first one out of me in any case, so you may as well have the better of the two."

She read it back to him word for word, in her own hand, dated the eighth, and signed it with her name and then read the signature out loud as though checking it belonged to her.

Then she read the whole thing back a second time, because she could not help herself, and put it down.

"It is in the file," she said. "You cannot un-tell me and you cannot un-file it, and I want you to understand what you have done in the last four minutes, because you did it on purpose and I have watched you do it before."

"You do not know that."

"I have been doing this for seven years and you have done it exactly twice, and both times you looked like a man putting a plate down in a queue." She put her hand flat on the paper.

"You have given the room to the Office before the day. In three weeks a hearing officer reads that you intend to touch no person in the room, and then a machine is put on a table in front of two hundred people who have been told in advance what the member intends to do with his hands."

"Every instrument in that hall is run by a person," she said, "and there is not one instrument in this city that can be worked by a person who has been told in advance what it is looking for."

He had not thought that far, and it took him about two seconds and it was not a small two seconds.

"That is not a reason to keep it off the file."

"No," said Wren Kallo. "It is a reason to know what it costs, and you already knew the cost, because you knew the cost at half past six on the Friday and you have been carrying it around a building for three days and you came up here with your coat brushed to put it in front of me."

Twenty-one days. Twenty-one less the eighth, and both in this month, and the Board repainting in a square four hundred yards away for a man who was not in it.

Cressida knocked at about eleven with a requisition and Wren told her, with him still in the room, that the member had stated his intention for the twenty-ninth in her own hand and that it was going into the bench file on her signature.

"Then the bench has it," said Cressida, and wrote it on the requisition, and looked at him for about a second longer than the work required, and went down the corridor.

At twenty past twelve Wren Kallo came back into the room with a slip of the desk's paper in her hand, which is not allowed, and put it on the bench.

"The clerk at the certification desk has written a note into the file," she said. "Four lines. It says that the member has stated in advance an intention to touch no person in the room, that the member was told the instrument is to be run on him, and that a member who has stated his intention has had the opportunity to prepare the room and to prepare the persons in it."

"Who told the clerk."

"The clerk at the certification desk. He read it on the bench paper about an hour ago, because everything ends up on the bench paper, and he wrote four lines and took them to the officer's clerk, and a clerk's clerk put them in the file with a number."

She squared it.

"It is a lie. You have not prepared the room and you have not prepared the persons in it, and you could not have, because there is nothing to prepare. And it is in the file with a number on it, and it cannot be withdrawn by the person it names, and it is the only document in this proceeding about a thing that did not happen."

"Then somebody says so."

"Says so to who," said Wren Kallo. "You are the only person in that hall who can say so, and if you stand up on the twenty-ninth and say it, then the first thing the room has heard from you is a complaint about a clerk's note."

She put the pen down.

"There is a hearing officer on a bench who will let you say it, and he will let you say it at length, and while you are saying it the machine is sitting on a table with a cloth on it."

He sat on the stool with the wobble for about a minute.

"Write it down," he said. "Not on bench paper. Not in the file."

"That is not what I am for."

"Write it down anyway and give it to me and I will use it or I will not use it, and that is mine to decide and not yours."

She wrote it down, and dated it, and did not sign it, and put it in his hand, and he put it inside his coat against the notice on the district frame that had been folded once since the first and against the slip from the eighteenth with a builder's handwriting on it.

Two slips in a coat. One of them was a woman who could not keep a secret and had signed it instead. One of them was a woman who had refused to sign it and had done that on purpose and had told him why.

He went down the back stair past the plate office, where the queue was standing with plates in their hands, and out into Cobb Street with twenty-one days and a bench file in the building behind him and a document in his coat that had been written by a woman who had decided, for reasons of her own and not for his, that he could be trusted with a piece of paper and not with anything else.

And the thing that had changed, which was not the file and was not the note and was not the four lines, was that he had said it out loud in a room to a second person, and the second person had put it in a file, and there was no version of the twenty-ninth after this in which the room found out at ten o'clock that the member intended to stand still.

He had wanted to be in control of one thing for four hours.

He had handed the control to a bench in an Exchange building and it was signed, and it could not be taken back, and every person in that hall who wanted to reach him would now have had three weeks to read about it.

# The Debt She Cannot Hand Back

She sent a runner to the Fourth Branch at half past six in the morning to tell him where to come, which was the first time in four months that anybody at the Vestry had sent anybody to a washroom attendant, and the runner said the whole of it in nine words at a gate and did not wait for an answer.

He cleaned five floors and left the fourth, which was discharged and which he had started doing on the twenty-first for reasons he had not written down anywhere.

In the arcade the stone bench with the brass plate on the end of it was wet, and the woman whose name was on the plate had carried trays up two flights for nineteen years, and he sat on it for a minute and a half because the bench was there and he was passing, and then he went in.

Tessa Okonkwo had the day book open on the table with a chair pulled out from it, and there was no second chair, and he understood when he came through the door that she had known he was going to try to leave.

"Sit down," she said. "I have the twenty-ninth and I have the first, and the first is in three days, and I have been in this room since seven working out how to say a thing I have never once had to say in eleven years."

He sat in the chair, because she had not left it out for any reason except that one.

She turned the day book round and squared it against the edge of the table. His name on one line. A date. No number.

"You come on the first of every month and I write the date down and you sit in that chair while I do it for about four minutes, and we do not talk about it again until the next one." She put one finger on the line. "That was June. It has been a pencil and about four minutes, and one of them you did not come and I wrote the date and your name anyway, because the whole point of the line is that it stands up."

"I know. I did not come on the first of this month."

"You did not come on the first of this month because a man put a notice into your hand at a gate, and I found that out on the third and I wrote you in anyway." She took her finger away. "That is the arrangement. I want out of it."

He waited.

"Not forgiven. Not written off. Released." She said it the way she said a figure. "I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because there is one way to do this and I have been sitting in this room since seven making sure I knew which one it was."

"Go on."

"You release a debt by paying it. That is what a debt is." She turned both hands over on the table and left them there. "So there is the release, and it is the only one, and here is what it costs and I am telling you before you offer it, because you were going to offer it and we will both know in about nine minutes that it was a mistake."

"I would not have offered it."

"You would have offered it in a fortnight. There is a difference and you know what it is." She looked at the rack behind her. "The thing you spend is the account. The account is spent by contact. My arm is the only part of anybody in this building you have ever been able to move."

"Yes."

"And in thirty-one days you are going to stand in a hall in front of a machine that reads a counterparty at the moment of contact and keep your hands off every person in it for four hours, and Nadia Ferrous is right about that and you know she is right, and I would like it said in this room, in front of you, before I ask you for anything."

"That is not a release," he said. "That is a payment."

"Yes." She did not soften it. "And it is the exact thing I have been paying for you to avoid since June."

"If you do it this morning, you will have proved to every instrument in this city that the account moves on contact, because I am the only person here whose body you can move at all, and there is nothing in Ostara that could tell the difference between a man touching a doctor to end a four-year debt and a man touching a doctor because he cannot help himself."

"It is worse than that and you know it." He heard his own voice do something he had not intended. "You have a plate at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, and a band on an instrument in another building, and on the twenty-ninth you are going to be the only thing that machine has ever been able to read."

"And what you would be doing this morning is putting the whole of it into the one person who cannot be examined for it, which is you, because you are the only one of us who would know and who would not say."

"It is worse than that," said Tessa Okonkwo, "because I would know, and I would be standing in the fourth rank of that hall with my name in the second column of a certified run about you, and there would be a hearing officer in front of me with a good voice asking me what it was, and I would not have a sentence."

She put her hands in her lap and was quiet for about three seconds.

"So that is the refusal," she said, "and I want it in my own words and not yours, and I have been saying it to myself since seven and it is better than what I had at seven. You cannot release me."

"That is the first honest thing anybody in this building has said to me about a debt in eleven years, and I am not going to thank you for it, because a thing that is true is not a favour, and I have never thanked you and I am not going to start in a room with the rack behind me."

"You are the only person in this city I cannot pay," he said.

"Yes," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That is what I am. I have been that since the twenty-third of last month and before that in a different way, and I would have gone on being it quite happily for the rest of my life, and I want you to understand that you have not done anything for me this morning and that I am not going to let either of us pretend that this morning was about you."

"Then what was it about."

"It was about me wanting out," she said, "and about me finding out, in a chair, with my own hands on my own book, that the only door out of it is a thing you are going to have to do in a hall in thirty-one days, and about both of us knowing it out loud in a shut room, in four hours, on a Monday morning." She stood up.

"And about the fact that you have about two minutes to decide whether you are going to be honest about the second half of it, and I would like you to be, because I have had a very bad week and I am not going to be managed."

"What is the second half."

"You came down here because I sent for you." She was not looking at him. "You cleaned five floors and you left the fourth, which is the floor you have started doing on the twenty-first, and you came because I said where. You did not come because you had a figure to ask me for."

"You have not asked me for a figure in three weeks and you are not going to today, because on the twenty-seventh you were told in a room four feet by nine what that machine does, and a man who has been told what a machine does does not need a doctor's two hands in order to know what he weighs."

The office had a lamp on a cord and a rack of boards on the wall and a corridor behind her with four shut doors at the end of it.

"So no," she said. "I am not releasing you from anything and you are not releasing me from anything, and if you want to go you may go, and I will write the first of next month down with your name on it at eight o'clock in the morning whether you are in this room or not, because that is what the book is."

He did not go.

"Then say that one plainly," he said, "because I am not going to guess at it in a room where you have been counting since seven."

She turned round and looked at him properly for the first time since he had come in.

"You are not going to touch me and I am going to touch you," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and neither of those two things is the debt."

"Both of us are going to be awake and awake and clear-headed and out of our heads about it, and afterwards nothing will have moved, and I will not thank you for it and you will not be thanked by anybody. That is the whole of the terms, and I have never once in this room had to say four terms out loud to a man in a chair, and I am not starting by accident."

"I heard you."

"Then take your coat off and hang it on that hook, not over the chair, because I will be able to see it when I turn round and I am not going to work with a man's coat over a chair." She unbuttoned her cuffs and rolled them above her forearms. "And sit on the couch, not the bed, because I have had four people in the beds behind me since the spring and I am not doing this four feet from a woman who cannot get up."

He hung the coat on the hook.

The room was a warm one and it smelled of the yard and of the lamp cord and of eleven years of paper, and the rack stood on the wall behind her shoulder with its clips, and the day book was shut on the table where she had left it with her hand flat on the cover.

She came and put her hands on his face and turned it toward the window the way she had turned an instrument sheet toward a window, entirely without force in it, and then she took her hands away and stepped back and looked at him from a foot off.

"You have spent thirty-one days' worth of hands being careful and I want you to know that I have watched a man take his hand back from four inches of a woman's plate and I am going to take your hands back for about an hour, and that the two of those things are going to live next to each other in this room for the rest of our lives, and that is the arrangement and I am not going to pretend it is tidy."

She got him onto the couch and got his shirt open between them, and she was quick and practical about it in the way of a woman who has undressed a hurt man a hundred times in a corridor and has never once been in a hurry with one she wants.

"It is going to be on your record that I said no to the release first," she said, while she worked his belt. "I would like that said out loud in this room with my hands on you, because that is where I am saying it from."

"I will say it."

"Say it now."

"You refused me," he said, "and I could not pay you, and I said it out loud, and you said you are the only person in this city I cannot pay."

"Good." She got her own blouse open and put it over the back of the couch. "Then that is what this is, and it is not a substitute for it, and it does not clear a line in a book."

There was no cold anywhere in that room.

He was aware of the whole of her body against the whole of his, of her weight coming down onto him on the couch, and of her hair falling forward over both of them. There was not one second in it of anything arriving early and settled, nothing owed between them that a hand could carry.

She did not feed him. She has never fed him. And the whole of what she was asking for on the morning was for him to spend something that does not move on the person it came from.

She made him put his hands behind his head and she counted him out loud against his collarbone, and when he came up at the end of it she took his face in both hands and made him look at her while she was finishing, which is not a thing she does for a man she is being gracious with.

"Still counting," she said. "You are always counting. Tell me when you have stopped."

He said it and she took it at face value and did not check, which she had never promised and had made a point of not promising.

Then she turned him over onto his front and got a hand under his ribs, and it went on the fourth one on the left without her looking for it, because she had corrected him out loud about that rib in the middle of last month and had never gone back to it, and the sound he made into the couch was not a sound anybody in the Vestry had heard from him before and she took no notice of it at all.

"Still there," she said. "I told you in the spring that you were guarding it out of habit and not out of sensation. It is not a habit this morning. It is a sensation and I am going to have it and you are going to let me."

He let her.

It went on for a long time and neither of them hurried at any point in it, and she talked to him the whole way through in the flat voice she used for figures, naming what was happening to him as though it were a return she was completing, and twice she made him say her name and twice he did, and the second time she put her hand flat over his mouth and said not yet and made him wait, and then took the hand away.

Afterwards she lay along him with her weight on his side and one hand flat on his chest, counting his heart, the way she had done it on the twenty-ninth of last month and twice since, and said into the collar of his open shirt:

"Thirty-one days," she said. "And nothing moved. I would know. I have read bodies for eleven years and I know what a thing arriving in one feels like from the outside, and nothing in this room went anywhere but where the two of us put it."

"That is what I said."

"It is what you said and it is why I wanted it." She took her hand off his chest and lay it on the couch instead. "You did not touch me to spend anything. You put your hands on me the way a man puts his hands on a woman, and I want the distinction written down somewhere that is not the book, because the book is for dates and there is not going to be a date on this."

"There is not going to be a date on this."

"Then it is the first thing we have had in four months that is not going anywhere." She said it and then went quiet for about a minute, and the corridor behind her had four shut doors at the end of it and the rack stood on the wall with its clips and had not moved since the twenty-eighth of last month, which was thirty-two days. "Do you understand what I have just done?"

"I have made a thing that a debt is not, in a room where the debt is, on purpose, and I have done it because I cannot be released and I am not going to be released and I would rather have this than the four minutes back."

"Yes."

"Say it out loud, because you have been very careful all day."

"You are the one person owed who cannot be paid," he said, "and you have just made something that is not a payment, and I cannot give you the account and I can give you that, and both of those are true in this room at once and neither one of them cancels the other."

Tessa Okonkwo got up off the couch and found her blouse and put it on before her cuffs, which is the order she did everything in.

"I am going to stand in front of that rack now," she said, "and look at it for a while, and I would rather you were not in the room, and that is not a dismissal and it is the only thing I have got that is mine."

He went out into the arcade and sat on the stone bench with the brass plate on the end of it for about ten minutes in the wet, with his sleeve pushed up because he could not think of a reason to push it up.

Then he walked the arcades home and did not clean anything when he got in, and sat on the stairs above the chop-house with the notebook shut in front of him and nothing owed and nothing paid and a debt four years standing with a pencil line in it on the first of next month, which he would go and sit in a chair for while a woman wrote a date down.

Thirty-one days. Two left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next, and both of them knowing it out loud in a shut room on the twenty-ninth, and neither of them able to do a thing about it.

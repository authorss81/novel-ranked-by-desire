# The Roll They Are Building

The eleventh was a Thursday, and the Board repainted at nine, and Lin Hao stood in the Reading Square with his hands in his pockets and watched a man on a ladder paint over the place where his name had been.

It took about forty seconds. The man did not know what he was covering. He had a tile in one hand and a brush in the other and a sheet with a name on it tucked under the tile, and he set the tile where the pale rectangle had been and brushed round the edges and climbed down, and that was the whole of it.

Lin Hao walked up to the wall after the ladders moved on. The tile had a stranger's name on it and a band he did not look at twice. The rectangle was gone. There was no way afterwards to establish that there had ever been a space, which was what he had said to himself on the fourth, and now there was not even the space.

A porter with a barrow stopped beside him and followed his eyes up the wall, and nodded at the new tile, and said the name on it belonged to a woman from the lamp-shop, and Lin Hao said she must have done something worth doing, and the porter laughed and went on with his barrow.

It was the first ordinary conversation he had had in that square in six weeks. It cost the porter nothing and it cost him nothing, and he stood there afterwards with his hands in his pockets and understood that being seen for a minute by a man with a barrow was the whole of what he had asked for on the fourth, and that he had got it by accident, and that accidents did not go into boxes.

He had been up since five and had cleaned six floors of washrooms before the square, the way he had for four years, and on the third floor a queue had formed at the plate socket while the machine ran, eleven people with plates in their hands, and he had mopped round their feet and taken the bucket through the queue twice without anybody stepping aside or telling him to wait.

It had gone into the book that morning without his noticing it, the way it had gone in four thousand times across four years. A corridor, a queue, an hour. The cause named on the page where it happened, and no number anywhere, because no count had turned and no panel belonged to it.

He stood there for about a minute with forty people going past him to the rail. Nobody looked at the tile. Nobody had looked at the rectangle either. That was the arrangement he had spent four months buying, and it had worked exactly as intended, and it had cost him a floor and a standing and a calendar and three women's files.

He was angry. That was the feeling, and it surprised him, because he had expected the flat nothing of the fourth and had got something with an edge instead.

What he wanted was plain enough to say out loud if anybody had asked: to see what his signature had bought. He had signed two sheets on the night of the eighth and put a book into a file, and a registrar had answered in four lines, and he had lodged the tube himself at ten past three on the ninth. Proving was supposed to be the Office's word for what happened next. He wanted to know what it meant for a man who had acknowledged with an objection.

He went to the Reading and up the outside stair, which was dry for the first time in a week.

Sabine Rooke was standing in the doorway of the third-floor room with her hands on the frame, the way she had said she would be. The eight chairs were against the far wall in two rows of four. The ninth was square to the door, four feet out.

The table had nine boxes on it.

They were grey archive boxes with lids, numbered on the ends in a clerk's hand from one to nine, and they sat in a row down the middle of the eight-foot table with about a hand's width between them. Two copy clerks were at the far end with a stack of exhibit sheets and a pot of paste. Nobody looked up when he came in, except a porter at the door with a trolley, who nodded at him the way porters nod at furniture and went back to his straps.

"That is the roll," said Sabine Rooke. "That is what proving looks like when it has furniture."

Tove Marlin was at the far end of the table with the exhibit sheets, checking each one against the box numbers before the paste went on. She looked up when he stopped at the foot of the table, and nodded once, and went back to the sheets, because there was nothing in the room that was his to be given.

"Read me one," he said.

Tove put her hand on the second box. "Item two. A visitors' book from a house in Bellhouse, opened on the first, with an hour column and a column for the caller's business. Eighty-nine entries. Twenty-nine ask about a person and thirteen of the twenty-nine ask about somebody in the subject's household. Those figures are the Office's and not mine, and I have checked them twice."

She pasted the sheet to the inside of the lid and smoothed it with the flat of her hand.

"That is what the hall hears," she said. "Not the book. The sheet. The book stays in Bellhouse and the sheet goes into the box, and the box goes into the hall, and a clerk with a good voice reads the sheet. That is proving. The paper travels and the thing stays."

"The book stays," said Sabine Rooke. "The women in it come up to this room anyway, on the morning, in those two rows, and they will hear a description of their own house read at them by a man with a good voice, and nothing inside that box knows one thing about any of them." She moved a hand at the chairs against the wall. "That is what the furniture is for. It gives them somewhere to sit and be described."

He went in and stood at the foot of the table. Box four had a slip tied to the handle with string. The slip said, in Tove Marlin's hand, four words he had to lean in to read.

"Excluded from reading aloud."

"She tied that herself," said Sabine. "Yesterday morning. She said a box with an objection on it ought to wear the objection where anybody can see it, and then she stood here for about a minute with her hand on the string."

One of the copy clerks pasted an exhibit sheet to the inside of a lid and smoothed it with the flat of her hand. The sheet had the Office's mark at the top and a number under it and a description under that, in the flat administrative voice of something made to be read out.

Lin Hao looked at box one. Then box two. He did not touch any of them.

"They are copies," said Sabine, before he asked. "The lodged set is in the file. These are the hall's set. Nine boxes for nine items, and the fourth one shut."

"And the objection."

"Travels with it." She let go of the frame. "That is what proving means, and I have watched it eleven times from this doorway. A proved roll is a roll nobody disputes enough to stop. Your objection does not stop it. It goes into the hall on top of the pile, and it is read first if you insist, and the Office will thank you for it."

He did the arithmetic without meaning to. Twenty days left in this month and twenty-nine in the next. Forty-nine days to the twenty-ninth, in a hall with a hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety standing places behind it.

"Two men who do not speak to each other," he said.

"That is the parties' wait," said Sabine. "While the hall is cleared. A hall takes time to clear, and longer when the Office has to put a hundred and forty strangers' names on a sheet. Until that is done the parties sit in here with the door shut. Not locked. Shut."

She looked at the ninth chair, square to the door.

"You will sit in here with it," she said. "With nine boxes of paper about your household and a shut door and a woman in the doorway. That is the whole of what this floor has been doing since the twenty-sixth, and now you have seen it."

He was quiet for a while. The copy clerks worked down the row, pasting and smoothing, pasting and smoothing. The paste smelled sharp in the cold room.

"What does the fourth box have in it," he said.

"Nothing anybody will read." Sabine put her hands on her knees, though she was standing. "A slip saying it is excluded, and a copy of your objection, and a copy of the four lines. That is all proving leaves a man who objected. The description sits in a shut file and the box sits shut in here."

He understood it then, standing at the foot of an eight-foot table in a room with two doors. He had traded a name for a description and got the worse half, which was what he had said to himself on the ninth. What he had not said was the rest of it.

By acknowledging the roll to protect the fourth item, he had proved the other eight.

The Field minute. The visitors' book. The clinic's four lines. Forty-one names in a delivery record. A district table with no clerk's name. A discharged standing. His own refusal. An empty space, photographed.

Every one of them would be read aloud on the twenty-ninth, exactly as written, because he had signed a sheet saying he had received them.

"That is what my signature bought," he said.

"That is what acknowledging buys," said Sabine Rooke. "Everybody handed one of these buys it. I have watched eleven men buy it from this doorway, and every one of them stood where you are standing and said some version of that sentence."

She picked up her card off the ninth chair and put it in her pocket, and then she put the chair square to the door again with two hands, exactly four feet out, the way she had put it on the first.

"It stays," she said, before he asked. "The eight go back to the floor when the hall is done. The ninth stays where I put it. Eleven years I sat against a wall, and then a member told me what the room was for in ninety seconds on a Saturday, and I am not going back to the wall."

"Go down the stairs," she said. "There is a queue at the door tomorrow and you are not in it. You spent your five days on the ninth, and the roll is proved, and proving is not a thing anybody unproves, and the next thing built in this building will be the Office's case, and it will be built out of what is on that table."

He went down the outside stair and stood at the bottom of it in front of the district frame, where his notice sat in the middle of forty others between the Exchange hours and a lost parcel.

Nine boxes on a table. A shut fourth box wearing his objection on a string. Forty-nine days.

He had proved the roll himself, with the only signature in the building that could do it, and there was no procedure anywhere in Ostara for taking that back.

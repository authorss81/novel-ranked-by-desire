# The Hearing In Writing

The Office put its case in writing on the twentieth, and Lin Hao saw it at ten in the morning on the third floor of the Reading, where Tove Marlin laid it on the eight-foot table beside the nine grey boxes and read the cover sheet exactly as written, because that was the only way she read anything.

He had cleaned six floors before coming, with the washrooms last because a sitting day filled them, and on the fourth floor the machine stood silent in a discharged room, and Drant's own entry beside the spike said received, and it had gone nowhere at four for sixteen days.

It had gone into the book with the floors. The cause named, and no number.

Sabine Rooke stood in the doorway with her hands on the frame. The fourth box wore the objection on a string. The copy clerks had finished, and the paste pots were capped, and the room smelled sharp and clean.

"One list," she said. "What the Office will put before the hall. It opens with a description, not a document, and the description is first because the Office's list is in the order the hall will hear it."

She turned the cover sheet round so it faced him. Four paragraphs. The heading over it said only description, in the flat administrative voice of something made by an office to be read out.

He read the first sentence. It was a description of him, in somebody else's hand, built out of nine boxes and forty-one names and fourteen entries and six lines a day. It did not name him. It did not need to. It was first on the list, and everything under it was his.

"That is the hearing in writing," said Tove. "Nine items, proved, with an excluding objection on the fourth. One witness named by the member." She looked at him. "Wren Kallo. Named yesterday, in your hand, on the form the hearing officer keeps. The Office received it this morning."

He had written it the evening before, at the table above the chop-house, with six women round him and one on the landing. One line. A name. The witness he had told Adeyemi on the third he would not bring.

He had put the pen down after the name and looked at the six faces round the table, and Nadia had read it back twice, word for word, the way she read everything that mattered, and Ola had dated it, and Marguerite had folded it without a word and put it in his coat herself.

Nobody at the table had argued. That was the thing he carried up the outside stair the next morning. Six women had watched him reverse the only position he had stated to the hearing officer, and nobody had said a word against it, because the woman in the workroom could not keep a secret and they all knew what that meant.

"Forty days," said Tove. Eleven days left in this month after the twentieth, and twenty-nine in the next, and she did the arithmetic without apology, as bookkeeping. "The hall is forty days out. The record is complete. The instrument is certified on paper."

She put the second sheet on the table. The certification desk's paper, two paragraphs, with the desk's mark and a date. The tool met its specification. Two columns. Subject and counterparty. The builder had confirmed she built it. The desk had run it. That was the whole of what paper could say.

He felt it as a weight settling, the way a press bed settles on a sill. Grim and complete. That was the feeling, and it had nothing in it like relief.

What he wanted was to read the description again. He did not. He had read it once and once was enough, because a man who reads his own description twice starts correcting it, and correcting it was the Office's work and not his.

"There is one more thing on this floor," said Tove, "and it is not the Office's. It is the Wing's, and it is in the fourth book, and Anneke Roos asked me to bring you down to see it entered, because she has carried paper up three flights for three weeks and she wants a witness that is not a clerk."

They went down to the register room at twenty to four. Anneke Roos had the fourth book open on the counter and a slip beside it in Ivo Sarn's hand, the hand three floors above hers that she had never seen. Drant stood at the end of the counter with his hands behind his back.

Anneke read the slip aloud, exactly as written, because Tove was in the room and accuracy was the discipline.

"A settled mass in the possession of a member, accumulating by the member's own condition, no source identified, no measure obtainable."

She put the slip down and took up her pen.

"It goes into the sixth space," she said, "which has been ruled and empty for a year and a half, and it goes in as received and not as agreed, and my hand puts it there and my name goes under it as the hand that received it."

She wrote it into the ruled space at the bottom of the page, in her own hand, copying the slip word for word. Then she signed beneath it with her own name, which was the first thing she had signed in four months, and dated it the twentieth, and blotted it, and shut the pen.

The room was quiet. The queue was gone. Drant stood at the end of the counter with his hands behind his back, and Tove stood at the side with the tube under her arm, and the fourth book lay open with five headings and a sixth space filled at last, eleven years ruled and a year and a half empty on purpose and now a paragraph in it, received and not agreed.

"It is not true," said Drant, from the end of the counter. He said it to the book and not to anybody. "A mass in his possession by his own condition. No source. No measure. Four offices have believed it for four months and it is false in exactly the direction that matters, because it makes him the maker of a thing he never made, and a man who makes his own mass is a man who can be regulated."

He stopped. Nobody asked him to go on. The sentence sat in the sixth space in Anneke Roos's hand, received and not agreed, and it was the Office's sentence now, and it would be read in the hall.

Lin Hao looked at the page. Five headings and a sixth space, filled at last. Eleven years ruled, a year and a half empty on purpose, and now a paragraph in it that was wrong, in a clerk's hand, signed and dated.

He understood what it was for. The Office had a record and a case and a certified instrument and a description first on the list. What it had needed since the nineteenth of last month was a sentence to put in the empty space, and now it had one, and the sentence made him the maker of his own mass, and a man who makes his own mass is a man who can be regulated.

"That is the sixth space filled," said Anneke. "It goes up at four with everything else, and it will come back with everything else, and from today there is a line in it where anybody can see it."

She closed the fourth book and put the slip in the tray for filing. Tove picked up the tube with the list in it. Drant put his hands on the counter and left them there, and then took them off, and squared the register the way he squared everything, because a register squared was a day closed.

Sabine's room stood empty above them with nine boxes on an eight-foot table and a ninth chair square to a door, waiting for two men who did not speak to each other and a morning forty days out when the hall would clear and the parties would sit with the door shut.

He walked home up the long road with forty days in front of him and the record complete behind him. Nine boxes on a table. A shut fourth box wearing his objection. A description first on the list. A sentence in a sixth space, received and not agreed. A certified instrument, two columns, a builder named as his witness who could not keep a secret and had already told a bench and a sister and a runner and a plate-office clerk.

At the house the notebook lay open on the hall table with the day's six lines in it, and the visitors' book sat shut beside it, and Sun was on the landing in her coat, off paper, holding the place the house could not hold. Marguerite read him the day's entries in four minutes. Nothing with legs. The house held.

The hall was forty days out. Everything the Office meant to read was written. Everything the Wing believed was in a book.

And the one question nobody in any office had answered, because no paper could answer it, went with him up the road like a second shadow.

The instrument was certified on paper. The record was complete. The hall was waiting.

What could the instrument actually do.

# The Only Movement Left

The thirtieth was the last working day of a month and Anneke Roos had the month-end return in the tray at eight in the morning, late again, and it would go up tomorrow at four with a month-end sheet that would sit in the tray until somebody put back the desk that sends it on the thirty-first, and that desk had been discharged on the eighteenth of last month and had not come back.

He cleaned six floors. The fourth was discharged and was the dirtiest thing in the building and he had started doing it on the twenty-first without writing down why, and on the last day of the month he did it first, because there was nothing on that floor to disturb and it took him forty minutes instead of an hour and a half.

It went into the book with the floors. The cause named on the page where it happened, and no number, because no count had turned and nothing had been scored since the third of last month and the plate had not moved a grain and would not move one on the fourth of next month either, and the band six was still seated on an instrument in a building he had never been inside, suspended pending a description.

Thirty days. One day left in this month after today, and twenty-nine in the next.

He had a bucket and a cloth and four weeks of not asking anybody anything, and on the second floor a clerk stopped him with a form about a tap that ran brown, and he wrote the tap's number on his cuff and fixed it by half past eleven, and the whole of the conversation was six words and one of them was thank you and none of them needed an answer.

He went up the back stair of the Exchange building at twenty past one and asked for the certification clerk and was let straight in, because he was on the file and the file had been on the file since the twenty-seventh.

"I want one thing answered," he said, "and it is a small one and it is the only one left."

"Sit down."

"Say it standing. I have been in a chair in this building twice and both times it cost somebody something."

The clerk put his pen down.

"What does it print," said Lin Hao, "on the twenty-ninth, when it is run on a person who has not touched anybody?"

The clerk did not answer for about four seconds, and then he went into a drawer and came out with a sheet of paper output with nothing on it at all.

It was the same stock as the certified run. The Office's mark at the head in machine print. A certification line ruled under it. Two columns ruled across the width.

And both columns empty.

The subject column empty, because nothing had been put into it. The counterparty column empty, because a room with no contact in it is not a thing this machine can put a name against. And under the two columns, at the foot of the sheet, in the place where a form has room for a finding, a line, and a signature, all three ruled and all three blank.

"That is what it prints," said the clerk. "It prints the head, and it prints both columns, and it prints the rule at the foot, and then it stops, because there is nothing else a piece of paper of that size can do."

"And the desk certifies that."

"The desk certifies the output as complete to specification." He put the blank sheet down flat on the counter. "That is what the desk does. It does not ask what came out. A machine that prints nothing has printed correctly, and this desk will sign it and date it and file it, and I have signed four of them in seven years and I can tell you that every one of them was a waste of a good morning."

"What happens to it on the twenty-ninth."

"It goes into the transcript in the Office's own voice at whatever point in the morning the hearing officer reaches it, and the hearing officer reads it out, and the reading is that nothing was found." He squared the sheet.

"And then the hearing does what it has to do at the end of a hearing, which is close, and closes with a document saying that on the morning of the twenty-ninth, in a room on Cobb Street, a counter-forensics instrument was submitted in evidence and produced no output."

"And that closes it."

"That closes the instrument." He put his hand flat on the sheet, the way he put his hand flat on everything. "It does not close the notice. The notice asks whether an account may remain undescribed, in public, and the answer to that is in the description, and the description is on the roll, and the roll is read whatever the machine does."

"A machine that reads nothing does not make the roll unread. A machine that reads nothing gives the Office one more document with his name on it, dated, signed by this desk, filed, and it is the only document about him in this city that will ever be exactly true."

Lin Hao stood at a counter and looked at a piece of paper with a sixth space on it.

It was the same shape. Ruled, empty, and kept empty on purpose for a year and a half by a clerk three floors below this one who had never once been asked what it was for.

And the Wing had written into a book on the third floor a paragraph to fill it, and a woman had signed her own name under the paragraph and blotted it and dated it, and a man four floors up had said out loud in that room that the paragraph was false in exactly the direction that mattered.

And here was the machine's version of the same shape, and it was going into the same kind of book on the same kind of morning, and the man who would sign it was the man with the ink on his cuffs who had signed four of them in seven years and called every one of them a waste of a good morning.

"It does not close it," he said.

"It does not close it," said the clerk, "and I will tell you the other half, because you have come up two flights to ask me one question and you are entitled to the answer rather than to the half of it that is useful to you."

"Go on."

"The Office knows that too." He capped his pen. "The Office knows it because the Office has to produce a hearing, and a hearing produces a document, and a document with an empty column in it is a document that says the instrument did not work, and an instrument that did not work on the morning is an instrument that gets withdrawn and a hearing that loses its reason. Nobody in Cobb Street wants that on a page."

"They do not want it the way a criminal does not want it. They want it the way an office does not want a procedure to come out with nothing in it, because a procedure that comes out with nothing in it has to be done again, and there is a calendar, and the calendar has the twenty-ninth printed on it in two places by now."

"What do they have to do about it."

"Nothing," said the clerk. "That is the answer and you may have it for nothing. They cannot make you touch somebody. There is no form in this city that compels a member to touch anybody, and I have looked, because I looked at yours on the twenty-seventh and yours was the only one of the four this month with a question in it, and there is not one about contact."

"So they can only wait for me to do it."

"They can only wait for you to do it," said the clerk, "and you are a man who cleans washrooms for a living."

He went down the back stair past the plate office, where the queue was standing with plates in their hands, and out into Cobb Street.

Five days after that with nothing in his hands at all, and then the rest of a month in which the only thing anybody in this house will be able to give him is an argument.

The whole of the arithmetic fitted in a corridor in about eleven seconds and it was not complicated and it was not a discovery and there was nothing in it that he had not known on the morning of the twenty-seventh in a room four feet by nine.

A reading is not a photograph of a hundred and twelve lines. A reading is a series of moments, one at a time, and a name goes into the second column on the moments when his hands are on somebody in the room, and there is no version of it in which one moment produces two names or in which the instrument goes back over ground it has already walked.

So the reading is survivable. It is survivable in exactly one way, which is that he does not touch one person in that hall for the whole of the morning, and a man can do that.

And if he does it, the second column is empty, and the instrument is withdrawn, and the Office has a certified document with his name on it saying that nothing was found on the morning of the twenty-ninth. It goes on a wall on the fourth of next month.

And it does not close the notice. The description is read out of a roll in front of whatever is standing at the rail, and the six lines of a Field minute are read and stop where a seventh name would have been, and a visitors' book is read out as a number, and forty-one names are read one at a time by a clerk with a good voice.

All of that happens with an empty column in the second half of the transcript.

That was the whole of the remainder and he had it in eleven seconds and it took him the rest of the afternoon to understand what it was.

He was not safe. He had been half expecting, in some room he did not go into, to be told that he was safe. There was no such thing.

There was a hall and a rail and a plate by the door and ninety standing places behind the back row, and a man who has spent four years touching people standing at a table with his hands at his sides for four hours. Every one of those ninety people was an opportunity somebody had paid for, and he would have to refuse all ninety.

Refusing all ninety was the only move on the board, and it was not a move.

There was one thing in that room he could move without putting a name in the column. He had known about it since the thirteenth of last month in a corridor with a woman with a card, and he had not spent it once in six weeks.

He was not going to spend it on the morning of the twenty-ninth in front of two hundred people, because he had not the least idea why it worked, and he was not going to find out by trying it on somebody.

So what was left on the board was the move he had already refused, and the arithmetic left it sitting there like the ninth chair in a corridor that had been standing empty since the twenty-seventh of last month.

He went home past the square and the Board was bare of his name and the district frame had forty sheets on it and his had been taken down on the third of the month and had gone into a house file in Cobb Street with about two hundred others, and a man who had spent a morning being told that a blank sheet of paper was going to be the truest document in the city walked up the long road with his hands in his pockets.

Six women in a house above a shop. A landing. A woman in a workroom behind a door.

What he wanted, and he could not have said it any better than that, was to not have to say it out loud to anybody.

Because the arithmetic did not end with him. It ended with the table above the chop-house and the notebook open and six lines a day in six hands.

Every one of those six women had spent the last four months asking him for one thing and being refused, and the thing they had been refused was a man in a room who put his hands on people. On the thirtieth of a month he was going to have to walk into that house and tell them that he was going to stop, in public, for four hours, in front of a hall, on the day he is looked at.

And Marguerite had priced him on the fifth of this month and Ines had refused twice and Nadia had put both hands flat on a table on the evening of the twenty-seventh and Beatrix had told him she would know, and Sun Meiling was on the top stair of that house with her coat on and had been since the first and would not be written down.

He got to the stairs above the chop-house at seven and sat down at the table with the notebook shut in front of him and put his hands flat on the wood on either side of it, and there were six women at that table by nine and the landing light was on above them and the visitors' book was shut under Marguerite's hand with the week's names in it and nothing with legs.

And the only thing left to do tonight was the thing he had been best at for five months, which was to sit at a table in a house full of women and say nothing at all.

He did it for about four minutes.

Then Marguerite Vale put her hand on the shut visitors' book and said, "No," and he looked up, and she did not take the sentence any further, because she had priced something in April and had not been asked and had not asked. She said only this:

"A woman in this city signed her name on a sheet on the twenty-second and carried a chair up four flights in front of two thousand people so that a clerk would find nine of something in a room on the twenty-ninth. She did that for a chair."

"You are going to walk into a hall in four weeks and stand still with your hands at your sides for four hours, and every woman at this table is going to read about it in a transcript before one of us has been asked a question about it. So I am not going to be the one who lets you sit in a chair being quiet for four minutes and then finds out afterwards that the price was never quoted."

He did not answer her, and the six of them looked at him, and he understood in the middle of that silence that he was going to have to say it out loud after all, and that it was going to be the argument, and that the argument was going to be about him and not about the Office.

A hall with a hundred and forty seats in it and a rail and a cloth on a table that had been down since eight in the morning, and a piece of paper with two columns on it that printed nothing.

A woman four streets from his own house whose name was going to be read out in a room because he had asked for it to be. An empty column. An Office that knew an empty column was worth nothing to it. And a man at a table who had four minutes of silence in him and had just spent them.

# What He Tells Them First

He told them on the sixteenth, at the table, at about nine, and he told them in that order because Ines Duarte had told him on Thursday night that a thing handed to somebody else stops being yours, and he had gone home and worked out that the only two people in Ostara he had left to hand things to were six women above a chop-house and a hearing officer on Cobb Street, and that a clerk with a day book had already beaten him to it.

Three of the six had the notebook open in front of them and one of them had not written her line down yet, which had happened twice in a month.

"Ines asked me to read item four," he said.

There was no answer for about four seconds.

"Not Tove Marlin. Ines. She has asked the Office to put her in the room's list as the reader of the household record, and the record is your book, and she wants to read it out loud on the morning of the twenty-ninth, in a hall, in front of whatever is standing at the back."

Marguerite Vale put her hand flat on the visitors' book and left it there.

"She wants to do it," he said, "because I shut it. I lodged the objection on the eighth and it is the only shut box on that roll, and a shut box on a proved roll is a shape, and the shape is six women and a building in Ferrier Street, and every person in this city who has read anything about this house can put a floor under it."

"You are going to let her," said Beatrix Halm.

"I am going to let her, and she asked me for nothing for it, and she is going to do it whether I let her or not, because the only power anybody in this house has over that roll is the power of not stopping it."

Nadia Ferrous had both hands flat on the wood, which everybody in the room recognised.

"Then say the arithmetic out loud," she said, "because you have come in here and told us a thing is going to happen and not one of us has the number yet."

"She is going to read it," he said. "Six lines a day, one woman to a line, from the twenty-first of last month to the morning of the twenty-ninth, in the order you wrote them, exactly as they stand, including the days a line says somebody was on a landing and did not come down, and including that there is no signature on any of it."

"Six lines a day for the whole of it," said Tessa Okonkwo, "in six handwritings, in a hall rated at two hundred and thirty."

"Yes."

"Then that is a document about six identifiable people and it is going to be in a transcript that anybody can buy at a counter for a shilling," said Tessa. "There is nothing in it that says who is who. There are six hands. I have watched three of you write and I could put four of the six to a name inside a week and I am a doctor and not a clerk."

"That is the price."

"That is the price you have decided somebody else pays," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and it is not you and it is not me either. It is six women who keep a book because you asked them to keep a book, and the whole of what they get for four months of it is a hole with their shape in it."

Beatrix Halm had not put her hands flat. She had one hand on the shelf above the sink, on an exercise book that had been there since the twelfth, and she did not take it off.

"That is the same trade," she said. "And I want everybody in this room to hear me say it out loud so that it is on somebody's page and not only mine. On the twelfth, at half past eleven at night, in the rain, I gave a year and four years of a district's cold minutes to a man with no condition on it. And I lost five years of my own handwriting on purpose to keep an office from having a map of when this city goes cold."

She turned round and looked at the shelf.

"That is this. That is exactly this, and it is the same size as that one and not the same size as it at all, and I am the one person at this table who has already done it."

"Beatrix."

"I am not refusing," she said. "I gave it away and I am not going to have it back and I said out loud on the twelfth that I am not forgiving a square in the winter and I am not forgiving it tonight either. I am telling you what this costs because I am the only one at this table who knows the price of this exact thing, and the price is that after the twenty-ninth there is no version of any of it that anybody can hand back."

The landing light was on above them and had been since eight.

"Second thing," he said. "And I am telling you this first too, before the clerk and before Wren, and I have been carrying it round since the twenty-eighth and I have put it down tonight because if I put it down anywhere else I will put it down on the morning, in a hall, and that will be the first time in five months that a thing about me has come out of my mouth without somebody having extracted it."

Five women looked at him.

"There is one thing in that room which might move," he said, "and it is me, and it is the only one, and I do not know whether it will, and I am going to find out in front of two hundred and eleven people rather than find out on anybody in this city first."

Five of them sat on that for about six seconds.

"You are not going to practise it," said Nadia Ferrous.

"I am not going to practise it on anybody. Not one person. If I find out what it does by putting my hand on somebody, then I find out on that somebody, and that is the whole of what I have refused to do for four months and I am not going to start on the sixteenth day of a month."

"Then you are going to find out in a hall," said Marguerite Vale.

"I am going to find out in a hall, at a table, with a cloth on it, in front of a machine, in front of the man who wrote the sentence that is wrong and the woman who built the thing, and I am going to do it or not do it, and if it does not do anything then that is the end of the only plan I have had since the fifth."

"That is not a decision," said Nadia. "That is a thing happening to you with a date on it."

"Yes," he said. "That is exactly what it is, and it is the first time I have handed one of them to anybody, and I have had four of them in four months and every single one of them was mine until about a minute ago."

The kettle had gone off the boil a while before and not one of them had moved for it.

Nadia Ferrous said, "Write it down, Ola."

And Ola Bright, who had had a pen in her hand since the second of the month, put it down on the table instead of picking it up.

She looked at the pen for about four seconds, which is an age for a woman who copies things.

"I am not writing this one down," she said.

The room changed.

"Since the twenty-first of last month I have copied three hundred and eleven sentences out of rooms," said Ola Bright, "and eleven of them are mine, and I have never once been asked for a page and that has been the whole of my safety for six months. And you have just told six people at one table that you are going to do something on purpose in a room, on purpose, in advance.

And a thing said before it happens is a thing somebody else can produce."

"That is not what she said."

"I know what it is not," said Ola Bright. "It is not about you. I do not care about you and I have never cared about you, and I copy things, and there has never once been a person in this city who could put a page of mine in front of a magistrate, because there has never once been a reason for one of my pages to exist."

She squared the pad and put her hand flat on it.

"This one is the reason. This is the first sentence anybody has said in this house in six months that could be used, and it would be used properly, and it would be used by somebody sensible.

And the woman who would hand it over is me, because I am the only one at this table whose whole life is copying things that other people said."

Marguerite Vale took her hand off the visitors' book.

"The broker's view, since you have all been waiting for it," she said. "Ola is right, and she is right for the first time since the first, and it is not sentiment.

"A fact that is recorded before an event is an instrument. A fact that is recorded after an event is a report. You have spent four months protecting everything about yourself by making sure nothing was written down before it happened, and you have just undone all of it in one evening because you were frightened of ambushing two hundred people.

"That is the whole of what I did it for," he said.

"I know it is. I priced it at the table in March and I have been wrong about you since March and you have made me wrong again tonight." She stood up and did not put the glass back on the mantel.

"And you cannot have both. You cannot say a thing in a hall before it happens so that a room is not ambushed, and you cannot also have the only unasked record in this house stay unasked.

"Those are one decision and not two, and one of them is going into a file somewhere, and it might as well be the one you chose.

He looked at the pen on the table.

"How many pages did you never show anybody," he said.

"Two hundred," said Ola Bright. "You saw the book. Two hundred pages of nothing and then four sentences on the inside cover, dated separately, which is me being careful about you for the first and last time."

She picked the pad up and shut it and put it under the table with her other books, out of sight, which was a thing she had never done in four months.

"I am not burning it," she said. "I am putting it under the table. And if anybody in this house ever wants to know what was said on the sixteenth of this month they can ask one of you, and that is going to be uncomfortable for about a year, and it is the correct arrangement and I have thought about it for eleven minutes."

Thirteen days. Thirteen less the sixteenth, and both in this month.

He walked the branch on the sixteenth morning with a queue twenty deep on the second floor and did the thing he had done four thousand times, which was take the bucket through and work round the outside of it, and on the way round a woman he had cleaned past for four years came back and took her coat off the rail and stood in the queue in her shirtsleeves and waited, and got her plate read at ten to ten.

She thanked him, which she had done once a week for four years, in the same four words.

It went into the drawer that evening with the other hundred and twelve and with a street and an hour on it, because nothing about a woman taking her coat off in a queue is written down anywhere in this city.

That was a hundred and thirteen.

At about eleven that night he got the exercise book off the shelf above the sink and did not open it, because Beatrix Halm had told him on the twelfth that a year of cold minutes by district was a map of when this city goes cold and not a list of people, and that if he used it like a book of names he would have taken five years of her work and used it as a way of finding out who owed him something.

He put it back with the four acts and the ruled sheet of hours on its face, where the whole of the record of himself now lived on a shelf a metre and a half above a sink, and went up the stairs and did not sleep much.

On the table in the hall, under the salt, where the four lines had lain on the fourteenth, there was nothing, because the notebook had been shut since the sixteenth and a woman who copies things had put three hundred and eleven sentences under the table where no hand in that house could reach them.

That was the price of announcing himself in advance to a house that had spent four months never being asked about anything, and it was a price Ola Bright had set without asking him and collected without telling him, and he had lost the only page in Ostara that had never once been asked for.

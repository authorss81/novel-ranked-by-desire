# The Cloth On The Table

The room was eleven feet by nine and it was warm, and that was the first thing he understood about it. There is a heat on in a room with no window on a second sub-level and somebody's budget carries it, and a budget is a document, and he stood in the doorway and let that go past him without touching it.

On the left, against the wall, was the single chair. On the right, against the wall, was a strip of rail with nothing on it, and the wall above the rail was bare and had been bare long enough to hold the grey of an unpainted rectangle where something narrower than a picture used to hang.

In the middle was the table. And on the table was the cloth.

He had been imagining a machine for six weeks. That was the thing he had gone down two flights of stairs to find, and it took him about four seconds in the doorway to understand that there was not one and that the not-finding was the entire content of the room.

A table. A cloth. A chair. A rail. A floor that had been swept since the weekend, because there was a grit line at the bottom of the skirting where the sweep had stopped and the grit line was a different colour from the rest of the dust.

His hands stayed at his sides for the whole of it.

He went in four steps, which is as far as a man can go into a room like that in a building where he cleans washrooms and still call it standing in a doorway, and he lifted one corner of the cloth with two fingers and put it back down exactly the way he had found it, which took him a second and a half and cost him more attention than anything else he has ever done with his hands.

Underneath there was paper.

Not one sheet. A stack of them, square, with the edges of about fifty visible where the cloth had not covered them, and the top one had the Office's mark at the head of it in machine print, and below the mark six lines ruled across the width in a hand, and under the six lines, ruled and empty at the bottom of every sheet the same way, a space.

He had seen that shape twice in his life and both times on a piece of paper somebody else was carrying. A space ruled and left empty on purpose, in a book three floors above a register counter, and on a table in the register room that went up at four and came down at half past.

Six headings and a sixth space. That is the whole shape of the Wing's paperwork and there were fifty copies of it under a cloth on a sub-level of a washroom building.

He put the cloth back where it was and stood looking at the stack from the side of it.

There were no names on any of them.

He made himself look properly, because a man who decides a thing in half a second is a man who has decided it before. He went along the top sheet line by line with his face about a foot off the paper, and there was no name and no plate and no number and no date, and the six headings were empty, and the sixth space was empty.

This was a room in which a sentence about a person was going to be written and had not been written yet.

He had been afraid of the sentence since the seventh, when a roll was unrolled in a corridor with no names on it. He had not once in six weeks been afraid of the room, and it turned out that the room was the part that was true and the sentence was only paperwork.

Then he saw the marks.

They were on the top sheet, in the sixth space, and they were not ink. Pencil, and light, and a kind of parallel scoring that a man makes when he has written a line hard enough to groove the paper and then gone over it with an eraser until the paper came up.

He did not touch them. He went down the stack with his eyes and found them on the fourth sheet down, and on the ninth, and on the fifteenth, and on the twenty-second, and then on every sixth sheet to the bottom of the stack, and the spacing was even the way spacing is even when a man has written something, sat with it, rubbed it out, put the sheet at the bottom of the pile, and taken a fresh one off the top.

Nine of them, from the top sheet to the bottom, evenly spaced.

Somebody had sat in that chair for a period measured in years and had nine times started to write a description of a person in the sixth space of a form and had nine times decided that the sentence they had was not the sentence that went in, and had nine times put the sheet at the bottom of the stack instead of in a drawer, which is the disposal of a man who does not want a room to have a copy of a thing he has thought about.

And the reason the stack was fifty deep and not nine was that the stock was new. That was the last thing in the room and it was the only one that was about him.

Fifty blank forms under a cloth, in the warm, on a sub-level of a building where the paper for this building's own classifications is kept, and not one of them has a name on it, and a man who has had six weeks to imagine a thing about himself has imagined a room with his name in it, and the room has paper in it.

The chair was pulled out from the table at an angle. Not squared. Not pushed in. Out at the angle of somebody who had stood up in the middle of doing something, and had not come back to it, and had not been dead.

A sound came up the stair from the bottom of the building, four sets of boots on boards and a tray going past a banister, and he was in a room on a sub-level of his own workplace with the cloth not square and his name not on it and nowhere in the world he had to be.

He got the corner of the cloth flat against the table and went out and pulled the door to behind him. It swung in and sat the way it had sat, with no handle and nothing in the hole where a handle had been, and the light in the corridor lay across the boards in a long wedge with the end of it on the pair of shoes he was standing in.

He had six seconds in the corridor and a thing on him that would have been worth nothing to a jury: a room on a sub-level of a building where he worked, fifty blank forms, and nine sentences rubbed out of the bottom of a pile.

What he had actually learned was a shape, and a shape is worth nothing to anybody on any hearing morning in the city, and he understood that on the stair with his heart going like a bird in a bag.

He went up the stair fast, which he had not done in four years of cleaning floors and which is the fastest a body can move without running. He came into the branch corridor at about six minutes past four and there was a runner on the bottom step with her hands on her knees, waiting for nothing, and she did not look up.

He walked past her and up the stairs and into a corridor that smelled of hot washrooms, and he was in the building and accounted for.

At the register counter Drant had the thin book open and shut and open again and did not have a pen in his hand.

"There is no public part of this floor," he said.

"I know."

"There is a stair that goes down off it and a goods lift on the sub-level and I have never in nine years been down either and I am not going to start, and I am telling you that I have said it out loud in this room today for the first time in my life." He put the book in the drawer under the counter, which is not where it lives, and shut the drawer. "I will not write anything about it."

"If anybody comes and asks me I have got nothing, because a register writes down that a thing moved and I did not see a thing move."

"That is not true."

"It is not true," Drant said, "and it is what the book is for, and you have known that since the eighth of last month and you have never once asked me to write a true thing, which is why I am going to carry it."

He finished the fourth floor at half past five and signed out at the gate and went up the long road, and the thing that stayed with him was not the plate and was not the sweep line and was not the heat.

It was nine pencil lines rubbed out of nine sheets at even intervals, by a man who was not frightened, in a room with no window, with a plate on the door saying what happens to a person who goes through one.

Thirty-six days. Seven left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next, and a hundred and twelve lines in a book in a drawer above a chop-house, and a sentence that already existed, in a clerk's hand, in a book on the third floor of a branch office, about a settled mass in the possession of a member.

He had wanted, for six weeks, to hand somebody one true thing.

He had wanted it the way a man who is not looked at wants to be looked at once, and there had been seven women in a house above a shop and a landing and a woman in a workroom with a machine on a bench, and not one of them knew that there was a room under his own building with fifty blank forms under a cloth in it and nine sentences rubbed out of the bottom of the pile.

He got as far as the corner of Ferrier Street and thought about turning round and going back up the stairs and putting it on the table with the notebook shut under Marguerite's hand.

Then he went home, and did not do it, and understood on the stairs above the chop-house that the not-doing was the thing he was best at in the world, and that he had just proved it about a thing no office in the city could have taken off him.

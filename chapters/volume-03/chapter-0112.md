# The First Exhibit

He had been up since five and had cleaned three floors before the yard, with the washrooms first because a Field sitting day filled them early, and on the ground floor the plate queue had been twenty deep by eight, and he had mopped round it, and it had gone into the book with the floors.

Runa Sato was in the yard behind the Field house on the twelfth with her sleeves rolled past the elbow and a press bed standing on end against the wall, and she looked at Lin Hao the way she had looked at him since the nineteenth of last month, which was as though he were a document she had already signed and could not take back.

The yard smelled of oil and wet brick. A divisional inspection sat at four o'clock, and everything not on a stand had to be in the far store by half past three, and the press bed was the last thing out. Two porters had got it down one flight and left it for the third man, and the third man was a card that had not come.

"You have come about the minute," she said.

"I have come because Ola said there was a clearance and a four o'clock inspection," he said. "The minute is the other half of why I stayed."

She jerked her head at the press bed. It was iron-framed, taller than either of them, with rollers at the foot and a chase still locked in it. Two porters had got it down one flight and left it.

"It goes to the far store," she said. "Down the ramp, round the post, up the sill. It wants three and there are two. The third was supposed to be a man with a card who has not come."

He took off his coat and hung it on the yard gate. The iron was cold through his shirt before they had it off the wall.

They got it down the ramp with Runa taking the weight at the front and him steering at the back, and halfway round the post the chase slipped in its frame and the whole bed slewed sideways and put his shoulder against the brick. He took the weight on his forearm and held it while she got her hands under the roller end and walked it round by inches.

It took eleven minutes. His shoulder burned and his hands were shaking when they got it over the sill. No cold came into his hands and no cold came into his chest. It was iron and brick and two people breathing hard, and nothing in him moved toward her or away from her, because there was nothing owed between them that morning that a hand could carry.

The chase had gouged a line in the brick by the post, pale against the wet. Runa looked at it and wiped her face with her sleeve and said nothing, because a gouge in a yard wall was the kind of thing an inspection noticed and there was no time to paint it.

She sat on the sill and he sat on the upturned chase. The yard was quiet except for the Field house windows going up one by one, clerks airing rooms before four.

She sat on the sill and wiped her face with her sleeve. He sat on the upturned chase and did the same.

"That is the first useful thing anybody has done for me in a month," she said. "It is also the last one I am going to let you do."

They went inside to the Field clerk's room, where the copy of the minute was on the table with a cover note beside it. Six lines in a serving officer's hand, being a household line entered at the member's word on the sixth of last month. The cover note carried a stamp and a date and a line for her name, and the stamp said the hall's copy wanted attestation as a whole.

A clerk with a bandaged thumb sat at the side desk copying names into a ledger and did not look up. The room smelled of the same paste as the Reading's third floor.

Runa read it aloud without being asked, the way Tove Marlin had read the roll, exactly as written. For a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word on the sixth, at the serving officer's request.

"That is my hand on the request line and your word on the sixth line," she said. "I wrote it and you said it and the Office has had it since the day after. It is the first exhibit because it is the only item anybody put on deliberately."

The cover note wanted one thing. The hall's copy to be attested as true, in her hand, with her name under it.

"Attest it," said Lin Hao, "and your name goes on hall paper. Refuse it and that is a second written disobedience against a dated demand."

"That is the whole of it." She put her hand flat on the cover note and left it there. "The first disobedience was declining to deliver you a second time in writing. That one bought me a hearing weeks out, and the hearing is not the hall's hearing, and it sits in no calendar but mine."

She was not frightened. That was the thing he kept coming back to afterwards. She was not frightened and she was not reckless. She was a woman looking at a price she had already agreed to pay and checking the figure.

He was sick with guilt. That was his feeling, and it sat in his chest like something he had swallowed wrong. Every cost in this business was a woman's and none of them was his, and here was another one being written in front of him, and his word was on the sixth line of it.

What he wanted was to take the pen out of her hand and sign it himself. He could not. It was her minute and her hand and her hearing, and a signature from him on a Field attestation would be a forgery and a finding in one.

"Do not attest the cover note," he said. "Attest the copy. The copy is true and you know it is true. The cover note is the Office asking you to carry it into the hall yourself."

"That is a distinction with no weight in it," she said. "A clerk will staple them together before nightfall."

"Then let the clerk do the stapling." He put his hand on the table, not on the paper. "Your hand attests what is true. The staple is theirs. I have spent four months learning the difference between what I do and what an office does with it, and the difference is the only thing anybody in this city is allowed to keep."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she picked up the pen and attested the copy in three words and her name, and left the cover note unsigned on the table.

"They will send it back," she said. "A copy without a cover is a thing with no road. It will come back with a date on it, and the date will be a demand, and the demand will be the second one."

"I know."

"And when it comes back I will sign that too, or I will not, and either way the hearing stands." She capped the pen and set it down exactly parallel to the cover note. "A first disobedience buys a hearing weeks out. A second one firms it into a date. That is the arithmetic of refusing in writing, and I did it the first time with my eyes open, and I will do it the second time the same way."

"That is my cost and it is still mine. It advanced today and it did not resolve, and I am telling you so that you stop carrying it up stairs for me."

She walked him to the yard gate and gave him his coat. The press bed stood in the far store with the chase locked in it. The inspection would come at four and find it there, and find the gouge in the brick, and write down neither, because inspections wrote down absences and not marks.

"Your shoulder," she said, at the gate.

"It is a shoulder. It carried half a press bed and it will carry a bucket tomorrow."

"It will carry six floors." She put her hand on the gate and left it there. "That is the job they let you keep, and it is the only engine anybody in this city is not allowed to take off you, and I want you to keep it the way I keep the minute. Mine attested where it is true. Yours mopped where it is dirty. Both of us doing the part with our name on it."

"Forty-eight days," she said, at the gate. It took him a second to understand she meant the hall. Nineteen days left in this month after the twelfth, and twenty-nine in the next, and she had done the arithmetic without him. "You will stand in that room with nine boxes and read nothing aloud. I will sit in a different room on a different day and answer for six lines."

He looked at the gouge in the brick by the post, pale against the wet, and then at her hands on the gate, raw from the roller end.

"Four o'clock," he said. "The inspection finds the bed in the store. That part is done."

"That part is done." She almost smiled. "Go and mop your floors, attendant. It is the only engine anybody is not allowed to take off you."

"Runa."

"No." She put her hand on the gate. "You carried half a press bed for me this morning and that is paid. The minute is not yours to carry and it never was. Your word is on the sixth line of it because I asked you for it, and I asked you because it was true, and I would ask you again tomorrow."

He went down the road with his shoulder aching and her attestation drying on a table behind him, and the ache was the only part of the day that belonged to him, and he was glad of it the way a man is glad of a thing that hurts in the right place.

He told the table that evening, standing at the foot of it with his coat still on. Half a press bed. An attestation on the copy. A cover note unsigned. A demand coming back with a date on it.

Nadia listened to the end and poured him tea without being asked, which for Nadia Ferrous was an apology and a salute in one.

"She carried the weight at the front," he said. "I steered. That is the whole of my part and I am keeping it."

# September Is Still Beatrix's

The yards at half past eleven at night were rain on steel and lamplight off wet roofs and the north gate had no guard on it, and Beatrix Halm had the exercise book under her coat and had been standing in the door of the number nineteen for about four minutes before he came round the corner, which she told him about afterward and did not apologise for.

"Six days," she said. "I have been not saying a thing for six days and it has come out of me in about the first nine seconds, so I am going to start before I decide anything else, and you are going to stand there and let me, because you did it to me in the spring and I have not forgotten and I am not going to let you interrupt."

He stood there.

"The ninth of September is mine," said Beatrix Halm. "It has been mine since June and I gave it to not one person and I have not written it down anywhere, and on the twenty-eighth of last month I said the word out loud at a table in your house and three women in that room have known it since, and one of us is not allowed to have it and I am the one who decided that, so I am the one who has to keep it."

"What happens on the ninth."

"In September somebody in a warm room asks my division for the cold minutes by district." She said it as though she were reading off a brake gauge. "That is a real request. It is made twice a year, and I have answered it twice, and the second time is the one I still think about."

"And when they ask?"

"They ask for a year of my handwriting on a map. I have a year of my handwriting on a map." She put her hand on the control ledge. "It goes on a table in a warm room and an auditor reads it and asks which operators have cold minutes and in which districts."

"That is you."

"That is me and four other operators in this city that I know of."

She let that stand in the yard for about four seconds.

"So the ninth of September is mine because on the ninth of September I decide whether my map goes in a drawer or on a table." She looked at him. "That is the whole of it and I have never told anybody and I am telling you at half past eleven at night in the rain because on the thirteenth of this month I am going to do something and you are going to be the one it is about."

He had been in this cab four times.

"The district's cold minutes," he said.

"The district's cold minutes. All of them. A year of them and the four before that, and the whole of it in one exercise book, and it goes to you on Saturday." She did not move. "There is no condition on it. That is the thing I want you to hear before anything else, so I am going to say it in the first four words: there is no condition on it."

"The condition died on the twenty-eighth."

"The condition died on the twenty-eighth and I am not pretending it was anything else." She took the book out from under her coat and did not give it to him.

"Spend them on people and not on yourself. And he spent them on himself in a square in the winter in front of sixty people, and I was in that square, and I did not go up and say a word."

She held the book against herself.

"That was the worst thing I have ever done and I have never said so out loud to anybody, and I am saying it out loud at half past eleven at night on the twelfth of a month."

He did not say anything, because there was nothing to say to a woman who has carried a thing for four months because it was hers to carry.

"It is not forgiven," she said. "It is not collected, because collecting it would mean I had forgiven it and asked for something, and I am not asking you for anything ever again, and you can put that in your book and it is not a figure."

"It is unpaid and uncollected and it is not forgiven, and it will be that on the twenty-ninth and on the ninth of September, and neither of those is a thing you can do anything about."

"Why now."

"Because you are going into a room on the twenty-ninth where the only thing you can move moves nothing, and where everything you have ever been able to give away costs a night of sleep and a grey temple for asking." She put the book down on the control ledge.

"I put a condition on that in April in a cab, and the condition has been dead since the twenty-eighth of last month, and I have watched you work out what you are going to do in that room."

"And I am going to be asked for my map in September by a warm room, and the only person who is ever going to be in a position to make it worth my handing over is you." She looked at him. "So I am handing it over first, at midnight, for nothing, with no condition, because I want the ninth of September to still be mine afterwards."

He looked at the book.

"You are giving me the thing you would be asked for."

"I am giving you the thing I would be asked for," said Beatrix Halm. "On the ninth of September a clerk in a warm room is going to ask my division for a year of my handwriting on a map, and I am going to have to say it is gone."

She took her hands off the ledge.

"I am going to say it in front of a man with a form, and there is no version of that in which a clerk believes an operator has lost a year of her own work in her own hand."

"You could tell him you handed it to a hearing."

"I could tell him I handed it to a hearing, and that sentence goes on a form with my name on it in a warm room in about three weeks, and then there is an office holding a district's cold minutes by district. I have spent nine years making sure there is not."

She squared the book on the ledge.

"So I am going to lose five years of work on purpose, and I am going to lose it in a way that makes me look careless instead of principled, and that is the price and I have costed it and I am not going to discuss it."

"Why."

"Because you have been carrying a hundred and twelve faces in a drawer for four years," said Beatrix Halm, "and not one of them has ever been told anything by you, and that is the only unfairness in this house, and it is yours, and I am the only person in it who can hand you the other half of the arithmetic and hand it to you for nothing."

The rain went on the roof of the cab and the yard did what a yard does.

"You will get it wrong," she said. "You will put it in a drawer and you will take it out in four months when something goes wrong in a corridor, and you will look at it the way you look at a face."

She put her finger on the cover.

"I am telling you now that a year of cold minutes by district is not a list of people. It is a map of when this city goes cold. If you use it like a book of names then you will have taken five years of my work and used it as a way of finding out who owes you something."

"Beatrix—"

"And you will not do that," she said, "because you are frightened of it, and because it is the only thing in this house you have ever been frightened of, and I have watched you for four months and I know it." She picked the book up. "That is not a favour either. It is me telling you what I know about you because you are about to be handed five years of my work and I would like it to be handed over honestly."

She gave it to him then, and it was heavier than a book of that size ought to be, and it had been in a coat.

The door was open and the yard noise came in and she did not shut it, and she stood with her hands on her coat looking at him the way she had looked at him in the spring.

"Six days I have had it in my hand and no condition," she said. "And I want you to know that not having one is the hardest thing I have done since the square, and I did it because I am not giving you a rule for the twenty-ninth. I am giving you the September, and I am taking the rule back, and I do not care what you do with it."

He got home up the long road at half past midnight with an exercise book in his coat against two slips of paper and a notice on a district frame. He did not open it and he did not sleep.

At about four o'clock in the morning he got up and put it on the shelf above the sink, under four true sentences and a sheet of ruled paper with the hours on it. That shelf in that kitchen had become, in five days, the only place in the city where anything about him had been written down by anybody but himself.

Seventeen days. Seventeen less the twelfth, and both in this month.

The broken price was not collected and was not forgiven and had been named out loud for the first time in four months by the only person entitled to name it, and a woman had put five years of her own work into his hands at midnight in the rain with no condition attached.

The ninth of September was not this month and it was still hers.

She had told him she was not forgiving it.

She had also told him, in a cab with the door open and the yard coming in, that the one thing she wanted for the twenty-ninth was not a rule and not a place at a rail and not a hand on his arm.

It was that somebody in that hall who had read a notice on a frame should not find out at ten o'clock that the man at the table was going to stand still for four hours.

He had been told the same thing by four women in one week, and the fifth one had come at half past eleven at night in the rain and put a year of a city's cold into his coat, and none of them had asked him for anything, and that was the first time in four months that the shape of it had reversed.

# The Woman Who Will Read It

The hall on Cobb Street on the fourteenth of the month had a hundred and forty seats in it and a rail and ninety places standing behind the back row, and the works department rated it at two hundred and thirty on a plate by the door.

There were nineteen people in it, and all nineteen were there because Tove Marlin had asked them. She had gone down the list herself, which was the thing he understood in about four seconds on the stair, and which was what made the room worse than the empty hall on the eleventh.

"Item one is a Field minute and the officer who made it is a party, and a serving officer cannot be in the room," she said. "Item two is a visitors' book and the woman who keeps it has no reason to be here. Item three is four lines from a clinic, and the clinic has a doctor in it who has already refused twice on paper, and I am not putting her in a chair fifteen days early."

"I did not ask for this."

"You asked me on the tenth for the roll to be read into a room with a clerk in it before the day. That is the procedure. The member is entitled to be present at the reading of every item on the roll, and so is anybody the reading is about."

She said that last part without any weight at all, and he understood that she had not been arguing with him for four days about whether to do this.

"So you asked them."

"They are not obliged to come. Two of them were told by me what it was going to sound like, and one of them asked me for the number of minutes, and one of them did not ask me anything and is sitting in the third rank because it is the third rank."

She put the sheets down on the table.

"I read this roll once for you alone on the tenth, and once for a serving officer in an empty hall on the eleventh. I am not doing it a third time for a member and a clerk."

Not one person in the building apologised in it.

She had said the words on the seventh of last month in a corridor and again on the tenth, and on the fourteenth she began to say them in front of nineteen people and stopped about four words in, and put her hand flat on the table instead.

"I am not going to say that," she said. "I said it twice on my own this week and both times it was for me and not for the room, and there are nineteen people in front of me who came here to be told what a document says and not to be managed by a clerk's feelings about a clerk's room."

Nadia Ferrous was in the second rank with her hands in her lap. Tessa Okonkwo was at the wall on the aisle side. Ola Bright was in the third row with a pad on her knee, because Ola Bright comes to everything and writes it all down. Marguerite Vale was at the end of the front row with her hands folded. Beatrix Halm was standing at the back by the rail, because Beatrix Halm does not sit in rooms.

Lin Hao stood in the place parties stand, which was four chairs behind a rail on the left of the bench, and did not sit in any of them.

She read the roll.

Nine items, exactly as written, in the order the hall would hear them, at the speed of a woman reading a form. The Field minute, six lines, and the four seconds of silence where a seventh name would have been, and she stood in the silence on the fourteenth exactly as she had stood in it on the eleventh.

The visitors' book. Eighty-nine entries, twenty-nine asking about a person, thirteen of the twenty-nine asking about somebody in the subject's household.

Marguerite Vale did not move. She had a hundred and six in the book in Bellhouse and thirty-eight and sixteen, and she had read both sets out loud at her own table every week since the first.

The hall was going to say eighty-nine and twenty-nine and thirteen. She had known it since the thirteenth of last month and had not said one word about it in this room, because the exhibit sheet is the exhibit sheet.

The clinic's four lines, and the last line of them, and the readings are not produced.

Tessa Okonkwo looked at the floor for about a second and then at the bench, and did not look at the wall.

Item four was shut and wore an objection on a string.

Then item five.

Nadia Ferrous did not move at all. Forty-one names went into a hall on Cobb Street at about twenty past ten on the fourteenth of the month, in the voice of a woman reading a form, at about four hundred and twenty seconds' worth of time, which is seven minutes.

There were women in it. Tove Marlin had found her place the second time as well and had not paused.

Tove Marlin stopped at the end of it with her finger on the number at the head of the third column.

"Forty-one," she said. "And that is a figure and not a person, and every one of them is somebody, and I have read it twice now in two rooms and I am going to read it a third time on the morning of the twenty-ninth in front of whatever is standing at the back."

She did not look up from the page.

"I will not be able to do anything different, and I would like that said while it is still the fourteenth."

The room was very quiet.

"That is not an apology," said Nadia Ferrous from the second rank, in an ordinary voice.

"It is not," said Tove Marlin. "It is a notice, and it is the only thing I am entitled to give, and I have thought about it for four days. A notice is worth more to that woman than an apology would be, because an apology is about me."

She went on. The district table with no clerk's name. The discharged standing and the five dates and the fourth floor. His own refusal of the seventeenth and the eighteenth, six lines in a clerk's hand, and Tove Marlin read his own sentence back to the room in the voice she read forms in.

And then she stopped, and squared the sheets, and did not go on, and there was a silence in a municipal hall with nineteen people in it that lasted about four seconds.

"Item eight is the member's own refusal, written by me on the seventeenth and returned on the eighteenth. It is the only document on this roll produced by a person in this room rather than by an office."

She put her hand down flat.

"It is on this roll because a member who refuses a demand in the correct words on the correct day has produced the truest document in this building, and there is not one Office in this city that can read it and not draw the wrong conclusion out of it."

That was Tove Marlin saying the thing she had said on the seventh of last month in a corridor, in a hall, to nineteen people, and not one of them had known she was going to.

Then she read item nine, which was a photograph of a ruled space on a page of a register, ruled and empty and empty on purpose for a year and a half, and she closed it.

Fifteen days. Fifteen less the fourteenth, and both in this month, and the hall rated at two hundred and thirty with nineteen people in it and a clerk with a good voice who had read a whole household out loud in it.

He got her at the table before the others were out of the seats.

"You read it in this order on purpose."

"I read it in the order the hall will hear it, which is the order the Office will put it, and that is the only order that is honest." She squared the sheets. "Did you want a different one."

"No. I want one thing and I have been not asking you for it since Friday."

"Say it."

He said it.

"In your day book, in your own hand, as a clerk's record of a member's statement and not as an Office document: that on the twenty-ninth of this month I will be in that hall from ten until two, that I intend to touch no person in it, that I intend to move nothing on any person in it, and that I am telling the room in advance so that not one person at the rail is reached for."

Tove Marlin took her pen out and put it on the table and did not pick it up.

"That is lawful," she said, "and it is the single most dangerous thing anybody in this proceeding could do, and I am going to tell you both halves before I write a word of it."

"The lawful half first," he said.

"You may say what you like in your own time and a hearing officer must record it. If you say it from the floor on the day, it goes into the transcript in the Office's voice, and it is true, and it is signed by two clerks."

She turned the pen over twice.

"The other half: in advance, in a clerk's own book, dated, it is a document. And a document in advance is a thing the other side can prepare for." She put the pen down flat. "Every person in that hall will have read it. Every person in that hall will know what your hands are doing four hours before they do it, and the hearing officer will have to rule on it, and he will rule on it, because it is on the record and he cannot leave it there."

"Then do not write it."

"I am going to write it," said Tove Marlin, "and I have not got to, and here is why I am."

She put her hand flat on the sheets.

"Because you are going to say it on the day anyway, in the same room, to the same two hundred and eleven people, four hours later and in far less control of it. And because a thing announced into an empty hall is not a thing that protects anybody."

She looked up.

"It protects nineteen people in the third row. It will protect two hundred and eleven on the morning. That is the whole of my reasoning and it is not sound, and I am a clerk, and I am going to do it anyway."

She picked the pen up.

"Before I write it," she said, "who is going to hold it."

He had been ready for three days and it still took him a second.

"That is not a question I expected from a clerk."

"It is the only question in the room that matters, and there are nineteen people in this building who have already worked it out, and one woman on a landing who worked it out on the fifth of the month."

She had the nib on the paper.

"An announcement is a speech, and a speech is worth what the room is worth. You have been in that room once in your life and you are not going to be alone in it."

"Who do I name."

"Nobody names anybody in a document," said Tove Marlin. "A document says what a member states, and if a person is going to be holding the hours then the document says so, in the plainest words I am able to write, and it goes in the book with my hand on it, and it goes to the hearing officer, and it cannot be withdrawn by you or by them."

That was the moment, and it lasted about two seconds, and it was the whole of the last thing he had.

He had spent four months making sure that nothing about him was written down by anybody. He had a hundred and twelve lines in a drawer that was not a document.

He had a shelf in a kitchen above a sink with four true sentences on it, and a woman with a pad, and an exercise book, and a sheet of ruled paper with his hours on it. Every one of those was a thing somebody else held. Every one of them could have gone back in a drawer in four minutes, and none of them had.

There was no longer a version of this in which the record of him was his own.

"Nadia Ferrous," he said. "Ola Bright. Beatrix Halm. And you."

Tove Marlin wrote for about forty seconds and then turned the book round.

"'The member states that on the twenty-ninth of this month he will be in the hall from ten until two, that he intends to touch no person in it, and that he intends to move nothing on any person in it. He states that he makes this statement in advance so that no person in the room is put in a position of reaching him. He states that the hours are held by Nadia Ferrous, Ola Bright and Beatrix Halm, and by this clerk.'"

"That is exact," she said. "And then I am going to date it, and you are not going to like what I am going to tell you first."

"Tell me."

"You have made the four hours shared, in a clerk's book, with a date on it, and it cannot be withdrawn by the person it names."

She was not looking at him.

"An hour that one man holds is a thing he can change at ten past ten with a glass of water. An hour that four people hold is a thing that has been agreed to by people who are not you."

She capped the pen.

"It is four people who have each already agreed to a sentence, standing in a hall, watching you not do it."

He looked at the book.

"That is the price," he said.

"That is the price and you knew it and you walked in here and asked me for it in front of nineteen people," said Tove Marlin, "which is the only part of this that I will not be able to stop thinking about for a very long time."

She dated it the fourteenth and signed her own name under it, which is a thing she does, and blotted it, and shut the day book.

Then she said the thing she had said on the seventh of last month in a corridor and on the tenth in a room with a clerk in it, and had begun to say that afternoon in front of nineteen people and stopped four words into.

She said it now, out loud, to the empty part of the room behind the bench.

"I am sorry about the room."

Fifteen days. The twenty-ninth less the fourteenth, and both in this month, and a hall that will hold two hundred and eleven and is rated at two hundred and thirty, and a bench with three chairs on it and a table below it with a cloth that has been down since eight in the morning, and a clerk's day book shut on a table with the four hours in it in four hands and a date.

He walked out of a municipal hall on Cobb Street at half past eleven on the fourteenth of the month with nothing in his coat except a slip of bench paper and a sheet with a builder's handwriting on it that said a thing he had not asked for.

He had told six women at a table on the fifth, a builder in a workroom on the eighth, a woman who copies things on the ninth, and a clerk on the fourteenth. And he had not told a serving officer in a corridor, because she had asked him not to, and she was right to.

He had put it in a book with a date. He could not take it out, and neither could the Office, and in fifteen days he would walk into a room at ten o'clock with a clerk's sentence in it and four women who had each agreed to it separately and in advance, and every person at that rail would have had two weeks to read about his hands.

The last private thing he had was the right to change his mind, and he had given it to four people at a table on Cobb Street, in front of nineteen witnesses, in exchange for the four hours.

It was not a trade he had ever been offered before. It was the first one in five months where both sides of it were true.

# A Reason To Be Measured

Ines Duarte had her table against the wall because the other two rooms were for people who came, and on the second of the month she had cleared about two feet at the near end of it and put a chair out from under it, and there was a sheet face up on the two feet she had cleared with her own hand flat on top of it, which is a thing she had done for eleven years and had never once done for a document.

"Sit down," she said. "Do not take your coat off, I am not going to be long, and if you take your coat off you will settle in."

He sat down.

She read it in about ninety seconds. She did not read it as a document. She read it the way she read everything that came through that door, with her head slightly to one side and her hand moving down the page ahead of her eye, and she got to the numbered paragraph and stopped there and read that one twice, and then she turned the sheet round on the table so it faced him and squared the edges with two fingers.

She had the May clipboard face down under the book at her elbow and it stayed there and neither of them put a hand near it.

"First, what I am not going to do," she said. "I am not going to ask you whether you are frightened, and I am not going to tell you that it will be all right, and I am not going to be kind at you, because you have had four months of kind and every single piece of it came out of a room where somebody felt sorry for you."

"I have not come for that."

"Then I do not know what you have come for and I have decided not to ask, which is a courtesy and costs me something, so use it." She took her hand off the sheet. "Read paragraph three to me. Out loud. You have not said it out loud and you are going to be asked to say it out loud in a room with a hundred and forty seats in it, and there is no version of this where you have not practised the shape of it."

He read it out loud.

"That is sixteen words," said Ines Duarte, "and there is nothing in them you can argue with, and I want you to understand in the next four seconds that this is the entire trouble with your life and it arrived this morning on the back of a monthly return."

"What is the trouble."

"The Office has gone looking for a procedure." She turned the sheet back and put it in front of her and read the numbered paragraph again, which she did not need to do. "Not a person. A procedure. Somebody three floors over a building on Cobb Street opened a drawer a fortnight ago and could not find a form that would close an inquiry with an unsigned paper on a clerk's desk, and so they went and found a form that does close one."

"What is that form."

"It is not that." She said it quite flatly. "It is a procedure that fits every case where the office cannot get a description, and it is fair, and it is the correct question, and the answer has already been given by law and it is no. It is not a trap and I am not going to call it a trap, because a trap has a lie in it somewhere and there is not one here.

"The answer is no because an account that nobody can describe cannot be administered by anybody. That is the whole of it. It is not that they want it. It is that a thing nobody can administer is not a thing, and this city has spent twenty years on the proposition that everything inside it is administered, and you are the first case in a long while of a man's account being real and unreadable at the same time."

"So they want a sentence," he said.

"They want a sentence in their hand and yours inside it." She let that sit. "And here is what is underneath the question, because you will be asked it in a room and you will not have had it from me. The answer being no does not close your inquiry. It is the reason the inquiry exists."

"A member who says no is a member who has said no, and that is a fact about a member, and a fact about a member is the only thing this building has ever wanted."

He put his hand on the table, not on the sheet.

"The Office does not care what it is."

"The Office has stopped caring what it is, which is a different thing and I want you to hold both of them, because you have spent four months assuming the first and building everything on it." Ines pulled the four-sided sheet out of her coat and did not open it. "They care about the sentence. Not the account. The account does not matter to the procedure at all, which is why the procedure is correct, and which is why it is going to run without either of us."

He looked at the flat thing in her hand.

"Is that what I think it is."

"It is four pages folded in quarters and it is the same four pages I had on the twelfth and it is not going into a file, and I am not going to tell you what is on it, and I want you to leave it alone." She put it back inside her coat. "You may ask me one question about it per visit and you have spent it on the way in."

"Then tell me the other thing."

"There is no other thing. I am going to read this notice out loud to you now and then I am going to say four things and then I am going to stop, and you are not going to like any of the four and none of them is a kindness."

She read the notice out loud. It took her forty seconds and she read it as it was written, including a sentence in the second paragraph with eleven words in it that did no work at all, and when she came to the line about withdrawal before publication she stopped on it.

"That is the only door in that paper," she said, "and it shuts tomorrow, when it goes on the district board in the ordinary way with the Exchange hours and a lost parcel, and it does not shut because you object to it. It shuts because of what it is once it is up. You cannot withdraw a thing that names you. You can only ask somebody not to say it, and there is not one person in this city who is obliged to hear the ask."

"And the date."

"The date is on it and I have checked it twice against the board calendar because I do not trust a date anybody hands me." She said it exactly. "It sits at ten in the morning at the top of the Exchange on Cobb Street, in a hall that belongs to the works department and that I have been in twice. It is not a courtroom."

"It has a hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety standing behind the back row and a plate by the door rating it at two hundred and thirty, and that plate is the only thing in that room anybody in it is entitled to trust."

"A man who was not interested could not get in, and there is no door on it for one, and the Office does not have the power to keep anybody out either." She said it in the voice she used for figures. "That is not a threat, it is a schedule. A hall is a room anybody may walk into, and every person who walks into it is a person somebody has to write down."

He looked at the sheet on the table.

"You said you were going to say four things."

"I said four things. This is the first, and it is that the answer is no before the hearing and no after it, and the hearing is not a place where anybody finds anything out." She squared the sheet.

"The second is that you have fifty-eight days and every one of them is a day somebody spends on you, and a notice does not stop working because a man is deciding what to do about it. Three desks are already turning. Somebody three floors over a building on Cobb Street went back to a list yesterday and found your name on it."

"That is not the list from the eighteenth of last month. I have asked about it once and been told it is nothing, which is an answer that means the opposite of what it says."

"And the third."

"The third is worse and I am telling you because you came to me and not to a corridor." She put her hands flat on the table on either side of the sheet, which was a thing he had learned in this room from somebody else.

"There is a form. It comes with the notice and it has not come yet, because they do not send a thing and a form in one envelope. They send them apart, so that the second one arrives after you have had a night to stop expecting it."

She looked up. "The form asks you for a reason. In your own words. In one paragraph. Why the account should be permitted to remain undescribed."

"And I will write one."

"You will not write one, and I want to tell you why before you have spent four nights on it." She looked up. "A reason for leaving an account alone is a description of it. Every sentence you can put on that paper tells somebody what the account does, how it came to be there, or what it is for, and all three of those are the answer to their question."

"The only part of that form which is not an answer is the part where you write nothing and they have to write something instead."

"So the form is a hole with a question painted round it."

"The form is a hole with a question painted round it, and it has been printed since before I came to that building and nobody has ever been clever about it, and the number of people who have filled it in honestly is the number of people who are now in files with a sentence in their own words at the top of them." She let go of the table. "That is the third thing and it is the only one I would have crossed the city to tell you."

"And the fourth."

Ines Duarte was quiet for about three seconds, and he understood that she was choosing between two things and that the choice was hers and was not going to be about him at all.

"The fourth is that I cannot help you," she said, "and I am not going to pretend that I cannot help you in order to be the kind of person you came here hoping to find, because you would spend the next fifty-eight days waiting for me to do it and the waiting would be worse than the hall.

"I read every record that passes this table and I have read yours, all of it, since the third of last month. I can tell you the day every paper about you was written, which office wrote it, and the two names that are on every one of them. I can do that in about ninety seconds and it is worth nothing to you."

"The room is not short of paper. The room is short of somebody who will say a sentence out loud in front of a hundred strangers and stand in the room while they write it down in their own words."

"There is one of those people in this city and she has a card on her table and she is sitting opposite you and she is going to do it in her own time or not at all, and I am telling you so that you stop waiting for me to arrive at the other kind of help before the twenty-ninth."

He stood up.

"Sit down," said Ines. "You have not asked me the thing you came for and I would rather you went out of here having said it, because you will not be able to say it to anybody else."

He sat down.

"How do I make a hundred and forty strangers stop looking at me."

"That is not a question anybody has ever answered, and it is not the question, and I am not going to pretend it is." She turned the sheet round again and squared it. "The question is whether you will let them look. Everything else is a version of that question wearing a hat, including the form, and including the refusal, and including the four months you have spent building a shape that was exactly legible in the one respect that cost nothing and invisible in every other.

"You have been invisible for a fortnight and it has worked, and it has cost you a floor, a standing, a calendar, three women who have files because of you, and one woman who has said no to her own house in writing and cannot be written down.

"And in fifty-eight days it stops working, because the way they have found you is not a mistake somebody made. It is the procedure doing exactly what it says."

She put two fingers on the numbered paragraph and left them there.

"You cannot win this one, and I want that said in a room with a lamp on it and not in a corridor, because you are going to spend fifty-eight days trying to win it and everybody in your house is going to tell you that you are close." She took her fingers off the paper.

"You are not close. And I am not going to tell you what to do about it, because there is nothing on this page that lets me, and because you have never once in your life done the thing that is printed in front of you, and this is the first time the thing printed in front of you is not a lie."

"Everything that happens next happens in front of people. That is the whole of what I have. Take the sheet with you and read paragraph three on the stairs, because you will want to be holding it when you do."

He folded it once, and put it in the inside pocket against the plate, and left the four books at the far end of the table where she had put them, and she had not moved a single one of them, and neither had he.

# The Lift Log Comes Up Again

Tove Marlin had the third-floor room on Cobb Street from ten on the tenth with a clerk in it from the Office, and the clerk had two sheets and a fountain pen and the look of a man who had been told to write down exactly what he heard.

"You have asked for this twice since Friday," she said, before he had his coat off.

"Once. On Friday and once on Saturday and the second time you were in a corridor."

"I was in a corridor on Friday and you asked me on Saturday at a counter, and I have been in this room since half past nine preparing for it, and I want to say at the start of this morning that I did not have to do it and I did it because you asked and because I would rather read it than have somebody else read it badly."

She put her hand on the pad in front of the clerk.

"This is the dry reading. Every item goes into the transcript in advance, in this room, in front of a clerk, so that the member may object to any of it before the day. That is the procedure and it is the reason I have a room and not a corridor." She squared it. "You understand what it means."

"I understand what it means," said Lin Hao.

"Then sit down, because item five takes seven minutes and I have never read it out loud in a room before and I am going to read it exactly as it is written, including the part where the number is wrong, and when I have finished you are going to tell me whether I was right, and you are not going to be able to un-hear it."

She read the first four and he did not listen to any of them.

Item five was a delivery record from a service in this district for the week ending the tenth of last month, produced by the holder in answer to a request, carrying forty-one names in the third column.

Nadia Ferrous had handed it over on purpose, in a lift, on the tenth. She had worked it out in about four seconds and had said, ever since, that if it was used the first thing that happens was to her and not to him.

Tove Marlin read the forty-one names out loud in a room on Cobb Street with a clerk in it, at ten in the morning on the tenth, in the voice of a woman reading a form.

There were women in the list. She had not known there would be women in the list, and she had to stop for a second in the middle of it and find her place again, and she found her place without a pause that anybody would have called a pause.

When she had finished she put a finger on the number at the head of the third column.

"Forty-one," she said. "The exhibit sheet says forty-one. The sheet also says the holder produced it in answer to a request, which is true, and it says the service in question is a district lift service, which is true, and it does not say which one, and there are four in this district, and I have looked at all four."

"Have you asked."

"I have not asked any of them," said Tove Marlin. "I am a clerk and I do not ask. What I have done is read four services' weekly records against a number and put a bracket round one of them, and that is not evidence and it is not a question and it is not going any further until somebody with authority sends a letter, and the letter will take eleven days because the Office's own rule is that a service's delivery record is asked for in writing."

She looked up.

"That is the whole of what is going to happen and I am telling you it before it happens rather than after, because the after is the part where people find out things from a document. And I am sorry about the room, and I have been in this building eleven years and this is the second time I have been sorry about a room, and I am not going to pretend it is the room's fault."

The clerk wrote for about six seconds and then stopped and looked at her, and she looked back at him until he started again.

"That is the promise cashed," said Tove Marlin, "and I would like it noticed that I said the words exactly as I said them on the seventh of last month, because the only kindness available to a clerk is to be identical on every occasion."

"You read her names in a room and apologised for the room," he said.

"I read her names in a room because I am obliged to read them in a room, and I apologised for the room because the room is mine and the names are not." She squared the sheets. "I did it in this order on purpose. If I apologise afterwards it is about the names. If I apologise first it is about the room. There is a difference and it took me eleven years to learn it and it is the only thing I have."

He went back to the branch at half past eleven and cleaned four floors, and at two o'clock in the afternoon Nadia Ferrous came down to the end of the second-floor queue at her lunch hour in a coat over her work clothes and stood at the sill beside him and looked at the socket for a while.

"Forty-one," she said.

"Forty-one."

"Read out loud."

"In a room, with a clerk in it, by the woman who reads things exactly as written." He worked the mop round the queue the way he always did. "You were in the list."

"I know I was in the list. I have been in the list since the tenth of last month and the only difference about this morning is that the list has stopped being a piece of paper and become a voice." She put her hands in her pockets. "How long did it take."

"Seven minutes," he said. "She said so before she started, and she told me she had never read it out loud in a room before today."

"That is four hundred and twenty seconds," said Nadia Ferrous, "and every one of them was somebody who rode my car between the Vestry and the arcade between the second and the ninth of last month and did not know my name. I wrote all of them down because I write every one of them down, and I have written every one of them down for two years, and the reason I keep the log is not because the Office asked for it."

"Why do you keep it."

"Because on a Tuesday in the spring I put an old woman off at the arcade when the car was full and she did not thank me, and I wrote her down." She looked at the socket. "I did not write her down because she was a name. I wrote her down because I wanted to know whether I would have got to her if I had been quicker."

"That is not a thing you put in a document."

"And I put forty-one names in a document on the tenth of last month to a stranger in a car, because a man I had been in a lift with said his business out loud in an ordinary voice, and I decided in about four seconds that I would rather be the one who handed it over than the one who had it taken."

"You did the right thing."

"I did the thing that was mine," said Nadia Ferrous. "I want that in whatever you are keeping, because you have started something this week and I do not know what it is yet and I can see you doing it in this corridor with a mop in your hand."

Then she told him the rest of it, standing at the sill, in the queue's hearing, because she had decided to and he understood about a second later that she had decided to because it was the only version in which it did not happen in a corridor.

"The service has had a letter," she said. "Two days ago. A clerk wrote to the manager and asked him to identify the operator who produced a delivery record for the week ending the tenth of last month in answer to a request."

It had not come the way Tove Marlin said it would, which was in writing and slowly. Somebody in Cobb Street had gone at it another way, and a woman reading a delivery record had been found in eight days by people who did not write down why they had looked.

She looked down the queue.

"The manager put it in my hand at the change of a shift with nine other people in the cab. He was not unkind about it and he was not cruel about it, and he did it in front of nine other people because that is where he was standing."

"Nadia."

"That is the part that is mine, and you do not get to make it better and you do not get to say anything about it at the branch."

She took her hands out of her pockets and put them flat on the sill, the way she did at a table.

"I answered him in writing in my own hand in front of nine other people. My answer is on the service's file, and the service's file goes to the Exchange counter on a Friday, and the Exchange keeps them for a year and a half."

"You should have refused to answer."

"I should have refused to answer and I did not, because if I had refused then the only copy of that letter in the world would be in a clerk's drawer in Cobb Street and there would be no version of it with my handwriting on it." She said it flatly. "That is the whole of what I have learned from you in four months and I have not thanked you for it and I am not going to, because a thing that is true is not a favour."

He worked the queue for another twenty minutes and said nothing else about it, because she had told him at the sill not to and she was right.

Nineteen days. Nineteen less the tenth, and both in this month.

The thing that happened to Nadia Ferrous afterwards was in a car with nine other operators in it and on a file that goes to the Exchange counter on Fridays, and it was not the hall and it was not the roll and it was not a clerk with a good voice.

It was a woman who wrote down every name that came through her car because she wanted to know whether she would have got to somebody sooner, handing forty-one of them to an office on purpose in four seconds in a lift, and then being asked for her name in front of nine people who work with her.

And the record carried his request into a hall, and the request had his name at the top of it in Tove Marlin's hand, and there was not a line anywhere in any of it that said she had chosen it.

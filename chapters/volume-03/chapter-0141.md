# What She Will Not Read

The sheet of four lines came back at four o'clock on the fourteenth and Ola Bright put it in the middle of the hall table under the salt, squared to the edge, in her own hand, and stood there while six women read it.

Then on the morning of the fifteenth, before the shift, in a wet coat, she made him stand at that table and say it out loud.

"That is what you said you would make me do," she said. "On the ninth. You are not getting away with the other half of it for nothing."

He said it. That she had held four hours of his life in her own handwriting for five days and had handed the page back on the day she said she would and not a day sooner, and had not put it in the stove, and had made him tell the table before she would let anybody else read a word of the day.

Six women at the table and the landing light on above them and the notebook shut where it always lay. He cleaned five floors and the fourth, and the branch was full by the time he came out on the second, and at nine o'clock the Board repainted four hundred yards away in the wet for two thousand people who had somewhere to be.

Ines Duarte had asked him to come on a Thursday, and he had understood why inside about four seconds of being told, and had not liked it and had gone.

"Because it is the day this city prints something," she said, at half past seven in the evening, in the two rooms on Lantern Row with her coat still on. "Everything you have done for four months happened on a day when nothing was printed. I want what I am going to ask you for to be on a piece of paper in this city before the next one of these days comes round."

She had a copy of the roll on the table. Not the roll itself, the copy, four sheets stapled at the corner, that a party is entitled to.

"Sit down. I have been in this room since four and I have read that thing three times and I have not decided anything, and what I am going to say to you I am going to say in the first four sentences, because I have watched you learn to do that in a month and I am not going to reward it."

He sat down.

"I am asking to read the fourth item aloud in a public hall on the morning of the twenty-ninth."

He waited for the rest of it and there was no rest of it.

"You lodged an objection on the eighth. Item four, the household record, excluded from being read aloud. It is the only exclusion on that roll. Eight items are going into a room that holds two hundred and eleven people and one of them is shut.

You know exactly what a shut box looks like to a clerk and to a hearing officer, and so do I, and so does every person in that hall who has read a notice with one numbered paragraph on it since the third of the month."

"You want me to lift the objection."

"I want to read it," said Ines Duarte. "Those are different and you have had four months to learn the difference and you still make it in about two seconds."

She put her hand flat on the four sheets, which she did with everything.

"Lift the objection and it goes into a hall. Do not lift it and it goes into a hall anyway, because a shut box on a proved roll is a shape, and the shape is six women and a building above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, and every person in Ostara who has ever read anything about that house can now put a floor under it."

"That is the argument for reading it."

"That is the argument for me reading it," she said. "Not for it being read. For it being read by the one person in this city who can hold a record in her head and knows the moment it has been touched."

The lamp on the desk was the only lamp in the room and it made the files on the table behind her into a grey wall.

"I have read four hundred files," she said. "I know what happens to a document about eleven minutes after an office gets it. It stops being what it says and becomes what it is about. A page of days, read aloud in a room, with six different handwritings on it, becomes six named people inside about forty seconds, and then the only question anybody has is where they sleep, and you know that, because you have spent four months making sure not one person knows where six of them sleep."

"I know it."

"So here is the offer, and I am making it properly." She turned in her chair to face him square, which she had not done since the seventeenth of last month.

"I will stand at that rail and I will read six lines a day, one woman to a line, from the twenty-first of last month to the morning of the twenty-ninth, in the order they were written, and I will read every word of it exactly as it stands.

"Including the days where a line says a woman was on a landing and did not come down, and including the fact that the book carries no signature, because the absence is in the document and I am not going to read round it."

"And while you read it you will say this."

"I will say, in the clerk's own formula, at the end of each woman's line for the whole of the month, that the line records a day's location and not a name, and that no signature is attached to any of it, and that the Office has no power to attach one." She put her thumb on the sheet.

"Six times. Once a woman. And a hearing officer has to sit there and let a rank-two auditor tell his own hall what a list of names is.

"And every person in the third rank who has ever been in a lift with the woman being read finds out at that moment that the woman being read cannot be described by the hall."

"You think that stops them."

"I think it stops them for about four minutes," said Ines Duarte, "and four minutes is what I have been selling all evening."

She let him sit with it, which she had never done before, and then she said the part she had come to say.

"What it costs is me, and I am going to price it before you are allowed to."

She turned round and moved one file a half inch to the left without looking at it.

"My review is in the second week of next month. It has been in the second week of next month since the spring and it has not moved and I have not let it move, and on the morning of the twenty-ninth it is going to acquire a date, because a member's witness is a person on the record of a proceeding and the proceeding is a proceeding I am in."

"You are the member's witness."

"You already named Wren Kallo on the nineteenth of last month in your own hand on the form the hearing officer keeps," she said.

"A hearing allows one witness. You have used it on the builder of the instrument, and the builder has to stand there and say what a machine does.

"So I am not a witness. I am a reader, and a reader is appointed, and an appointed reader goes in the room's own list with a name on it, and that list goes on the file of the proceeding."

"So you are on the file."

"I am on the file in a way that a review cannot ignore," said Ines Duarte, "and there is no version in which a woman reads the worst thing in that hall and comes out of it with a district office behind her. I have been told what that costs in one number and I do not intend to be told it twice."

"You should not be doing this."

"I have refused twice." She said it flatly. "In March and again in May, and both refusals are in my own hand and neither of them is wrong, and I have not reversed either of them and I am not reversing them tonight. This is a third thing and it is smaller than the other two and it is the only one of the three I am not frightened of."

"Why."

"Because a refusal is a thing you do with a file," said Ines. "This is a thing you do with a mouth."

She let that sit, and then she said the other half, and she said it without any preparation at all.

"Now the other half, and then you can decide, and I want both halves before you decide."

"What."

"There is one paper in this city I have read and have not filed, and I am not going to read it in a hall, and I am not going to tell you in this room what is written on it." She turned back to the table and squared the four sheets against the edge.

"That is the whole of what you are entitled to know about it from me tonight and it is all you are getting. You are not going to ask me. I am saying so now so that you cannot spend an hour deciding whether to."

He had spent about two seconds deciding whether to and put it down.

"And I am not going to file anything," she said. "Not the sentence I am about to ask you for. Not tonight, not in a week, not in a month. Whatever you hand me in this room stays in this room and in my hands and does not go into a district, a file, a box, a tray, a roll or a corridor, and if you ever want it back you will have to ask me for it in person and I will decide."

"That is the first thing you have asked me for since March."

"It is the only thing I have ever asked you for," said Ines Duarte. "Everybody else in your house has wanted something from you for four months and has been refused, and you have let them be refused, and it has made you the only man in Ostara that an office cannot buy. I am not trying to buy anything. I am trying to be the voice."

She took her coat off then, on the second half of the sentence, and hung it on the back of the chair, which she had never once done in four months.

"Four terms," she said. "And then I am going to ask you for one sentence, and you are going to write it, and then we are going to have an hour."

"Go on."

"One. I am awake, I am not in anything, I have not taken anything, and I know exactly what I am doing and so do you. Two. I am going to ask you twice, and the second time is a question and not a performance, and if you answer it wrong the first time I will leave.

Three. You stop me with one word and I will stop instantly and I will not be insulted and I will not make it into a thing afterwards.

Four. You say one true sentence about yourself that you would not put in a document, before anything else happens, and I do not care what it is and I am not going to help you find it."

"That is a strange fourth term."

"It is the only one that matters and I know it is strange and I have thought about it for two days," said Ines.

"You have spent four months handing back things to people who could not ask. In March I watched you do it to a woman in a clinic. It is the only thing I have ever seen you do that is actually generous.

"And it is the reason I am asking you to do it again in a room where I am going to touch you afterwards, because I want to know whether the sentence and the touching are in the same man."

"Say the fourth one when I have said mine."

"No." She shook her head. "You say yours. I am not going to set you up."

He sat with his hands on his knees and it took him about eleven seconds, which was about four seconds longer than he had expected, because everything true he could say about himself that he had not written down was in the wrong order.

"I have never minded," he said.

She waited.

"Four years. Six floors, twice a day, and I have been scanned at the gate of my own workplace and stepped around by anybody holding a reading card for four years, and I have never once minded it, and I have been proud of it, and it is the worst thing about me and it is the reason I am any use to anybody." He stopped.

"There. That is true and I would not put it in a document, and it is the reason a hundred and twelve people in this city are owed something by a man they have never met."

Ines Duarte did not say anything kind about it.

"That is the sentence," she said. "Now write one, and it will not be that one, and it will be about the twenty-ninth."

"Where."

"On this." She pushed the back sheet of the roll across the table, the blank one at the back, which is what a party is given for notes. "In your hand. Because a sentence in your mouth is a speech and a sentence in your hand is a document, and I am asking you for a document, and it will be the only document in this proceeding that is not true because somebody wanted it to be true."

He wrote it. It took him about two minutes and she did not read it while he wrote it, and she did not ask to read it while he wrote it, and she went and stood at the window with her back to him for both of the minutes.

He turned the sheet round and pushed it back across the table.

She read it twice, which is what she did with everything, and put it down flat between them with both hands on the edges.

"That is four sentences," she said. "And the second one is going to cost you about nine seconds every time anybody in this city reads it for the rest of your life, and you wrote it on your own, at a table, in a wet coat, on the fourteenth day of a month."

"Yes."

"Then we have an hour."

She was not a woman who was quiet in a room. That had never been true of her and it was not true that evening either. She talked the entire time and not a word of it was small.

She made him stand up first and told him what she was going to do before she did any of it, and he understood about four seconds in that this was not modesty either, it was her discipline, and that she would have run the same order in a cold room with a stranger who had asked her to.

She undressed him the way she read a record, left to right, and named each thing out loud as she found it, and made him say her name once and then made him say it again because the first one had come out wrong and she wanted it right rather than fast.

The lamp made the files behind her into a grey wall and the room was small and warm and had four hundred documents in it and none of them was about this.

"Ask me," she said.

He asked her.

She said yes.

She had him on his back with her hand flat between his shoulder blades and she put her mouth against his ear and told him, without any pleasure in her voice at all, exactly what she was going to do to him and in what order, and then she did it, and he understood somewhere in the middle of it that she had done this before in rooms like this one and had never once been in a room like this one.

She was exact and she was not gentle and she did not tell him it was all right, not once, and when he got a hand on her she let him have about two seconds of it and then took it away and put both his hands flat on the mattress on either side of her and made him leave them there.

"Say it again," she said. "Not to me. Say what you said at the table."

He said it, with her sitting on him, and she made him finish it.

Then she turned him over and went at the ribs with her thumb, and found the fourth one on the left without looking for it, and put her weight on it, and the sound that came out of him went into the wall of a room with four hundred files in it.

"That is the one you have been guarding since the spring," said Ines Duarte. "It is not a habit. You have told a room you know how to stand still and you have told them nothing about that, and you have been carrying it round this city like a plate in your pocket."

She worked it for about forty seconds and he could not speak for any of it.

"It is not the season and it is not the cold and it is not a woman at a rail," she said. "It is a thing a man does to a room for four hours. You have known that since the thirtieth and you have been carrying it round as if it were weather, and the reason you have been carrying it round as if it were weather is that weather is not a decision."

She got up off him and sat on the edge of the couch and reached for her shirt and did not put it on, and put her hand flat on her own knee instead, and looked at the wall for a while.

"How long," she said.

"I do not know."

"That is the correct answer and I want it recorded that you gave it to me in one second and did not improve on it." She turned her head. "And there is a second thing, which is that nothing in this room went anywhere that the two of us did not put it, and I can tell, because I have been the only person in this city who can tell since about the eighth of last month and I have not wanted to be able to for about as long."

"You can tell."

"I can feel the shape of a thing arriving in a body from the outside." She said it the way she said a figure. "It is the only thing I am actually good at and I have never once been thanked for it and I am not going to start."

She put her shirt on, and then her coat, which she had taken off on the second half of a sentence an hour before.

"Now take that sheet," she said, "and do not let anybody see you folding it, and do not put it in the roll, because the roll is the Office's and the copy is the Office's and the only copy of that sentence in the world is in my hand."

"You are not going to file it."

"I am not going to file it," said Ines Duarte, "and I am going to stand in a room on the morning of the twenty-ninth and read a description of you that four offices wrote, and then I am going to read what you wrote on that sheet underneath it, in your own hand, and it is going to be the first sentence anybody has said in that room that the room cannot do anything with."

She put her hand flat on the four sheets and squared them.

"Fourteen days," she said. "Fourteen less the fifteenth. And you have two things to do before then and one of them is to stop arranging my week for me, because I am going to be at that rail whatever anybody tells me I cannot be, and the only thing you can do about it is to be in the room and let me read."

Fourteen days. Fourteen less the fifteenth, and both in this month, and a hall rated at two hundred and thirty that a clerk on Cobb Street was going to try to fill.

She walked him to the door of the two rooms and opened it and did not follow him out, and then she said one more thing from the doorway with her coat on.

"You told me to stop you with one word," she said. "I am not going to use it. I want that in the room with the other three, because in fourteen days you are going to be standing at a table in front of two hundred and eleven people and you are going to want somebody in the room to be able to end it, and there is going to be nobody in that room who can."

She shut the door on him.

He stood on the steps outside her door in the wet for about a minute, with a sheet of her copy of the roll in his coat against a builder's statement and a bench slip and an exercise book he had not opened, and the whole of what he had left in the world was a sentence in his own hand that he could not have taken back.

On Friday morning he was going to have to stand in a kitchen above a shop and tell six women that a woman he had refused twice had just volunteered to read their days aloud to a room full of strangers, and that she had asked him for nothing for it, and that it was hers to ask.

# He Gives It Away In Public

He cleaned six floors between six and a quarter past nine on the morning of the twenty-ninth because he had not slept and there was nothing else to do with his hands, and at the third floor a queue of eleven people stood at the socket with their plates and he took the bucket through twice and worked round the outside of it, the way he had for four years, and not one of them knew where he was going.

The sixth-floor washroom had a tap that dripped and he tightened it and it stopped, and a woman came in behind him with her plate in her hand and said thank you, and he said nothing because there was nothing to say to a woman with a plate at ten to ten in the morning.

That went into the drawer that night with the other hundred and twelve, with a street and an hour on it.

That was a hundred and fourteen.

He was at the door of the Cobb Street hall at two minutes to ten and the door was not open yet, and he stood on the step while a porter took a chain off and a clerk counted something inside, and then it came open.

Two hundred and eleven was the number on the sheet.

He knew it before the door was open because Tove Marlin had the room's own list on a card in her hand and she read it out on the step, once, to him and to the four men in front of him, in the voice she read forms in.

Seats filled one hundred and thirty-nine of one hundred and forty. Standing behind the rail, seventy-two.

The plate by the door said two hundred and thirty. The number on the card said two hundred and eleven. That leaves nineteen places in the building where a person could stand and not be counted.

"One hundred and thirty-nine seats," she said. "One empty seat."

"Where."

"Do not ask me that in a doorway."

The hall was not clean and it was not new money. The high windows had been washed in the spring and the light came down in four grey columns and the columns landed on the front four rows and did not get past the eleventh. The chairs were municipal. The bench had the Office's mark on the front of it and a lamp on a cord and the hearing officer's chair was four inches higher than the table.

Tove Marlin had the sheets squared on the table with a cloth over the machine at two feet left of the middle.

He had been in that room on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth for nine minutes and had counted the distance from the door eighty-one times wrong. It was not eighty-one feet in a room with two hundred and eleven people in it. It was a walk.

The machine had a cloth on it and a brass plaque at the front with the certification desk's mark and a date four years back, and he looked at the cloth and did not touch it.

Wren Kallo was at the near end of the bench with nothing in front of her.

Ivo Sarn was at the other end of the bench with a folder he did not open for eleven minutes.

Runa Sato was in the hall. She was in the third row, on the aisle, in her yard coat, and she had come, and he did not look at her and she did not look at him, and at ten o'clock in the morning a clerk with a good voice stood in front of a hundred and thirty-nine seated people and began to read.

Item one was a Field minute of the sixth of last month. Six lines. The subject's word. At the serving officer's request. Above a chop-house in Ferrier Street.

She read it at the speed she read everything else, which was fast, and she got to the end of the sixth line and did not stop and did not slow down, and there was no seventh line, and she went straight on to item two, and there was a gap of about four seconds in a hall with two hundred and eleven people in it and not one of them understood why.

He understood why. He was not going to be able to say one word about it at any point in the rest of that day, because saying it was disclosing a demand that was not his, and he had known that on the eleventh of this month in an empty room and had known it since the sixth of last month at a table, and this was what six had cost and it had been charged to her on the sixth and it was being collected on the twenty-ninth.

Item two was a visitors' book in Bellhouse. Eighty-nine entries. Twenty-nine of them asking about a person. Thirteen of the twenty-nine asking about somebody in the subject's household.

She read the figures and did not say anything about them.

Item three was four lines from a clinic and the finding on the end of them was that the readings are not produced.

Item four was shut and wore his objection on a string, and she said the words the registrar had written, which were that item four is excluded from being read aloud by the member's objection of the eighth, and then she said something the registrar had not written, and she said it in the voice she read forms in.

"I am going to read it."

The hall did not make a sound.

"Item four is excluded by the member's objection of the eighth of this month, and it is excluded because the member lodged it, and I am telling this room that the exclusion stands and that I am going to read it anyway, and that I am a reader appointed to this sitting and not a party to it, and that if anybody in this room would like to stop me they should stand up now rather than at eleven o'clock."

The rail stayed exactly as it was.

Item five was a delivery record from a district lift service for the week ending the tenth of last month, and it carried forty-one names in the third column, and there were women in it, and she read all forty-one of them at the speed of a woman reading a form, which is about four hundred and twenty seconds, which is seven minutes, and Nadia Ferrous was in the fourth row and did not move for any of it and had a hand flat on her own knee.

Seven minutes. He stood at a table with his hands at his sides and listened to a woman who worked in a lift have her own handwriting read out loud to a hall because she had made a decision in four seconds in a car on the tenth of last month, and there was not one line in any document in this city that said she had chosen it.

Then item six, a district table with no clerk's name on it, and item seven, the discharged standing and the five dates, and then item eight, which was his own refusal, six lines in a clerk's hand, written on the seventeenth of last month and returned on the eighteenth.

She read it in her own voice and he heard himself say nothing about his own hands.

Yusuf Adeyemi came in at five past ten with a folder and sat down and let the roll finish and then let the room be quiet for about eleven seconds, which is a long time in a hall.

"I am going to state the Office's case in one paragraph and I am going to read it as it is written and I am not going to improve on it, because it is the case and the member is entitled to hear it in its own words."

He turned a page over.

"A settled mass in the possession of a member, accumulating by the member's own condition, no source identified, no measure obtainable."

"That is the description," said Adeyemi. "It is first on the list because it is what the Office has put forward, and it is four paragraphs long and the clerk has read the four paragraphs. I am not going to read them again because the roll has them in it and the member has had them since the twentieth and has not lodged an objection to any of the four.

"I am going to say three things about that sentence and then I am going to ask the reporting officer to say one thing about his own instrument.

"It is a mass. The Office has no measure of it, has never had a measure of it, and has told the member repeatedly and in writing that it cannot be produced on the member's own plate.

"It is in his possession. The Office has no instrument that shows it moving from one place to another, and the instrument it does have has never produced a line, and I will come to the instrument.

"And it accumulates by his own condition. That is the whole of the Office's case, and I am telling you that I do not believe it, and I am telling you that my not believing it is not evidence and has no standing in this room, and I am telling you that I am obliged to put it to him anyway."

He squared the folder.

"Mr Sarn."

Ivo Sarn stood up at the end of the bench. He had a folder and he did not open it for about four seconds, and he was a man who had been at that desk for nine years and had cut a column off a form because the form had no line for it.

"The instrument is submitted," he said. "It is certified to specification by the desk that certifies instruments. The builder is present. I have nothing to add to what is on the desk's paper."

"Wren Kallo."

The builder stood up at the near end of the bench with nothing in front of her.

"I built it," she said, "on the second bench, seven years ago, on the seventh of a month, off eleven clauses, and the ninth clause says the counterparty column is filled at the moment of contact between the observed subject and a person, in the room, while the tool is running, and that it reads nothing else.

Clauses four to eight say it stores nothing between runs. That is what the tool does. I said it in this room on the eleventh of a month seven years ago and I have said it four hundred times since and it has not been asked for once in seven years."

She sat down.

Adeyemi looked at the man at the table.

"The member has been called to submit," he said. "The member may speak at any point in this sitting, and the clerk will find him a microphone."

"There is something I have to say before that machine is run," he said.

The clerk got up with the microphone and brought it and he held it in his hand and did not put it on the table and did not know what to do with it for about four seconds, and then he held it the way a man holds a thing he has decided to be honest with.

"I am going to stand at this table from now until two o'clock this afternoon and I am not going to touch one person in this room. That is in a clerk's day book in Cobb Street, dated the fourteenth, and a builder's statement in a file in the Exchange building, and both of those are dated before today and neither of them can be taken back by me or by the Office.

"And I am going to tell you now, before it is run, what you are going to see, so that not one person at that rail is standing here in the dark about it."

There were two hundred and eleven people and he could not see the back of the room from the table and he said the next part to the middle of it.

"It is going to print nothing. Not because the machine is broken. Because I am not going to touch anybody, and the only thing in this room that anything in me can move on is me, and I am not going to do that in the first hour.

"And when the second column has printed nothing for a while, somebody is going to ask me whether it moves at all in this room, and it does, and I am telling you that now instead of waiting to be asked, and I am going to do it three times, and I am going to do it here, at this table, where you can all see my hands.

"Because I have got no other way of finding out, and I am not going to find out on anybody who did not ask me for it."

He put the microphone down on the table.

"That is everything. Ask the question or don't."

Adeyemi looked at the room for about four seconds.

"The member has answered the question before it was put," he said, "and I am going to put it anyway, because the question is the officer's and not the member's. Mr Chen, run it."

The machine made a noise for about nine seconds and then stopped making it, and the clerk lifted the output off the roller and put it on the table face down, and Wren Kallo came down off the bench and stood at the table beside him, because the rule is that the builder stands at the table while her instrument runs.

The sheet had the Office's mark at the head and the certification line and two columns ruled across the width.

The first column had a figure in it.

The second column was empty.

"That is complete to specification," said Wren Kallo. "That is what it prints on a subject who does not touch anybody, and I have watched it print on a desk four times in seven years and every one of them was a waste of a good morning."

The hall made no sound at all.

Twenty past ten, and the counterparty column was empty, and there were two hundred and eleven people in a municipal room watching a man at a table.

The first one came at about eleven.

He had been standing for fifty minutes with his hands at his sides of a table, and at some point in the fifty minutes his right hand had come up onto the table edge and had stayed there, and at eleven o'clock he took it off the edge and turned it over and put it flat against his own body on his left side.

It went in the fourth rib. He felt it arrive the way you feel a stair go out under your foot going down faster than you meant to, and it was in him for about two seconds, and it was the whole of the thing, and then it was gone.

He had felt that once. In a corridor on the thirteenth of last month, at a table four feet by nine, with a woman with a card, and he had spent seven weeks since then telling himself it was the spring and the cold.

It was not the spring and it was not the cold.

There was nothing in the hall at all. He had not touched anybody. The sheet on the table was face down and the second column on it was empty and would be empty at two o'clock this afternoon, because he had not touched one person in the room.

Four seconds, the room. Then a man at the back said something that was not loud, and a woman on the rail said, "That is all right," to a person next to her who had not asked, and the hearing officer's clerk stood up and said the word "order" once and the room came back.

Wren Kallo was at the table and had not moved and had seen it.

"The counterparty column is empty," she said, to the room, without turning round, in the flat voice of a woman reading a specification. "Nothing left him. Nothing entered the second column. That is the reading and it is complete to specification and I am certifying it as such."

He kept his hand where it was.

The second one was at about half past twelve, and he did it because he had said in a microphone that he was going to do it three times, and a promise made in front of two hundred and eleven people is the only kind he has ever been any good at keeping.

It landed in the same place. Two seconds, maybe three. Somewhere behind him a chair went over and stayed on the floor for about twenty seconds.

And on his own face, in the front of it, in the two hundred people who could see him from the front, something went out of it.

The third one was at two minutes to two.

He did it with his hand on his own side and he did not look down and he let the room watch, and it went in the fourth rib and stayed about four seconds this time, the longest of the three, and when it came out of him he took his hand away from his body and put it flat on the table, both hands, where two hundred and eleven people could see them, which was the thing he had promised in a microphone.

The sheet stayed face down.

"Say the reading," said Adeyemi.

Wren Kallo turned the sheet over.

"The subject column is filled. The counterparty column is empty." She did not look up. "It has been empty since the instrument was switched on at twenty past ten. Nothing has entered it. In four hours, at a table in a full room, with a machine running and two hundred and eleven people in the room, nothing entered it."

She wrote her name on the certification line and dated it.

"That is what it prints when the tool does its job," she said. "I built it. It works."

Not one person in that hall told him it had been brave, and not one of them was wrong not to, and he understood that about four seconds later, standing at a table with both hands flat on it, lighter than he had been in five months.

There was nothing in it like relief.

Adeyemi read the certification and read it twice and then put his hand flat on his own folder.

"I am going to close the instrument," he said, "and I am going to say why, because the reason is not the reason anybody in this room thinks.

"I am not withdrawing it because it failed. It did not fail. It has produced a certified document, dated and signed by the builder, saying that in four hours in a public hall, while it was running, nothing left the member and nothing entered the second column of it."

He shut the folder.

"The instrument is closed because the Office cannot use it. There is a certified output with a column in it that a person has read, and a person has read it aloud to this room in front of me, and there is not one document in the world that contradicts it and there will not be one."

"And the notice?"

"The notice asks in public whether a member's account may remain undescribed. The description is on the roll. The roll has been read." He looked at the hall. "I am obliged to say that this hearing does not close the notice, and it does not, and that is not a trick and it is not a courtesy, it is the shape of the thing.

A member's account has been described by four offices and it has now been described by the member, and both descriptions are in front of two hundred and eleven people. The question the notice put is for the city to answer and not for this bench."

He stood up.

"The sitting is adjourned at two o'clock."

It was one minute to two and he had not been told the time and had not looked.

Down at the front of the second row, on the aisle, a woman in a yard coat put both hands on the rail in front of her and stood up and started to come out of her seat, and then stopped, because the hall had not emptied, and because a Field officer coming forward through two hundred and eleven people on the morning her own minute had been read was going to be a thing that appeared in a record somewhere and she would answer for it in a fortnight.

She sat back down and put her hands in her lap.

Not one person in that room stopped the man at the table, and there was not one of them who could. He had told them in advance and three of them had said in advance that there would not be, and they had been right, and he had gone ahead anyway, and he was the only man in Ostara who could now be found by a doctor, a tram operator, an accountant or a relative who was simply told to watch for a man who goes white for four seconds.

He knew that already. He had known it at the third one.

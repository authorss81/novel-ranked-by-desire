# The Hall Fills

The five days between the sixteenth and the twenty-second went by the way days go by when every one of them has been counted in advance, and there was nothing in any of them that a clerk would ever ask about, and he cleaned six floors every morning of them and went home and did not open the notebook.

Then it was the twenty-second, which was a Thursday, and the Board repainted at nine the way it does, and the Office published its sitting notice on the same morning at the same hour.

The sheet went up on the district frame at nine o'clock with the room and the hour and the plate by the door, and a second one went into the Exchange entrance, and a third went onto the rail outside the Reading at ten past the same hour, and the office that posted them had done all three before the Board's own man had come off his second ladder.

Tove Marlin was in the corridor on Cobb Street when he came up the outside stair, and she had a list in her hand and the list was about chairs.

"They have booked it to the rating," she said.

"To the rating."

"Two hundred and thirty, which is the number on the plate by the door and the number the works department prints, and this morning at about eleven they sent four men with a handcart and put out ninety more chairs than were there on the eleventh." She turned the list round. "One hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety standing places behind the back row was a room with people in it. Two hundred and thirty is a room with no space in it at all."

"You asked them for it."

"I did not ask them for anything." She squared the list against the wall. "I told the hearing officer's clerk on Wednesday that the room will be full on the morning of the twenty-ninth and I did not tell her a number. She came back at four o'clock with a number."

Yusuf Adeyemi had the file on the eight-foot table in the parties' room on the third floor with the door shut, and he had been in there since eight, and when he came out he had a sheet of grey pasteboard in his hand with two columns ruled across it and about a third of the top filled in.

"Sit down," he said. "Not there. There is nowhere in this building I would rather say this in and it is not the hall, so we are here."

"There is nothing to say."

"There are four documents in this file that do not add up," said Adeyemi, "and they have not added up since the twenty-seventh, and they added up in front of a clerk that day because you asked for them to be put there, and I read the whole thing last night because I could not sleep and because I am the officer who has to rule on whether an instrument goes on that table."

He put the sheet down and turned it round.

"Read it with me and do not stop me at the end of it."

It was the certified run of four years back on a subject whose name had been burnished off the line. Six counterparties in the second column, certified complete to specification, carried into four quarterlies under the same six names.

"That is a builder's best work and it is on your file because you asked for it," said Adeyemi. "Now the case file of the same date. Twenty-one recorded movements under the same heading, in a different hand, in a different building."

He put a second sheet beside it.

"Twenty-one. Six. Fifteen."

"I have had that for a month."

"You have had the numbers for a month and neither of us did the subtraction, and I want to tell you exactly why, because the reason is the whole of my trade and you should hear it once from a man who admits it."

He put his finger on the margin.

"There is a note on this run in a hand older than the reporting officer's. It says, in capitals, INSTRUMENT OUT OF TOLERANCE. It is dated the day of the run and it has been carried forward four times and never reconciled, because every body that touched it was waiting for the next reading and not for the last one's arithmetic."

"So fifteen movements were never seen by the machine."

"Fifteen movements were never seen by the machine, and the machine does not keep anything, and the reason it does not keep anything is clause nine of its own specification, which is in your file because Wren Kallo's confirmation is in your file and the confirmation carries the specification."

He turned a third sheet over. Eleven clauses. He put his thumb on the ninth.

"The counterparty column is filled at the moment of contact between the observed subject and a person, in the room, while the tool is running, and it reads nothing else. Clauses four to eight say it stores nothing between runs and has no memory and no drawer."

The room was quiet for about four seconds.

"So the tool cannot read a backlog," said Adeyemi, "because it never had one. Fifteen of those twenty-one were read on paper by an office, out of what the subject said, and six were read by an instrument in a room, live, with the man's hand on somebody's hand."

He did not say anything else about it. He put the three sheets together and squared them and put them down the way a man puts down something he has finished carrying.

"Now I am going to tell you the thing I told you on the third of this month and could not have told you on the third, and I have been carrying it since Friday and I would have liked to leave it a week longer."

Adeyemi put both hands flat on the table.

"On the third I handed you a trap and I handed it to you as a courtesy. I said the instrument has never once worked in an empty room, the Office expects a full hall, a hearing is public, and I have no power to keep any of them out and would not if I had." He looked up. "That is the whole of what I knew about it on the third and it was the only thing I knew that was true."

"You have worked out the rest of it."

"I have worked out that I was half right in a way I did not know I was half right in." He took his hands off the table. "It does not need a full room. It needs contact. The room is only what the Office wants, because the Office wants witnesses, and because a hearing with an empty chair at the back of it is not a hearing anybody prints."

"And on the twenty-ninth it gets both."

"On the twenty-ninth it gets two hundred and thirty people and a subject who has spent four years touching people in rooms, and it is the best-equipped instrument in this city for the only thing it can do." Adeyemi said it without any heat at all. "I am telling you because you are entitled to it before the morning and because I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out standing at a rail."

He picked up his pen and squared the file.

"And now the second half, which is what I have actually done about it, and you should know that before you decide what you think of the first half."

"You have not told anybody."

"I have not said one word of it to the reporting officer, to the certification desk, or to the builder, and I am not going to, and the reason is not tact."

"Then what is it."

"It is that a rule announced to a room becomes an instruction," said Adeyemi. "If two hundred and eleven people are told on the morning that the second column fills at the moment a member's hand touches a person, then every person in that room who wanted a name in that column would know exactly what to do, and there is not one of them who would need to be a criminal to do it. Every one of them could do it by accident, in a crowd, being kind."

He put the pen down flat.

"The only safe use I have for what I know this week is to keep it in this room with you in it. That is not a favour to you. If the Office understood this instrument's reach in September they would stop holding hearings in rooms and start holding them in corridors, one subject and one clerk, and everything this office has done since the spring would look like what it is, which is a queue with a hearing attached."

"So it goes on the table."

"It goes on the table at ten past eleven and not before the roll has been read." He wrote on the file's flyleaf and turned it round. "An instrument is not run on a person who has not yet been heard. I have ruled instruments out for less than that. The Office will object and the objection will be on the record in about nine seconds and they are entitled to it."

"And then it runs."

"And then it runs for the remainder of the sitting, which is four hours, and it runs in front of two hundred and eleven people, and the output goes in the transcript whatever it says." He put the pen in his pocket. "That is the ruling. Read it back to me and do not improve on it."

He read it back.

"One more thing and then you can go." Adeyemi stood up and put his chair back under the table, which he did at every sitting. "On the morning you may speak at any point. Before the roll, during the roll, after the instrument, or at the end."

"You have been asked to give me a slot."

"I have been asked to give you a slot by four offices and a hearing officer's clerk, and I have refused all of them, and it is in the file that I refused." He had his coat on his arm.

"A slot is a queue. You may stand up when you have something to stand up with, and the clerk will find you a microphone and the room will hear you.

"And if you say nothing at all then the transcript will say that the member spoke and did not, which is the only sentence about you in this proceeding that I have been able to arrange since the second of this month."

Wren Kallo had the machine on the second bench in the certification suite with a cloth over it and the bench paper pinned to the wall by her shoulder, and she had been on paper review since the nineteenth of last month, and she had a shelf of it, and she had got through all of it.

"Six days," she said. "You told six women on the sixteenth and it has been on my bench by Saturday and on a plate-clerk's desk by Sunday and my sister told me on Monday that she had heard it from a woman in the arcade."

"I did not tell you."

"You are entitled to tell me. I am the member's witness and I am going to be standing up in that room on the twenty-ninth being asked what a machine does, and you are not going to be asked cold." She wrote it on the bench paper, dated it, and read it back, and signed it.

"That is what I do instead of keeping it. A thing I hold is a rumour by Thursday. A thing I date is a document by Friday, and I would rather answer for a document."

She put the pen in the rack.

"I have told four people about this by the seat of my own chair and I am telling you the number because you have never once guessed it wrong."

Seven days. Seven less the twenty-second, and both in this month.

At about eight in the evening he walked past the front of the Cobb Street Exchange and the hall doors were open and a clerk was letting people in off the street to look at a room, which the Office does when a room is going to be full, and there were people standing on the steps who had come to look at a room.

He counted about ninety of them on the steps and the rail and the pavement, and they were not all the same kind of person, and one woman on the third step was reading the notice on the frame with her hand on it the way you read a notice when you intend to come back.

Seven days, two hundred and thirty chairs, a machine under a cloth on a table, and a hearing officer in a room on the third floor who had spent the week deciding what he was going to say about what he knew, and had settled on the truth, which was that the safest thing he could do with it was to keep it and that this had cost the Office nothing and had cost the man in front of him the whole of his cover.

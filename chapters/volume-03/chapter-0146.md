# The Ninth Is Himself

[Ledger. A hundred and fourteen lines. Nine turned.]

The parties' room on the third floor of the Reading had the door shut and the nine grey boxes on the eight-foot table where they had been since the seventh of this month, and Sabine Rooke's nine chairs were against the far wall, and there were four people in it: him, Wren Kallo, Tove Marlin, and a hearing officer's clerk who had a sheet of the certification on it and had been told to wait outside for four minutes.

Wren Kallo had her coat off. That was the first thing he noticed and it meant that something had happened to her that morning that had cost her the right to be professional in a room.

"You saw all three," he said.

"I saw two. The third one you did at two minutes to two and I was at the table." She put her hands flat on the box nearest the door, which was box one, and did not open it.

"I saw a man put his hand on his own side and go out of colour for about two seconds and then come back, in a room with two hundred and eleven people in it, and I did not see anything enter the second column, because there was nothing to enter it."

"That is the finding."

"That is the finding and it is signed and dated and it goes in the transcript," said Wren Kallo, "and it is the only document about you in this city that is exactly true, and I want you to sit down and hear the rest of what I am going to say, because I have been standing up for four hours."

He sat down.

"I was the first turned line you made in the winter, on a tram, before the year turned." She said it without any weight. "That is a cold minute and it is four seconds and it is in a hand and then it is gone, and I wrote it down because I was annoyed, not because I was frightened. Do you know what the difference is between a person who has felt one and a person who keeps track."

"No."

"A person who has felt one thinks they were cold," said Wren Kallo. "A person who keeps track knows it was not the weather."

The room was very quiet in the way a room is quiet when four people have all heard the same thing and only one of them is going to say it.

"I felt it once before this morning," he said. "On the thirteenth of last month, in a corridor, with a woman with a card. And I have thought about it every day since."

"Twice is a coincidence. Three is a habit." She stopped. "You did it three times in a hall in front of two hundred and eleven people."

He did the arithmetic and it took about four seconds and he had known the answer before he started.

"That makes four," he said.

"That makes four," said Wren Kallo, "and four is where a person becomes a line. A line can be led to a source by a doctor, or a tram operator, or an accountant, or a relative, and it is not led by anybody clever, it is led by somebody who is simply told what to watch for, and then they watch for it."

"Who is watching for it."

"You are," said Wren Kallo.

He put his hand out flat on the table on his own left side, without meaning to, and then took it back, because there was nothing under it that he wanted.

"Not on purpose," he said. "I want that understood. I have spent five months making sure that a hundred and twelve people in this city do not know there is anything to watch for."

"You do not need to make sure. You made yourself, on the record, in front of a hearing officer and a certified instrument." She took her hands off the box. "There is a document in this city that says a man at a table on Cobb Street on the twenty-ninth of this month did a thing three times that made him go white for between one and four seconds. That is the document. It cannot be withdrawn by the person it names."

Tove Marlin had not said anything at all, and she said it now, from the end of the table, in the voice she read forms in.

"I am not going to pretend I did not see it," she said. "I was four feet from him for the whole of the fourth hour and I read the certification off the sheet and I wrote the time on the transcript and I will write the time again. A clerk records what is in front of the clerk. If it goes in, it goes in as what it is."

"Do not put it in," he said.

"It is not mine to keep and it is not mine to lose," said Tove Marlin. "I will write it as a member's movement, three times, at the table, and I will write it at eleven, at half past twelve and at two minutes to two, because those are the times, and a clerk does not get to round them off."

He looked at the nine boxes on the table.

"Say the rest of it," he said. "Because you took your coat off and you have been standing up for four hours and you did not take your coat off for the finding."

Wren Kallo looked at the hearing officer's clerk in the doorway.

"He is a party and so am I," she said. "Go and wait outside."

The clerk went out.

"I told four people in this building on the morning of the eighteenth of last month before breakfast and it was on my bench by noon," she said. "And I have told three more about this morning since two o'clock, and I am telling you the number because you have never once guessed it wrong in four months and I am not going to start being the woman who keeps things."

"Three more."

"A plate clerk on the second floor, because he asked me in the corridor what the certification said and I told him. Cressida, because she came up at half past two with a requisition about the cloth. And the sister of the man at the washbasin on the certification floor, who is not in this building and has not been in it since the spring and who came into the vestibule at ten past two and asked me how my mother's knee was, and I told her."

"You told her in a vestibule."

"I told her in a vestibule at ten past two," said Wren Kallo, "because she asked me a question and I have never in my life not answered one. It is four people deep and three of them have it, and one of them is my sister, and she is not a party to anything and does not know what she is holding, and I have never been able to work out whether that makes it better or worse."

She put her hand flat on the box.

"You chose this on purpose in a workroom on the eighth and you did it with your coat brushed, and you chose a woman who cannot keep a secret because a woman who cannot keep a secret will answer for it as a party, and I would rather be a party than be a stranger who found it out in a corridor." She stopped. "That is still true at four o'clock this afternoon and I want it minuted."

"It is true."

"It is also going to cost you the thing it was supposed to protect," said Wren Kallo, "and I am the only person in this city who builds the instruments that find people and I am telling you what one of them looks like from the inside. An address is not a thing anybody has. A turned line is a thing anybody has. You have got one, and it is yours, and it is the only one in Ostara that points at a person rather than at a street."

He walked down the outside stair of the Reading at about ten past four with his coat on and nothing in it except a card about a chair, and he got four steps down and stopped, because the thing that had happened to him over the last four hours was not that he had spent something. He had been spending it for five months and he knew what it cost.

The thing that had happened to him was that he could feel one coming.

It sat in the fourth rib on his left as a place rather than as a pain, the way a room sits in a house when you know which door it is, and he understood while standing on the fourth step of a stair that he had never in his life been able to feel that before, and that from this afternoon he could.

Which meant that the next time it came it would come with about a second and a half of warning, and that a second and a half is enough for a man to know that he has just given something to somebody who did not ask for it.

He had made himself the only account in this city that can be audited by its own owner.

There was one thing standing between that and a room with a locked door in it, and he had said it out loud in a hall about three hours ago in front of two hundred and eleven people and a certified instrument, and it was in a transcript in Cobb Street, and it was the only piece of cover he would ever have: a man who felt a cold minute once, in public, on the record, and never again.

A doctor needs two. A tram operator needs two. An accountant needs two. A relative needs two.

He had one, and he had said out loud in a room that there would not be another, and he had said it in the sentence immediately after doing it, while his hand was still on his own side, which meant he had not even had the decent interval between the act and the promise that most men would have had.

And not one person in that hall could stop him, and none of them had ever been going to, which a rank-two auditor had told him on the Thursday night in a doorway and had been entirely right about.

He got the rest of the way down the stair and out into the street with two hundred yards to the rail and the Board's man on a ladder two floors above him on a building that had nothing to say that day, and he stood in the square for about a minute and a half looking at a stranger's tile on a wall where his own had been since the eleventh, and he understood the whole of what he had bought.

A plate three grains above a floor that had not moved. A band on a piece of paper in another building. And a sentence in his own hand that could not be unsaid.

Nine turned lines in a season is a legible pattern, and there were eight of them in this city that belonged to other people, and the ninth was him, and nothing in any office in Ostara was going to refund it.

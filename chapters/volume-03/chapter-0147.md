# What Goes In The Sixth Space

The branch shut at five on the last working day of a month and the counter queue was gone by half past four, and Anneke Roos was standing behind the register with the fourth book open on the slate in front of her when he came in off the stair with his bucket, because he had cleaned six floors on the morning of a hearing and had come back at the end of it because there was nowhere else he was required to be.

Drant had gone. He had a habit of being gone by four on a day like this and of leaving the lamp on over the counter, which was nine years of the same small faithfulness.

"There is a thing at this counter tonight," said Anneke Roos, without looking up. "Put the bucket down."

He put the bucket down.

Tove Marlin was standing at the end of the counter with the day book under her arm, and she was not in uniform in the way that a clerk is never not in uniform, and she had a sheet folded in four in her hand.

"I am here on the officer's instruction and not on my own," she said. "And I am going to read a finding in this building at a quarter past five on the evening of a hearing because the finding has to be entered on the same day it was made, and there is no Office counter that stays open, and this counter does."

She squared the sheet against the slate.

"In the matter of a member whose account remains undescribed, heard in public at Cobb Street on the morning of the twenty-ninth of this month, the instrument submitted in evidence having produced a certified output in which the counterparty column was empty from the moment the instrument was switched on until the sitting was adjourned."

She looked up.

"The hearing officer finds that the account of the member is not such a mass as the description on the exhibit roll asserts, and that the description on the exhibit roll is superseded in the room by the member's own statement of the twenty-ninth, and that the account is therefore not undescribed."

"The band comes off the suspension," said Anneke Roos.

"The band comes off the suspension," said Tove Marlin. "And it does not go on the plate. It goes back on the instrument it was always on, which is the requisition paper of the nineteenth of last month and this finding, and both of them are pieces of paper, and the finding is dated today and the requisition is dated the nineteenth and neither of them is a score."

"What does the plate read."

"Your plate reads what it has read since the winter and it will read it again on Thursday morning." She put the sheet down. "Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one. Three grains of margin.

Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two short of a band that is not on your plate and has not been on your plate since the twenty-first of last month. It has not moved a grain this month, or the month before it, or the one before that."

She said it without any weight, because she had read a hundred and nine of them.

"And the plate cannot carry it," said Tove Marlin. "That is not me being kind. A plate carries a score and a band comes off a score, and there is no mechanism in this city by which a piece of paper with two dates on it adds three grains to a metal disc. If anybody upstairs expects your plate to have moved this month they have been sold something by somebody, and I would like to know who."

Anneke Roos had not moved.

"You have both been standing in my branch talking about a man's plate for four minutes," she said, "and I have got a book open on this counter that has got a ruled space in it that has been empty for a year and a half, and it is going to stop being empty tonight whether or not either of you says another word about brass."

She turned the fourth book round so it faced the middle of the counter.

Five headings and a sixth space, ruled and filled since the twentieth of last month with a paragraph in a clerk's hand from three floors above, entered as received and not agreed, signed underneath by a woman who had put it there as the hand that received it.

"Sit down," said Anneke Roos. "You are not going to stand over my book."

He sat down on the stool with the wobble, which was the same stool a builder had once told him not to sit on in a different building in a different month.

Anneke Roos took the pen out of the drawer and turned the book about ninety degrees so the sixth space ran away from her, and then she struck the paragraph through.

One line, from the first word to the last, in her own hand, with a rule drawn across it and no apology attached to it.

"That is mine to strike," she said. "I wrote it in as received and I have struck it as received, and a clerk can only enter and strike what has been entered, and I have entered that paragraph and I have struck it, and I am the hand that did both halves of it."

Then she squared the book again and ruled a line under the strike and set the nib.

"Now the other half, and I am going to do this slowly and I am going to say what I am doing while I do it, because there is a woman at this counter with a hearing's finding in her hand and I am not going to have either of you surprised in my building.

"His sentence goes under mine. In his hand, copied by me, word for word, because he wrote it and I am not going to improve it by so much as a comma. And it is entered as superseded in the room. And under it goes my name, as the hand that wrote it, which is not the same signature as the one from the twentieth."

She looked up.

"I have signed nothing I received in four months," said Anneke Roos. "On the twentieth I signed as the hand that received it. Tonight I am signing as the hand that wrote it, and that is a different signature in the same book, and I have not done it before in eleven years and I do not know anybody who has."

She had the four sentences on the face of a transcript page, because a hearing officer's clerk had been told in advance to record what happened in front of her, and she had done it at four minutes to eleven in the morning of a hearing, and the finding said the account was not such a mass as the roll asserted.

That was the first time in five months that a true sentence about him had been written down by somebody who did not write it and could not take it back.

She wrote.

It took about ninety seconds, because a clerk who has not copied a man's handwriting in four years does not hurry and does not ask him to dictate. The sentence went in underneath her own strike-through in the ruled sixth space, in her hand, and the entry at the foot of it said superseded in the room, and she signed it with her own name and dated it the twenty-ninth and blotted it and shut the pen.

Then she did the thing that had not been on the page in any of the four months anybody had been carrying this paper about, which was to put her hand flat on the open book and keep it there.

"It is going to cost me something I have not been told about," she said.

Neither of them said anything.

"I do not know what it is and I have not asked and I am not going to, because I have been in this building eleven years and every single time I have asked to be told in advance what a thing will cost me, the answer has been that it is the job." She took her hand off the page.

"What I do know is what the book is. It is the branch's record of what this branch has received and entered and agreed to.

"And tonight the sixth space in it has got a sentence in it that came from a man, and a strike-through on the one above it, and a name under mine that is not the one from the twentieth. And in about nine months somebody three floors above me is going to open that book to use the sixth space for something."

"And it will not be empty," he said.

"It will not be empty and it will not be available," said Anneke Roos. "That is not mine either. I have entered it. And I have signed for it twice in two different ways, and a clerk who enters a thing and then strikes it and then enters another thing over the top is a clerk who has a position on the content of that thing, and there is not one office in this city with a box on its form for a clerk having a position."

Tove Marlin said, "That is the whole of the finding and I have to get it up three floors before the building empties."

"Give it to me," said Anneke Roos.

She gave it to her, and the clerk put it in her bag, and she went up the back stair with a finding in her hand at twenty-five to six.

At four o'clock every working day since the nineteenth of last month the classification paper had gone up three floors and come back down with nothing on it.

At four o'clock on the twenty-ninth of this month a man carried it up in his own hand because he was passing the machine room on his way to a lift with nothing in his coat to do, and put it in the slot.

It was sitting on the spike when he came back down. Unsigned. With the date on it still the nineteenth.

He looked at it on the way out.

Then he went down the long road past the square with a bucket under his arm and a stranger's tile on the wall where his name had been since the eleventh.

Eleven thousand eight hundred and sixty-two grains between him and a band that had come off a suspension that afternoon and gone straight back on to a piece of paper.

And a sentence in his own hand, lying in a ruled space in a book on the ground floor of his own workplace, with a clerk's signature under it that had cost the clerk something neither of them knew yet.

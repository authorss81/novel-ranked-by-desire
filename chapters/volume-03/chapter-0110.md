# What Comes Back Signed

He went to the Fourth Branch at eight in the morning on the ninth because the answer to a thing he had signed at a table in Ferrier Street was going to arrive in a building he worked in, and he wanted to be standing in it when it did.

The register desk had a queue at it at eight, the ordinary first-of-the-week queue, and he cleaned washrooms until twenty to nine and then went and stood at the end of it with his hands at his sides, and Drant did not look at him for about an hour and a half, which was the longest hour and a half of a working morning Lin Hao had had since the middle of last month.

At half past ten the thin book came off the shelf under the counter and Anneke Roos squared it and opened it and turned it round, because she had done that three times in five weeks and it was a thing she had started doing.

"Two things," she said. "The first is mine and you are going to be bored."

"Go on."

"The return goes up at four on this floor." She had not looked up. "It is the month-end, it is due on the thirty-first, and it was late, and I signed it out on the first because the desk that sends it on the thirty-first has been discharged since the eighteenth.

"It will go up late at the end of this month too, and it will go up late every month until somebody reinstates a standing on this floor or tells this office to stop printing it."

"And the second."

"The second is not mine and it is not about this floor. It is on the counter because it is addressed to the register." Anneke Roos put a sheet down face up and kept her hand flat on it. "It came down from the fourth floor of an Exchange building at six o'clock last night with a stamp on it that has an office's name and no person's, and that is how a registrar signs when the thing he is answering is not a thing anybody has looked at."

Lin Hao did not put his hand on the sheet.

"Read it," said Anneke Roos. "It is four lines and it is addressed to a member and it has been read by a clerk in a corridor on the third floor of the Reading and by a man with a bad shoulder on a floor of this building who has never seen you, and it is on the counter because somebody has to hand it over, and I am somebody."

He read it.

*Item four is excluded from being read aloud in the hearing.*

*The member's objection is upheld.*

*The item excluded is a record kept by the household of the member: six lines, one line per woman per day, from the twenty-first of last month, kept by the member's household. Signature refused.*

*No name is attached to the item and none is required.*

Four lines. He had signed eight words and had put a book into a file, and a registrar four floors above a clerk who was not told why had written four lines back, and not one person in the whole of it had decided anything.

There was no refusal in it and no concession in it. It was not even a judgement. It was a clerk, or a stamp, or a man with a bad shoulder, reading two pieces of paper and finding that one of them was valid, and writing down what the one that was valid described.

He had traded a name for a description and he had got the worse half.

The name was gone. The description was in a file on the fourth floor of an Exchange building with a member's notice in it, and the description was not four lines long, it was twenty-six words long and it had been written by somebody who had never been in the room and had seen the item only as a description, and it was longer and more specific than anything six women had put in that book in nineteen days.

Six lines. One line per woman per day. From the twenty-first. Kept by the member's household. Signature refused.

It did not say six women. It did not say Ferrier Street, or a chop-house, or a room, or that the lines were written in six different hands by people who had gone to bed and got up and gone to work and had not been asked.

But somebody, somewhere, had put *kept by the member's household* into a Bureau file, and from that day the household was not a thing that lived in a room above a chop-house and nowhere else. It was a thing with a shape, and the shape was four clauses long, and it had been written by a stranger who was being accurate.

And it had said the only sentence in it that nobody had put on the page, which was *signature refused*, which had been the whole of the protection for three weeks and was now a clause in somebody else's document.

That was the whole trade and it had taken four minutes.

"Six lines," he said.

"That is what it says." Anneke Roos turned the sheet over. There was nothing on the back.

"How long is it going to be kept."

"That is not in the four lines and it is not mine." She said it in the flat voice of a woman who has said that sentence before. "I have never been asked to enter anything of this kind in that register and I am not going to, and if you want it entered say so and you will be the reason it is in here for the next forty years. I would rather you did not."

"I will not."

"Then it goes back in the tube at four with the roll, and everything that has ever been true about it in that room is going to be read out by a clerk in the voice she reads things in." Anneke Roos squared the sheet.

"And I am going to say the other thing, because you have been standing at this counter for an hour and a half and you did not come here for the four lines and we both know it."

He looked at her.

"The paper that has gone up three floors every working day since the nineteenth of last month is not in that file." She said it to the page and not to him. "It is unsigned. It has been unsigned for three weeks and it will go up at four today, and there is nothing on the fourth floor of this building that knows anything about it, including me, and I am not permitted to want to know."

"That is fine."

"It is not fine and you know it is not fine, because a clerk four floors above me is going to read your four lines and then read the finding number at the top of his tray, and those two things are about the same man, and nobody in this building is going to say nothing about it." She squared the sheet. "That is not mine and it is not this floor and I am not going to say anything else about it."

He stood at that counter with a plate against his ribs carrying three grains of margin, and eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-one, and a band six on an instrument in a building he had never been inside and never would be, and none of it had moved and none of it had been lost, and a hundred and twelve lines in a book in a drawer above a chop-house with one of them reading himself.

Then he went to the Vestry and sat on the stone bench outside it with his sleeve pushed up, because the bench had a brass plate on the end of it and he had read it on a Thursday and had thought about it for five days.

Fenn was coming out of the ward office with a chart under her arm and she saw him and stopped.

"The form went back," she said, before he had said anything, and then she said, "You did not make me say it and I have been waiting to tell somebody since the third."

"I heard."

"Then you know, and I am saying it anyway because you are the only person I am permitted to say it to and I have had six days." She put the chart down on the bench. "Two lines in the column and one empty and she did not sign her name against the empty one. That is a service that has failed to reply twice, and twice is a conversation, and the third one is a procedure, and the procedure has a man in it."

"He will not be the girl with the wrist. She is back up two flights from yesterday."

"What happens to her."

"Nothing for about six weeks, and then a man comes up the arcade with a card and looks at a floor, and the floor is going to look like a floor and the rack is going to say twenty-eight, and there is not one thing on that floor that says a woman has kept it looking like a floor for eleven years on purpose." Fenn picked the chart up. "And she knows that. She knew it on the third when she put nothing on it."

He went in and Tessa Okonkwo was not in her office and the door was shut and there was a runner at the desk at the end of the corridor with a chart under her arm, one-handed, sorting, refusing on the grounds that the piles were more use than the filing.

He did not knock.

He came out of the arcade at two in the afternoon and the light on the square was flat and white and there was nothing in it, and the Board at the head of the Reading had repainted on Thursday morning with his name not on it and no word under it, and a sheet of paper on the district frame forty yards away with his notice on it between the Exchange hours and a lost parcel.

And a runner came up the arcade at four minutes past three with a leather tube under her arm and stopped about nine feet away from him on the pavement and held it out without coming any nearer, because she had been told to hand it over and not to hand it over.

It was on the seventh that she had handed a roll to a man in a corridor. This one was on the ninth and it was not for him, and the difference was a thing neither of them had any instruction about.

"Lodged at ten past three," she said. "Second clerk's floor, the antechamber."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome," said the runner, and went back down the arcade, and it was the first time in four years that a person had said that to him for any reason at all.

He stood on the pavement outside a clinic on the Vestry with a hearing in fifty-one days and the roll in an antechamber two floors under a clerk who was not told anything, and a hearing date he had not had to move because he had been given it, and a household notebook in a shut file that had just become six lines, one line per woman per day, from the twenty-first, signature refused.

Then a runner came back up the arcade at ten past three and stopped in the same place.

"Sorry," she said. "They want it back in the tube."

"How long."

"Twenty minutes. They are copying it." She looked at her own list. "And they want to send a member's list."

"What is on it."

"It is not mine." The runner said it the way everybody in this city said the four or five sentences they had been given. "It is what they intend to put in front of the hall."

He took it.

It was a single sheet, headed with the Office's mark and the date, and the first line was not a document about him.

It was not a document about him because it had no date on it of its own and no signature and no name on it at the side of it, and it was four paragraphs long, and the heading over it said only *description*, in the flat administrative voice of something that had been made by an office in order to be read out.

Lin Hao stood on a pavement outside a clinic on the Vestry and read the first sentence of it, and the first sentence was a description of him, written in somebody else's hand, in the fourth paragraph of a paper on which the three things above it were his household's paper.

It was going to be read aloud, in a municipal hall with a hundred and forty seats and a rail and ninety standing places behind the back row and a plate by the door that said two hundred and thirty, to every person in Ostara who cared to stand there, on a day that was already printed, by a man who had told him to the face that he was honest and did not care in the slightest who anybody was.

He read the first sentence again, and understood that the trap was not the machine and had never been the machine, and that the Office had known since the first that he would walk into the building.

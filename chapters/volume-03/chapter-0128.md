# Nine Weeks To The Hall

The twenty-eighth was the first working morning of the rest of his life in which his hands were the problem.

He had cleaned the second floor first because a reading queue forms there on sitting days, and the queue was there, eleven people with plates in their hands, and he worked round it with the mop the way he had worked round it for four years, and on the way round a woman he knew by the shape of her coat put her plate down on the sill and it slid off the sill and she said thank you before it had finished falling.

She said thank you to the man with the mop, and he had his hand out and it was four inches from a piece of metal and he took it back and put it on the mop handle instead and said the thing he says, which is that the sill is wet.

She thanked him for the sill. She said the sill was always wet. She went off to the socket with her plate in her hand and she never knew that a man in a corridor had taken his hand back from four inches of a woman's plate in about a quarter of a second, and he stood in a wet corridor with a mop in both hands and understood that the next thirty-two days were going to be made out of quarter-seconds like that one.

It went into the book that morning without his noticing it, the way everything went in. A queue, a floor, an hour. The cause named on the page where it happened, and no number.

At eleven o'clock a runner came up the outside stair of the branch with a hand envelope and a wax and no docket, which was the second time in a month a piece of paper had come up those stairs without a docket, and the first time had been the note he had written for himself with no envelope at all.

The wax said W KALLO, SECOND BENCH, EXCHANGE.

He read it standing at the register counter. Anneke Roos served the queue and did not ask.

It was four lines and it was dated the twenty-seventh and it said that the Office had asked the builder to answer for the certified run of the ninth, four years back, that she had answered it, that her answer was in the file and could not be taken out of it, and that she was telling him this by hand because she was no longer permitted to put a form through a tube.

Then there was a fifth line, in a quicker hand, at the bottom.

*I have answered it more than once and I have not been able to keep the count, and every copy has been out of my hand inside a day, and I would like you to know that about me and not to be surprised by it in a hall. W.*

He read it three times. The four lines were the business and the fifth line was the woman, and she had put the business first and the woman second, which was the first time in the ten days he had known her that she had put them in that order.

"That is a builder," said Anneke Roos, from the register, without looking up. "That is a person who has confirmed a machine's output in a proceeding and is telling a subject about it the same evening. Put it in your coat and do not read it twice in a corridor."

The table had six women at it and the notebook open with a date ruled at the top and thirty-nine days of lines in it, six a day, one per woman, in six hands, and his pen in his pocket where it had been for a month.

He put the slip on the table face up and told them the whole of the twenty-seventh in Tove Marlin's words as near as he could hold them, and he read out the two columns and the two numbers and the fifteen, and then he read out clause nine, which he had written on the back of Ola Bright's pad on the train because he had known on the stair that he would need it in his hand.

Nadia Ferrous had both hands flat on the table by the time he got to fifteen.

"Twenty-one," she said.

"Twenty-one."

"So the machine saw six and the office wrote down twenty-one, and the office is the one that wrote the other fifteen." She had not said a word through the first half of it. "And they are the ones who did it. Not the machine. The machine did six and printed six and everybody in this city has spent four years assuming a machine that says six means six."

"Yes."

"That is not the part that frightens me." She looked up. "The part that frightens me is what a column is for. A column is for putting a name in. That is what it is built to do and everything in this city is built to do it, and you have just told me the machine will put one in every time you put a hand on somebody while it is running."

"That is what clause nine says."

"Then count the hands." Nadia Ferrous put her own hands on the table, flat, and did not take them off.

"A hall is not an interview. You are not going to stand at a table with your arms at your sides for an hour."

"You are going to stand at a table in a room that anybody in this city may walk into, and somebody is going to come up to you and put a hand on your arm because that is what people do when they have read about you for eleven weeks."

"Somebody is going to be at the rail who has been in a queue with a plate in her hand since the week the notice went up, and there is going to be a woman at the back who has been to a vestibule in the rain looking for the only building in the city where a man can be read."

"And you will do the thing your hands do. Not out of generosity. Out of four years." She looked round the table. "And every one of them is a name in a column and a hearing with a subject in it and a number on a wall on the fourth of next month. That is all this thing has to do. That is all it takes. Not a conspiracy. Not a woman in a dark coat."

"A queue on a wet morning and a man who cannot stop himself putting his hand on somebody."

The room went quiet for a while, and the building went on being a building four floors below them.

"And clause nine does not say who starts it," said Nadia Ferrous, before Beatrix Halm got her second sentence out, and she said it to the table and not to him. "It says the subject and a person, in contact. It does not say the subject reached."

"The Office has to stand a machine in that room and then it has to leave a man in a room with a hundred and forty people in it for an hour, and he is the man that people reach for, and every one of them is going to reach."

"It is not the instrument's fault."

"It is not a fault," said Beatrix Halm, and then stopped, because she heard herself and did not want it. "Not an office's, not a clerk's, not that hearing officer's."

"That is what makes it worse, and I am the only person at this table who has been paying attention to the arrangement of this thing since the seventeenth of last month, and I gave him the district's cold minutes on one condition, and the condition was that he spends them on people and not on himself, and it was a good condition and it is the only protection anybody in this house has ever had, and it is dead."

"What do you mean."

"I mean that for two months he has been able to give away whatever he wanted, and every time it went to a person who needed it and the giving cost him something he could not pay back and he would have paid it twice. That is what this house is. That is the whole engine." She had her hands on the table and she was not gentle, and she never was.

"And now there is a room where the only thing he can give away moves nothing at all and takes a night of sleep out of him for asking, and the room chooses that and not him. And I am the woman who put the condition on and I am telling him the condition is worth nothing on the twenty-ninth, and that if he goes in there and gives anything to anybody I will know."

"Beatrix—"

"I am not finished." She turned round to him. "You have told this table in one evening that for thirty-two days you are going to have to walk about a building with your hands in your pockets. Do not do it alone. That is the part I will fight you about. Not the room. Your hands."

Sun Meiling was on the landing at the top of the stairs with her coat on, where she had been since the first, and Nadia went up to her with it because news went up that staircase in that house even when the woman on the landing would not come down for it.

She came back in four minutes and sat down and said nothing, which for Nadia Ferrous was a considered act.

Thirty-two days. Three left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next. Nine weeks is what a woman with two hands called it in a clinic corridor on the twenty-ninth of last month, and she had been right to within a day, and there are thirty-two days of it left.

At the end of the shift he signed out at the gate and went down the front path of the branch and a woman he had cleaned past for four years came round the corner of the building with her own plate in her hand and said good evening.

He had his hands in his pockets.

She said good evening to a man standing in the wet outside a branch office with his hands in his pockets, and he said good evening back, and she went in, and he stood there for about a minute with the rain going into the shoulders of a coat that had no umbrella on it, and understood that this was what the next thirty-two days were made of and that not one person in the city was going to know he was doing it.

# The Chair She Never Took

Sabine Rooke sent word up to the Fourth Branch on the twenty-second by a runner with a wax on a string and no docket in her hand, which Lin Hao understood before the runner had said anything, because the last person in that building who had come up with no docket had been a girl on the seventeenth who left an envelope at a bench.

The wax said one line on the outside of it. *The third floor. Before the hall is cleared.*

He cleaned two floors and went, and took the outside stair, and the square below the stair was doing nothing at all in the middle of a working day, which it never is.

The room on the third floor was open and the door was propped with a broom and there was a smell of paste in it and the windows had been opened at both ends for the air. Nine grey boxes stood down the middle of the eight-foot table with about a hand's width between them, and box four had its objection on a string, and the eight chairs were against the far wall in two rows of four.

The ninth chair was in the corridor.

She was sitting on it with her grey armband on and a card on the seat beside her and her hands on her knees, and she had been sitting on it long enough that there was a mark in the dust of the board on the wall behind her where the back of it had been.

"Sit down," she said. "Not in the eight. The eight are the Office's on the twenty-ninth and there is a list and they are on it."

He sat down on the floor against the door frame, which was where a man sits in a room when the only chairs in it belong to a hearing.

"They asked me a third time," said Sabine Rooke.

The woman had come at half past ten with a card and no coat, which Lin Hao understood was a professional decision and not an accident, because a woman with a coat is a woman who is leaving.

The question had not been about a Thursday.

That was the thing he could not get past for the first minute, standing in the corridor afterwards with his back to the stair wall. The twelfth of last month and the twenty-seventh of last month, both about a Thursday in a month that had gone, and on the twenty-first of this month a woman stood in this corridor and asked whether the room on the third floor had always been arranged for nine.

"Yes or no," she had said. "Not which Thursday. The room. Was the room always for nine."

"Eleven years," said Sabine Rooke. "Yes. Nine chairs and nine people and a man at the front with a sheet, every week, and two of the nine were for people who did not come, and I could not tell you which two and I have never been asked which two."

"And the answer you gave them."

"The true one." She put her hands on her knees. "That I arranged the room and the man at the front brought the sheet and I took the minutes afterwards and kept them in a drawer in this corridor, which is a drawer that is not in any register on this floor because I have never entered it in one. Eleven years of sheets in a drawer in a corridor. That is what I said to her, and she wrote it down, and she thanked me, and she left."

"You told them where the sheets are."

"I told her the truth, which is that I kept them, in a drawer, in this corridor. I did not tell her the drawer. That took me eleven seconds and she was not in the room for nine of them."

"And now they have a file."

"Now they have a file." Sabine Rooke said it without heat. "I told you on the fifteenth of last month what the difference is between two and three and I have not said it since and I am not going to say it now, because it is mine and it has already happened and I would only be saying it to hear it."

Lin Hao looked at the chair she was sitting on.

"Where did the nine chairs come from," he said.

"The eight went down the Wing stair on the morning of the second of this month," she said, "in a handcart, with a man who did not give his name and did not ask me anything, and I stood at the top of it and watched them go, which is the third time in two months I have stood at the top of a stair in this building and watched something of mine go down it."

"Then eight men came up the outside stair at about six that evening with the same cart and took eight of them back up, and set them against the far wall of that room in two rows of four, and did not ask me anything either, and they left at about twenty past six. I sat on the ninth chair until the building shut and then I went home."

"And the ninth."

"The ninth is the one I am sitting on." She stood up off it and put her hand flat on the back of it. "Eleven years ago I put nine chairs in that room because there were nine people who did a job on that floor, and one of them was a ward clerk from the Vestry who came up once a week for a year and a half and then stopped, and I never found out why, and I kept her chair."

He did not say anything, because there was nothing to say to a woman who has kept a chair for eleven years for somebody who does not come.

"It is not furniture," she said. "There is a form somewhere that says nine chairs went to the second sub-level and eight came back. That is what the handcart means."

"There is no line anywhere on any sheet in this building for the ninth, and there has not been one since the twenty-sixth of last month, and on the twenty-ninth a clerk is going to stand in that room and count the chairs against a list and there is going to be one chair missing and she is going to write that down."

"What do you want to do."

"I have wanted to do one thing since the second of this month and I have not done it, and you are going to tell me that this is not my room and you would be right." She squared the chair against the wall with two hands, the way she squared the eight on the third. "I want it in that room on the morning. Not in a store. Not in a corridor with a broom-propped door. In that room, with the eight, where the list says it is."

"That is nine chairs in a room that is holding nine boxes for two men who do not speak to each other."

"That is nine chairs in a room that is holding nine boxes for two men who do not speak to each other and a clerk and a hearing officer and, because none of them has been told otherwise, a woman who arranges rooms."

She said that last part the way she said everything, which was as though it were a piece of furniture she had noticed in a corridor and had decided to move.

"I have not asked anybody for anything in my life," she said. "I told you that on the fifteenth of last month and I said I was not starting until the fourteenth of this month, and it is the twenty-second and the fourteenth has gone, and I have come and found you first because I said I would, and I am asking you for the chair."

That is what it cost, and she had priced it before she asked, which was the first thing he understood about her.

A chair is furniture and it goes into a store.

A chair that goes into a room on the morning of a hearing goes into the room on a sheet, and the sheet has a name on it, and the name is the person who signed for the chair, and on the twenty-ninth there will be a room on the third floor of the Reading containing nine boxes and nine chairs and a signature belonging to a clerk who has held a grey armband for eleven years and has never in that time entered anything in any register.

"Three is a file," he said.

"Three is a file," said Sabine Rooke.

"And a file is made out of paper. I have eleven years of paper in a drawer in this corridor that has never once been asked to see, and I am the only person who knows what is in it and where it is."

"In about four weeks a woman with a card is going to come up this stair a fourth time and she is going to ask me for the drawer, because I gave her the corridor yesterday and a corridor is one word away from a drawer."

"You could refuse to answer her."

"I could refuse to answer her and it would be the fourth and four is not a thing that happens to a person who arranges rooms, it is a thing that happens to a person who is under review." She said it as though she had costed it and found the figure acceptable. "I am not asking you to protect me from a question. I am asking you for a chair."

"And if the chair goes into that room on my signature then the question about the drawer is a different question, because I will have given them something, and a woman who has given a thing is not a woman they are still taking things from."

He looked at the nine boxes on the table and at the eight chairs against the wall and at the ninth in the corridor with a mark in the dust behind it.

What he wanted, and he had wanted it since the seventh, was to be in a room where nothing was about him. He had wanted it on the sixth when she had told him what the room was for, and he had wanted it in the corridor on the eleventh when nine boxes had gone onto an eight-foot table, and it had not happened once and he had understood each time that it was not going to.

"I cannot put a chair in that room," he said. "I have signed nothing in that building except two sheets on the eighth of this month and I signed them on a table in a room with no window and one of them was about your corridor."

"I know what you signed."

"Then you know I have nothing there. I have no standing and no post and no floor and a name on a finding in a hand three floors above a woman who has never seen me. If I carry a chair up that stair on my own time, a porter sees me carry it, and the porter writes it in a book the way a book was written on the eighth of last month, and the book stays on that floor."

"Then the book has you in it," said Sabine Rooke.

"Then the book has me in it."

She nodded once and put her hand back on the back of the chair.

"That is the whole of the answer and it took you eleven seconds and you did not make me ask twice, which is more than most people manage." She lifted the chair. It was heavier than a chair ought to be, and she had been carrying it, and she carried it four steps to the door and set it down in the frame. "It comes in at eight on the morning. That is when the hall is cleared and that is when the parties go up."

"You have an hour before that because I am going to be here from seven and this floor is empty at seven."

"You are going to be here from seven."

"I am going to be here from seven and I am going to sign for nine chairs and a table and nine boxes, and when the clerk counts she is going to find nine, and she is going to write the number nine, and no chair is going to have to be explained." She straightened up. "You have an hour. Do not be early, because a porter can tell early from late and I have spent eleven years learning what a porter can tell."

He got the chair off the floor of the corridor and took the outside stair down and across the square with it, which is four hundred yards of street in front of two thousand people who had been going past him for four years without looking, and not one of the two thousand turned round, and a porter at the foot of the Wing stair in the Exchange building looked, and said the one sentence porters say.

"Corridor furniture."

"Third floor," said Lin Hao. "Going up."

The porter wrote it in a book. Lin Hao did not ask to see the entry and did not need to, because he had learned in a corridor on the eighth of last month exactly what a book on that floor is for and exactly how long it stays there.

He carried a chair up four flights and put it in the room where eight others stood against a wall, and he put it in the ninth place, four feet out, square to the door, where it had stood for eleven years before eight men came up the outside stair with a handcart and took the room apart around it.

Thirty-eight days. Nine left in this month after today and twenty-nine in the next, and a woman downstairs signing her name to nine pieces of furniture for a morning she has arranged for eleven years, and a porter's book on a floor with no standing on it, and the ninth chair in the room where it was always going to be.

# The Counter-Forensics Desk

The instrument certification desk was on the second floor of the Exchange building on Cobb Street, past the plate office and up a stair that smelled of paste, and Lin Hao went up it on the fifteenth because Tove Marlin had told him on the seventh that an instrument was not evidence of anything until somebody in the room had said what it did, and nobody had said it yet.

He had cleaned four floors before coming and left two for the afternoon, and on the ground floor the plate queue had been the longest of the week, thirty deep by nine, and he had worked round it, and it had gone into the book with the floors.

The clerk at the desk was a man with ink on his cuffs and a tray with three instruments' papers in it. He read Lin Hao's notice without interest and put it down, and went back to ruling lines on a certification form with a ruler and a pen that scratched.

The waiting bench along the wall held a Field officer with a requisition and a woman from the Exchange with a sealed box. Neither looked up. The stair outside smelled of paste and the plate office below thumped every few minutes as the socket came down.

"You are the member," said the clerk, without looking up. "The hearing is forty-five days out. Sixteen days left in this month after today, and twenty-nine in the next, and I have checked it twice because everybody checks it once."

"The Office is bringing an instrument," said Lin Hao. "I want to know what the Wing says it does."

The clerk pulled the middle paper out of the tray. It was a Wing requisition for a counter-forensics run, two paragraphs long, with a finding number in the corner and a signature at the foot that was not the hand that had written the paragraphs.

"The Wing says it reads a subject and a counterparty," said the clerk. "That is the whole of the claim on this paper. The Office proposes to put it on a table in the hall and have it read you, and the reading will go into the transcript as what came out of the machine."

He lifted the requisition so the light fell on the paragraphs. "Two paragraphs. The first says the Wing wants a run. The second says the run is for the hearing of the twenty-ninth. There is no third paragraph. The Wing has not said what the reading will show, because the Wing does not know, and this desk does not ask a proponent to guess."

"And who says it can do that."

"The man who wrote the output the Wing is relying on." The clerk turned the paper over. On the back, in a different hand, was a name and a post. "Ivo Sarn. Counter-forensics reporting officer. Nine years at this desk. His hand draws the headings in the fourth book three floors above the register room, including the sixth one, and the woman who keeps that book has never laid eyes on the man."

Lin Hao looked at the name. It was the first time it had been on any paper in front of him.

"There is a standing rule," said the clerk, "and it belongs to the hearing officer and not to this desk, and I am telling you because you asked what the paper says and the rule is written on the back of every one of them. An instrument offered in evidence must be shown capable of producing what its proponent says it produces."

He tapped the rule with one ink-stained finger.

"That means the Office cannot walk into the hall with a machine and a claim. It has to walk in with a paper from this desk saying the machine does what the Wing says it does. That paper does not exist yet. Nothing goes into a hall without both hands, and that rule has held since before I came to this desk."

The Field officer on the bench shifted his requisition from one knee to the other. The woman with the sealed box counted the tiles on the opposite wall.

"Yours is the eleventh this year," said the Field officer to Lin Hao, conversational, the way men talk on benches. "Mine is the twelfth. The desk does them in order and the order is the order they arrived. Yours arrived two days back. Mine arrived yesterday morning. That is the whole of the queue and there is nothing behind it."

"Then we wait together," said Lin Hao, and the Field officer laughed, surprised, and they waited together, and the clerk ruled his lines, and the stair smelled of paste, and for ten minutes nobody in the room was anything but early.

The woman with the sealed box finished counting the tiles and started again at the other corner. The socket thumped below. A runner came up with post and went down with paper, and the tray filled and emptied, and the whole building went on administering itself round two men waiting for machines.

"Who writes it."

"This desk writes it, and this desk does not write it alone." The clerk put the requisition back in the tray. "A certification wants two things. The desk's own run of the tool against its specification, and the word of the officer who built it that she built it."

He said it without weight, the way clerks say things that move buildings.

"The tool was built by an officer outside the standing roll," said Lin Hao.

"It was built by the only officer outside the standing roll who ever built one of these." The clerk squared the tray. "That is why the request goes to her and not to a bench. A bench can tell you what a machine does. Only the builder can tell you she built it."

Lin Hao stood at the desk with the smell of paste in his nose and worked out what he had been given. The Office had a machine and a claim and forty-five days. The machine needed a paper. The paper needed two hands. One of the hands belonged to a woman nobody had named to him, in a division he had never entered, who had built the thing that would read him.

He was wary. That was the feeling, cold and clean, like a hand on a rail in winter. Every instrument in this city had a builder, and builders had reasons, and reasons had postures, and he had spent four months learning to read rooms and had never once read the room where his machine was made.

What he wanted was the request paper itself. Not the requisition. The request this desk would send to the builder. He wanted to see what it asked, and what it promised, and whether it told her what the machine was for.

"Show me the request when it goes," he said.

"It goes tomorrow." The clerk ruled another line, straight and exact. "It goes by hand, because a request to an officer outside the roll does not go in the tube. Come at ten and stand where you are standing and I will let you read it before it goes. That is more than the desk does for proponents, and the desk does it because you are the subject and the subject has a standing the proponent has not got."

"And if she refuses to confirm."

"Then the desk runs the tool without her word, and the paper says it was run without it, and the hall hears that sentence first." The clerk put the ruler down. "A builder who will not say she built it is a fact about the machine, and the hall is entitled to the fact. That is the whole of certification. Not that the machine works. That the paper about the machine is complete."

"And the desk's own run."

"The desk runs the tool next week against its specification." The clerk capped his pen. "That is paper. Whether the tool produces what the Wing says it produces in a hall with a hundred and forty strangers in it is not paper. That is the twenty-ninth, and I will not be in the hall, and neither will anybody from this desk."

He went down the stair past the plate office, where a queue stood with plates in their hands, and out into Cobb Street with the notice in his coat and a name in his head that had not been there that morning.

Ivo Sarn. Nine years at a desk. The hand three floors above Anneke Roos.

He cleaned the last two floors in the afternoon with the name going round in his head the way names go round when they have just been read for the first time, and Drant watched him take the bucket through the ground floor and said, "The desk's paper will come to the hall in due course," which was the first time Drant had named the certification desk out loud, and Lin Hao understood that everybody in every building was keeping the same schedule now, and the schedule was his.

At the corner he stopped and made the decision he had come up the stair without knowing he was going to make. He would not ask for a private run. He would not ask to see the machine work, or to have it worked on him in a room with the door shut, or to have anybody tell him quietly what it found.

Whatever the tool did, it would do in the hall, in front of strangers, on the day, or it would not do it at all. He had written that sentence once before, on a blank strip under a printed line, about a reason. He would hold the machine to the same condition.

He walked back to the branch with forty-five days in front of him and two papers behind him that did not exist yet, and the wariness stayed with him the whole way and did not warm up once.

He told the table that evening, standing at the foot of it with the notebook shut under Marguerite's hand. A desk. A rule. A reporting officer with nine years in the chair. A builder outside the roll with no name yet.

Marguerite listened to the end and priced it in one sentence. "A machine that needs two hands is a machine that can be stopped by either. The Office has neither yet."

"And when it has both."

"Then the hall has a machine and a paper, and you have forty-five days to be the man who stands next to it." She put her hand on the shut book. "That is the whole of the position and I am not pricing it further tonight."

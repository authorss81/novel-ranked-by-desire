# The List Without Names

She came up the outside stair at ten past two on the seventh with a leather tube under her arm and no coat, because the room was standing open and the corridor had nobody in it but a ward clerk who decided she was furniture.

"Lin Hao," said Tove Marlin.

"Tove."

"I am not here on your business and I am not here on the Wing's, and I want to say that first, because I have been in three rooms this week being somebody's business and I have about nine words' worth of ordinary left." She set the tube down against the wall. "I am here because I produced the roll."

He looked at it.

"You produced it."

"I am the officer who produced it. My name is on the schedule and my hand is on the cover. It is my job to carry it up three floors, put it on a table on the seventh, be in this corridor on the eighth for anybody the Office has put on it, take it down to a registrar on the ninth, and carry it up to Cobb Street on the date." She said it flatly. "I have done it eleven times."

She unrolled the cover, tied the two straps back, and stood looking at what was inside the way a woman looks at a tray before she starts.

"You have not read it," she said.

"No."

"Everybody handed one of these has not read it, and everybody who reads one stops being able to sleep. I have watched it happen nine times." She put her hand flat on the cover sheet, not on the roll.

"I am going to read it exactly as written, because I read Runa Sato's household line back to her in a room on the nineteenth of last month and told her I thought it was decent of her. She has not spoken to me since and she was right not to, and I would read it back the same way tomorrow."

"You said you would still think that."

"I said exactly that, and I said it because I have learned that the only kindness available to a clerk is to be identical on every occasion." She took her hand off the cover. "So here it is, and I am sorry about the room, and I have been in this building for eleven years and this is the first time I have been sorry about it, and I am not going to pretend it is the room's fault."

"Go on."

"There are nine items," she said, and turned the roll a quarter turn so it faced him. "There are no names on it."

"It is not laziness and it is not an oversight." She put two fingers on the corner. "A document is an exhibit and a person is a witness. They live in two different columns and this roll has one column in it. There is not a name on any page of it because the Office has not decided what you are in this hearing, and it has until the morning of the twenty-ninth to decide it."

"That is not an insult and I want it read the way it is meant. It is an office that has prepared a very large amount of paper about a man and has not yet decided whether to put him in a chair and ask him questions or put a machine on a table and read him. Both are lawful, both are in that form somewhere upstairs, and it decides at about seven in the morning on the day."

She looked up at him.

"Item one. A Field minute of the sixth of last month from the brute-force division, six lines in a serving officer's hand, being a household line entered at the member's word."

"That is Runa's."

"That is Runa Sato's, and it is on the roll because it is a minute and it is signed, and it is the only item anybody put on deliberately." Her voice did not change at all and her hands stayed flat on the cover. "I am going to read the six lines, because I read them, and because it is worse if I do not.

"For a household kept above a chop-house in Ferrier Street, retained by the subject's word on the sixth, at my request."

Down the corridor a broom was not being used by anybody.

"Item two. A visitors' book from a house in Bellhouse, opened on the first, in a clerk's hand and in a hand that is not a clerk's, with an hour column and a column for the caller's business."

"That is the house."

"Eighty-nine entries," she said. "Twenty-nine ask about a person and thirteen of the twenty-nine ask about somebody in the subject's household. Those figures are printed on the exhibit sheet, they are the Office's figures and not mine, and I have checked them twice because I always do."

"Item three. Four lines from a service return of the Vestry clinic, dated the eighteenth of last month, being the answer of the holder in person to a request for an instrument the clinic is alleged to hold, and the last line is that the readings are not produced."

"That is Tessa Okonkwo's handwriting."

"Yes, and it went into a Wing file on the day it was written, in those words. It is the only item on this roll a member of the subject's household wrote herself, with her name at the top and no instruction to." Tove Marlin turned one page. "Item four is a record kept by the subject's household."

She did not stop for effect and she did not look at him to find out how he was taking it.

"A record kept by the subject's household, to which the subject is not a party and has never been a party, referred to by its own description rather than by its name. Six lines to a day. One line per woman per day. Kept from the twenty-first of last month. Signature refused."

"How many days."

"It does not say." She said it exactly and softened it by no degree. "That is what an Office may write about a document it has seen and is not producing. Four different hands have written the same sentence, because it is the one that does not require the Office to say what the record is."

Nadia Ferrous's lift log came up into Lin Hao's mouth and he did not say it.

"Item five. A delivery record from a service in this district for the week ending the tenth of last month, produced by the holder in answer to a request, carrying forty-one names in the third column."

"I know what that is."

"Nobody on the seventh floor of the Office knows whose it is, because it went into a document without a name attached to it, which is what happens when a person hands a stranger their own page in a lift." Tove Marlin looked at him. "It is on the roll as an exhibit. It is not on the roll as anybody's evidence about anybody. Those are two different sentences, and I have watched the difference get lost eleven times."

"Nadia Ferrous handed that log over on purpose on the tenth. She worked it out in about four seconds, and she has said ever since that if it is used the first thing that happens is to her and not to me."

"Then it is in a document, and being the woman who handed it over is not a defence and is not a mitigation and is not on the roll." She turned a page. "Item six. A district table with a finding number in the corner and the date it was assembled on it, quoted whole, and no clerk's name on the table or on the sheet it is on."

"Is there supposed to be one."

"There is a clerk who rules that table twice a month and there is no requirement that her name appear." She said it in the voice of a woman reading a form. "If somebody upstairs had wanted a hand on that line they would have had one attached, and there is not one, and I am telling you because in about three weeks somebody in this building will notice that there is not one."

"Item seven."

"Item seven is the branch standing discharged on the eighteenth of last month at ten past eleven. Five dates, the sixth and the seventh, both closed, the fourth floor with it. It is on the roll because an Office that cannot produce a member's standing produces the moment it stopped."

"Item eight is mine," she said, before he asked.

"Your refusal."

"His." She said it exactly as written. "Six lines in a clerk's hand: the demand of the seventeenth of last month and the refusal of the eighteenth. It is on the roll because a member who refuses a demand in the correct words on the correct day has produced the truest document in this building, and there is not one Office that can read it and not draw the wrong conclusion out of it."

"Which is what they will do."

"Which is what they will do, in public, in a room with a hundred and forty strangers in it, and I am obliged to say so at the table when it comes up, because it is exhibit eight and the Office is entitled to put it in." She squared the cover. "Item nine."

She turned the roll to the last page and there was a photograph of a ruled space on it, which meant it was a photograph of a space and not a document, and she looked at it for about two seconds longer than she had looked at anything else.

"Item nine is a page from a register on the third floor of this building," she said. "It is ruled in the same book for eleven years, and there is a space at the bottom of a page, and the space is ruled, and it is empty, and it has been empty since before you came to this building.

"It is the only thing in that book that is empty on purpose, and it is on this roll because an Office that has run out of documents puts in a space."

"Since when."

"Since the space was ruled. A year and a half. Every clerk on that floor has walked past it." She said it in the voice of a woman reading a form. "And a space ruled and left alone for a year and a half is evidence of a thing somebody meant to write and did not."

Tove Marlin rolled the cover back over the nine items, tied the straps, and picked the tube up.

"That is nine," she said. "And there are no names on it, and there will be none on it when it goes down to a registrar on the ninth, and none on it on the morning of the twenty-ninth. And if it goes into that hall with a name on any page of it then I will have known about it and I will not have signed it."

"What happens on the seventh."

"On the seventh I give it to you and you have five days. You may acknowledge the roll or object to it, and both are lawful.

"If you acknowledge it, the roll is proved and the documents go into the hall and the Office reads them aloud exactly as they are written. That means a household line, a visitors' book, a clinic's four lines, forty-one names and an empty space will be read out in a municipal hall on Cobb Street in front of everybody who wants to stand at the rail."

"And if I object."

"If you object, your objection goes on the record where nobody has to answer it." She looked at him for the first time in about four minutes.

"That is the whole of it, on both sides, and I have never seen a third thing happen in eleven years. An objection does not stop the roll. It is a piece of paper that says you do not agree, filed next to the roll, and the roll goes into the hall and is read, and your objection is read first if you insist, and the Office will thank you for it."

"So I can lose either way."

"You can be right either way," said Tove Marlin, "and neither costs anything, and that is the sentence I have been saving for the end, because it is the only one I have."

She got as far as the top of the outside stair, stopped, and turned round.

"The twelfth is the last day a registrar on that floor will take anything off me," she said. "It goes down to him on the morning of the ninth and it comes back stamped the same day, and the two sheets are in my hands by noon.

"Lodging is a different thing from answering. Lodging is me carrying the whole set down and handing it over, and I can do that on the ninth or on the twelfth, and if I do it on the ninth then it is in on the ninth.

"And if you do not lodge anything then the Office lodges nothing and the roll is proved by the member not disputing it, which happens more often than not, and which I have never once seen anybody in your position do. I want you to know that I have noticed you have never once not done anything, and that this is the first week of my career in which that has been in my interest."

"Why are you telling me that."

"Because you said no in that room on the nineteenth of last month and put it in a file at a cost to yourself that made no difference whatever to me, and because I read your notice at the window on the second, and I have been uncomfortable for five days.

"I do not think I am the only person in this building who is uncomfortable. I would like to see whether you are the kind of man who does something about it or the kind who is careful."

"I have been the careful one for six weeks."

"I know." She went down the outside stair. "It has not gone well and I am not going to pretend to a woman in an empty corridor on a day off that it has."

She was gone by the time he looked down the stairwell.

Nine items. No names.

There are seven women in this city who belong to him and six of them keep a register, and not one of them is on it, and every document any of them has produced is sitting in a leather tube at the bottom of the stairs with a number against it, waiting to be read out loud in a hall with a hundred and forty seats.

They had not asked the household a question. They had asked about the household, out loud, in four offices and two districts and one lift, and had come back with nine documents and no names, and in about eight weeks a clerk will read every one of them into a room that will not be interested in the difference.

He went down the outside stair and stood at the bottom of it in the wet, in front of a frame with forty sheets on it and one of them his.

He put his hand flat on the wet rail and found that his hands were cold, and that the cold was not the grey and was not in his temple, and had nothing to do with anything except being a man in the rain at the foot of four flights of stairs holding a decision he had five days to make.

Behind him, on the third floor, a ward clerk was putting a broom against a door and had not been asked a third time about anything at all.

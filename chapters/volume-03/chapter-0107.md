# The Chairs They Put Out

He went to the Reading on the sixth because it was a Saturday and half the building was in and the district board outside it did not change, and because a man who has been told a date and a room and a number on a plate ought at least to go and look at the building, and because the third floor was the one floor in that building he had a reason to know about.

There was no lift on that side. He went up the stairs past a corridor with the boards bare and the marks of four table legs on the floor, the same four marks that had been there on the fifteenth of last month, and at the top of the last flight a door stood open on a room that had been shut since the twenty-sixth of last month.

Eight chairs stood against the far wall in two rows of four, and one chair stood on its own, square, facing a door four feet out from the wall.

He knew it in about a second and a half and he had to put his hand on the door frame.

It is the same arrangement. That is the whole of it and it was put there on purpose by somebody who has read a corridor on a sub-level, or who sent somebody to read one, and it is the arrangement eight chairs and a ninth square to a door, which he had stood on a stair and looked at in the dark three weeks ago with two of them set at an angle for people who were not coming either.

The door four feet out from the wall was not a classified door. It was an office door with a glazed upper panel and a knob on it, and it was unlocked, and it opened onto a store room with brooms in it.

"So that is not what you came to see," said Sabine Rooke.

She was sitting in the corridor on the ninth chair. She had her hands on her knees and her grey armband on, and there was a card on the seat beside her with a number in the top corner, and she had a broom-propped door at the end of the corridor holding it open because the stairwell draught came up it.

"Eleven days," she said. "I told you the room was shut and it was staying shut and that was not my decision and it was not my room any more, and I was right, and on the second of this month eight men came up the outside stair at about six in the evening with a handcart and put them where you are looking."

She did not get up.

"They put eight up and they did not put a ninth anywhere, and they did not ask me, and they left at about twenty past six, and I sat on this chair until the building shut and then I went home." She looked into the room.

"I moved it on the first, before any of that, and I want that in the right order. I came in on the first with a broom and a floor cloth and I put it four feet out from that wall, because it had been against that wall for eleven years and I had been sitting on it for a fortnight, and a chair against a wall is a room nobody has arranged.

"I squared the eight on the third, on my own time, and it took me twenty minutes. I have not told anybody and nobody has asked me."

Lin Hao looked at the chair.

"You moved the ninth one."

"The ninth one is the one I have been sitting on since the twenty-seventh of last month and the one nobody came back for." She said it without heat. "It does not belong to a set and it does not belong to a floor, and I have never been told to move it and never been told to keep it.

"Eight chairs are furniture. A ninth chair on its own is a statement about a room, and I am a woman who arranges rooms. If I left eight against a wall and nothing square to the door, then whoever comes in here will make the room out of the eight, and that is what eight chairs are for."

He understood that he had walked up the outside stair and was standing in front of the arrangement that had been made out of his own household's furniture on a night in the middle of last month, and that a woman had looked at it for eleven days and had decided that the correct answer was to complete it.

"You have been asked twice about a Thursday," he said.

"The twelfth of last month and the twenty-seventh of last month, both by a woman with a card, both very polite, both about a Thursday in a month that has gone." Sabine Rooke said it with her hands on her knees.

"And I told her the true thing both times, which is that I set that room up on the same schedule every week for eleven years and there were nine people who worked there, and I could not have told you which Thursday was which without the sheet."

"You told me on the fifteenth that the third one is a file."

"I did." She nodded once. "Nobody has asked me a third time. I want you to notice that, because you came up here with a face on you that says a Thursday has been used up, and nobody has asked me about a Thursday and nobody has sent anybody with a card to this corridor since the twenty-seventh. Whatever they want, it is not a Thursday."

"Then what is it."

"That is the question I have been asking since the second," said Sabine Rooke. "I have an answer and I do not like it, and I am going to give it to you anyway, because you have been in this building twice and both times you did not make me repeat myself."

She stood up off the chair for the first time and crossed to the door of the room and stood in the frame with her hand on it, and did not go in.

"It is not a hearing room. It has never been a hearing room. There has never been a hearing on this floor in eleven years and there is no bench and no rail and no plate on the door, and it is not that kind of room and nobody in this building has ever pretended otherwise."

"Then what is it for."

"It is the room the parties are put in." She said it exactly. "While the hall is cleared. A hall takes time to clear, and longer when the Office has to put a hundred and forty strangers' names on a sheet to find out which of them are on the roll.

"Until that is done the parties sit somewhere with the door shut. Not locked. Shut. And they sit in here with each other and with whatever the Office has sent up in advance, because the alternative is a corridor, and a corridor is public."

He looked at the eight chairs.

"And there are nine items in this building right now," he said, "and they are going to be in this room."

"There are nine items on a roll on the floor above this one and they have been since the second, and I have seen them go past this door twice, and I know it is nine because I counted the boxes." She said it with the flatness of a woman who counts things.

"A roll is a list of documents. Every document that goes into a hearing goes on the roll first, and the roll is built in the one room in the Reading with two doors and a table that takes eight feet. That is this room, because I set it up eleven years ago for nine people and it was already the right size."

"So the Office has decided to put the roll in this room."

"The Office has decided to put the roll in this room and to put you in it with it." She let that stand for a second. "That is the whole of what this floor has been doing since the twenty-sixth and nobody has told me and I have not asked, and I have worked out the rest of it on my own from what came past my door, and I would like you to hear it from me before you hear it in a hall."

"That is worse than a threat."

"It is a room," said Sabine Rooke. "It is a room with nine chairs and a table and a shut door, and tomorrow a man who arranges rooms is going to put nine boxes of paper on that table and two men who do not speak to each other are going to sit down on two of those chairs and wait to be called. And everything that has ever frightened anybody in this building happened to a person in one of those chairs."

He was aware, standing in that doorway, of how bad it was, and of how completely ordinary the room was. The floor had been swept. There was a cloth folded on the table. There were marks in the wax on the window ledge where somebody had put a cup down on a day with nothing in it for eleven years.

"You have not asked me to take anything," he said.

"No."

"You have not asked me for the sheet."

"No, and I am not going to, and I want you to understand that I have decided that and not the Office and not anybody." She took her hand off the frame.

"I have been asked twice about a Thursday and both times the honest answer was that I could not tell you which Thursday was which without the sheet. That is still true.

"If somebody sends a woman up here a third time with a date on a card, then somebody in this building has decided that a schedule for eleven years is evidence of something. On that day I will say the true thing again, which is that I do not know. And after that this floor stops being a corridor that arranges furniture."

"You have thought about that a great deal for eleven days."

"I have had nothing else to do with them," said Sabine Rooke. "There is no meeting on this floor. There has not been a meeting on this floor since the twenty-sixth of last month and there were nine chairs in it then and nine people in them, and two of those nine were for people who did not come, and I have known that since the seventh and I have told nobody, and I have just told you."

She sat down again on the chair that was not one of the eight.

"There is a queue at the door on the seventh," she said. "Not today. It is there on the seventh and the eighth, because the roll goes down to a registrar on the ninth and a registrar comes back with a line on a sheet, and there will be a queue of people on this floor who are allowed to read the roll before the date, and you are one of them, and you will be let up one at a time."

Lin Hao stood in the doorway.

At half past two that afternoon, in a corridor on the third floor of the Reading, eleven people had come up the outside stair in forty minutes.

Two of them had a plate in a bag and had taken it down to the socket on the ground floor and come back up with the sound of a machine in their ears.

Three had come up from the lamp-shop at the bottom of the road with a card and a folded sheet and had not wanted to go back down for it.

A woman had come up with a boy holding her sleeve and had gone down four flights with him rather than use the side door, and had come down them again with him holding on to the rail.

A man had stood in the corridor and looked at the eight chairs and the ninth one for a minute and gone away without saying anything. A boy had come up to look at the room because the room was open. Two men had worked out between them which of the nine chairs they wanted.

Eleven people, up those stairs, in forty minutes, on a day when nothing in that building was closed, in a building with no standing in it. And every one of them had been let in by somebody.

It had gone into the book that afternoon without his noticing it, the way it had gone in four thousand times across four years. It was the first thing he had ever written down that was not a face and a street and an hour, and it was a day when nobody wanted anything from anybody, and he understood now that the Office had understood it too and had built a room out of it.

"Sabine," he said.

"Do not."

"You have been arranging this room for eleven years and nobody has ever asked you what it is for, and a woman with a card has been up here twice, and tomorrow there is going to be a table with nine boxes on it and two men who do not speak to each other sitting in it."

"Yes," said Sabine Rooke.

"Then you know what it is for."

"I have known since the second," she said. "I have arranged a room for a member who never came for eleven years and I have never once been told what the room was for, and you have just told me in about ninety seconds, and you did it in a corridor on a Saturday with a sweep in the bucket and me sat on a chair."

She picked up her card off the seat.

"Go down the stairs," she said. "There is nobody on the desk at the bottom and the outside stair is wet. And do not come up here on the seventh. You will be let up with the queue, and you will read the roll in here with me standing in the doorway, and I am telling you now that I will be standing in the doorway, because that is where I stand, and I am not going to be asked to stand anywhere else."

He went down the outside stair and it was wet, and the district board at the foot of it had his notice on it in the middle of forty others, and two people were reading it in the rain with an umbrella between them and neither of them was looking at the man coming down the steps.

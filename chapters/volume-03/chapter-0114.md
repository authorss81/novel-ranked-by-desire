# Down And Unsigned

The classification paper went up three floors at four o'clock on the fourteenth, unsigned, and Lin Hao watched it go from the register counter with his hands at his sides and understood that he had been watching it go every working day since it was written and would go on watching it.

He had cleaned six floors before noon with the fourth-floor washrooms last, because the fourth floor was discharged and nobody used it and the dirt there was a week old and undisturbed, and on the second floor a clerk had stopped him with a form about a tap that ran brown and he had written the tap's number on his cuff and fixed it by half past eleven.

It had gone into the book without his noticing, the way everything went in. A tap, a floor, an hour. The cause named where it happened, and no number anywhere.

At the register counter the morning queue had been the ordinary queue, and Anneke had served it and squared the book, and the month-end return had sat in the tray waiting for four o'clock, late again, the way it would be late every month until somebody reinstated a standing or told the office to stop printing it.

Anneke Roos carried it up herself that day, because the runner with the wrist was back up two flights and the other runner had a queue at the Exchange. She put it in the tray and squared it the way she squared everything and came back down with the tray empty and her face doing nothing at all.

"It is dated the nineteenth of last month," she said, at the counter, to Drant and not to him. "Five paragraphs and a ruled sixth space. It has gone up every working day since the day after it was written and it has come back every evening with nothing on it."

Drant did not look up from the register. "Then it goes up tomorrow."

"It goes up tomorrow and it comes back tomorrow, and the hand three floors up draws the heading and does not sign it, and I enter it as carried and returned." She put her hands flat on the counter. "I am the woman who carries an unsigned paper up three flights of stairs in both directions and writes down that she did it. There is a word for that and the word is not clerk."

Drant turned a page of the register without looking at it. "The word is register. A register writes down that a thing moved. It does not say why it moved or what it means that it came back. That is the whole of the post and you have held it five weeks."

"I have held it close on four weeks and I will hold it four more, and I want it entered that I said so." She looked at Lin Hao for the first time. "You are standing at this counter watching your own paper go up. Go and mop something. A man watching paper does not make it sign itself."

He went and mopped the ground-floor corridor, which was the only answer anybody had given him all day, and the corridor came clean, which was more than any paper had done.

Lin Hao said nothing. He was cleaning six floors in a building where the paper about him moved on a schedule and he moved round it, and that was the employment he still held and would go on holding.

He was frustrated. That was the feeling, and it had been building for days under the other ones like damp under paint. He wanted something in this city to move because he had pushed it, instead of moving round him while he stood still.

What he wanted, that afternoon, was for one of the two papers to be signed. Either one. The classification paper three floors up, or the terms in a drawer in Bellhouse. He wanted a hand to come down on something and end it.

He said so to Marguerite Vale that evening, standing in her hall with the visitors' book shut on the table between them.

"Take the terms up," he said. "Out of the drawer. Put them on the table and sign them and let them be in force. The house needs something that binds and the Office has nine boxes and we have a shut book and a landing."

Marguerite looked at him for a long time. Then she went up the inside stair and came back down with a folded sheet that had been in a drawer on the second floor since the season before last, unsigned, and put it on the table without opening it.

"No," she said.

"Marguerite."

"No, and I am saying it in my own hall so that you hear the shape of it." She put her hand flat on the folded sheet. "These terms have been down since the fourteenth of last month. They are unsigned and they are out of force and they cannot come back into force, because a term taken up to meet a hearing is a term the hearing owns."

She took her hand off the sheet.

"I priced you in three rooms and I am still pricing you. That is outside the Vant and it stays outside the Vant. The moment I sign this, what I do in those rooms is guild business, and guild business is attachable, and everything I have built since the fifth walks into the hall on a leash."

Nadia Ferrous came in from the scullery with a kettle and stopped in the doorway, and set the kettle down, and stayed.

"He is asking you to bind the house," she said.

"He is asking me to bind myself," said Marguerite, "because he is frustrated and he wants a hand to come down, and he has mistaken a hand coming down for a thing happening."

"Then bind me instead," said Nadia. "I am already in a delivery record with forty-one names on it. I am already the consequence and it is already mine. Put my name on your terms and let the house have one signature somewhere."

Marguerite looked at her for a long time. Then she shook her head, once.

"No. My terms take one hand and one hand only, and the hand is mine, and the hand stays in the drawer. I will not spend you to settle him, and I will not spend myself to settle him either. That is the whole of what down and unsigned means. It means nobody's hand comes down, including mine."

Lin Hao picked up the folded sheet. It was light. Two pages, creased at the fold, with his name nowhere on it. He put it back down.

"An office argues about objects," said Marguerite, "and nothing happens. That is the whole of the discipline. Your paper goes up at four and comes back unsigned. My terms sit in a drawer and stay down. The roll sits in nine boxes and waits. Nothing happens, and nothing happening is the only thing anybody in this city is allowed to do on purpose."

He went home up the long road with the knowledge in his coat that neither paper had moved. Forty-six days. Seventeen days left in this month after the fourteenth, and twenty-nine in the next, and both papers exactly where they had been.

At the corner of Ferrier Street he stopped and looked back at the road he had walked for four years. A porter went past with a parcel. A woman read a notice on a wall and walked on. Nothing in the street knew that two papers had failed to be signed that day, and nothing in the street would have cared.

On the Vestry arcade the stone bench stood empty with the brass plate on the end of it, given in memory of a woman who had carried trays up two flights for nineteen years. He sat on it for a while with his hands on his knees, because the bench was there and he was passing.

He climbed the stairs above the chop-house and sat at the table with the notebook shut in front of him. Six lines a day. One line per woman per day. Signature refused.

Ola Bright came in with her pad and sat down opposite him and dated a page.

"Say it," she said.

"Two papers," he said. "One goes up and comes back. One sits in a drawer and stays down. Nothing happened today."

She wrote it down and read it back, and he corrected one word, which was the second time in a week he had corrected anything anybody had written.

"That is the whole of the day," she said.

"That is the whole of it, and the whole of it is that nobody's hand came down." He looked at the shut book between them. "Yours is the only hand in this house that writes things down without being asked, and I want it entered that I noticed."

She wrote that down too, and dated it, and read it back, and he did not correct a word.

"Then that is the whole of the day," she said, "and I will read it back to you the same tomorrow, and the day after, until one of us turns up with something that is not a day."

She went out and he sat at the table with a shut book in front of him and two unsigned papers in two buildings behind him, and the frustration went out of him all at once like water off a sill, and what was left underneath it was something colder and more useful.

Nobody was going to sign anything for him. That was the answer, and it had taken him four days to arrive at it, and it was the first true thing the fourteenth had given him.

The papers would go on going up and sitting down, unsigned, out of force, every working day until the twenty-ninth, and the only hand in Ostara that was going to come down on anything was his own.

# What She Cannot Keep

The eighteenth was a Thursday, and the Board repainted at nine, and Lin Hao did not go to the square to watch it, because he had an hour at the second bench past the plate office and nothing in the city could have moved it.

He went up the back stair of the Exchange building at half past nine with his hands clean and his coat brushed, which was the most preparation he had ever made for any room, and knocked on the second door past the plate office, and a woman's voice told him to come in and not to wipe his feet.

The square below had been full when he passed it, ladders up and the repaint going on without him, tile over tile, name over name. He had not stopped. For the first time in four years a repaint had gone on without his eyes on the wall, and the wall had not minded, and neither, to his surprise, had he.

The workroom was narrow and bright, with a bench along one wall and a machine on the bench and paper output stacked beside it in three piles. The machine was smaller than he had expected. It had two sockets on the front and a slot at the side where the paper came out, and a handle on top worn pale by seven years of hands.

The woman at the bench was Wren Kallo.

She was standing with a screwdriver in one hand and a plate in the other, and she looked at him once, fully, from his boots to his face, and put both down on the bench.

"You are the member," she said. "You sent a note with no envelope. I read it three times and I showed it to Cressida on the bench and to the runner who brought it, because I show everything to somebody, and then I answered it, which is more than I do for most paper."

"I am Lin Hao."

"I know who you are. You are forty-two days from a hall." Thirteen days left in this month after the eighteenth, and twenty-nine in the next, and she had done the arithmetic the way everybody in this building did, as bookkeeping. "Six weeks. Sit down. Not on the stool with the wobble. That one is for people I like."

He sat on the other stool. The machine clicked once, by itself, and was still.

"What I want first," he said, "is to look at the woman who made the machine, before the Office tells us what we are to each other."

She leaned back against the bench with her arms folded. She was direct in a way nobody in his life was direct, not even Runa. It was not rudeness. It was a woman who had decided years ago that the time spent softening a sentence was time stolen from the bench.

"I built it seven years ago out of a Field request and a broken adding machine and a winter with nothing in it," she said. "The desk has run it since. Nobody asked me about it until the sixteenth, when a runner brought a request up the stair and waited while I read it twice."

"And you confirmed."

"I confirmed in my hand on the line at the foot, and I stated the form under it, two columns, subject and counterparty, and then I told the runner what it was, and the plate-office clerk, and Cressida, and my sister on the tram home." She counted them off on her fingers. "Four people. It is ten o'clock on Thursday morning and four people know your name came up my stair."

"Why outside the roll," he said. "Seven years benching and no standing post."

"Because a bench is a place and a post is a leash." She said it without heat. "A standing post signs a roll and sits a schedule and can be compelled by it. I bench by request and I answer by hand and no office owns my mornings."

"The desk tried twice to put me on the roll and twice the bench told them a builder on a roll is a builder who stops building, and the desk went away, because the desk needs the building more than it needs the roll."

She picked up the screwdriver and put it down again.

He looked at her. She looked back without apology and without defiance, the way a woman looks at a thing she has stopped trying to change.

"I cannot keep a secret," she said. "That is the whole of me and there is no other version. It made me an excellent auditor, because an auditor who repeats everything she reads is an auditor nobody has to watch, and it has made me a poor friend for longer than you have been cleaning washrooms."

"Everybody on this bench knows it. It is why I am seven years outside the standing roll with nobody minding."

He felt something turn over in his chest. Not pity. Recognition. He had spent four months refusing to be described, and here was a woman who could not stop describing.

"What I want," she said, "is the thing I have never asked anybody for, and I am asking you for it now, in this room, with the machine on the bench and the door shut but not locked."

She unfolded her arms and put her hands flat on the bench edge behind her.

"I am off instruments from tomorrow. Bench order, dated yesterday, effective the nineteenth. A builder named in a hearing cannot go on benching the tool she is named on. Seven years on this bench and from tomorrow I am paper review behind a door."

"That is the post you are losing."

"That is the post." She nodded once. "And here is the ask, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because nobody in this building has ever let me get to the end of anything."

She took a breath.

"Make me a party to it. To the proceeding. To the thing I am losing my post over. Name me as your witness, the one the form allows, the one you told the hearing officer you would not bring. I would rather answer for this machine as a party than discover what it does as a stranger, read back to me in a hall by a clerk with a good voice."

The machine clicked once, by itself, and was still.

He was quiet for a long time. He could hear the plate office queue through the wall, and Cressida's bench on the other side, and the trams on Cobb Street four floors down.

What he felt was not what he had expected. He had expected wariness, or calculation, or the particular hunger he had seen in rooms where women wanted something from him. What he felt instead was a clean, cold clarity, like water off a sill.

She could not keep a secret. If he made her a party, she would repeat everything. The decision, the reason, the hour, the room. By the end of anything, the secret would be four people deep and she would have told three of them.

He decided it on purpose, having calculated that a woman outside the standing roll who cannot keep a secret is a woman who will have to answer for it, and that he would rather she answered for it as a party than discovered it as a stranger.

"I will name you," he said, out loud, in the room. "As my one witness. You will stand in the hall on the twenty-ninth and answer for the machine you built, as a party to the proceeding, and not as a stranger the Office reads back."

She repeated it back, word for word, because she could not help herself. "You will name me as your one witness. I will stand in the hall on the twenty-ninth and answer for the machine I built, as a party to the proceeding, and not as a stranger."

Then she laughed, one syllable, with her hand over her mouth.

"There," she said. "It is already out of the room. Cressida will have it by eleven. That is what you have bought, and I told you the price before you paid it, and you paid it anyway."

He stood up off the stool. She was still leaning against the bench with her hand over her mouth, and she took it away, and what was on her face was not triumph and not grief. It was relief, plain and unguarded, the relief of a woman who has been carrying a thing alone and has just been told she will carry it in public.

"That is the decision," she said. "It cannot be unmade. A witness named is a witness named, and the Office has your refusal of one on file from the third, and now it has your naming of one, and both are in your hand."

"I know."

"Then here is the rest of it, and it is mine, and it is not part of the decision." She stood away from the bench. "I have wanted you since the sixteenth. Not the member. You. Since the runner came down the stair with my signature on a line and three clerks initialled the envelope and I understood that the man at the bottom of it had sent a note with no envelope so that anybody could read it."

She said it the way she said everything, directly, with no softening and no hurry.

"I am not impaired and I have had water and bread and nothing else, and I am awake, and nobody has told me to say this, and if you do not want it you will say so and I will go back to the bench and we will never mention it."

He looked at her for a long time. The workroom was bright. The door was shut but not locked. The machine sat on the bench with its handle worn pale.

"I want it," he said.

She locked the door. It was the only lock in the room, and she turned it herself, with her own hand, and put the key on the bench where they could both see it.

"You cannot be sworn to me," she said, unbuttoning her cuffs. "Sworn to a man at six, I am a woman nobody in my division speaks to. I am telling you so that you know what you are not getting. What you are getting is this room and this morning and me, and all three are outside every roll anybody keeps."

She took off her coat and hung it on the back of the wobble stool, and then her blouse, with the quick practical movements of a woman who works with her hands. Underneath she was pale from seven years indoors, with a burn scar on one forearm and ink on the other wrist, and she stood there in the bright workroom and let him look.

"Come here," she said.

He came. She put her hands on his face and turned it toward the light, the way she had looked at him when he came in, fully, from boots to face, and then she put her mouth on his, and it was not careful and not practised. It was hungry, and certain, and glad.

They got his shirt open between them, her fingers on the buttons, his on hers, getting in each other's way and laughing once, briefly, into each other's mouths. Her skin was warm from the bench room's heat. His hands were shaking and she took one and held it flat between both of hers until it stopped.

He laughed, surprised, with his forehead against her shoulder. She smelled of paste and oil and soap.

She got him onto the bench room's narrow cot, which stood against the wall under the window with a folded blanket on it, and went down on him with her hair falling forward over both of them, and took her time, and told him to put his hands behind his head, and counted nothing, and did not hurry at all.

The light came through the window in a bar across the cot. Dust moved in it. Somewhere below, the plate office queue shuffled forward one place.

He said something out loud that he had not planned to say, and she laughed against him, one syllable, and held on, and did not let him go until he had said it twice more, and the third time it was her name.

Then she climbed up over him, straddling his hips with her knees on either side, and took him inside her with one hand between them guiding, and sat back on him with her head thrown back and her hands flat on his chest, and moved, slowly at first and then with nothing slow in it.

The cot creaked under them, once, twice, finding its rhythm. The bar of light moved a finger's width across her shoulder. Below, the plate office queue shuffled forward one place, and somebody laughed, and the trams went past on Cobb Street with the wire humming.

He got his hands on her hips and she let him keep them there, which was the first thing that morning she had let anybody keep, and she worked against them and said so, plainly, in words with nothing in them but want, and then said his name again, twice, the second time with nothing in it but arrival.

She came with her hands flat on his chest and her head thrown back, and did not cover her mouth, and the sound went up to the ceiling of the workroom and came back down, and neither of them hushed it.

Then she bent forward over him, breathing hard, with her hair down round both their faces, and held him inside her until he spent, with her eyes open and on his, and it took longer, and she let it take longer, and told him twice to stop apologising with his hands.

Afterwards she lay along him with her weight on his side and one hand flat on his chest, counting his heart, and said into the collar of his open shirt:

"Nothing moved. I would know. I have run that machine for seven years and I know what moving feels like, and nothing in this room went anywhere but where we put it."

"That is the only thing anybody can say about it."

"That is the only thing anybody can say about it, and it is worth more than every paper on that bench." She sat up and reached for her blouse. "No draw. No cold. No minute. You touched nobody you owed and nobody owed you, and the machine sat on the bench and watched, and that is the whole of the record."

She dressed before he did, quick and practical, cuffs buttoned, hair pinned back with two pins from the bench. She looked exactly as she had before, except for her mouth, which was the whole of the cost and which she did not intend to repair.

She unlocked the door herself and put the key back in the drawer, and as she did it there was a knock, quick and familiar, and a woman's voice called through the wood.

"Wren. Eleven o'clock. Have you got the member in there or have you got a fault."

Wren looked at Lin Hao, and smiled the smile with nothing professional in it, and opened the door.

Cressida stood in the corridor with a requisition in her hand and took in the cot and the blanket and the two of them with one practised glance.

"The member," said Wren Kallo, before Cressida could speak. "He named me his witness for the twenty-ninth. I answer for the machine as a party. He decided it on purpose and I repeated it back."

Cressida looked at Lin Hao. Then she nodded once, slowly, and wrote something on the requisition.

"Then the bench knows," she said. "Eleven o'clock, as predicted." She looked back at Wren. "Paper review tomorrow. I will miss your clicking."

She went down the corridor with the requisition, and Wren shut the door, and leaned her forehead against it for a second, and laughed, one syllable.

"There," she said. "Four people, and the bench by noon. That is what you have bought, and the price keeps being paid, and you paid it anyway."

"Go down the stair," she said. "Cressida has been. By noon the bench will know you were here, because I will have told them, and by evening my sister will know, and by tomorrow the paper review door will shut behind me with my name on a list."

He stood in the doorway with his coat on. She put her hand on his chest, flat, over his heart.

"You decided it on purpose," she said. "A woman who cannot keep a secret, answering as a party. Say it once more, so I have it straight."

"I decided it on purpose," he said. "You answer as a party. I would rather you answered than discovered."

She repeated it back, word for word, because she could not help herself, and then she smiled, and it was the first smile he had seen in that building that had nothing professional in it at all.

He went down the back stair past the plate office, where the queue stood with plates in their hands, and out into Cobb Street with forty-two days in front of him and six weeks exactly, and a witness named where no witness had been, and a woman behind him who had told four people before breakfast and would tell a bench by noon and a sister by evening.

He had made her a party to the thing she was losing her post over, out loud, in the room. He could not un-tell what she would repeat. That was the decision, and it was made, and it could not be unmade, and he walked home up the long road with it sitting in his chest like a second heart.

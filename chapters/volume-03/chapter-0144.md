# Nine Minutes Before

The five days from the twenty-third to the twenty-seventh went by without a single thing in them that belongs in a proceeding, and he cleaned six floors on each of them and went to bed and did not sleep, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth he asked the hearing officer's clerk for a look at the room.

It is the ordinary thing. A party may view the hall on the afternoon before the sitting, and the rule is there because a man who has not seen a room can be ambushed by a room, and the Office put the rule in itself in the spring and has been quoted back at itself twice since.

The caretaker let him in at four in the afternoon and told him he had nine minutes, and the doors are locked at nine, and she said the nine minutes the way a woman says a thing she has said every day for eleven years.

He had a piece of chalk in his hand and she had given it to him at the door without being asked, and he did not know what she was going to use it for until she came back with a pail at about the fourth minute.

He counted. He counted all nine because he is a man who counts, and he counted them out loud for the first three and then stopped and did the rest in his head.

The first minute was the doors. Two, off the back row on the left, and eighty-one feet from the near one to the table, and the table set square to the far wall with its long side to the bench, and a hall that is eighty feet long means that anybody who comes in has two and a half minutes of walking before they arrive, which is enough time to decide and not enough to change your mind.

The second was the table itself. Eight feet by four, a cloth on it that had been down since eight in the morning, and in the middle of the cloth a rectangle in chalk where the machine's base goes, and the rectangle was about two feet square, and it was not in the middle of the table, it was two feet to the left of the middle.

Two feet to the left. Facing the bench.

The third minute was the rail. It is four feet off the table's left edge from end to end, and there is a gap at the end for the parties to come and go, and the gap is three feet wide and it is the only way into the area.

Four feet, and three feet of gap, and a hundred and forty chairs behind it, and two hundred and thirty people in the building.

The fourth was the bench. The hearing officer sits four inches higher than the table and nine feet from it, and the clerk sits square to the door so that the clerk can see the room and the officer can see the clerk, and Ivo Sarn has been given a chair at the end of the bench where he can get up and go to the table without crossing in front of anybody.

And Wren Kallo, who is going to be asked what a machine does, has been given a chair at the near end of the bench with nothing in front of it at all, because a witness who has to stand up through two hundred people to reach a table is a witness who will not stand up.

The fifth was the ninety. They had come in on a handcart on Thursday morning and gone in at the back in two rows and they are the same as the bench chairs, and there is not one rank in the room that can be told from where it is sitting, which is the single most useful thing the Office has ever done to that hall.

The sixth minute was the cleaner.

She came in with the pail and worked from the far wall toward the table, and she was going to take the corner by the rail where the light comes off the high windows and put them low, and he was standing on the wrong side of the table for her.

He put his hand flat on the table.

"Sorry. I am in your way."

She went round. Her wrist passed within four inches of his hand at the second flag and he kept the hand where it was and stayed where he was until she had gone past, and he did not say anything else and she did not look up.

Four inches. It had been four inches on the second floor of the Fourth Branch a fortnight ago and he had put a hand back off a plate and told a woman the sill was wet.

He stood there for a moment with his palm flat on a cloth on a table in a hall he was going to be in for four hours in the morning, and understood that in four hours in a full room there would not be one moment like that one. There would be ninety of them, and every one of them would come at him from four feet away and without a pail in the way, and none of them would be reaching for anything.

The seventh minute was the chair.

There is a chair at the party's table. It is in the works department's list along with the table and the cloth and the chalk, and it is a chair like any other chair in that building and it has a back on it.

He said it out loud to the caretaker, who was at the door.

"No chair."

She said, "They put it out every time."

"I am not having it. Take it out of the room on Thursday morning and put it back on Friday, and if the hearing officer's clerk wants it there for the record I will say it again to the clerk on the steps and she can write it down."

She looked at him for a second longer than the request needed, and then she said, "Right," which is the whole of what a caretaker says about a request that will be obeyed.

She wrote a line on a card and gave it to him, and the card said the chair had been struck from the sitting at the member's request. That is a thing that exists in the world, on paper, with a date on it, because he had asked for it and a woman had gone and got a card.

He put the card in with the builder's statement and the bench slip and the sheet of a clerk's copy of the roll in his own coat.

The left-hand end of an eight-foot table is where you stand to be examined, and he had worked that out in the second minute and had not let himself finish the thought, and standing there without a chair in it at four in the afternoon in the wet he finished it: a man without a chair is a man who expects to be there a long time.

The eighth minute was the only one he had not thought about.

The machine is on the table. The machine is two feet square and it is put down two feet to the left of the middle of the table facing the bench, and a machine is a thing you stand next to, and standing next to it puts him at the left-hand end of a table eight feet long with the whole of the front row about eleven feet from his hands.

He did not write it down. He had never written it down. It was in the one drawer in the one room he had in this city, in the one hand that had ever been written in and it was not on the roll and it was not in Tove Marlin's day book and it was not on Wren Kallo's bench paper, and there was no version of the morning in which that got read out.

Not one thing in that building was going to put it into a document on the strength of one afternoon. It had been that way for four years and it was going to be that way at ten o'clock tomorrow for as long as he kept it, which was the whole of what he had.

The ninth minute the doors were locked, and she said so from the corner with the flag in her hand, and he walked the eighty-one feet back to them and counted it wrong on purpose, because he did not want to arrive knowing the number, and the caretaking woman stood at the door and let him have it.

He stood outside on the steps in the wet at four in the afternoon with a card about a chair.

One day. One less the twenty-eighth, and both in this month.

A clerk had written down that he would be in that room from ten until two, that he would touch no person in it, and that he would move nothing on any person in it, and had written it in her own hand on the fourteenth and put her name to it, and it could not be withdrawn by him or by the Office.

A builder had written down the same four lines in a workroom on the eighth and signed them and put them through Cressida into a file.

A woman who copies things had held the four hours for nine days and given the page back on the day she said she would.

He stood on the steps for about a minute with all of that, and none of it was his any more, and the thing he noticed, standing there in the wet with a pail going on somewhere behind a locked door, was that for the first time in five months he had nothing at all left to say in advance about himself.

Six women had a notebook and no signature on it. A woman on a landing had a refusal that was four months old. A woman in a corridor four feet by nine had a hearing that was not hers. A woman in a clinic had a pencil line in a day book on the first of every month. A broker had priced a house at less than nothing. A woman in a room four streets away had forty-one names and a file that went to a counter every Friday.

Every one of them had already put something down. Every one of them had done it before he asked and most of them had done it against him, and in fourteen hours he was going to walk into a room and find out whether any of it had been worth doing.

The doors were locked behind him and the hall was dark and it held two hundred and thirty.

# What The Visitors' Book Shows

Marguerite Vale read the visitors' book the way she read it every week since the first, out loud, at the hall table, with the glass off the mantel beside her and six women round the table in their coats, and Lin Hao sat at the end of it with his hands round a cup and listened to his house being counted.

Past ninety entries. The porter numbered every one and the week's numbers ran past the nineties, and eleven were the courier with the badge and four were the fish woman who wrote carp in the column, which was the porter's decision and which Marguerite had not corrected in six weeks.

"Thirty-one of them asked about somebody," she said. "Not about the building. About a person. And on fourteen of the thirty-one the person they asked about is one of you, and I am not going to say which, and that is still the rule."

Six days since Tove Marlin read that number out of the roll on the seventh, and the book has taken five more entries since, and two of the five asked about a person, and one of those two asked about somebody in this house.

She did not say it in those words. She put her thumb on the line and let the two sets of numbers do it. The numbers on the page were hers. The numbers in the grey box three streets away were the Office's, and the Office's still said twenty-nine and thirteen, and would go on saying twenty-nine and thirteen on the twenty-ninth.

She turned two pages. The room waited. This was the part of the week nobody hurried.

"And this one is in the porter's hand," she said, "and it is dated the eleventh, and it is a caller who came at half past seven in the evening with a buff square in her hand and would not leave it and would not be without it."

She put her finger under the entry.

"The caller gave the business column one sentence. The porter wrote it down because he writes down the business and because a man with a glass can see better than a man without one. Five words."

She read them out.

"'Nell Ardery holds one square.'"

Nobody said anything. Beatrix Halm put her cup down.

"That is somebody else's hand," said Marguerite, "on our page, naming one of three women who each hold one buff square and have never been named on any page in this house. The caller did not give her own name. She gave somebody else's, and the somebody else's is a woman with a square dated the nineteenth of last month, and the porter has drawn a box round the name because he does not know it."

Lin Hao looked at the book. The box round the name was in the porter's careful hand, square and exact.

"One square is named," said Nadia Ferrous. "Two are not. And the caller of the third is still a caller with four words and no name."

"Ask him what it is," said Ola Bright, from the end of the table. "That is still all it says."

"That is still all it says." Marguerite turned the book round and pushed it down the table to him. "A stranger who cannot get a name out of six women in six weeks has walked into my house and written somebody else's name in my porter's book. Somebody has been giving this address away somewhere that is not this desk, and giving it to somebody who writes things down, and now one of three squares has a name on a page."

He felt it go through the room like a draught. Six women and a book and a house that kept no registers except the ones it kept, and every document any of them had produced sitting in nine grey boxes on an eight-foot table in the Reading.

He was ashamed. That was the feeling, and it had a shape he recognised: the shame of a man who has brought an office to a house and is sitting at the table eating its bread.

What he wanted was to take the page out of the book and burn it. He could not. The book was Marguerite's and the page was the porter's and the name was somebody else's, and there was no version of burning it that did not make a second document out of the burning.

"Leave it," said Beatrix Halm, before he spoke. "It is one name and the Office already has forty-one others. A name in a porter's box is not a name on a roll."

"It is a name on a page in my house," said Marguerite, "and my house is not on the roll, and that is the whole of the protection anybody in it has. Six women keep a record and the record is shut and the signature is refused. That is the logic. It has held for three weeks."

"It held until somebody else wrote in it," said Nadia.

They argued it for twenty minutes, in their own words, six women round a table with a book open between them. Tessa said the book was a glass and a glass showed whoever stood in front of it, and a house that stopped showing people was a house with the curtains drawn, and curtains were the first thing an office cut through.

Beatrix said a square was a square and naming one did not name two, and the Wing had held her own square since the nineteenth of last month without her address coming off it, and a square with a name on a porter's page was still a square and not a summons.

Ola said the porter should stop drawing boxes round names he did not know, and then said the porter would not stop, because a porter who stopped writing things down was a porter who stopped seeing, and she wrote both sentences in her pad and dated them.

Ines said nothing at all, from the sill, for nineteen minutes. Then she said one sentence without opening the day book. "A name in another hand is not a production. It is gossip with a date on it." She closed her mouth and went back to saying nothing, which was its own position, and nobody at the table mistook it for indifference.

Nobody left. That was the thing he carried out of the room afterwards. Six women argued about a name in a book and nobody got up and nobody went to the stairs, and the house held.

Sun Meiling was on the landing at the top of the stairs with her coat still on, where she had been since the first, and he went up to her when the room broke, because she had not come in and he had understood on the fifth why.

"You heard it," he said.

"I heard it through the floor, which is how I hear everything in this house." She did not look at the stairs. "One square named. Two unnamed. A caller with no name. Forty-seven days."

Eighteen days left in this month after the thirteenth, and twenty-nine in the next. She had done the arithmetic too.

"The house wants me in the book," she said. "Not the visitors' book. The other one. Six lines a day, one line per woman per day, from the twenty-first of last month. Twenty-four days of lines, counting the day it started on, and every day the six of them write down where they were, and every day I do not."

"Sun."

"No. I am going to say it on my own ground, which is this landing, where no document is ever read aloud, and I am going to say it in my own words, and then you are going to go downstairs and tell them, because I will not say it in a room with a book in it."

She turned to face him, with her back to the stairwell.

"I am not in the book because a line in a book is a thing a man can produce in a year. I refused a direction to my own house in writing and I refused to let the sentence about it be written down, and both of those were about the same thing, which is that Veil-Research keeps paper and paper keeps people."

She put her hand on the stair rail.

"Your house protects people by writing them down together. Six women in rooms, on days, for no reason anybody has given a good one for. That is the logic and it has held for three weeks and it held tonight with nobody leaving, and I am telling you it does not hold me."

"Because you will not be written."

"Because I will not be kept." She said it exactly, and it was not the sentence he had expected. "A kept woman is a woman somebody knows where to find. The best finder in the city has my name at the top of a list. The only thing standing between that list and me is that there is nowhere on paper that says where I sleep."

He understood it then, the hit the household's protection had taken without breaking. The house kept its people by keeping them together on paper. Sun stayed alive by staying off paper. Both could not be true for the same woman, and she had chosen, and the house had held anyway, with six women and an empty place at the table.

"Tell them that," she said. "Tell them I said the logic does not hold me and I am still on the landing. Both are true. If they want me off the landing they can come up the stairs and ask, and I will still be here."

He went downstairs and told them, in her words, standing at the foot of the table with the visitors' book shut under Marguerite's hand.

Marguerite listened to the end and nodded once.

"Then she stays on the landing," she said, "and the book stays six lines, and the logic takes the hit. A house that cannot hold one woman off paper is not a house that can hold six on it, and we are going to find out which one of those we are."

Nadia looked at the shut book for a long time.

"One square named," she said. "That is the page. Somebody else's hand, somebody else's name, our book. And the caller with four words still has no name, and this one has no name either, and there are two callers in my house with no names and one square with a name on it that never had one before."

"I said what the book says." Nadia got up and went to the mantel and put her hand on the glass. "Somebody is giving this address away. It is not this desk. It is somewhere else, and somewhere else has a porter with a pencil, and I am going to find out where before the twenty-ninth."

The room broke up with the book shut and the glass on the mantel and six women going to six rooms, and each of them stopped at the notebook on the way out and wrote her line for the day, six lines, one per woman, in six hands, with the date ruled at the top, and his pen stayed in his pocket, and the signature stayed refused.

The landing above them held one woman in her coat, off paper and on the stairs, keeping the place the house could not keep, and the house below her held six, and neither place gave way.

Nadia Ferrous went down to the cellar for her own notebook and was gone a while. When she came back up she had a coat on over her work clothes, and Marguerite let her take the hall lamp without a word about it, and the door she went out of was the front one, which in that house nothing had gone out of for three weeks.

Whatever she was going to do about two callers with no names, she had not told the table, and Lin Hao understood that he was not going to be told either. Not being told was itself an answer, and it was the first one all evening he had not had to ask for.

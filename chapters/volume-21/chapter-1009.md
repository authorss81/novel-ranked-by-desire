# Told By Somebody Who Was Told

Four thousand seven hundred and eighteen was the ninth of this one and the sixteenth of this one is day four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five, and four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five less four thousand seven hundred and eighteen, the ninth of this one, is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

## A Woman Up Four Flights In A Market Coat

She climbed to that landing at about eleven on her own two feet, with the door of that room open behind her, wearing a coat that had not been off a hook in about a day, and she was in that building because a man in a lane off Cordwain had told her there was a man at the top of it who says three lines out loud and had no idea that was worth anything.

She is Sanna Ruhl and she works a floor at the far end of a market.

She had not been in that building before and she came about four hundred yards of it with a hand on the rail, and when she got to the landing she put her back against the wall for about nine seconds and then stood up straight and would not go first.

"Before I say anything," she said, "I want it understood that I did not come up here to ask you anything, and I want it understood twice."

## What She Came To Say

"I have got two words wrong."

Lin Hao stood on the landing and did not say anything.

"Somebody came to me at the end of my floor on the fourth and he wanted to know where that office is because he had been sent away from two halls with nothing in his hand, and I told him, and I told him fast, and I have told that man more strangers than I could count in nine years and I have never once been in a hurry about it until that morning."

She put her hands in her pockets.

"And I got two words of it wrong and he went off with them and he did not know and I do not know whether he got there or he did not, and I have not been able to put it down for twelve days."

## How Long She Has Been Saying Them

"You have been saying them since before I was saying them."

"I have been saying them since about the middle of the autumn," said Sanna Ruhl. "Before your man at the bottom of the arch ever did, I want to say that plainly, because a man in a lane told me last month that there is a man at the top of some stairs who does it and I have not been able to find out whether that is you and I am not going to stand in this corridor asking you about it."

"You are not going to stand in this corridor asking me about it."

"No," she said. "I have got a floor to work. If you turn out to be that man then you are that man, and if you are not then it makes no difference to a single person on my floor."

## Who She Says Them To

"They are people who cannot read a clock," she said. "That is not a thing I worked out. It is a thing I found out by saying them for nine years at the end of my own floor and watching which ones stopped."

"Not people who cannot read."

"Plenty of people on my floor cannot read, and they have their husbands and their brothers and the man at the front, and they get to that door in groups and one of them does the reading for the rest." She moved the weight off the other foot. "The ones I say it to are the people who can read every letter on a sign and still cannot tell you whether they have an hour or not."

"That is a strange thing to know about strangers."

"It is not strange if you have ever tried to say to somebody, come at four, and watched their face." She said it flatly. "You get the street out of them, because a street is a thing a person can see in their head. You get the four flights out of them, because four flights is a thing everybody has done at some point in their life. You do not get the quarter to ten out of anybody and you do not get half past five out of anybody."

She looked at the stair going down.

"And those two words are the whole of whether a person turns up in the morning or turns up at four in the afternoon and stands in the dark. I have known that from that floor for nine years and I have never once been told it."

## The Two Words

"What are the two words."

"The court," said Sanna Ruhl. "I said the second court instead of the third, and he did not stop me, because a man in a hurry does not stop a woman at the end of a market floor to ask her which court she meant."

"And the other one."

"The other one is mine," she said, "and I have not told anybody yet and I am telling you because you are the man at the top of these stairs. If somebody says them to me and they go wrong I do not say them wrong. I say them again, the same, a bit slower, and let them hear that the second time was a second time and not an argument."

## What He Has Never Had To Do

"That is the whole of what is wrong with it," said Lin Hao.

"Which part."

"The part before it." He came out into the doorway and stood where she could see him and not over her.

"You say them a second time so that a man knows they have been said twice. I have never once had to do that. I have said those three lines to people who were standing still, who had come up four flights, who were not going anywhere."

"I have said them at the length. I have been able to say them a second time whenever I wanted to, and if I wanted to nine times. Not one of them has ever had to check whether they heard it the first time."

"Because they were not going anywhere."

"Because they were not going anywhere," said Lin Hao. "I have been doing this for a year and every single time I have done it there has been time. And you do it on a floor of a market to a man who is going to be gone in a minute, and there is no time, and you got two words wrong, and it is the speed. It is not that you were careless. It is that you were in a hurry and there was no second time."

## What He Gives Her And What He Does Not

He said the three lines out loud to her on that landing at about half past eleven, at the length, in the ordinary voice, the street, and the door at the head of four flights off a third court, and the hours between a quarter to ten and half past five on working mornings, five days in seven, and at no other hour.

She had all three of them, and she did it in the order they come in.

He did not ask her how many people she had said them to. He did not ask her to stop. He did not ask her to be more careful, and he did not tell her she was the first one, because she had told him before she had said anything that she had been at it since the middle of the autumn.

"I am not going to be able to check you," he said.

"I have wanted to say that to somebody for about four months and it has not come out of my mouth before, and it is not coming out now in a way you are going to believe, so here it is flatly. If you go on saying them you will go on saying them with whatever you have got in your mouth that morning, and if that is wrong there is not one thing in this city that will ever tell me or tell you or tell the man."

"I know that," said Sanna Ruhl. "I have known that for nine years."

## Why He Is Not Going To Thank Her

"Do not," she said, before he had got to it.

"I was not going to."

"You were going to do it with your face," said Sanna Ruhl. "I watched you start. Thanking a person for a thing they were doing anyway is the way a thing stops being done anyway, and the next morning you would be standing in my market wishing you had not thanked me."

"That is it exactly."

"It is what it is exactly," she said, "and I was not going to ask you for it, and I have said about half of this floor out loud on my own feet for nothing and I am not going to have it turned into a thing somebody has to be grateful for, because the day that happens a woman on my floor says what a man at the top of a stair said and not what she said."

## What Is Left When That Is Taken Away

He went and stood on the top landing with the door open while she went down.

He had about half an hour left and he had the room and the two of them and a book with a row in it for everything anybody has ever asked that office, and the finding on that landing was that he could not give her anything.

Not a correction, because a correction is a copy.

Not a card, because the card is the thing that has gone soft at two top corners and it is four hundred yards away and she cannot read one anyway.

Not a sentence to say after him, because she has one, and it is better than his, and it has been going since before he did.

Not a way of finding out whether she gets a word wrong, because that would be a roll, and a roll of who has said a thing wrong is a roll of who said it.

He had gone through every instrument that office has ever owned, on a landing, in about four minutes, in daylight, and had come out with his hands empty.

The card on the door was the only one left, and it was four hundred yards off and going, and it was a piece of paper with three lines on it, and it could not say anything to a woman on a market floor who was going to be gone in a minute.

At about two he went down the four flights to the bottom of his own stair.

Four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five days. Less four thousand seven hundred and eighteen, the ninth of this one, is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven.

A woman who had been saying those three lines out loud on a floor of a market since before he began said two words of them wrong to a man who was in a hurry, came up four flights to say so, and would not be thanked, and told a man standing in a doorway that the whole of what is wrong with a free mouth is that not one person ever gets a second time.

*Less four thousand six hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is seventy-six, and that rail has carried the same sheet through every one of the last seventy-six of them, and it does not move again until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand three hundred and five days, which are seventy-six months and twenty-five days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

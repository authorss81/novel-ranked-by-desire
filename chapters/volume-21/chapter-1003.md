# Heard Three Times

Four thousand six hundred and seventy-six was the twenty-seventh of last month and the fourth of this one is day four thousand six hundred and eighty-three, and four thousand six hundred and eighty-three less four thousand six hundred and seventy-six, the twenty-seventh of last month, is seven by the month count, with thirty less twenty-seven taken out of the month that ended in between being three, and three with four taken out of the next month is seven, with the crossing named as itself.

## The Filing Floor

He climbed that building at about eleven and went the length of the filing floor to the desk at the far end of the top run, which is Wren Kallo's, and which she has had for eleven years.

The floor is one aisle between two runs of open shelves that go up to about the height of a man, and the shelves carry ledgers in half-year bindings.

The aisle is about two feet wide at the pinch where the presses stand and about four feet wide where the light comes in off the stair head. A step-ladder on castors is against the far end. There is a window with a bar across it and a table along the wall with one lamp on it that is on in the afternoons and not in the mornings.

She was sorting returns into a cage. She had nine seconds for one thing at a time and she had been giving it to him before she had put the lid down on the cage, and she said so while she was doing it.

"Two seconds of it have gone on you and I have not started," said Wren Kallo. "I want that on the record before I say anything else, because you will think I am slow and I am the opposite of slow."

## She Will Not Begin With A Number

"You came up here to ask me how many people in this division have been saying those three lines."

"Yes."

"I am not going to give you a number, and it is not because I do not have one." She put the lid on and took her hands off it. "A number about a division is a roll, and this building keeps none, and if I give you a figure you will put it somewhere, and in about four years somebody will ask that figure where it came from and it will be me."

"Then tell me the shape of it."

"I will tell you the shape of it and it is going to take about nine seconds and then you are going to tell me I have not answered you, and you will be right."

## What A Returns Desk Weighs A Thing By

"A thing comes back to this floor and it is a statement, and a statement is not weighed by whether it is true. It is weighed by how many separate hands are on it."

She turned round to the shelves and put her thumb on a spine. "One hand cannot be checked at all. That is not a rule anybody wrote, that is what the trade is. A thing one person has said is a thing you have to take on trust or not take at all, and every desk in this building will take it and every desk in this building will be guessing."

"And two."

"Two is worth a little more than one and not much. Four hands is stronger than two. That is how it works everywhere in this building, and it is why a division will believe a thing off a bench of four and not off a counter of one."

"It is also why I can look at a return of yours and tell you at a glance which floor it has been through."

## The Part Of It Her Own Trade Cannot Do

"And here is the thing I have been carrying up these stairs for about a fortnight," said Wren Kallo, "and I want to be exact about it, because everything else in this building I can weigh and this I cannot."

She came round the end of the run and leaned against the ladder.

"Nobody in my trade can tell the difference between a thing that happened and a thing that has now been said three times."

"I can weigh it. I can tell you that a statement has been through four hands and I can tell you which four floors. I cannot tell you whether it went through those four floors or whether somebody told the second floor what the first floor had said."

She held her left hand up and looked at it, and two of the nails on it are gone at the beds, which is a thing she has done for nine years and has never told anybody the reason for.

"That is the entire of what I have got, and it has been sitting on me since about November."

## Why It Is In Her Division

"Since November somebody in this division has been saying those three lines."

"Since about the middle of November there has been a man on the second floor who tells them to anybody who asks him where an office is, and he is a good man and he is being kind, and he has never once been up four flights in his life and he has never read them off anything."

"And it is in your division."

"It is in my division because I cannot keep it out of my division, and that is not a fault of anybody's except mine."

"I have been at this desk for eleven years and I have never told anybody a thing they did not ask me for, and that has never once been a difficulty to me. And then a sentence I already knew starts going round a floor of this building, and I find that I know where every copy of it is and who said it first, and I cannot make myself not know."

She put her hands in the pockets of her skirt.

"So I did not write it down, because I am not going to be the person who wrote it down, and I came up here instead, which takes about nine minutes on a working morning, and I have got about nine minutes."

## What She Will Not Do

"Which of the three of you is right."

"I am not going to tell you that." She said it fast. "I have not got it, and it is not a thing anybody in this building has got, and I will not agree with you about anything today, not once, so do not wait for it."

"Why not."

"Because you have come up four flights to be agreed with, and I have watched three people agree with you in about a fortnight, and every one of them was chosen because they agreed with you."

She took her hands out of her pockets. "I have spent nine years being the only person in a room who cannot keep a secret. The consequence of that is that I can tell when a thing has been said more than once. It is the one thing I am good at. It is also the one thing that cannot tell you whether it is true."

## She Says What She Wants Before She Does It

"Right," she said, and dropped the bar on the stair door.

The sound of it went up through the floorboards and came back about a third of a second later off the shelves, which is what this room does to everything. "That is half past eleven. The tallies on this floor are done at half past twelve and the top run has not been taken from in three years because there is nothing on it anybody wants."

"I have got about half an hour and I am going to spend about nine minutes of it on you, and you are going to be standing up."

"I do not think I am going to argue."

"You are not going to argue, because there is no price on this and your chest has been done for years, and because you get short of breath on stairs. I have watched you do it four times and I am not asking you about any of it."

She came round the ladder. "Take your coat off and put it on the shelves, and do not touch anything on this floor. A thing that is on the wrong shelf in this building is a thing that is lost, and I am not going to be the woman who lost your coat."

## She Comes First

She took her own apron off and dropped it on the table by the lamp and came at him between the two runs with her back into the shelves and her hands up on the frames either side of his shoulders, and she said what she was going to do before she did any of it, in the ordinary voice, standing about four inches off him.

"I am going to enjoy this and I am not going to think about a single thing while I am doing it, and that is the first time in nine years I have said that sentence out loud to anybody."

Then she got his belt open where it was and had him down onto the side of the step-ladder where it lies on its castors, and the whole thing went about four inches on the floor and one of the wheels said something under the boards, and she laughed at it.

She got him inside her in one push with her spine against the shelving and her face turned into his neck, and the shelves took his weight behind her and carried it away a little into the building, and about four feet of open aisle came back at both of them about a third of a second late and better than it went out.

The aisle is the thing. It is two feet wide at the press end and it is four feet wide at the light, and she worked him up against the presses and then back down into the wide part, so that the room gave her the loud ones late and took the quiet ones straight, and he could feel her finding out about that in about nine seconds and enjoying the finding out more than the work.

She had been slick between the legs before she had touched him and she let him know that by taking his hand and putting it there and then taking it away again and making him watch her instead, and after that she stopped talking and made a noise instead, and it went up into the floor and out over the top run where there was nothing on it to hear it.

Then she put his hand where she wanted it and had him finish, and he came hard with both fists locked into the shelf frames, and afterwards she stayed against him with her head under his collarbone and let the room give the two of them back late.

## Afterwards, With The Bar Still Down

She got the lamp off the table because she could not see the door without it, and went down onto the step of the ladder with her knees apart and her forearms on them, and let about nine seconds go by.

"I have not counted anything," she said. "I want that said. I do not count anything at that desk and I did not count anything just now and I am not going to count anything about what I am going to tell you, and if you write any of it down I will stop talking."

"I am not going to write any of it down."

"I know. That is why you are on this floor."

## Three Hands

"You have three hands in that room," said Wren Kallo. "You know that. I have worked out what that is, at my own counter, on my own time, and it has taken me about a fortnight, and I am going to say it once, and then this counter is going back to its cages."

"Say it."

"A statement in three hands is not three witnesses." She put her chin on her forearm. "It is one statement three times."

He did not say anything.

"That is the trade's own arithmetic and I am not inventing it for you. Four hands is stronger than two and two is worth more than one and one cannot be checked at all. Three is worse than two, because two people disagreeing is a thing you can go and look at, and three people agreeing is the same sentence three times with your name still off it."

She looked up at him.

"All three of them are yours. Every one of the three said their first sentence in that room and every one of them said it after you, and I have seen two of them say it this morning in a corridor without knowing I was at the end of it."

## The Woman At The Wrong Door

"There is a woman at my counter every week or so who has been three flights and came back down again," said Wren Kallo, "and she is not a stranger in a hurry, she is a woman who does her own accounts, and she is never once angry about it. She says the stair was the wrong length and that the man was kind about it."

"I will go and see her."

"You will not go and see her to tell her she was sent wrong," said Wren Kallo. "She knows she was sent wrong. She has told me she was sent wrong. What she has not said to me is what she wants instead, and she is not going to say it to you either, and if you go up to her and say the true three lines at her you will have made a copy, and it is not me who will have to carry the copy."

She got up and lifted the bar off the stair door.

Four thousand six hundred and eighty-three days. Less four thousand six hundred and seventy-six, the twenty-seventh of last month, is seven, with thirty less twenty-seven taken out of the month that ended in between being three, and three with four taken out of the next month is seven.

A woman who has spent nine years unable to keep a secret told a man on a filing floor that his office has three hands and all three of them are his, and that a statement in three hands is one statement three times.

*Less four thousand six hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December, is thirty-four, and that rail has carried the same sheet through every one of the last thirty-four of them, and it does not move again until the thirtieth of March.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is two thousand two hundred and sixty-three days, which are seventy-five months and thirteen days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man's name under it.*

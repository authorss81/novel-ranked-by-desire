# The Half In Her Pocket

The returns line at Cordwain runs at the weekend because the collection goes up at six, and a field counter that shut its shutter would stand over about two hundred dockets until eight in the morning.

Runa Sato was on it from eight and she had her coat on and her pencil behind her ear, and she had a half carbon in her inside pocket that she took out and put flat on the counter the moment she saw him.

"It is not a hearing any more," she said. "You know that. It stopped being a hearing at midnight on the thirtieth and it is now a date that three people once mentioned, and I am one of the three and I mentioned it at a counter in front of a witness."

"I know."

"Then why are you standing in here on your day off."

"Because you asked me to come."

"I asked you to come on the twenty-fourth of July." Runa Sato took the pencil from behind her ear and put it in her pocket and did not take the carbon back. "That was a fortnight ago. It is the sixth of August. It is twelve days since I stood at this counter and turned down the conversion, and twelve days since you pushed a half sheet of paper across it to me, and in twelve days neither of us has said one word about it."

She laid her hand flat on the carbon.

"I have decided that I am the one who says it today and then I am going to put it away."

"Go on."

"A stale paper can be revived by a fresh application. That is the only route and there has only ever been the one. The fresh application is the instrument I turned down at this counter eight days ago, and it is the identical instrument the guild reached for over Beatrix's hearing in February, pointed at me, and the reason I turned it down is not a principle."

She put her hand flat on the carbon again.

"The reason is that in February they did it to a woman who could not afford it, and in July I did it to myself."

"You do not have to do it again."

"I have to do it again or I have to do nothing." She lifted the carbon off the counter and put it back in her pocket. "That is the whole of my position and I have been sitting with it for twelve days and I have not found anything in it that is not about me."

"If I revive it I get a hearing on a date I pick, in front of a panel, with a security behind it, and the security is the conversion, and the conversion is the thing that is identical to what was done to Beatrix.

And if I do not, then in about nine years some office will read a note that says a woman of reading age at Cordwain once had a hearing and it went stale on the thirtieth of July. It will not be a live thing and it will not be anybody's fault."

"You did not decide it. You refused to be bought and you called it a decision."

"Identical, at this counter, since February." Runa Sato took the parcel ticket off the spike and squared it. "Do not come back here at a weekend to ask me whether I have thought of a third thing. I have. There is not one."

He was a third of the way down the corridor with the flat light of the yard on his back when he stopped, and went back, and put his hands on the counter.

"One question," he said. "And it is about the sixth of June and it is not about the paper."

"Go on."

"A district premises return went up on the third of June with seven lines on it and one of them left blank at ten past seven in the evening. I have been working for four days as though that line is the name of a woman in my house."

"It is not."

He did not say anything for about two seconds.

"Say it again."

"It is not the name of a woman in your house." Runa Sato put the ticket down. "The composition of premises is the number of persons of reading age **holding an address at the premises**. Not sleeping at them. Not working at them. Holding an address at them."

She tapped the counter once with the side of her hand.

"A woman can hold an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen for four years and never once sleep in it, and she is counted, and there is a difference between that and the eight women in your building, and the eight women in your building is not a number anybody in this city has ever been asked for."

He put his hand on the counter.

"The certificate of the fourth of May carries six members on page two."

"Six. Five of them at the premises and one entered as absent with reason in four hundred words Sun Meiling filed herself on the sixth of July." Runa was already counting and she had the pencil out and she had not been asked to have it out.

"Six named lines. Seven lines on that return. Seven read off the return of the third of June and the seventh of the seven is the blank one and the blank one belongs to the seventh person of reading age holding an address at those premises."

"Nadia's page has eight names on it."

"Nadia's page has eight names on it and all eight of those women sleep in that building, and six of the eight are at the premises on the roll and three sleep there and are on nothing at all, and two are on the roll and are not there at all."

She wrote it on the back of a counter slip and turned it round.

"That is five, which is what I said to you on the first night in the kitchen and it has not moved in six days. Five sleep there and are on the roll. Two sleep elsewhere and are on the roll. One sleeps there and is on nothing."

"And there is a seventh person holding an address at that address and she is not one of the five and she is not one of the eight."

"Sun."

"Sun is the sixth line of the certificate with a reason against her that she wrote herself." Runa Sato put the pencil down. "So subtract. Six named. Seven read. One blank. And the blank one is a woman of reading age with an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen and she is not in the building, and there has never been any question in this city about the sixth of July."

He stood in a corridor with a light on in it and worked it out, and it took him about nine seconds and it was the fastest anything had ever gone through him.

On the first of August, at the bottom of sixty-one steps, a woman had got on a tram and said, from the step, without any weight at all: **I am not living at your address.**

She had not said she did not hold one.

She had been out of the flat since the sixth of July. She had said on the twenty-seventh of June, in a file, in her own words, that her address was at Ferrier Street with five others, and she had said it walking out of a flat she was still in on paper.

She had gone out of that flat in the dark on the ninth of July and made him swear on it, and she had held him to it, and he had kept it. For five days he had been sitting in a kitchen telling women that a woman who does not live at an address cannot be counted at that address.

He had heard the wrong half of the sentence. It was the only sentence she had said to him from a tram step and he had taken the half that let him go on with what he was doing.

"Ten o'clock," Runa Sato said. "You have been standing in this corridor since ten o'clock."

"Since I came out of that counter."

"Since the first, then. Five days of it in a kitchen and on a wall." She squared the counter slip and put it in the spike with the others. "Write the name on a piece of paper and take it to that counter on the first of September and you will have a certificate with no bar on it and a woman in ink, and it will take about eleven seconds."

He did not move.

"There is no second of September in this city," he said. "There is a first, and there are twenty-nine days after it, and the line closes when the certification is made."

"Then you have got until the first." Runa Sato looked at him properly. "And you have got a woman who holds an address at those premises and does not sleep in it, and the figure of persons holding an address at those premises is seven, and six of the seven are named on a certificate and the seventh is not, and the seventh is the only line in this city that anybody can write on, and there is one hand that can write it. Go and write it."

He went and sat on the wall at the north end of the Reading Square for about forty minutes, in the sun, with the wall to himself, and he got it as far as the last step and then he stopped, because there was exactly one way to be certain and it was the worst one available.

She could tell him.

Not by telling him. She could not tell him, because he was not going to ask her, because asking was the term and the term had held for twenty-eight days. But she could tell him by being handed something, the way a reader is handed a record, and telling him what was missing from it.

She held a record in her head and she knew when a record had been touched, and if you put an empty frame in front of her she would tell you the shape of the thing that ought to be inside it before you had got the frame a second foot off the counter.

It was the whole of what she was. It was the reason an auditor of record had stood in his file for four years and the reason a division had put her at a desk where she was the subject.

And nothing in this city will put a sheet in front of a woman where she lives, and there is no version of telling her any of this that is not doing precisely that.

He had sworn to it in the dark on the ninth of July and he had kept it for twenty-eight days by never once going up a stair, and the whole of the last five days he had been congratulating himself on keeping it, and the term had never been about a room.

It was about the counter. It had always been about the counter.

He sat on the wall until the shadow of the ranks wall reached his feet, and then he got up and went and found Wren Kallo, who was the only person left in that building who had ever turned a rule into something that could be put in front of a court, and he wanted half an hour in a room with somebody who knew the difference.

# The Room He Will Not Give Up

The twenty-second gave him a piece of paper before nine in the morning, folded in four, with a guild's office stamp on the corner and nothing written on the outside of it.

He read it at the stair foot of the washrooms with his coat on.

**A weekly reconciliation, carried out on the twentieth, shows an entry against premises 34-6-118 at an establishment not maintained by this guild. The account is otherwise priced by its broker of record. Two hands, one figure.**

Underneath that, in a different ink, four initials.

S.R.

He had ten minutes before the building filled. He used four on the stair and six in a washroom with his coat on.

What he had in his head at the end of them was not Rask. It was a woman who had picked her papers up and walked them out of a kitchen on the fourteenth of July and had not put them back on the table since, and who had been told by a guild's weekly difference that the thing she prices is being priced two floors under a sales floor, in a room with no window and a stone floor that has never been warm.

The north desk opens at ten and Marguerite Vale was there with her coat off and her own copy of the reconciliation square in front of her, and she did not sit down.

"Sit down," he said.

"You are going to want to sit down for about an hour," she said, "and I am going to stand for the first quarter of it, because I have been standing since seven and because if I sit down this is a conversation and if I stand up it is an audit."

"Say what you are going to say."

"The guild has had my client at a stranger's counter on a return day. That is not a complaint, that is a posting. It went up on the twenty-first with a reconciliation difference on it and a keeper's initials, and my broker's ledger shows that account priced by me out of my own book, and four divisions have that posting and three of them are not mine.

"A stranger took a question on a return day and the question was entered against the account it concerned, and that is correct, and the account it concerned is yours, and I did not know about it until yesterday afternoon because it is not my guild's return."

"Did you know on the thirteenth?"

"I knew there had been a question taken," she said. "The question is entered against the account the same day the book goes up. I did not know it was yours until the twentieth, when the weekly difference came round, and I have spent two days with that."

"Two days and you told me on the twenty-second."

"I told you today because yesterday I read my own ledger from the front and could not find one mistake in it. When a keeper of somebody else's book can produce an entry against your account in a week and your own book cannot produce one entry against anybody's, you want a witness present."

She squared the copy. "You will do it. You will not like it, and you will do it, because you have been writing things down in front of people for eleven days and this is the same habit at a different desk."

He sat down.

"You have had it in your head for two days and you have not come to Ferrier Street."

"I have not been in that kitchen since the fourteenth and I am not going to start on the twenty-second," Marguerite Vale said. "You have a woman in that house who has been on the point of asking me a question since the sixteenth and if I walk in there before she has asked it I will answer it in a broker's register and she will hear it as an answer about her."

"So this is the after."

"This is the after." She turned the copy round. "And what I am going to say now I have said once and I will not say it again, and it is the only thing I have in this room that is worth the two days."

She put a single sheet on the desk, face up, with a form number printed at the top, a space at the foot for a head's sworn answer and a space for a broker's signature. The head line read DISPUTED COMPOSITION OF A HOUSEHOLD OF RECORD.

"A head may give up a room," she said.

He looked at it for a while. Six weeks ago a form with a head line on it had come into his kitchen on the back of a household return and he had walked past it for a fortnight.

"It takes a week and my signature," she said. "You said that on the seventh of July and you turned it down, and I have let you go on turning it down for fifteen days on a reason that was not the reason.

"It does not need her hand. It never did."

The washroom behind him filled and filled and somebody turned a tap on.

"A composition is sworn by the head and certified by the auditor of record," she said. "A re-issue at a figure is a composition. That is what the auditor certifies and that is the only thing in this city she has ever been asked for.

"You are not re-issuing. You are diminishing. A head may lower his own sworn answer about his own household without an auditor at all, and a diminution is signed by the head and witnessed by the broker and entered in the real book, and the office does not ask whether it is right."

"Then you told me on the seventh that it took a week and your signature and let me stand on a reason that was not there."

"I told you what was true," she said. "You built a reason out of it, and the reason was better than the truth, and it kept you out of a kitchen at seven in the evening for every one of fifteen days.

"It does not need her hand. If I had taken that off you on the seventh you would have used it before the seventh of August, and I was not going to hand you a thing that does not need her hand while she was still sitting in the room."

She capped her pen.

"You did not stand on a term," she said. "You stood on a reason I let you have, and that is a worse thing and it is mine and not yours, and I have thought about it for two days and I have decided to give it to you anyway, because it is cheaper than the other version."

He read the sheet. The head line, the figure line, the week line, the signature line. Four lines and a date and a place to say which room.

"Say the week," he said.

"It has to be entered and held open seven days before it is of any effect, and inside the seven days the head may withdraw it in writing, and after the seventh day it cannot be withdrawn by anybody, including me." She turned the sheet a quarter turn so the space at the foot pointed at him.

"If you signed it this morning it would be open until the twenty-ninth, and on the twenty-ninth it would die because nothing had been done to it. An instrument that is not carried out inside its own week is an instrument nobody has used."

"And then it is gone for the life of the account, the same as the twelfth of July."

"Say the price."

She did not answer that straight away, which told him she had known it for the fortnight and had put it after everything else on purpose. Seven days to the eighth of July and twenty-two to today, and she had been the only person in the city who knew which of the two mattered.

"The office does not choose the room," she said. "The head chooses the room, in writing, at the foot of the form, by which one. A head may give up a room that is standing empty. A head may not give up a room that a person is sleeping in, because that is a removal and a removal is a different instrument and it has a hearing.

"There is one empty room in that building and it has been empty since the sixth of July and it has a name on it and an address on it and a notebook on the shelf, and it is the only one of the seven the office will accept off you."

He did not say anything for a while, and the washroom behind him went on filling up.

"If I sign it," he said, "what does the schedule say in August."

"Five."

"And the certificate."

"The certificate says five and the index says seven and the office reads the certificate first." She said it flatly. "There is no seventh anywhere. There is no row, no pencil row, no name, no notebook on a shelf, no address, and no figure to dispute, because a thing with no row cannot have been miscounted.

"You would be right, Lin Hao. In about eleven weeks the index reads five and every figure you have ever sworn matches every figure anybody holds, and no division in this city will ever look at it again, and it will be the cleanest account in the Vant."

"That is what I am frightened of," he said.

"I know what you are frightened of." She turned the form back to her own side. "You have been in the same room as that thing since the seventh of July and you have not once asked yourself what it would do to the person it makes correct."

He looked at the foot of the form where a head writes which room.

"I am not signing it," he said.

"Write that down."

"No."

"Then it dies on the twenty-ninth because you did not do anything, and I want that on your side of the desk and not only mine." She squared the copy again. "There is a difference between a refusal and a silence and in about four years there will be a division with an interest in which one of them you were."

He stood up and did not say the number on the form.

At ten to six that evening he was on the landing with the rectangle cold under his hand and the stair lamp on behind him when Sun Meiling came up and stood at the top of the last four steps and did not come any further, which she had not done once in four years.

"I have got a question and I have had it since this morning," she said, "and I want it answered in the passage and not at the table, because Marguerite Vale was in this building today and I want to know whether it was for me."

"It was not."

"Then it was for the room," Sun Meiling said. "And the room has been empty since the sixth of July and it has her name on it."

He said it to her on the landing, standing, with the lamp on and the paint cold, and it took about ninety seconds because he made her do most of the arithmetic herself.

She came down one step.

"That would be five," she said.

"Yes."

"And I would be in it."

"Yes. If I signed it, you would be in it. You would be in it the whole of August, and every month after, and there would be no figure in this city for me to be wrong about."

Sun Meiling stood on the step and thought about it, and the arithmetic of another woman went across her face the way it goes across a face when it is being done properly and not quickly.

"So it is not about me," she said.

"No."

"It has been about her since the seventh of July," she said, "and I have been standing in a kitchen every day since the fifteenth asking to be a member of a household that two people who are not in it have been arguing about, and I have been pleased about it. I have been extremely pleased about it and I have been standing on the other end of it."

"Sun—"

"I am not going back to my coat." She said it fast and then said it again slower. "I am not going back to my coat and I want you to know I have worked out why, and it is not because you said yes.

"It is because the whole reason I wanted to be a row was so that somebody would have to answer for me. And there is a form on a desk upstairs that makes three divisions stop answering for anybody at all, and I would rather be argued about in a kitchen by eight women than be correct in a return."

She went down the stairs and did not go the sixty-one steps slowly and she did not go them at the bottom standing still without moving her lips. She went them at a speed that told him everything about what she thought of the room and the woman on the roll whose name would go off it.

He went back up and stood in the dark passage with the sheet's four lines in his head, and the number was written on it and he would not write it, and the number would be written on Thursday or it would never be written at all.

Seven days from the twenty-second of July is the twenty-ninth, and there is nothing he can do on the twenty-eighth, and nothing he can do on the twenty-ninth, and nothing he can do at nine o'clock on the thirtieth with a pen in a chained book and a woman on sixty-one steps with a bundle.

Tomorrow it will still be there.

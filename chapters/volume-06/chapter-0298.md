# The Quarter Going Out

The card comes off the board before the crew are on the ladder on the last working day of a quarter, because the floor goes up at midnight, and Beatrix Halm was under the north end of the ranks wall at ten to six with her notebook already out.

"**Eight thousand one hundred and forty-four**," she said. "And it still says the same two words it has said since the end of July."

One of the two men on the ladder glanced at her and went back up.

He came round the corner into the square at ten past six with his coffee going cold in his hand.

Eight thousand one hundred and forty-four. Six under, on a public wall, in a shade this square has never had, in the two words that have been on that stone since the thirtieth of July and have not changed once.

"Six," Beatrix Halm said, writing. "Three under for four years and a month. Four under for eleven days. Five under since the thirtieth of August, which is thirty-one days."

"You have been counting."

"I have been counting for nine years and I have got four dates in this book that I can arrive at and one of them is the day the last plate came off that wall." She squared the notebook. "And that is the second one of them."

At about half past eight the clerk from the district came down Ferrier Street with a handcart and a crate, and he had one book in it, and the crate had a label on the lid with a quarter and a year on it and a premises number.

Petra Ilyin came out with the bundle under her arm and stood in the doorway and watched the book go into the cart.

The cart had one wheel that had been louder than the other one since she had started delivering on that street.

"That is the third quarter," she said.

"That is the third quarter."

"It goes on the store shelf and it does not come out again." She shifted the bundle. "I deliver in this street once a month and I have carried that crate twice this year and I know what it weighs, and a quarter of a whole street weighs about four pounds and a bit."

"So the book is closed."

"The book is closed." Petra Ilyin looked at him. "And there is a line in it about four inches up from the bottom of the third leaf with a rule under it and nothing on it, and it was blank on the third of June and it is blank now, and in about nine hours a fourth-quarter book comes out for that door and it will not have a line in it at all."

He had nothing to put after that.

"You had twenty-five days." She said it without any edge at all, the way she says the time. "I am not going to say anything about what you did with them and neither is anybody else in that house, and I have carried paper for eleven years without ever knowing what a single piece of it was for, and I know now that one piece of it was for you."

She went back in and left the door open behind her.

He told them at the kitchen table at about ten, with Ines Duarte in the room, which she had asked for on the ninth and which he had not refused.

"I am not going to enter anything in the seventh line," he said.

Sun Meiling did not move.

"There is a pencil note in the margin of the district book opposite the seventh line, written on the eighth by a man who signs shelves, and it says in six words that the head may enter in a blank line once, and that the book closes today."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"The note is correct. I could have gone to the premises desk on any working morning between the eighth and today, with my own hand, and put a name in that line, and it would have been lawful, and not one office in this city would have asked me a single question about why."

"And you did not do it," said Ines Duarte.

"No."

She had a pencil and no folder and she had not taken them out of her coat, and she had been sitting at that table since seven and had not eaten.

"Say the reason and do not make it noble," she said. "I have had eleven days to work out why you would not and I have two and they are both ugly and I would like to hear whether either of them is the one."

"Two."

"Go on."

"If I put a name in that line tonight it is in a closed book for ever, and it will be read by every division that reads a premises book, which is all of them, and it will be the seventh line, and the seventh line is what the whole of this month's business was about."

He let her get all the way through it before he answered.

"And in about four years somebody is going to want to know why a household of eight produced a return with a seventh person on it who never slept there, and the answer will be written in a leaf that I put my own hand into tonight."

Ines Duarte took the pencil out of her coat, laid it on the table, and left it lying there.

"That is the reason," she said. "It is the right one and it is mine and you have been carrying it since the ninth of July in a form that does not have my name on it."

"I know."

"Do not." She turned the pencil over once and put it back. "I have not given you that to carry. I gave you a term in a room and you put it in a file in front of three officers and a clerk on the sixteenth of August, and that was your decision and it was correct, and it is not going to be the reason you did this tonight."

"Then what is."

"You did it because ines duarte." She did not say it any differently. "And I want you to say that back to me, out loud, in a room, because I have been a line on a return for forty-nine days and this is the last chance either of us gets to say it in words."

"I did it because Ines Duarte asked not to be written down on the ninth of July, and because she asked again on a tram, and because she paid for it herself on the sixteenth of August by putting her own address into four divisions in her own hand."

He said it into a kitchen with seven women at it.

"And because she did that on purpose to make the file read the right way round, and because she made her own name unwriteable on purpose on the thirtieth of July, and I have not been able to think about that line since."

The room was very quiet.

"That is all of it," said Ines Duarte. "That is more than all of it, and you have said it once and you will not say it again, and I am not going to be grateful about it, because a woman who is grateful about being unrecorded is asking for a certificate."

Sun Meiling put her hand flat on the certificate on the shelf over the sink without taking it down.

"Then it is blank," she said. "For ever. The line is blank and the book is on a store shelf and there is no instrument in this city that can put a name in it and not one office will ever be able to find out from any paper in this house who it was for."

"That is what you asked for on the fifth of August."

"That is what I asked for on the fifth of August and it is what I have got, and I have cost you six lines in a clerk's office and twenty-five days of a man in a kitchen and a whole quarter of your sleep, and I am not going to be ungrateful about it either, and I have told you both of those in the same sentence once already and I am not going to say them again today."

Runa Sato counted.

"Six," she said. "And the fifth thing is on a cart in Ferrier Street going to a store, and the sixth went out of a counting room on the sixteenth in four lines with two letters at the foot of it and it is in four divisions, and there is not one document about this house in this city that anybody can get off the file."

Petra Ilyin came in at about four with a slip of paper and put it on the table.

"Lodged," she said. "Quarter round, lodged at the Vant this afternoon in the ordinary way, twenty-one addresses, the twentieth yours, figure seven under it. It goes to the house tonight and gets served tomorrow morning, and not one office will look at it before then."

"And after eight tomorrow."

"After eight tomorrow I am on your step, and I knock twice, and you are not there." She picked the slip back up. "The clerk read the figure off the record and the clerk did not ask anybody where it came from, because that address carries one figure and it belongs to you, and a man in a chained book put it there on the first of August in nine seconds."

"That is the last time anybody says it that way," said Runa Sato.

"It is the last time anybody says it that way," said Petra Ilyin, "and I will be on the step at eight and I will not be in this building before noon, and I have not been in this building on the first of a month since an evening in a year I am not going to tell you about."

The window was two inches open and the dark came in off the hill about an hour earlier every week that month.

At midnight the quarter turned, and nothing happened, and nine women in a building on Ferrier Street did not move.

At ten to nine the next morning a woman who had taken the keys of a counting room on the seventeenth of September was going to stand at a chained book with a mark to enter and no mark entered, and she had not met one person whose name was on any of the six documents.

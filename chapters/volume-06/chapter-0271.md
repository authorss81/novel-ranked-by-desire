# The Booth And The Blank Page

The cage is eight feet by seven with a door at this end and a door at that end and about forty people go through it a night, and by half past twelve the yard tram had been gone for an hour and nine of them had been through since midnight.

He came down at a quarter past midnight with his hands flat in his pockets, because a sheet of paper with a figure on it has to arrive at that building inside a coat, and he had been told so on the sixteenth of July by the only person in the household qualified to know it.

Nadia Ferrous had the book open on the seat beside her and the eleventh page was still empty from the top of it to the bottom.

"Last night you told me not to help you write it."

"I did."

"And you have not come down here to say you have changed your mind."

"No. I have come down here because it is the last night of a month and there is a gate at the bottom of this cage at six in the morning and I want to be standing in it."

She set the book on the bag on the floor between their feet.

"Then be standing in it," she said. "I have got ten pages written and one empty and one to go, and not one soul down here is hurrying me, least of all a man who did not come down to hurry me."

The doors opened on the seventh floor and a woman in a launderer's coat got in with a basket, went down four, and got out, and the doors closed, and Nadia Ferrous watched the numbers go down over her head the way she had watched them for two years.

"Ten nights," he said. "One page. No gaps."

"The nineteenth to the twenty-eighth. Ten." She squared the book against the rail. "The twenty-ninth is the one I showed you. The thirtieth is tonight and it is the last page in this book with a night on it that is still in the month."

"And the twenty-ninth stays empty."

"The twenty-ninth stays empty." She said it without any weight at all. "I told you on the twenty-ninth why and I am not going to tell you again, because you came down here at eleven o'clock on the twenty-ninth and I asked you for the one thing I wanted said out loud, and you said it out loud, and you have kept it for three nights, and I am not a woman who asks twice."

He put his hands on the rail.

"Say it again anyway. Out loud in a cage, so that about forty people a night have it."

She looked at him.

"That is a different request," she said, "and you know that it is. The twenty-ninth is empty because the twenty-ninth was the day you went two floors under a sales floor and told a woman to put a broker's name on a dated page, and I was at this wicket and I did not see it."

"On the third a woman four divisions away reads a page about it. There is nothing in my book on the day it happened and nothing in my book on the day it is read, and the empty page goes into four divisions with my name on the page before it and the page after it."

"That is all of it, and it is not an accident." She turned to the thirtieth. "I do not write down what another person did in a room I was not in. I have done that twice in this month. Both times I was right. One of those two times being right cost a woman her office."

The cage dropped a floor without being asked and then hung there, and she held the rail the way anybody holds a rail in a building that has stopped.

She wrote for a while.

He read it upside down from where he was standing, which is a thing he had done four times in eleven years and which nothing in this city prevents.

It was the thirtieth, in her small level hand, with the hour against it: that at six in the morning a card came down the ranks wall in a shade the Square had not had before, and a man on a ladder read an address and a plate and a floor and the word out of it to about two hundred people, and she had got to the Square at about a quarter to nine and had heard the last of it.

Then, lower down, and in the same hand, and with the hour against that as well:

8.

And under the 8, a list of the eight, in the order they had come out of their rooms.

And under that, in a smaller hand, three lines:

Five keep an address there and are on the roll.

Three sleep there and are on nothing.

Two are on the roll and are not there.

"Five," she said. "That is the answer. Not eight. Eight is how many of them are in the building. Five is how many are in the building and on the roll, and if a division in this city wanted to know how many people keep an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen and not one of them had an opinion, five is what it would find."

"It would be right," he said.

"And it would be the only one of the four numbers about this house that is not wrong." She turned the book round and put it in front of him, which she had not done. "The roll says seven. The certificate of the fourth of May says six and has said six since before any of this started."

"Your hand says six on the fourteenth of June. The index says seven, because an officer of a district office left one returned line standing empty on the evening of the third of June and the office read the lines and counted it as a person on the twelfth."

"Seven, six, six, seven. And eight. And five. Every one of them is a true number about a different thing, and no office in this city is ever going to add those four up, and that is not because the offices are stupid."

She had written eight, and she had written the working under it, and she had handed the open book to a man at the other side of a cage at one o'clock in the morning on the last night of a month.

"Do not say anything about it yet," she said. "That page goes into four divisions on Thursday and I want to be able to say that I wrote the household's own count of itself in my own book on the same morning an office read our address off a wall. That is the only sentence I want to have about it."

"If you say anything now it becomes a thing two people did."

The dispatch man came in at twenty past one with a parcel under his arm and stood at the back and said nothing, and Nadia Ferrous waited until they had gone a floor.

"You are going to write a figure on a line tomorrow at ten to nine."

"Yes."

"And it is going to be seven."

He did not answer that.

"A valuation is not a room where a person is certified. I have listened to two years of men saying figures at counters and I know the sound of a number going into a chained book, and a number that goes into a chained book at the Exchange goes in as a figure and not as a list, because a list is a schedule and a schedule takes four pages in front of a clerk."

She turned the book back and put her hand flat on the open page.

"Seven. Or six, and six is a lie you wrote on the fourteenth of June and it is the only number in that file that is yours."

"You are not going to write six."

"No." She said it without any cruelty in it, which was the worst of it. "Then it is seven, and it is not yours, and it came out of a clerk's pocket on the third of June, and there is nothing you can put on a line tomorrow that will make it yours."

"And in four days two women in this building are going to be read in four divisions at once. One of them is a broker's account that does not carry a call. One of them is a lift operator's book with an eight on it and the working under it. And they are going to disagree in front of people who have never heard of each other."

"You are going to swear her number and not mine."

"Yes." The lift stopped at the ground and the doors opened on the yard and it was empty. "That is the whole of what I had to say and I said it at one in the morning because it cannot be said at ten to nine to a man who is about to do it."

She got the gate key out of her apron.

"Back booth," she said. "It is mine from half past twelve to six. It has a door that shuts and a lamp and a table and nine feet by six, and it is the only room in this building that belongs to me, and I have never once had anybody in it."

She shut the door and turned the key and put it back in her apron.

"Right," Nadia Ferrous said. "Sit down, and do not be gentle, because I have watched you be gentle with six women for a month and I am the seventh and I have been standing at the end of the line since the fifteenth."

The thing she wanted was not a thing he had to work out. She had told him once, in a cage, in four sentences, that she was the youngest of them and the loudest and that she had wanted him specifically and out loud from the first page she had ever appeared on, and she had said it without any confusion at all, as a woman reading out an address.

"Take your coat off," she said. "Say my name once. Not the address. Not the room. My name. Once, and then do not say it again for a while."

"Nadia."

"Good." She got a hand into his hair and pulled his collar loose with the other. "Now stop talking about figures and hold on to the table, because the table is the only thing in this room that is bolted down."

It was not quick and it was not fast. It was four hours of her night coming out of her in the order she had been keeping it since the fifteenth, and none of it was a speech.

She said his name once and then not again for a long time, and then again with his face in her hands, and after that she stopped saying anything at all for so long that he lost track of the hour and lost track of the fact that the last night of a month was going out of the window in a yard this size.

The chain of the wicket went once, because her elbow caught it, and neither of them laughed about it afterward.

She was the only one of them who had ever asked him for anything she had not already decided to give away. That was what made it different, and she knew it was what made it different, and she had known it before she shut the door.

Every other thing he had done in this building in two months had been a man putting himself on paper. Nadia Ferrous had been in the room for all of it, had written down four times what another person wrote, had been right three times out of four, and had been asked for nothing in return.

She asked him for nothing in return.

That was the whole of it, and it was why she had shut the door, and at about four in the morning she lay with her ear on his chest in a nine-by-six room with a wicket that did not lock properly and said the thing she had been standing at the end of the line to say.

"The eighth," she said. "The one on the list who is not on the roll and not on anything and never has been. Do you know that I have put her on a page that goes into four divisions on Thursday?"

"Wren."

"Wren." Nadia Ferrous lifted her head. "She is in this house and she cannot be on the roll, and she has told about four people in this city what she found in April. If my page goes into four divisions with her name on it, then Wren Kallo is a member of a household that is four under a rising floor and out of band in a public square, and her division reads that page, because her division reads everything."

He had not thought about the eighth name.

He had thought about seven for two days, and about five, and about six, and he had gone from end to end of that list twice on the thirtieth at twenty past nine in a Square with a new blue wall behind him. He had gone over the name at the bottom of it as a line he was not going to put down. He had not once thought about what her own division would do with it.

"Then take it off the page," he said.

"No."

"You cannot ask me to take it off and then say no."

"You cannot ask me to take it off." She said it into his shoulder. "You told me in that cage on the twenty-ninth that you were not going to help me write it, and I wrote it, and I wrote it because you asked me for it in the only way anybody has ever asked me for anything, which is by not asking."

She sat up and pulled her shirt over her shoulders and put nothing else on.

"If I take eight off that page now then four divisions will read a blank and a blank and a woman who was careful."

"That is not an answer to it."

"That is the best answer there is at four in the morning, and I have been awake since half past eleven. And I am not taking it off anyway. I want her in it."

"I have spent eleven years writing down what other people did and being right about it, and Wren Kallo is the only one of them who has never once been in anything.

If my page goes into four divisions then she is in it whether anybody likes it or not, and I am the one putting her there, and I am doing it at one in the morning without asking one single person in this building, and all of you can be as angry as you like about it in the morning."

She said it the way she said a rule at a counter.

He said her name a third time and she came back over.

It went on, and it was not quick, and she was not gentle at any part of it, and somewhere near the end of it she put her mouth against his ear and told him the number on a page in four divisions that was going to be wrong in four separate places.

She had counted this house herself at one o'clock in the morning. If the whole of the city could see it on Thursday then the only person in it who could not see it was the man who lived there, and she was going to have to write that down as well if he did not put it somewhere.

Then it stopped, and they lay in nine feet by six with the lamp on and the wind under the door, and she slept for about fifty minutes with her hand flat on the open book, and he did not sleep.

He looked at the ceiling of a lift operator's booth and worked out the whole of a day.

At five minutes to six the yard tram started to come back.

She got up first and dressed in about ninety seconds and pinned her hair square and put the book in the cloth bag and tied it, and she unlocked the door and stood with her back to it while he got his coat on.

"Two things," she said.

"Go on."

"One. At ten to nine tomorrow you are going to write seven, and you are not going to write it because you believe it. Every woman in this building is going to find out what you believed by Thursday, and I have decided that I do not mind."

She turned the bag over so the thirtieth was on top, and then turned it back so the eleventh page was on top, empty, the way it had been for two days.

"Two is that I did not write on the twenty-ninth," she said, "and I did not write on the twenty-ninth because you asked me not to help me, and I want it understood that I did not write on the twenty-ninth because it was mine to leave."

## Six In The Morning

He went up the far stair at six, because there is a stair on the far side of that building and it comes out into a yard with no lamp on it at six in the morning.

The first of August came up behind him on the third landing, and it came up the way it came up behind him four years and one month ago, which is that not one person in this city says a word about a month turning over and every one of them acts on it.

He was at the foot of the sixty-one steps at about ten past six, and there was a woman standing on the bottom one.

She was in her own coat with a division's copy of a sheet folded in four in her hand, and she had not come up the steps and she had not gone up to Ferrier Street. She had come to the one place in this city where a thing can be said at six in the morning and be in no one's rooms.

"Say the first line and then stop," Ines Duarte said. "And do not tell me that you cannot read a paper to me, because I am standing in a street."

He read the first line.

"A correction to the composition record of premises thirty-four, six, one one eight, filed on the thirtieth of July at twenty-five minutes past eleven at night by the auditor of record of the certification schedule of the fourth of May, in which the figure of persons of reading age keeping an address at the said premises is returned as seven, and in which the seventh returned line of the district premises return of the third of June is entered as returned blank and counted as a person."

"Put it down," she said. "You have about four seconds and I want the market to open."

He put it down.

"That was on the thirtieth," she said. "At twenty-five minutes past eleven at night, in a room in Cobb Street with one clerk and a gas lamp, and in the hand of the auditor of record of the fourth of May. Because a change of auditor takes effect on the day a division acknowledges it and a division acknowledges on the first, and on the thirtieth I was still the office."

"You filed it at eleven at night."

"I filed it at eleven at night because that is the last hour a filing counter takes from a member, and because I did not want to do it in the morning in a corridor with the whole of that division walking past." She turned her collar up against a market that was starting to make a noise two streets away.

"Say the part you are about to say, and then I will say the part I came to say."

"What it costs you."

"Fourteen days." She said it at once. "A member who is required to attend a sitting and does not attend can be put at a desk where she is the subject on the fourteenth day after the sitting. The sitting was set for tomorrow at ten. There will not be one tomorrow, because I am not going."

"There will be one on the sixteenth of August," she said, "and on the sixteenth of August I will be at a desk in a building two miles from here with somebody else's name on the head line of it, and the thing I will be asked to do there is read something."

"You could have answered it tomorrow."

"I answered it on the thirtieth, in a document that says seven, and it is on the record, and a correction on the record is an answer on the record."

She looked at him down the length of the sixty-one steps. "I answered it on the last day I held that office, in the last hour the counter takes it, and I filed it in a form I could not have filed on any other day of that month, because on the first of August that office belongs to a man living at the address it describes, and I would have had to lodge it over him."

The market was open. Somebody had a handcart on the cobbles at the bottom of the steps and its wheels went over the same two stones he had been walking past for four years.

"Seven," he said.

"Seven. And I did not put a name against it, and you are going to have to use it at ten to nine in about three hours, and you are going to have to use my figure and not yours, and I want to hear you say that you know what I have done to you before I get on a tram."

He said it out loud, on the step, to a woman on a tram.

"Then I will tell you what it is worth, and you will not thank me for it, because it is not a gift and I want that on both sides. A correction filed on the last day of an office is the only kind of answer a division cannot send back for a fresh one."

"If they had asked you tomorrow for the seventh name and you had given them one, that would have been a composition, and a composition is read against the index, and mine is read against the index and mine is already in."

She got the tram at the corner, and it was early, and it was nearly empty.

"I am not living at your address," she said, from the step, without any weight at all, "and I did not file that so that I would. And there is going to be a day this month when somebody asks you what a correction bought, and you are going to have to say it out loud to a woman in a kitchen with a bare table on it, and you are not going to be able to say my name."

The tram went.

He stood at the foot of the steps for about a minute, in daylight, in front of about forty people who had come out of the doors of this building in the last hour, and two hours and thirty-eight minutes went by.

At ten to nine he would write seven on a production line at the Exchange, under his own signature, with the date under it. The figure would be a woman's. The woman's name would be in a folder in a building two miles away and not on the page, and there was no office in this city that could serve her a copy of it in her own rooms.

At seven Petra Ilyin came down and stood at the bottom of the steps with her hands behind her back, and did not say good morning, and neither did he.

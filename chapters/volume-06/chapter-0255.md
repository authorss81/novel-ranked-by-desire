# Twenty-Two Stitches

The kitchen table had been bare for a day and he used it anyway, which was the only thing left in the building that still belonged to them.

Sun Meiling had her coat over the back of her chair on the fifteenth of July and she had the flat end of her own pocket held down under her hand the way she held it everywhere, and he said the debt out loud to the room.

"Sun. I owe you three years of a question nobody put to you."

The kitchen stopped. Not the way it stopped for an argument. The way it stops when six people find out at once that a thing they had each decided privately was their own has been on a list in a man's head.

"That is the debt," Sun said. "That is exactly the debt and I am not going to correct it, because you have it right and it has taken you three years to say it and I would have taken three more."

"Then tell me what you want."

She took her hand off her pocket and took the coat off the chair and shook it, and out of the lining came a strip of paper about two inches long with a row of names on it in pencil and a corner turned back, and she put it on the table in the middle of nothing.

She did not unpick the stitches. She shook the coat and the row came out of the seam the way a thing comes out of a place it has been living in, and twenty-two stitches held the pocket and none of them had ever held her.

"That," Sun said. "I want that counted."

Runa put her pencil down. Beatrix shut her book. Petra took her hands from behind her back.

"I want my name on the roll in ink," Sun said, "as a member of this household, in the same hand as the other six and under the same head. Not as a correction. Not as a footnote. As a row."

"Sun—"

"I am not asking for a favour and I am not asking to be forgiven and I am not asking you to tell anybody." She put one finger on the strip of paper without covering it. "I have been in pencil on a seventh row in my own coat pocket since the twenty-second of March. A pencil row is not a member. A pencil row is a rumour with my handwriting on it.

"It is the only thing about me in this house that a division cannot produce and an office cannot index, and I have been proud of it since March the way a person is proud of a stone in a pocket."

"You have been the seventh row and not one of the six," he said, "because six is what the certificate says and the certificate is mine."

"Then the certificate is wrong." She said it fast and she said it gladly. "Yes. Today. I have wanted to say that sentence in this kitchen since the twenty-second of March and I have wanted it out loud, in front of all of you, and you are asking me and I am not going to be careful about it, because being careful about it is what I have done since March."

"Yes," he said.

Nadia Ferrous had her book open before the word was out of his mouth. She wrote the hour against it and read it back and then, without being asked, wrote her own name against the line.

"The head answers in his own name for every member of the household," she said. "That is printed under the signature on the sheet you signed in May. So this is not me writing down something you said at a table. It is me writing down something the head has answered for in front of a witness, and I am the witness, and my name on it is the part I chose."

Sun looked at the book and then at him and did not say anything for four seconds, which from Sun was a considerable amount.

"A roll with a pencil row is not a roll with seven," she said at last. "It is a roll with six and a rumour. That is exactly what the blank line was on the third of June. Somebody left an empty space and wrote six underneath it and four divisions away it became a woman, and now I am the rumour and the six is the paper."

"So what does it take."

"It takes a re-issue of the schedule at seven." Sun sat down. "It takes your sworn answer again, in your own hand, with a date, and it takes the auditor of record to certify it, and the auditor of record on that schedule is a woman in two rooms above a laundry who has been out of this flat since the sixth of July and who told you she would sign when signing is what it needs."

The kitchen went quiet in a new place.

"So it cannot be done," Beatrix said.

"It can be done," Sun said. "It is the only thing in this city I have ever wanted and it has a form and a date and a person's hand on it and all three of them are available. It has simply never been asked for by anyone with the standing to ask."

Runa spoke without looking up from the table.

"Then ask him to do it and look at what you have done." Her voice was level and it was not raised, which was worse. "The day that schedule is re-issued at seven, the six on it is a false figure in a head's own hand, on a document that is on a board in a square and in three divisions.

"And when that happens the woman who carried six into a fourth office in June is carrying a lie with a name on the top of it, and there is a half sheet of carbon in a coat pocket that a division cannot produce without her."

"I know what it does," Sun said.

"You know what it does to me. You have known since the seventh of this month and you asked anyway."

"I asked anyway because I am thirty-three years old and I have been a pencil row in a coat since March," Sun said, "and I would rather be a false figure in a room where my name is on it than a true one in a pocket."

Runa put her pencil in her pocket and went out. The door she went out of was the front one and it was not slammed.

The pencil row lay on the table in the middle of nothing. Not one of them touched it, and after a while Beatrix put a saucer over it, which was not a thing she did to paper and which every woman in the room understood at once and none of them asked about.

"For the dust," Beatrix said. "It is pencil, and it has been in a coat since the twenty-second of March."

"It is a row of names," Sun said. "It will keep in a saucer until the first of August and after the first of August I am not putting it back in a coat for anybody, including the head of this household."

Wren stopped him on the stair after supper with her folder shut.

"You have not answered a reconciliation charge and you are not going to, and that is correct, because a charge is not answered by the head. It is answered by the officer who raised it hearing from the person who filed against the schedule, and there is one of those and she is in two rooms in another part of this city and she has not been asked."

"Not one of us has asked her."

"That is not the same as not having asked," Wren said. "You have gone six days now with a piece of paper in your hand and not asked her. You can keep doing that for about a week. After that it stops being a thing you are doing and becomes a thing that has happened."

He went down to the drawer under the bread because he wanted a sheet of clean paper to write Sun's request out on properly, and he had not opened that drawer for a purpose since March.

The receipt was on top. The certificate was under it. The valuation copy was at the bottom with a stranger's figures on it now. The charge was on top of the valuation copy with its form number and its sum.

And under the charge, flat against the back of the drawer, was a fifth, where it had lain since the first of July. It was a single sheet of counting-house paper with a head line on it and no form number at all.

It was the clinic's count. It had come into the flat on the back of a household return, folded in with the other papers that come in this building every month, and he had put it in the drawer without once looking at what was on it.

He had never opened it. He had known where it was since the first of July, the way a man knows where a chair is, and it had never once been on the table or in his hands.

He carried it up to the landing and sat on the step with his back against the wall and opened it on his knees in the light off the stair lamp. The paint on the rectangle was cold where his shoulder was not.

The line at the foot of it was blank. It had been blank since the first of July, and two hundred and three people had gone through that hatch every morning since, and the name of the woman who ran it had not been on anything anywhere.

He turned it over. On the back, in a small level hand, was a day book entry for the ninth of this month that said the clinic worked out at nine years and a quarter of eight and had never once been in a return, and that the head of the household answered in his own name for every member of it, and had therefore better put his name on the line while there was still a clerk who would take it.

He took the sheet back down to the kitchen and put it on the bare table with the pencil row beside it, and Sun came in from the market and saw it there and did not touch it, and neither did he, and the drawer under the bread was open on the tablecloth with four papers left in it, and a fifth was lying on the wood with a blank line at the foot of it that he had been walking past for a fortnight.

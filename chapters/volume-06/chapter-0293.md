# The Clerk Who Signs The Shelf

The third-quarter book for that address went down to the district on the eighth, because the shelf is signed before the store and a book does not go to the store unsigned.

Wren Kallo found him on the bottom four steps of the sixty-one at about half past six in the evening with her coat on and her hat still in her hand and a piece of paper folded into quarters in the other one.

"Do not sit down," she said. "I have been carrying this for about four hours and if I sit down I am going to have to explain it twice and I have only got it once."

"Tell me."

"Tell me you know what a clerk does in the last week of a quarter."

He did not answer, which told her.

"They sign the shelf." Wren Kallo put her hands behind her back, which she does when she is holding something in one of them. "Every premises book for the quarter goes down to the district and a supervisor signs the shelf, and the signature is in pencil, and it is against the shelf and not against the book, and it is the single most boring act in this city."

"And on the way through the book they look."

"On the way through the book they look." She took the folded paper out and did not open it. "And a supervisor who is signing a shelf is a man doing about four hundred lines, and he is not reading a house, he is checking that the lines are in order and the totals are on the right leaf and that nothing has been added in a hand that is not one of ours."

"And a pencil note."

"A pencil note."

He waited.

"A clerk's number with a date under it, on an addition about a return of the fourth quarter, went into my division on the twenty-second of August at ten past three, and it went in the ordinary way, because I filed it." Wren Kallo unfolded the paper and turned it round and held it out at the level of his knee, on a step, in the street.

It was a rubbing. Somebody had taken a soft pencil over the page afterwards and brought the indentations up.

Six words.

*7th line blank, returned 3 vi. Head may enter. Book closes 30 ix.*

"That is in the margin of the third-quarter book for your premises," she said. "Opposite the seventh line. In a hand that is not mine and a pencil that is not mine and it is dated the eighth of this month, and it was written by a man who has been in the district thirty-one years and whose whole office is a pencil and a date and a tick."

He did not take the paper.

"You did this," he said.

"I did the filing. He did the note." She took it back. "And that is the exact division of responsibility, and I want you to sit with it, because you are about to do something stupid and I have not been able to stop you for six hours."

"What note would you have wanted."

"None." Wren Kallo said it without any weight at all. "There is a finding in a drawer in a division that reads everything. It has a date and a clerk's number and a drawer in it, and after the winter it will be moved along a shelf to make room for a bigger file, and that is the whole of what I did on the twenty-second of August."

"And on the eighth a man with a pencil read it."

"In three weeks a man with a pencil read the number on it, and the number was mine, and he checked the number against the book." She looked up the sixty-one steps.

"A supervisor signing a shelf checks two things. That the lines are in order, and that nothing has been added in a hand that is not one of ours."

"The addition was in our hand, so it was in order, so he wrote the next thing a man writes when he finds an empty line with a rule under it and a count above it, and then he signed the shelf and went home."

"What is the next thing a man writes."

"He writes what the margin is for. A blank line that is counted as a person is a bar. And a bar on a premises book is lifted by the one person in this city who is allowed to lift it."

She did not raise her voice, because there is nowhere on that hill to raise a voice into.

"Which is the head. Once. In his own hand. Before the book shuts."

A tram came up the hill and the bell went at the top of it.

"So the one thing in the world that could put a name on the seventh line is now written down in a district book in pencil, in six words, by a stranger," she said, "with a date on it, and it will never be argued with, and no office in this city will ever read it as an accusation or as a record or as a warning. It is a note in a margin about a book that is going on a store shelf."

"You did that on the twenty-second of August."

"I did that on the twenty-second of August in about four minutes in a room with the door open, and I did it because I could not stop myself, and I told you then that I was not going to be sorry about it and I am not." She was quiet for about two seconds. "I am not sorry. I want you to hear both halves of that in one breath, because if you only take the first one you will do something I will have to watch."

"Say the second half."

"The second half is that a clerk's number is in that margin." Wren Kallo held up the folded paper. "It is not my name. It is a number, and a number in a pencil margin in a quarter-book in the district store is the most permanent thing there is, and it is mine, and it is in a book about your premises, and from today anybody who has ever wondered whether something in my division was filed sideways can answer the question in about four seconds with a pencil."

She put the paper in her coat.

"That is what boring costs. It is the price of a thing that cannot be argued with. You cannot argue with a pencil and you cannot argue your way out of a number."

"How long have I got."

"Twenty-two days, and the last one is the thirtieth, and that is the day the book closes and a fourth-quarter book comes out on the first and not one leaf in it for the quarter that has just gone."

She was already going up the steps.

"You are going to tell a woman on Lantern Row and a woman in your own kitchen about a pencil note. And both of them are going to tell you not to enter anything in it, and you are going to do what they tell you, and that will be the first sensible thing you have done in five months."

"What if one of them tells me to enter something."

"Then you will have a harder morning than the one you are going to have and I will come and find out how it went."

"Can it come out."

"No. It is a pencil note in a public book and the only hand that can take a note out of a public book is the officer who wrote it, and he wrote it because it was correct, and he is thirty-one years in the district and he will be here after all of us."

"What about your number."

"My number is a number." She had her back to him by then. "Nineteen years of filing and four hundred drawers and I have never once been the most interesting thing in one, and I have been perfectly happy about it for nineteen years."

She went up the hill and he sat on the fourth step from the bottom with his hands on his knees and thought about the order things arrive in, which is the only arithmetic he has ever been any good at, and which had been arranged against him since the third of August by somebody who filed things in drawers for a living and never once thought of him.

Then he went and got the copy from the shelf over the sink and stood in the kitchen and read box four again, all six lines of it, in his own handwriting, in a certificate he had signed twice nine days ago.

The first line said the seventh returned line stood blank. The third gave the hour and the office and the evening. The sixth said he certified it in that state.

He had put the whole of it in a box for the certification office, and the certification office was one of the four divisions, and one of the four divisions keeps the premises books.

A man signing a shelf checks that nothing has been added in a hand that is not one of ours. A certificate is not an addition. But a certificate that names a blank line, against a book with a blank line in it, is a thing a clerk in the division that keeps the books will look at twice.

He had written it. He had asked for the defect to be described where a reader would trip over it rather than shut in a file somewhere, and the clerk had said it was the first time anybody had asked for that, and he had gone out into the street feeling like a man who had done a difficult thing correctly.

Seven days later it had produced a pencil note in a margin with a clerk's number on it.

He folded the sheet and put it back on the shelf over the sink and stood with his hand on it.

There had been a bar on a certificate in four divisions, in his own hand, with a minute.

There was now a rule in a pencil margin in a district store with a clerk's number on it, and a pencil is the last thing in this city anybody argues with.

The rule said that the seventh line could be filled.

Only by him.

And the door on that shut on the thirtieth of September.

A woman had spent eleven days asking not to be written down, and a woman had spent a month being permanent about a bar, and in three weeks both of those things were going to be tested by a sentence a man in a grey coat had written with a blunt pencil because it was the correct next thing to write.

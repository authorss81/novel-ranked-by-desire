# The Only Room She Owns

He went to Lantern Row at half past seven on the seventeenth with six slips in a fold and one figure in his head and a form-numbered envelope in his coat, and he stood at the bottom of her stairs and did not go up.

The stair smelled of soap and heat and it was eight feet wide at the top with a window on the street and the shutter open on its hinge. Her door was shut. Above it a floorboard took weight and did not come back.

He put his hand on the rail. The rail was hot.

He was holding the valuation copy with a stranger's figures on it and the six slips from the counting room and the charge from audit intake, and if he had gone up four steps and knocked, she would have read all of it at her own table with her own lamp on it and told him in one sentence what the household was worth and what the twelve of July had cost and what the mark on the first of August was going to take off Beatrix Halm.

She had said no paper in her rooms. He had promised her that in the dark on the ninth of July and he had meant every word of it, and this was the ninth day of keeping it and the first day it had been worth anything to either of them.

The door opened and she came down four steps and stopped.

She had a candle end in a saucer behind her and the stair had no lamp. She was in her stockings and a shift and her hair pinned square, and she came down to the seventh step and put her hand on the rail above his, and she did not come down any further.

"Say them from there," she said. "From where you are standing. If you come up four steps I will go back in and shut the door and I will be right to do it."

He said it all from the seventh step down.

He gave her the four that had a figure, in the order the woman in the counting room had given them, without a sheet in his hand.

The examination was a chain with a person at each end of it. The open security was a week he had not fallen: the floor went up every quarter and his plate did not, and the day he was four under, the standing he lodged on the seventh of June was called, and a pen on a chain answered for it. The thirty-year account was cheap to carry and the figure was all of his risk. The clinic was the only one of the five that went up.

Ines Duarte listened with both hands on the rail above his and did not interrupt once.

"The fifth," she said.

"The certified figure. Six sworn, seven indexed, and nothing has ever moved between them, so the real books have nothing to write about. She said a thing that has never been entered is a thing that has not happened."

"That is correct." Ines said it without any expression at all. "It is also not what you came for and I want to be fair with you before you spend the rest of the evening on this stair."

"Then what did I come for."

"A price." She said it plainly. "You have been using the word all week. A price is not a figure. A figure is what a thing costs to somebody. A price is a figure against a security, held by a named holder, on a date, and it can be called."

"You know all of that."

"I know all of that and I cannot do it for you, and the reason is not the term." She shifted her weight on the step. "The reason is that a price has a holder.

"To price a debt of yours I would have to hold the security it prices. The security is your plate, lodged against a broker's account, and the broker is a woman who took her papers off your table yesterday. The moment I hold it I am inside that account again, and I walked out of it on the sixth with a true report behind me."

"So you are the pricer and the pricer cannot price."

"I am the only person in this city who can price what is in your house, and I have put myself somewhere a court cannot make me hold anything, in two rooms over a laundry I cannot afford, and those two facts are the same fact and you asked me for both of them at once on a stair at half past seven."

He stood with the slips in his hand and did not put them up the stair after her, because putting them up the stair would have meant going up four steps.

"When can you be put at a desk," he said, "where you are the subject."

She was quiet long enough that the laundry below shut a door.

"You worked that out upstairs before you came," she said. "Go on then."

"The sixth of July," he said. "The day you filed. The entry went up with it, and an entry that goes up with a report is a date the division holds from the day it was filed, and a fortnight is the ordinary answer to a question of that kind."

"Six and fourteen."

"The twentieth."

"Yes." She said it without surprise, which was the part he had not been ready for. "I told you on the ninth that it was about a month. A month is what I would need. I would need a room and a removal and a hearing of my own and a fortnight of answering in between, and I would have won most of it, and I priced it the way you price anything you have to live in afterwards."

"And they will give you the fortnight."

"They will give me the fortnight, because a fortnight is what a division allows while it has not yet decided to be careful." She looked down at his hands. "I was being generous to myself on the ninth and I was wrong in the direction of hoping. That is the whole of what a fortnight is. It is not a shorter procedure. It is the shortest one they can hold you to without appearing to hurry."

Three days. Not one word had been said on the twelfth and three days had come off it since.

"The charge in my coat," he said. "It has to be answered inside fourteen days and it runs to the twenty-sixth, and I am not going to pay it, because a paid reconciliation is a schedule that agrees with itself at six."

"It is not the twenty-sixth you are worried about." She said it as a person reading a figure off a column. "No charge is ever answered by the person it is raised against. It is answered when the officer who raised it has heard the other side in person.

"She filed on the sixth. On the twentieth an intake clerk will put that filing in front of the panel, and the panel will find that the only person who can certify the schedule is the person the schedule is about. She will not be able to refuse. Refusing is what people do who are still on a roll."

She came down one more step and then stopped herself.

"I am going to say the rest of it standing where I am, and then you are going to go home, and neither of us is going to pretend that any of this was difficult for me."

"Say it."

"You have not asked me for the re-issue." Her voice did not change at all. "There is a woman in your kitchen with a pencil row in a coat and four months of wanting her to be a member, and there is exactly one hand in this city that can put her in ink, and you have had that fact in your head since the fifteenth and you have not come up those stairs to ask me."

"That was the term."

"The term is paper." She said it flatly. "The term is not my name. There is a difference between those two things and you have been standing on it since the ninth, and in three days it will be too late to stand on it, and you will spend the rest of your life having been correct."

She turned round on the step and went up, and stopped on the fourth from the bottom with the candle behind her and the street going past the open shutter.

"Take the paper with you," she said. "Do not send it up. If you had sent it up I would have read it and put it down and gone back to my table, and you would have had a figure and a clean conscience and two rooms with a woman in them, and the woman in them would be the same woman as the one who signed your schedule in June. That is not what you wanted and it is not what I am offering."

"I know what I wanted."

"Then say it, because it is the first thing you have said on this stair that I have not been able to check."

"I wanted to come up," he said.

"Yes." She stood on the fourth step with the candle and did not come down. "You did that eight days ago, in the dark, in about eleven seconds, and it cost you the only thing you own, and I have put you on this stair to find out what that is worth.

"It is worth what a stair is worth. It is cold and it is not a table, and I am standing on it in my stockings because you are on the other side of a door you have promised me not to come through."

He went down to the street and stood under the shutter a moment and heard the candle go out above him.

He walked home through the Square because there was no other way to Ferrier Street. The side board stood under its lamps in the shade the first of the month had laid down, with 34-6-118 on it and the schedule's date under that, and the date was the twenty-sixth of last month, and the crew would not touch it again until the first of August.

That was the whole of what the city knew about his household and it was six, and it had been six since the twenty-second of March, and it would go on being six through the twentieth and possibly through the whole of August.

Three days up the road there was a panel at Cobb Street that was going to find out whether the six or the seven was the lie, and a woman was going to be sitting in it who could answer that in about four seconds, and the answer was going to be that both of them were true and one of them was his handwriting, and no instrument in this city could take a man's own figure off his own sworn page.

The three days he had wanted were gone and the door at the top of that stair was still shut, and the four debts he could get priced were priced in a book that reconciled and had a date on it, and the fifth one was free, and the twentieth was three days up the road with a woman in her stockings on the wrong side of her own door.

# A Docket Under The Door

He was on the hill by ten past seven with a cup of tea he had not paid for, in a doorway four hundred yards from his own gate, which is the whole of what he did that morning and the reason for it.

At eight o'clock a woman with a bundle knocked twice at a door at the bottom of Ferrier Street.

He was not there.

He was not in the yard and he was not in the building and he had not been in the building since about eleven the previous night, which was the promise he made on the nineteenth of August and had kept for twelve days and about which nobody had ever once asked him.

Petra Ilyin knocked twice, and then she stood on the step for about eleven seconds, and then she put a folded docket under the door and came back up the sixty-one steps with the bundle under her arm.

She wrote the hour on the outside of it and she did not read it, because she has not read one in eleven years.

Then she went on to the twenty-first address and did the whole of the rest of the round, and not one person on Ferrier Street saw her do it, and the docket lay on the mat of a door with his own name on the inside of it and a figure of seven under the address and the hour written on the outside in a hand that had carried paper for eleven years without ever once putting it down.

He saw her an hour later from the top of the hill, coming back down with the bundle, and she did not look up and he did not go down.

## Ten To Nine

The chained book at the Exchange is on a table in a room with one window and the window does not open, and the pen that writes in it is on a chain that is fixed to a bracket in the wall, and it has been on that chain since before either of the men alive in this room were born.

The woman who held the keys had taken them on the seventeenth of September and had been in the office two days, and he had not met her and she had not met him, and there was no instrument in the city that would have introduced them.

She read the four lines out loud in the order she found them, which was not the order he had written them.

"Carried at the address of premises thirty-four six one one eighteen." She had her finger on the last of them. "Figure: seven."

"Yes."

"Dated the sixteenth and initialled and not signed." She looked up. "You are the address."

"I am the head of the premises."

"You are the address." She put her hand flat on the page. "I am going to write the mark now. It goes in against the account of thirty years, it is a movement, it reconciles, and once it is in, nothing in this city lifts a movement out of a book, because a movement is not a statement."

"Write it."

She wrote it. It took about four seconds and she did it the way people do a thing they have done eleven hundred times.

"Mark against the account, at the figure of the head, on the first of October." She read it back and then she put the book round and squared it against the edge of the table. "And now the answer, sworn by the head of the premises in his own hand with the date under it, and the answer is the count of people of reading age keeping an address at those premises this morning."

"Seven."

"You are going to say seven because there were seven on the composition fourteen days ago and because the book you are describing carries seven, and both of those are true and neither of them is a reason." She put the book back in front of herself. "Count me the seven."

He counted her the seven.

Six of them were on page two of a certificate of the fourth of May and one of those six was a woman who had put four hundred words on a record about her own absence on the sixth of July in her own hand.

The seventh line of that June return had stood empty since it came back off the district counter, and it had been counted as a person the whole time.

"Eight women sleep in that building," he said.

"I have not asked you and it is not in any book I hold."

"And seven of them keep an address there."

"Which is the figure, and I want it from you in your own hand with the date under it, because that is the only thing that answers anything in this city, and because in about four years somebody is going to ask why a composition of seven stands against an account that has never been priced by anything, and the answer will have to be a page."

He wrote it and dated it and signed it.

*Seven persons of reading age holding an address at premises 34-6-118 on the first of October. Sworn at the valuation by the head of the premises.*

He put the date under it and signed it, and it took him about nine seconds both times, and the second time he counted them.

The woman holding the keys turned the book round and read it and did not say anything about the nine seconds.

"That is the second figure of persons out of those premises that I have had in nine days," she said, "and both of them are the same figure, and I want you to know that when a book has the same number twice from the same man it is a habit and not a coincidence, and I have marked habits before."

He had not thought about it and it had not occurred to him once.

"I will go now," he said.

"Go." She squared the book. "And the account of thirty years will carry that mark for as long as the account is open, which is a great deal longer than either of us."

## Nine Hundred Yards

Beatrix Halm was at a guild counter at about ten in the morning with a notebook and nothing in her hands, and a man she had never met read her a price out of a file, and she wrote it down and did not take it, and the whole of it took about six minutes.

He was nine hundred yards away with a cup of tea he had bought at last, and he kept the thing he had promised at eleven o'clock the night before in a room over a shuttered shop on the tram line, which is to say he kept it completely, and did not go, and was not near the door, and she was not helped.

Runa Sato's count on the morning of the first was six.

Nadia Ferrous's page, blank on the twenty-ninth leaf on purpose. The composition and the correction. The reconciliation, initialled. The broker's statement, signed. The certificate, his name twice, with a bar on the face of it in his own handwriting. The quarter-book, on a store shelf, with a pencil note in the margin and a clerk's number on it.

And the priced line of the sixteenth of September, four lines, two letters at the foot, an address and a figure of seven, in four divisions by the end of the week and in the ordinary stream of the fifth on the twenty-first.

Six documents about one address in one city in twenty-four days, and not one of them had his name at the top of a page.

A page of accounts carries a name at the top.

A correction carries a name in the hand of the officer who made it.

A certificate carries a name at the foot of it and at the head of it, and he had put both of those there himself, on the first of September, in his own handwriting, with a minute.

And there was a quarter-book on a store shelf with his initials on the shelf, and a certificate with a bar on the face of it in six lines that he had written to describe the defect of the instrument he was certifying, and a broker's statement about a pen that a man in a grey coat at the bottom of a street had been in the room for.

He stood on the hill at about half past ten and found out that he had been legible in four buildings for twenty-four days without ever once being named at the top of anything.

**A classification does not need a man in it.** It needs an address, and a figure, and a reason.

The system had not looked for him.

It had read six papers, taken them in the order they arrived, and drawn a single line between them, and that line was an address.

He had the address.

He had been the reason.

He had gone down two flights of a cold stair on the last day a woman was allowed to be the only person who could write it, and had put it into her hand himself, in a description, in eleven minutes, because he was a man who would rather be written down than looked at.

There was nothing in this city that could undo a dated line in a real book, and a dated line in a real book was not an offer.

It was a fact.

Somewhere below that, two floors under a sales floor, a keeper he had never spoken to was doing the ordinary work of a quarter on a floor that had never been warm, with his address in a book and a figure of seven under it and no name on the page anywhere, and every reason in the world to ask where an address like that is fed from.

The page said it was carried at that address.

That was on the page.

And there was not one grain in five years, and one ledger with a hundred and eighteen lines in it and nine of them turned and the ninth still himself, and not one of the six documents had ever seen any part of it, and not one person in this city had ever once asked him a question about a thing that could not be asked about, and that had been the whole of his life up until the sixth of July.

Six pieces of paper, four buildings, one line drawn between them, and a date on it.

The first of October had been the turn of the quarter and a docket round at the same door at the same hour, and both of them had gone through in the ordinary way.

There had been a figure of eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight standing against an account of thirty years since the twenty-fourth of August, and it stood five below a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-three, and six below the floor that went up the day before, and on the first of October it had been answered with a production of seven persons sworn by a man in his own hand, and it stood.

A called figure answered by a production is answered, and answering it is not the same as erasing it.

The answer goes into the book and the book reconciles and the figure stays where it was, in a column, under an address.

And in four years, or in nine, or on the day the book changes hands again, somebody in this city is going to read a column with an address at the top of it and a figure at the bottom of it, and ask the only question there is to ask about it.

And there is not one office in this city that has never asked it.

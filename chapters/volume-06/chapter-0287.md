# The Drawer She Took Nothing From

He got to her rooms at about four in the afternoon on the twenty-seventh of August and made her let him in past the outer door, which took nine minutes and a conversation at the hatch about a parcel that did not exist.

Then he stood in the middle of the best room he will ever stand in with his hat still on and asked her to undo a page.

Marguerite Vale did not sit down and did not look up.

"Say the whole of it quickly," she said, "because I have a thing on a date I am not going to tell you and it is not this."

"A reconciliation went out on the twenty-fourth at eleven with a figure against an account of thirty years that nothing else secures, and the figure is his own plate, and it was initialled and not signed, and it is the truth."

"And it is a figure of me on a page in four divisions before the end of next week."

"Yes."

"And your own account is the book on the other side of that disagreement. Your account says that standing of the seventh of June was discharged at twenty-five minutes past four on the twentieth of July, and the real book carries it whole."

"If you strike the discharge out of your own book tonight, the two books agree, and the figure on her page is a figure about an entry that is not there."

She turned a chair a quarter round and left it.

"When did you work that out?"

"The twenty-second."

"Five days." She turned the chair back. "You have had it since the twenty-second and you have carried it about this city for five days in your coat."

"And you have come to the one woman whose book it would cost, and I want you to hear yourself, because you have not."

"I have heard myself."

"You have not." Marguerite Vale stood up and came and sat down on the arm of a chair that was worth more than his year's wage.

"You have worked out a way of taking a figure off a page. You have not worked out what happens to the page, because you do not like the shape of what happens to the page."

"And so you have brought it to a broker and asked her to eat it, and brokers do not eat. They charge, and I am the broker, and you have known that since the sixth of July."

"What happens to the page."

"That it becomes a charge against me, and it becomes it precisely because I would be the only person who could have caused it."

She put her hands on her knees.

"A reconciliation that publishes a disagreement is a fact about books and it convicts nobody at all, and a woman initialling it is a keeper doing arithmetic, and that is what makes it survivable."

"If I strike the entry on the twenty-seventh of August, then on the twenty-ninth a division has a page in four buildings saying two books disagreed, and by the thirtieth of August the entry that page is about will not exist."

"And a page that cannot be about anything is a page somebody manufactured. And there are two hands in this city that could have manufactured it, and one of them is mine."

"And I will be standing in front of four officers on a date I am not going to give you, with my own signature at the foot of something."

He took his hat off then, and held it, and did not sit down.

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that it does not work." She said it flatly. "The page went out at eleven on Thursday. It is in four buildings."

"There is no before, and there has not been a before since about eleven o'clock on Thursday morning, and every hour you spend wishing for one is an hour you are not spending on the twenty-ninth."

"Then tell me what is happening on the twenty-ninth."

"No."

"Marguerite—"

"You were in a room on the second of August when a broker was told the date of a thing three times and asked to say it once more."

"You were in a kitchen on the ninth of August when I said I would stand in front of four officers with a number on a table before the month is out, and you said that was right, and I told you what right costs."

She got off the arm of the chair.

"You do not get the date and you do not get the number and you do not get the shape of it, and that is not caution. That is the only way the twenty-ninth is worth anything at all."

"In a room, a thing said by a person who has been told it in advance is worth nothing, and you have known that since March and you used it on me then."

"And you are not going to get it back by asking me now."

He stood there with his hat in his hands.

"One more thing, and then I want something practical out of you," he said.

"There is one instrument in this city that can move a figure like that, and it is not a payment and it is not an hour of somebody's life, and I have known it since the ninth of July and I have not done it and I am not going to."

"And I am telling you that before you find out about it from somebody else in about four months, and I would rather you had it from me in a room with a door on it."

Marguerite Vale looked at him for a long moment.

"Say what you have just said again, and say it to my face and not to my hat."

"I am not going to put merit into a figure through a person so that it scores against them and the figure comes down," he said.

"That is the road and there is one road, and I am telling a broker in her own rooms that I am not going to walk it."

"And if you ever hear that I did, you will be able to say that I stood here and said the opposite first."

"That is the first time in a month anybody has given me a thing I can use." She got up. "I am not going to use it. I am going to put it in a drawer."

"And in about four years, when it is completely irrelevant and you are a man other people have opinions about, it is going to be the only piece of paper in my office that was given to me by somebody who was frightened."

She went across the room and opened a drawer and did not take anything out of it.

"One practical thing first, and then I want something from you, and you are going to have to do without the practical thing."

"Go on."

"A figure standing against an account nothing else secures does not lie there." Marguerite Vale had her hand in the drawer and her eyes on the middle distance.

"It is called at the turn of the quarter, the same as anything else in this house that has been waiting for a date, and the first of October is a turn."

"And every year for four years I have watched an account go quiet for nine months and then get very expensive on the first of October."

"I have known that since March. I did not tell you in March and I am not telling you now to be kind."

"I am telling you now because a man who is going to stand in a corridor being told things should at least know which corridor it is."

"What happens on the first of October."

"Nothing happens on the first of October unless somebody calls it, and nothing in this city calls a figure that is not against something."

She shut the drawer.

"Which is why I said it twice on the way up your stairs, and why I meant it twice, and why it is worth about four seconds of a careful woman's attention to make sure that on the first of October there is somebody standing on a step at your address who is not going to answer a question about an account."

"That is not advice. That is a woman telling you what she has been doing since March without saying she has been doing it."

He stood very still.

"Who have you been keeping it off."

"Do not," Marguerite Vale said. "And if you ever work that out, do not come to this room and tell me."

"Because the second I know it I am a woman who has been managing a man and not a woman who has been managing an account, and the first one gets cross-examined and the second one does not."

"Now the practical thing is over," she said. "You are not going to come to this room again before the thirty-first of August, and I am going to tell you why, and then you are going to go away and not think about it, which is a thing men do not do."

She turned round.

"I am not able to see you between now and the thirty-first and not be able to put you in it. That is not a feeling."

"I have said it to you in the exact words I am going to have to use in front of four officers, and you have heard them, which is a mistake on my part, and I am making it anyway."

"Because I am going to be in a room on a date I am not giving you, and I would like there to be one person in this city who has heard them and is not going to use them."

He did not move out of the chair.

"That is not fair," he said.

"No." She picked up her hat. "Go and be somewhere else. I have four lines in a drawer that have been in a drawer for nine days and I have read them every night."

"And I have not shown them to one person. Not to a clerk, not to the man who reads the Vant's floor, and not to you."

"And in that room I am going to read them out loud to four officers, and I am going to say a number, and the number is not yours."

"And that is the whole of what I am able to give you this month."

He went out past the hatch and the nine minutes of it and got as far as the street.

Three days to the card and four to the first of September, and a figure of eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight on a page in four buildings that was five under a floor from the thirtieth onward.

And a sentence in a drawer two feet from where he had been standing.

He had come to that room with one thing and had left with two of somebody else's.

# A Page That Names No One

The counting room has two floors of stair between it and the sales floor, no window at all, and a stone floor that has never been warm. On the third of August Solveig Rask had left the door open at the top, which she had never done.

"There are four books on this table," she said, "and as of seven o'clock this morning there are five, and the fifth one is in a leather folder that came out of the press at half past five with my name on the foot of the page."

She had her sleeves turned back. She had a cup of something she had not drunk beside her right hand.

"Sit down. I am going to read you what I wrote, because I wrote it to you in advance on the twenty-fourth and I am not going to make you hear it a third time in the shape of an accident."

He sat down. The room was cold enough that he could hear the page.

"A reconciliation of the account of the broker holding the second book in this room against the real book, for the week ending the third of August, in the matter of a call which the real book records at twenty-five minutes past four in the afternoon of the twentieth of July."

"The broker's own account of that day carries the call as discharged, at the hour of entry, out of a standing lodged against an unsecured account of thirty years under the second form at the Exchange on the seventh of June, secured at the figure of the head."

"The real book carries that standing as standing at the figure of eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight at the close of the thirtieth of July."

"The two do not agree, and they do not agree by the whole of it."

She turned the page round.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," she said. "The call is not a movement and it has no figure of its own, and what it moved was the standing, and the standing was the whole of your plate, so the disagreement between the two books is your plate. That is the sentence and it is the whole of what I am capable of writing, and I wrote it to you in a cold room ten days ago."

"You are certain."

"I am certain because I have reconciled it three times." She put both hands flat on the open page. "Read the third line again. **At the hour of entry.** That is the part that matters and it is not the part anybody will read out loud in a division."

He read it again.

"It means the discharge was entered at twenty-five minutes past four on the twentieth of July," he said. "At the same minute as the call. Not at the end of that day. Not at the next sitting. In the same minute."

"At the same minute," Solveig Rask said. "Which means whoever discharged it was standing at that desk with that pen at twenty-five minutes past four in the afternoon on the twentieth of July, and wrote two lines with one hand, and the two lines do not agree with each other and have never agreed with each other since."

She let that sit in the cold room for the length of a count.

"That is not a thing I can put on a page," she said, "because it is a shape and not a movement. And I will tell you now, once, and I will not say it again: there are four hundred chained pens in this city and a device cut into the shoulder of one of them can be cut again by anybody who has ever held it, and a keeper keeps movements and not hands."

"It is on the page and it is not on the page and both of those are true and I have priced my own answer for it."

"How much is it worth to the woman it is about."

"That is not a figure I can put anywhere either," she said. "What I can tell you is what a division will do with it.

"A broker whose device and whose account disagree by a figure on a date cannot produce her pen. She will be asked to produce it, out of four divisions, in an order she does not choose, and she will not be able to.

"And after the second division has asked her there will not be a version of her that is a person with a book and an office and four years of unbroken attendance. It will be a person who wrote something in a chained book and cannot say what."

"You knew that on the twenty-fourth."

"I knew that on the twenty-fourth and I have not enjoyed one working morning since," Solveig Rask said, "and I did it anyway, and I would do it again next week, and that is the second time in a month I have said that to you and I am aware of how it sounds."

He had twenty minutes and he used them badly.

"There is one thing on this page," she said, "and one thing off it, and I am going to tell you both, because I signed it and a keeper who signs is not a keeper afterwards."

"Signed."

"Signed." She turned the page round again and there was a line at the foot of it and a name on that line and it was not initialled. "I have kept that page four years and I have left it exactly where it was and looked at it twice a week, and I have never signed anything in a real book in my life. A reconciliation that is initialled is a keeper's arithmetic. A reconciliation that is signed is a woman's evidence. There is no third thing."

"There is no third thing."

"There is no third thing and I signed it at seven o'clock this morning and a signature in a real book goes to four divisions, and a woman who has signed a page can be cross-examined on it, and I have never been cross-examined on anything, and from this morning a woman in this room can be put in a chair."

She squared the five books.

"You will want to know what I got for it and I am going to tell you that I got nothing, and that you did not ask me for it, and that I did it because on the twenty-fourth I told you I would do it if you asked and you asked and I have been thinking for ten days about the difference between the two and I have stopped being able to tell them apart."

"That is not a virtue."

"It is the opposite of a virtue and it is the first free thing I have done in four years."

"You wanted one sentence out of the page underneath," he said. "You told me that on the twenty-fourth."

"I did."

"Are you going to ask me for it."

Solveig Rask looked up for the first time since he had come down the stair.

"No," she said. "And I am not going to tell you why, and you are not going to get anything else out of this room today, and if you ask me a third time in four months I will put you out of it myself."

He had not got to the fifth thing for another twenty minutes and he only found it because he was looking for it.

"Your production," she said. "The hundred and thirty-fourth. The figure of persons at your premises, entered at ten past nine on the first, in the chained book at the Exchange."

"Seven."

"I have not got it." She said it flatly. "I have the real book, which holds movements, and I have the broker's own book, which holds movements, and the figure of persons against an account at a valuation is neither of those things. It is in the Exchange's book."

She put her hand down on the leather folder.

"It went into four divisions this morning through a different office from the one my page goes through this morning, and there are two books in this city that see each other about once a year, in a room, in the spring."

"So the reconciliation does not see it."

"The reconciliation does not see it, and it did not see it on the second, and it will not see it on the ninth."

She put her hand off the folder. "Sit with that for a moment. You are a man who has spent four years learning to be invisible in two offices and you have just put a figure about your household into a third one. There are three now, and they do not see each other, and on the first of September all three are going to be read by the same four divisions in the same week."

He sat with it.

"Answer me one thing," he said. "Does the page say a woman held that pen on the twentieth."

Solveig Rask looked at him for about four seconds.

"No," she said. "It does not say a woman held it and it does not say a woman did not hold it and it does not say anything at all about who held it, and I have made sure of that in three passes."

"Put one word in it about a hand and the whole thing would be a charge and not a reconciliation, and an office would strike it in a week. What it says is that a call was entered and a discharge was entered and the two disagree by a figure. That is a fact about books."

"It is available to anybody who wants to use it and it convicts no one and it destroys a woman anyway, and I told you that on the twenty-fourth and you said do it."

"I said do it."

"You said do it once and you did not qualify it." She put her hand off the folder. "Go up. The page goes out at eleven and by the afternoon there will be four copies of it in four buildings, and by tomorrow morning there will be a woman reading it who has never heard your name."

Marguerite Vale was at her own table at four o'clock in the afternoon and she had the leather folder's contents in front of her, because the Vant circulated on the third of August afternoon and she had been sent a copy in the ordinary way like everybody else.

He read it to her from the top, all four paragraphs, the way she had written it, and he did not stop at the part that mattered.

She listened with her hands flat on the wood and did not move and did not look up, and when he had finished she sat like that for a long time.

"At the hour of entry," she said at last.

"Yes."

"That is the only line in the whole of it that is about a person." Marguerite Vale took her hands off the table and put them in her lap, which he had never once seen her do. "Everything else in that page is about books and about a figure and about the difference between two of them, and there is one line in it that says somebody was standing there."

"You did not write it."

"I did not write it and a woman two floors under a sales floor wrote it, and she wrote it because it is arithmetic." Marguerite Vale looked up. "Do you understand what I am telling you? She has given me the whole of it without giving me any of it."

"She has put a sentence in a real book that a woman reading it in a division in four months will understand means a hand was on that desk, and not one word in it about whose.

"I could have said it on the thirty-first in your kitchen with five women in it and it would have been worth nothing, and this is worth a great deal, and I will never be able to prove that I did not already know.

"You asked me for the rest of my life of not asking you."

"I did, and I have it, and I am not spending a penny of it." She put the copy down. "I am going to stand in front of four officers with a number on the table and put my own signature under it before this month is out. I gave you that date on the twenty-eighth and I have not moved it and you are not going to be anywhere near the room."

"That is right."

"It is not right because it is not my doing. It is right because a broker can take a thing somebody said in a kitchen and make it into an instrument, and I am the broker, and if I say it to you in a kitchen it goes into a mouth with no date and no witness on it, and you will spend four months telling this city that I told you."

She put the copy in a drawer and shut it.

"Go and be somewhere else for an hour," Marguerite Vale said. "I have a device cut into a pen-holder that a woman who is not me used at twenty-five minutes past four on the twentieth of July, and I have been not saying so since the twenty-second of July, and there is a copy of that page lying on my table and I would like an hour in which I am not a woman with a theory."

He went and found Nadia Ferrous.

She had been on the roof since the middle of the afternoon with the cloth bag and she came down into the yard at about half past four, and she had the thirtieth's page out flat on the back of a crate in the loading bay, in daylight, in front of the open doors, where about thirty people a day walk past without looking at anything.

"Read it," she said. "Not to me. I have read it about forty times since one o'clock."

It said what he already knew it said, in her small level hand, with the hour against it, and under the wall it said 8, and under the 8 the list of eight, and under the list the working: five keep an address there and are on the roll, three sleep there and are on nothing, two are on the roll and are not there.

A reconciliation went into four divisions at eleven o'clock that morning and it said a call was entered on the twentieth of July and a discharge was entered at the same hour and the two books disagree by eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight.

This page said how many women slept at that address and which of them were on nothing.

They were not about the same thing. That was the whole of what he had, and he stood in a loading bay and looked at it for a while.

"There is a third one going into four divisions before nine tonight," he said. "A correction filed in Cobb Street on the thirtieth at eleven at night by the auditor of record of the fourth of May. It says the composition of those premises is seven persons of reading age keeping an address there and the seventh returned line is entered as blank and counted as a person."

Nadia Ferrous put the page down on the crate.

"Then there are three documents going into four divisions about one household inside thirty-six hours," she said, "and not one of them is about the same thing as the other two, and there is not one office in this city that will ever put them in the same drawer, and the only person alive who could is a woman on a tram who has told me three times that she does not live at your address."

She rolled the page and put it in the bag and tied it.

"Go up," Nadia Ferrous said. "It is five and the market is going and I have to be behind that wicket at six, and if you stand in that yard for another minute I am going to do something I have been not doing since the fifteenth and you will not enjoy it."

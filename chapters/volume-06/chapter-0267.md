# Beatrix Reading A Wall Aloud

On a Thursday the crew comes at nine and the figures go up at ten, and they work the ranks wall from the bottom along, and they have never once in this city's memory got above the middle before the light goes.

They paint numbers, and the numbers come off a card, and the card comes down on the Thursday before from an office that keeps a wall the way a man keeps a horse.

The rule of the trade is that you read a wall before you paint it.

The foreman stood on the bottom step of the ladder and read the lower half out loud to the two men on the boards and to about nine people on the pavement, which is how it has been done since before he was on it, and he read at the speed of a man reading a shopping list.

Beatrix Halm came off the yard tram at ten to five and walked down Ferrier Street on foot, because her good ear wanted nothing at all out of a tram that morning. She reached the wall four minutes past ten with her notebook under her arm and her season squared in her coat and stopped about thirty feet off with her head turned.

The foreman got to the security column at about ten past ten.

He read the ordinary lines first: an office, a date, a figure of standing, a guild's device. He read the device out as a word because a man on a ladder has to say a word or he says nothing. Then he read the line under it, which was the one the guild had sent up two days before and which carried a mark at the end of it that was not a figure but a shape.

"Beatrice Halm," he said. "Hearing, rating, fifth. Held from the first of July. Guild of Transit Operators, sign and hold."

He waited, because there were two more figures on the same line and he read those as well, and then he came down off the step and got his rag and started on the next block.

The Square did not do anything. That is the thing about a wall. It is the loudest object in a place and it is not an event, and about nine people on the pavement looked at the foreman and about six of them looked at Beatrix Halm, who was standing thirty feet off with a shut notebook, and then everyone went back to what they had been doing.

It went around the Square in about ninety seconds and it was in nine mouths in the tea cart's queue before the foreman had finished the block.

The woman who keeps the tea cart on the north end had a Thursday with that wall for eleven years and she reads it the way other women read a price, from the bottom up, in about four minutes, and she did not do it today because the foreman had read it out loud and she had heard it and hearing it is worth more.

"So the girl is on it," she said to the man who was waiting for a cup, and the man said that was the first time anybody in that Square had said the girl's name.

"He read it out in the street, Elsie. He did not know her from the fruit and he read it out."

Beatrix wrote it down standing up, with the notebook against the wall, and she wrote it down the way she had written four years of traffic in a small level hand without a single adjective in it, and she read it back to herself once and then tore nothing out and closed it.

"I want you to read that," she said, and gave it to him.

He read it. It said what the foreman had said and it said the date the guild had sent it up, which was the twenty-fifth of this month, two days ago, in a fortnight in which she had not once been asked about it.

"Two days," Beatrix said. "They sent it up on the twenty-fifth and this house did not know until a man on a ladder read it out in a square."

"Who put it up."

"The guild." She took the page back and looked at the wall rather than at him.

"A security that is not published binds only the two who made it. One that is published on the ranks wall binds everybody who takes from the account afterwards, and that is why it goes up. They posted their hold on my hearing because they are afraid, and they are afraid because an account under its floor is an account that can be called, and an account that can be called takes its securities with it."

"They posted it because of you. That is the whole of it and I want it said in the open air where I am standing, because I have been carrying it since the first of July and I carried it on the nineteenth into a notebook in a kitchen and I told you not to forgive you about it, and now it is in a shade on a wall with my name on it in letters a foot high and a man has read it out loud to a street."

"I know."

"You do not know that, and no one else does either, because there is not one person in this city who could read your name off that wall and tell you what it means." She put the notebook under her arm.

"Every word of it is now in a hand I do not keep. It is on a wall, and the wall gets repainted every month and the words do not go anywhere, and when I am answering a panel in a year I am going to have a foreman's Thursday in my favour and a guild that cannot say when it decided.

"And I have been the woman who keeps the copy for four years, and I have been wrong about that for four years, and you worked it out in three sentences on the nineteenth."

"You want it struck."

"No. I want it left." Beatrix Halm looked at the lower half of the wall, where the shade was still wet enough to show a brush mark in it. "If they strike it they can strike it again. It is up, it has a date on it, and it has been read out in a public place by a man doing his job, and that is the only copy of it that four hundred people heard at the same time."

Isolde Marchetti came along Bellhouse Row's end of the Square at about half past four with her coat open and no case, and she went under the wall and started on the old tune with the six and the seven in it, and the Square agreed with her in about four seconds the way it does, and the man with the barrow put it down again.

Beatrix Halm looked at her for about a minute and then went and stood under the wall.

"You told me on the twentieth not to stand under it," he said.

"I told you not to stand under it, and I have been right about that for seven days and I am going to stand under it now for a different reason and I am not asking you to come and you are not coming."

She stood in the middle of the crowd with her eyes shut and her good ear turned up at the tiles and let four thousand people agree with a woman about a thing that was not true, on purpose, so that the thing that was true would stop being the loudest thing in that square for the length of a song.

That was the second time in nine days that a thing in this household had been put in front of a room that could not argue with it, and the first time it had been done to him by a man in a good coat who would not give a name, and the second time had been done by a woman with a shut notebook because she could hear her own name and she wanted the tune over it.

He waited for her and then went round the Square and came at the wall from the other end and read the whole lower half of it, which takes about four minutes.

There were nine hundred and something figures on that wall and every one of them was a thing somebody could lose, and he had walked past this wall twice a week for four years as a man walking past a row of numbers, and on the twenty-seventh of July he read it to the end and it took him four minutes and it did not contain one single thing about him except four words on the bottom band.

The man on the ground with the ladders came past him about halfway along and stopped.

"You're on the card," he said.

"I am on the card."

"Don't go up to look for it, then." He shouldered the ladder and went to put it on the barrow. "They go up on the last working day and it is not our shade. It is a different office.

"It comes up in a colour you have not seen on this wall, and it does not get painted over till the next month, and the men who were on the crew the last time a figure on that wall went wrong still talk about it in the tea hut."

"What figure went wrong."

"You do not want to hear it from me and I do not want to have said it." He got the barrow moving.

"I will tell you the half of it that is free. That wall has never once said *out of band* about anybody. It has said within since the wall was painted. In three days it is going to say it about one building, in a shade this square has not had before, and every one of us on this crew will be asked what it means by somebody with a right to ask. I am telling you now that none of us knows."

Near the end, on the bottom band, in the shade the first of the month had laid down, was the address.

34-6-118. Plate 8,138. Floor 8,141. Tolerance three. Within.

It was the third line of that entry that he could not get past. Within. A household with four turned lines and a plate three grains under the floor of its band, sitting on a wall in a public square on the line that says it is where it is supposed to be.

The quarterly bands go up on the last working day of the quarter. That is not a Thursday's business and that is not the crew's business; it is a different office and a different hand and a different shade, and it goes up in three days.

Five. A form on a table at Ferrier Street signed the night before with his name at the foot of it. A hearing paper at Cordwain good until the last day of the month and refused by the woman it belongs to. A form at the north desk with two days of life left in it that will be dead on the twenty-ninth whoever does what. A reconciliation on the Thursday after this one that will put a broker's mark on a dated page.

And a plate at 8,138 against a floor that goes to 8,142 in three days, written on a wall in a shade that has never been there before, where four hundred people can read it and a woman with one ear can hear it read out.

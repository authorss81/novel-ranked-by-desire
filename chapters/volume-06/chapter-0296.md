# The Sentence Under The Column

He went down at ten in the morning on the sixteenth of September, which was the last day, because on the tenth of August she had told him not to come down those stairs again before the sixteenth of September and she had bolted the door behind him to prove she meant it.

The door at the top was open six inches, which was how she opened it in July.

He went down two flights in the dark and neither of them has a window on it, and the floor at the bottom has never once in four years been warm.

There were four books open on the table and the leather folder was out, and it had not been out in four years, and the page under his column was face up on the folder with a figure on it and a date under the figure and two letters at the foot of it.

She had put it out for him.

"Sit where you sat in July," Solveig Rask said.

He sat down in the place he has taken at that table on and off for five years without ever once being asked to sit anywhere else.

"I am not going to ask you for it."

She did not look up.

"I know. You have got down two flights and a cold floor to not ask me for it, which is the most expensive way of not asking a question that I have ever been offered." She squared the folder. "Say what you came to say."

He set both hands down on the table, flat, which is a kitchen habit and has never once been one of that room.

"In July you told me you would not price a thing you could not describe."

"That is what I told you."

"And in July I asked you three times and you said no three times and you told me the reason, and the reason was that a figure out of my mouth onto your page would be evidence about a man and would not be an arithmetic."

"That is still what it is."

"It is." He had rehearsed this for eleven days and he had not rehearsed it well, because there is not much of it. "So I am not going to bring you a figure. I am going to bring you a description."

Solveig Rask put the pen down.

"It is fed at one address."

She did not move for about four seconds.

"Every grain of it comes to thirty-four six one one eighteen." He said it the way he says things into rooms with a door on them. "It has never come to anywhere else. It comes there because of what I am and where I stand and what I am made to do between five and seven in the morning, and it has been coming there for four years. There is a single address, and I said that badly and I am taking it back and saying it again properly."

"Say the last part again."

"One address." He stopped. "That came out as a phrase and I am sorry for it."

"Do not be sorry for it, take it out and put something in its place." She turned her chair round to face him and folded her hands. "What do you mean by a third party."

"An account is fed by somebody and it goes to somebody. Mine is fed by people I have never met and it goes to me." He looked at the page on the folder. "And it is fed at one address and it is fed by a whole quarter of a district going past a door at five in the morning, and none of them know, and that is the only reason there is anything to describe at all."

"How long."

"Four years."

"Which quarter."

"The third. Every quarter. The second as well."

Solveig Rask sat quite still for a length of time.

"On the tenth of August I told you that a number a man is holding gets smaller every day he carries it," she said. "And you have come down here on the last day I am allowed to be the only woman who can do this, and you have not brought me a number. You have brought me a street."

"That is what I brought you."

"Do you know what you have done."

"I have made it describable."

"You have handed me the only sentence I have wanted for four years," she said, "and I told you in July that I would not price a thing I could not describe, and you have spent four months refusing to ask me and eleven minutes describing it, and I want you to understand that you could have asked. That is the part I cannot put down."

She picked up the pen and wrote for about a minute and a half and did not let him see it.

Then she turned the folder round.

"There."

Under the column, in her hand, four lines.

*An unrecorded account standing against no ledger. Carried at the address of premises 34-6-118. Answerable by the composition of that premises. Figure: seven.*

The date was the sixteenth of September and it was initialled, and the two letters at the foot were the same two letters that had been at the foot of the reconciliation of the twenty-fourth of August, and not one name appears anywhere on it.

"Seven," he said.

"Seven." She squared the folder. "I did not choose it. It is the only figure that premises can carry and it is the figure you certified fourteen days ago in your own hand over a defect you described in your own hand, and it is the answer to a called figure in this city, and if I had put any other figure under that column it would have been a lie with a date on it."

"Answerable by the composition."

"Answerable by the composition." She put both hands flat on the table, one on each side of the folder. "A called figure is answered at a valuation, and it is answered by paying it or by a production, and a production is a figure of persons answerable at the premises on the day. So the only thing in this city that can answer that account is a production off those premises, and the only figure that premises can give is seven."

He did not speak for about four seconds.

"You initialled it."

"I initialled it."

"You signed the one of the third of August with your whole name at the foot of it."

"The third of August concerned a pen-holder and the hand that held it, and it wanted a named person standing behind it, and I knew what that would cost and I paid it in nine seconds for a man who told me to."

Solveig Rask did not raise her voice at all.

"This is about an address. A signed line about an address is evidence about the people who keep it, and I stop being the keeper of this room this afternoon."

"And I am not going to put eight women in a real book in the last hours of an office I am handing over."

"So it is yours."

"It is not mine. It is initialled and it is dated and it goes out of this room in the ordinary way, and a woman holding these keys tomorrow will read it, and she has never met you and she will not." She closed the folder. "I priced it for you, Lin Hao. Not one person in this city asked me to, and you came down two flights of a cold stair to do it yourself."

He put his hand on the table about an inch from the folder and did not touch it.

"Read it back to me."

"You have read it."

"Read it back to me," he said, "because I want to hear whether it says what I think it says."

Solveig Rask turned the folder round again and read the four lines out loud in a room with no window, at the rate of a woman reading an address.

Then she shut the folder and put her cup beside her right hand.

"Now hear the rest of it, once, because you have been carrying arithmetic since the seventh of July and I am going to give you the only three lines that matter."

"Go on."

"You could have refused it." She did not move. "You had four months to come down here and ask me for a figure and get a no, and a no costs you nothing, and a described account that nobody prices cannot be called, cannot be answered, and cannot be requisitioned. That is what you had. That is what four years of me saying no was for."

"I know that."

"You did not know that. You came down here to make it true in a book because you are the kind of man who would rather be written down than be looked at, and because a number you are frightened of is smaller than a number you cannot find."

She looked at him over the folder.

"And now there is a date against it, and a figure with a date against it does not behave like an offer. Offers have an end to them. That one does not have an end to it."

The stone floor was doing what it had done every day for four years.

"So it will be called."

"It will be called at the turn of the quarter, and it will be answered by a production of seven persons sworn by somebody answerable at those premises."

She turned the chair back to the books.

"If that figure is short by one, the mark stands at the figure of the head instead, and the difference is taken out of a book a woman has kept open for thirty years."

"You did that to a broker on the fifteenth of yesterday and she wrote it down and dated it, and it is in four divisions by the end of the week."

He got up about halfway and then sat down again.

"What is the price of it."

"You have just been told the price." Solveig Rask looked up. "The price is seven. It was always going to be seven. You wanted a figure instead of a house and you have got a house, and in about four years the examination passes to whoever is standing in this room, and that person will be answerable for seven people they have never met and will never be able to ask any of them a question."

The door at the top of the stairs was open six inches and the two of them could hear the city above it.

"One more thing," she said, "and then I am going to put you out of this room for the last time."

"Say it."

"I have not looked under that column since the twenty-fourth of July." Solveig Rask closed her hand on the pen. "Three months and twenty-three days, and I did it for a reason I gave you in August, and the reason was that this room changes hands on the seventeenth and after that I would not be the only woman able to put a figure on a page about your account."

"You were not going to be one of several."

"I was going to be one of a great many, and every one of them will read that line this week." She stood up and squared the four books with her hip. "There is one of them now and it is in a book that has an address on it and not a name, and you are not in it, and that is the last of the room."

She held the door open for him with her foot and did not come up the stairs.

He went up the two flights and out into the street with nothing in his hands except a number he had asked for.

Fifteen days to the turn, and a price in a real book that nobody could call back, and it was not his.

It was the figure of a household, and every woman keeping an address inside it was in a dated line in a keeper's book from the sixteenth of September, and not one name is on the page, and none ever will be.

# The Book Of The Quarter

She went on the fourth, because she wanted a day of it she did not have to spend, and she came back on the fifth with her coat off and a piece of paper in her hand that was a counterfoil.

"Say it quickly," Runa Sato said. "I have been wrong twice about this week and I would like to be right the third time."

"You have been right both times."

"I was right about the count and I was wrong about what I was counting. Those are different failures and I only discovered that at about eleven o'clock this morning in a corridor."

She put the counterfoil on the table.

"The clerk at the premises desk wanted to know why I wanted to see a spine," Runa Sato said. "I said I was counting documents about an address and she said that is a thing you count at a counter and not at a shelf, and she was right, and then she went and got the book off the shelf and put it on the counter because it is a public book and anybody may read a public book."

"And she read the seventh line while she had it open."

"She read the seventh line and she put her thumb on it and she said there was nothing in it, and she said it the way you say there is nothing in the second drawer." Runa Sato turned her pencil over in her fingers. "And then she shut it and asked me whether I wanted a copy of the leaf or a copy of the book, and I said the leaf, because a leaf is a document and I am a person who counts documents."

"There is a fifth thing."

Sun Meiling turned her head.

"The return of the third of June," said Runa Sato. "The district premises return for thirty-four six one one eighteen for the third quarter. The one with the seventh line empty and the count at the top of it saying seven."

"That is in the file at Cobb Street. It has been in four divisions since the second of August."

"It is in the file at Cobb Street as an exhibit. That is the same sheet and it is not the same object."

Runa Sato sat down and put her hands flat on the counterfoil.

"The return of the third of June is not a sheet. It is a leaf in a bound quarter-book, and the district keeps one book per premises per quarter, and the leaf is ruled against the ones before it and the ones after it."

"And there are about forty other premises in the same book with their own lines and their own totals," she said, "and a clerk who reads the page reads a line and not a spine, and that is the entire reason your five became four."

"How many books."

"Three on the shelf for that address. The first quarter, the second, and the third, which opened on the first of April and has been open ever since." She turned the counterfoil round. "And a fourth comes out on the first of October and that one will be open for three months and then it will be closed."

"Is it a book."

Runa Sato looked up.

"Yes."

She said it the way she says the figure of a plate, and then she let the room have about four seconds.

"That is your fifth, and it is the only one of the five that is a book, and I did not count it on the first of September because a clerk counts documents and not spines."

She put the counterfoil down again.

"A document is a thing somebody writes and sends. A book is a room, and you do not count a room when you are counting what somebody has done to your family."

"It has been in four divisions since the second of August and it will be in the others inside a week," she said. "And not one clerk in this city has ever thought of it as a fifth anything, which is the safest place a thing can possibly be."

Nadia Ferrous said something from the settle that the room did not take up.

"Does that matter," said Sun Meiling.

"It matters because of the seventh line," Runa Sato said. "And it matters because of a rule I have known since the second of August and have not had a reason to ask about until this morning."

She read it off the counterfoil, which she had copied out of the premises rules at the counter with a clerk watching her do it.

"A premises book opens on the first of a quarter and it closes on the thirtieth of the last month of that quarter. It is not reopened. A head of premises may enter once, in his own hand, in a line of that book that stands blank, and only while the book is open, and the entry is made against the leaf and not against a copy."

"And after it closes."

"After it closes there is a bar on the certificate and a blank line in a closed book, and the two of them go on existing together for as long as the book exists." Runa Sato put the pencil behind her ear. "Sun, you have spent a month telling everybody that a bar on a certificate is permanent. It is. But it is permanent because a book closes. It was never the paper that did it."

Sun Meiling did not say anything for a while.

"How long."

"The thirtieth of September. That is the day the third-quarter book for those premises closes and goes onto the store shelf, and on the first of October a new one comes out and there is no leaf in it for that quarter with anything in it." Runa Sato tapped the counterfoil once. "Twenty-five days, counted the way I count things, which is elapsed, and the last day you could enter is the thirtieth and not the first."

He had believed since the first of September that the seventh line was permanent because of a thing he had written in his own hand into box four of a form.

It was still true. It was simply true for a second reason, and the second reason was not his and had a date on it and belonged to a district office that had never heard of him.

"And I can enter in it," he said.

"You can enter in it once, in your own hand, while the book is open, and there are twenty-five days left in which to do it." Runa Sato did not look up. "That is not news to me. That has been the shape of my morning since eleven o'clock, and I have not said it to anybody, and I am saying it to you because somebody in this kitchen has to know it before the ninth."

"That is her."

"Do not go up to her rooms," said Sun Meiling.

"I know. There is a term and it holds and I have not put a paper in front of her where she sleeps for forty-nine days and I am not going to on the fifty-first."

"Then you cannot tell her."

"I can ask her to come to the kitchen."

"You can ask her to come to the kitchen and you cannot tell her why, and if you ask her to come to the kitchen without saying why, then a woman who reads records for a living will work out why in about eleven seconds and then she is on a tram for two hours deciding whether to come."

He went up the hill at about four in the afternoon and stood at the bottom of the stairs on Lantern Row, two floors above a laundry, for about twenty minutes, and then went up and asked her in the passage, in the street, with his hat in his hands, whether she would come to Ferrier Street on the ninth.

She did not answer him for a count of four.

"No."

He put his hat away.

"That is fair."

"It is." Ines Duarte had her back against the wall of the passage and she was looking at him the way she looks at a form. "You have worked something out since the fifth and it has a date on it, and the date is before the seventeenth because everything you have found out lately has a date on it before the seventeenth."

"Yes."

"And you cannot tell me, because telling me means a paper, and a paper means a room, and a room means the ninth of July."

"Or a kitchen at seven in the evening with eight women in it."

"Which is a public document with legs."

"It is a kitchen."

"It is eight women who have all been told something this month and not one of them by me."

He did not have an answer to that and he stood in the passage for another count of four.

"Ines."

"I will come on the ninth," she said. "At seven. And I will come because you are not able to tell me, which is the only reason in this world that has ever made me get on a tram."

"You said no."

"I said no to the asking." She came out of the passage to the top of the stair. "I have known since the third. You went out at ten past nine and you came back at eleven and you have not said a word to anybody in this house since and you have asked Runa Sato for a paper and Runa Sato does not hand out paper."

She went down one step.

"You have found a book," said Ines Duarte. "And there is a line in it that is about me, and there is a day on which it closes, and you are afraid of the day and not of the line."

He stood in the passage with his hat in his hands.

"How long have you known."

"Since the third," she said. "And I have spent two days working out whether I would come, and I have not worked out whether I will be angry, and I would like you to make the tea before I get there because I have not eaten since the first and there is nothing in this house that I am going to be able to eat standing up."

"Nina—"

"Ines." She looked at him down the stair. "You have been getting it wrong for five months and you are going to get it right in twenty-five days or you are not going to get it right at all, and today is not the day to practise on me."

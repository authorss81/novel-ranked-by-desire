# The Room After The Sitting

He was not in the building on the seventeenth and he was not in the building on the eighteenth, and on the nineteenth Runa Sato put a sixth thing on the kitchen table between the sugar and the docket.

"Six," she said.

"Read it out."

"The page that went into four divisions on the third of August with a leaf left empty on purpose. The composition and the correction. The reconciliation of the twenty-fourth, initialled and not signed. The broker's statement of the twenty-ninth, signed, four lines, a number on it. The certificate of the first of September, your name on it twice." She put her finger on the sixth. "And the quarter-book."

"That was the fifth."

"The quarter-book was the fifth thing and it is the only one of the six that is in a spine rather than in a division." Runa Sato sat down. "And there is a sixth thing that is not on this table and I am not going to write it out, and you have had it since yesterday afternoon and you have not said a word about it to any of us."

"A priced line in a real book," he said.

"Dated the sixteenth, initialled, four lines, no name, an address and a figure of seven." She shut the notebook. "It went out of that room three days ago in the ordinary way and it is in four divisions tonight and by the end of the week it will be in a fifth. And it is the only document in this city that cannot be unsaid."

Sun Meiling had her hand flat on the table.

"You asked her for it."

"She priced it because I described it, and I described it because I had four months to ask her for a figure and did not, and on the tenth of August she put me out of a room and bolted the door on me for asking." He looked at the sugar. "I could have asked. She gave me the reason in one sentence, and it was about a man putting a number into a book about himself, and she was right then and she is right now."

"And you went down on the sixteenth and got a figure out of her mouth."

"I went down on the sixteenth and gave her a sentence she had wanted for four years," he said, "and she priced it in ninety seconds, and the price is seven, and it is the figure of this house."

The room did not say anything for a while. The window was two inches open.

"So the answer," Runa Sato said, "has to be sworn by somebody in this room."

"Or by me."

"Not by you. You have promised a woman on a step that you will not be on the step, in the yard, or anywhere near that door between the hour before the round and the hour after it, and she said she would write a second report about a man she lives with, and she will write it correctly."

"A valuation is the mark at ten to nine on the first. A production is sworn in a chained book at the Exchange." He turned his pencil over on the table. "I can be at the Exchange at ten to nine and be in this building at seven, because the Exchange is not this building."

Runa Sato wrote that down.

"My own name goes first again, and it stays first whatever you decide. Sworn evidence is Bureau work, and there is exactly one of us at this table who can lay it down without a mess. I told you that on the second and I have not moved since."

"No."

"You do not have to think about it."

"I thought about it on the second," he said, "and I have thought about it every day since, and I am saying no in about nine seconds so you cannot accuse me of having considered it slowly."

"That is not an answer either." Runa Sato put the pencil down flat. "You made me put my name first. In August you gave a broker your word, standing in that broker's rooms, that merit would not go into a figure by way of a person."

"And I told you on the second that the promise covers merit and not persons, and you agreed with me at the table, and you have been hiding behind the word for four days."

He did not answer.

"You are not refusing me because of the promise."

"I am refusing you because of the first of August," he said. "At ten past nine, with a pen on a chain off somebody else's wrist, and the number written down in nine seconds before I had finished deciding, and that figure has been in four divisions ever since and it cost me a house eight women live in."

"That is a feeling and not a reason."

"It is the reason and you know it is the reason." He put his hand flat on the table next to the notebook. "A number of seven sworn by a Bureau field operative at the turn, against premises she lives in, is not an answer to a standing. It is the Bureau entering my household into its own book in its own hand, and every office in this city will read that as the Bureau having found it."

"Then it is you."

"Then it is me," he said, "at ten to nine on the first, in my own hand, on the same page as the one I swore on the first of August."

"And the figure has to be seven."

"The figure has to be seven."

He waited for somebody to say it and Beatrix Halm said it from the other end of the table.

"Seven," she said. "And I have written the number of persons at thirty-four six one one eighteen on the morning of the first in a notebook with a question mark under it, and I did that on the twelfth."

"How many days out are you."

"Nineteen. And it stops changing on the thirtieth." She had her notebook open on the table. "Because it is the nineteenth, and it stops on the thirtieth, and the answer has to be seven on the morning."

"Say what you mean."

"There are eight women in this building," Beatrix Halm said, "and seven of them keep an address here. The seventh line is a woman who keeps one and has not slept in it since the sixth of July, and the eighth is a woman on no roll in this city who has never had an address here at all, and that is not an accident, it is a thing we decided, and it is the only reason the answer is seven."

"And if one of the eight took an address tonight."

"Then the answer is eight and there is no production of seven and the mark stands at the figure of the head." She shut the notebook. "And if the seventh line gave up the address she has never slept in, then the answer is six and the mark stands at eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and the difference is taken out of an account a woman has kept open for thirty years."

Nothing was said.

"So that is what I am asking the kitchen for," Beatrix Halm said. "Three days. From the thirtieth at midnight until ten to nine on the first. Nobody moves in or out, nobody takes an address, and nobody gives one up."

"That is not a thing a house does."

"That is exactly a thing a house does, and this house has never been asked for it, and I have written it in my book."

She did not raise her voice.

"And I am not asking it because of the guild and I am not asking it because of my hearing. I am asking it because eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight grains is the price of me."

"And I would like to know that I have not cost this house a figure before I find out what the figure was."

He stood up and put his hand on the back of a chair.

"Three days," he said. "From midnight tonight and not from the thirtieth, because there is nothing to be gained by pretending it starts late. No one moves and no one takes an address and no one leaves until I come back from the Exchange on the morning of the first with a mark in a book."

"That is a rule."

"That is not a rule, that is me asking eight women."

"Then it is a rule until somebody disobeys it," said Runa Sato, "and you should say it as a rule, because a thing that is only a request gets tested at about two in the morning by somebody who did not hear it."

"Then it is a rule."

Wren Kallo had been at the table since about a quarter past five and had not spoken, which she has never done at that table in five months.

"What happens," she said, "if one of us wants to leave."

The kitchen went quiet in a way he had not heard it go quiet before.

"That is not a hypothetical question and you have not been in this house all afternoon," Beatrix Halm said. "You have not had an address here in nineteen years and you are on nothing at any time, and there are nine people at this table and every one of them has worked out by now that you are the only one in this room that could take an address tonight and change the figure without anybody noticing that they had done it."

"Yes," said Wren Kallo.

"So ask it properly."

"I am asking it properly." Wren Kallo had both hands flat on the table and she was not looking at him. "I have told six people a thing in five months and I cannot stop, and on the twenty-second of August I filed it and made it ordinary, and seventeen days later a man with a pencil wrote my number in the margin of a quarter-book and that is the price of boring and I have known it for eleven days."

She looked up.

"And if I take an address at that door on the thirtieth the figure is eight and a woman I live with carries the difference for the rest of her life, and if I take one and go again on the morning of the first that is fraud, and I have not committed one in nineteen years."

"So do not take one."

"I am going to." Wren Kallo said it flatly, with no performance in it at all. "Not on the thirtieth and not on the first. On the second of October, at about eleven in the morning, at a counter, in the ordinary way, where a clerk will take it because there is a rule that says a person of reading age keeping an address at a premises may be added at any time except while a book is closed."

She put her hands in her lap.

"That is the only shape it can take and I have had it since the sixteenth and I have been sitting at this table for two hours waiting for somebody to give me a day to do it on."

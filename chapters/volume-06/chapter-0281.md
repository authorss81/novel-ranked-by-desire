# Two Flights Of Stair

Beatrix Halm came in at about ten past nine with the painters' card under her arm, which is a thing no one in this house has ever done before, and put it face up on a table that has been bare for twenty-seven days.

"Ten August," she said. "It came off the board at the north end yesterday and it says the thirtieth of this month and it says eight thousand one hundred and forty-three."

"And it is the same card and the same shade and the same word that has been on that wall since the end of July."

Sun Meiling looked at it without picking it up.

"So the word is the word," she said, "and only the number moves."

"The word is the word." Beatrix took her hand off the table and put her notebook down on it, which she does before she says anything she wants kept. "Five under from the thirtieth instead of four. I have written four dates in this book since the twenty-seventh and they are the only four dates in it, and I would like somebody to tell me what a fifth one is for."

Nobody answered her, and she put the notebook away.

He had been up since five and had come in with the first tram, and there were seven of them in the kitchen with the window open two inches, because Sun Meiling will not have a shut window in August.

He told them the shape of the month in the order he had it in his head, and it took about eleven minutes, and not one person in the room interrupted him once.

"Six things," he said. "The sixteenth is a desk and it is hers and I am in the room for it."

"Some time before the month is out a broker is going to put a number on a table in a room with four officers in it, and I am not going to be near that building."

"The thirtieth is the card. The first of September is a certificate with a bar on it and Sun Meiling's name in ink underneath the bar."

"There is a woman in a division who is going to tell a sixth person something this week and cannot stop herself. And a keeper two floors under a sales floor has given me a date and has not given me a price."

Runa Sato had her pencil out before he finished and had not been asked.

"Twenty-one days," she said. "To the first. And your pencil row is a hundred and forty days old this morning, which is the figure I have had in this house since the fifth."

"And I am going to say the other one, because there is nobody else in this room who will."

"Go on."

"The keeper's sitting is the sixteenth of September. That is three hundred and eighty-one days. I have been counting it out loud since the ninth of July and it is the only figure in this house that has not changed direction."

Sun Meiling took the notebook and shut it.

"A cage has a door at this end and a door at that end," Nadia Ferrous said from the settle, "and about forty people go through it a night."

"And every one of those six things is going to be read by somebody who does not know it is six things. They are going to read them one at a time in four buildings and each of them is going to look ordinary."

"Nadia."

"I am not being brave, I am being arithmetic." She had her hand flat on the cloth bag. "It went in on the third with a blank page in it on purpose, and I am not sorry about the blank page."

"On the first of September there is going to be a certificate with a bar on it, and on the twenty-ninth of this month there is going to be a broker's statement with a number on it."

"And by the end of next week every division in this city is holding one piece of my house and not one of them is holding two."

She stopped, and Runa Sato wrote something down without being asked and did not read it back.

Sun Meiling had the notebook in both hands.

"Go on," she said. "You have had the sixth one in your mouth since the sixth and you have not put it on that table."

"And Runa has been counting out loud for a fortnight so that you would not have to say it yourself. So say it."

"A page under a column in a room with no window," he said. "Thirty-one lines in it and a figure, and two letters at the foot of it that are mine."

"She has not looked at it since the twenty-fourth of July, and she told me on the fourth of August that she will not look at it before the sixteenth of September."

"And you want it."

"I want to know what is behind it before the first of September," he said, "because I am going to be asked to certify a document on the first and I would like to know what I am standing on while I do it."

"Twenty-one days," Runa Sato said.

He went down at four o'clock.

Two flights of stair with no window on them, and a stone floor at the bottom that has never been warm in the four years he has been going there.

The door at the top had been shut every single one of those four years except the third of August.

It was shut.

He knocked and waited and then knocked again, and she opened it about six inches and did not step back.

"You have been out of this room once," Solveig Rask said, "and it was five weeks ago and you were not asked to leave. Now you are going to be told."

"I know."

"You do not, because you have come down with something to ask for and you have not been told anything yet."

She turned and went in and left the door where it was, and he came in and shut it, because a door in that room did not stay open once anybody had been told to leave it.

There were four books on the table and not five, and the leather folder was not on it, and her cup was beside her right hand and had not been in it.

"Sit down," she said. "You have about nine minutes and I am going to use all of them, and at the end of them I am going to put you out of this room."

"And it will not be like the last time."

He took the chair at the end of the table nearest the door, which was a thing he had done every day for five years and had never once done in a room he was going to be put out of.

"The sentence under the column," he said. "That is the third time. I will put it once and then I go when you tell me to go."

Solveig Rask did not look up from the page.

"Twenty-one days," she said.

"That is not an answer."

"That is Runa Sato's figure, and I have carried that clock every working morning since the thirteenth of July, and I did not need it from your kitchen."

She put the pen down.

"I am going to tell you what you are doing, and then I am going to do it, and you are not going to argue with the second part, because the second part is not an argument. It is the door."

She squared the four books.

"You are not asking me what your account is worth because you want to know what your account is worth. You have wanted that since the thirteenth of July and you have had four weeks and you did not come down once."

"You are asking me now because on the first of September you are going to sign a piece of paper that a woman asked you to sign, and you are frightened."

"And a frightened man goes and looks for a number to put under the fear."

She gave it the length of a count in a room that was very cold.

"And if I gave you the number tonight," she said, "you would have it for twenty-one days and you would get smaller every day you carried it, because a number a man is holding is a thing he is holding."

"I have watched a great many people in this city discover that over about a month."

"It would also stop being true in about eleven days, and that is the part that matters and I am going to say it once."

Solveig Rask looked up.

"If you have that figure in your head before the first of September, then on the first of September you will write it somewhere without deciding to."

"And on some day in October a division will read a certificate with your name at the foot of it and a number on the same page, and no one will be able to establish where you got the number from."

"That is not innocence. That is a woman being cross-examined on a coincidence."

The room was very cold and the page made a noise when she turned it.

"You have asked me twice before," she said, "and both times you were asking about what you would be able to do with it. This is the first time you have asked because you are afraid."

"And I am not going to reward the difference."

She squared the page.

"I am going to tell you why in one sentence and then take it back out of this room for good," she said. "A figure that comes out of your mouth and onto my page is not a price. It is evidence about a man. I will not do it."

"Get up."

He got up.

"Do not come down these stairs again before the sixteenth of September," she said. "Not because of what has happened in this room."

"Because on the sixteenth of September this room changes hands, and after that I will not be the only woman who can put a figure on a page about your account. And I am not going to be one of several."

"That is the reason."

"That is the reason and it took me nine days to find it," she said. "I am telling you it now because I have said no to you three times in five weeks."

"And a woman who says no three times owes the fourth one a reason, and that is all I owe you. It is not very much and you may take it upstairs."

He went up the two flights.

The door at the top was shut before he got to it, and he heard the bolt go, which she has never done, and he stood on the landing for a while with his hand on the rail.

He had gone down that afternoon with one thing and come up with two, and the second was worse than the first.

It was the only one in this city that could stop him writing on the first of September: **there was a date after which she would not be the only woman able to write a figure about him.**

She had been promised exactly one thing for the seventeenth of September, and it was that the book changed hands.

He walked back up the hill in the last of the light with nothing in his hands, the way he had walked down it, and there were twenty-one days left and six things on a list.

The keeper who had just told him, in a cold room, in nine minutes, that the first woman he had ever been frightened for had closed a door on him to keep him from making a mistake in her own name.

He had been four years learning to be invisible in two offices.

He had now made himself legible in a third, and the woman who ran the third had told him it was for his own good, and had shut a door on him to prove it.

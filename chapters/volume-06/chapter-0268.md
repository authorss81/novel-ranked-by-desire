# The Book In Her Own Rooms

She came at nine in the evening on the twenty-eighth with a leather bag under her arm that had a strap worn shiny on one side, and she put it on the bare table and did not take her coat off.

Fourteen days. The table in that kitchen had been bare for fourteen days and in fourteen days a broker's account had come back into it and been put down on it in front of five women.

"Nobody has touched it," Marguerite Vale said. "I would like that understood before anybody in this room decides what the next four minutes are."

Nobody in the room had been in the building at nine that evening except Beatrix, who had come in off the yard tram and read a wall in the Square, and Wren, who had come out of her room with a book in her hand and stood at the top of the stair, and Nadia, who had her own book open on the settle with the hour going on the page.

Runa Sato had taken the last house's carriage. Sun Meiling was at the market. Agnete Lindqvist was asleep on the landing by the stair lamp with the blanket over her that Wren Kallo had put there four nights ago and that neither of them had mentioned since.

The bag was the size of a satchel and it was not heavy and it had the shape of a thing that has been carried out of a room and put down and picked up and carried somewhere else several times in a fortnight.

"That is my working book," Marguerite Vale said. "That is my attestation book. That is the key to the chained room, which has been on a ring round my own wrist every working night for four years. And that is a sheet of paper with six dates on it, which is my own record of where those three things have been since the fourteenth of July, written out in my own hand this afternoon, with nothing on it but my hand."

She squared it where it stood.

"You asked a woman two floors under a sales floor on the twenty-fourth to compare this account with this," she said. "She told you what it would say and she told you what it would cost and you said do it, and it is done, and on the Thursday morning it goes into a real book with a date on it."

She took her coat off at that point, and hung it on the rail by the stair where it had hung in March, and came back and stood with her hands on the back of a chair.

"It will say that a call was entered in the real book on the twentieth of July at twenty-five minutes past four, and that my own account does not carry it, and that by how much. That is the sentence. That is the whole of what she is capable of writing and she wrote it to you in advance and she was exact."

"And the reason it does not carry it," he said.

Marguerite Vale looked at the bag on the table.

"You have a theory and I am going to give it to you for nothing, and then I am going to ask you for something, and I am telling you the order in advance so you know which one you are being bought with."

"Say the theory."

"The pen is chained to a wrist and the chain is opened with a key, and the key is on my ring, and my ring has been in that bag in two rooms above a shop on the north side since the fourteenth of July." She said it without any weight.

"And I have not been in the chained room since the eighteenth, because on the eighteenth I put the working book in the bag and took the key with it. I have never in four years left that key on a floor.

"On the twentieth I was at home at twenty-five minutes past four with my coat on and my bag on a chair, and I am telling you that as a fact about a woman and not as a thing anybody can check."

"You were in the building on the twentieth," he said. "You signed the attendance book at ten."

"I signed the attendance book at ten and I was at home at half past four and both of those are true and neither of them is evidence." Marguerite Vale put one hand flat on the bag. "Now the part I am asking you for."

"Go on."

"I know what hand made that entry." She did not lower her voice and she did not raise it. "It was a hand, and the device was mine, and it was not me, and there is one reason in this city for that being true and it is not a reason you are going to enjoy, and I am not going to tell you what it is tonight."

"No."

"No." She took her hand off the bag. "I will say it to a panel, under my own signature, with four officers in the room and a number on the table, before the end of the month, and I will not say it to you or to anybody in this house.

"The second I say it to you it is a story a head has been told by his broker, and a story like that is worth nothing in the only room where it could be used.

"You have spent the last month putting things on paper on purpose in front of people. If I say this to you in a kitchen it goes in a mouth with no date on it, and there is one man in this city who can make a thing out of a mouth, and it is me, and I am the person it would have to be said against."

"So you are asking me for a week of not knowing."

"I am asking you for the rest of my life of not asking me," Marguerite Vale said. "On the ninth of July you gave a woman a term in the dark and you have kept it for nineteen days. I am asking for the same thing in the same light and with a worse reason, and I am not going to insult you by dressing it as anything else."

The kitchen had the shape of an argument in it and none of the five women in it moved.

"Is there anything else you are putting on this table," he said, "besides the reason I am not to have."

"One thing."

"Then put it down."

She took the bag off the table and turned it round so the strap was facing him and squared it again.

"If it goes back onto the fourth floor tonight it is on the floor at nine, and the working book is where the reconciliation expects to find it, and on the Thursday a keeper reads the real book against my own account and finds a disagreement on a date and a figure, and that page goes into four divisions by the end of the week.

"That is what you asked for on the twenty-fourth. That is what it costs. That is not new to you and I am not bringing it to you as new."

"And if it does not go back tonight."

"Then on that Thursday there is no broker's own account to reconcile against, because the broker's own account is in a kitchen in Ferrier Street, and a reconciliation that cannot find the other half of a figure does not publish a disagreement, it publishes a gap." She picked up the bag. "A gap closes in about eleven days with a form and a signature. A disagreement closes in about four years, if ever, and goes in four divisions first."

"Eleven days," said Beatrix Halm from the doorway. She had her notebook in her hand and had been in the doorway for some time. "And the form and the signature would say that a broker's own account was in a private address for nine days. That is a removal. That is the thing that takes a broker off a floor."

"That is the thing that takes a broker off a floor," Marguerite Vale agreed.

"So it is the disagreement or the gap," Runa Sato said. She had come in behind Beatrix without either of them hearing her. "And the disagreement has a woman on it by the Thursday and the gap has a woman on it by the ninth of next month."

"Both."

"Both." Runa Sato looked at Sun Meiling's coat on the rail, because Sun Meiling had come in from the market and was standing in the doorway behind them with her coat still on and her flat pocket held down under her hand. "There is a third thing and not one person in this room has said it, and I will say it now and then I am going upstairs.

"There is a key in that bag to a room where accounts are kept, and on the first of August at ten to nine a woman with a pen on a chain is going to write one hundred and thirty-four in that room.

"If that bag is in this kitchen then the room on the floor has a key on a wall in Ferrier Street and a woman on the wall who can open it, and every office in this city that has been trying since June to find out what happens at thirty-four six one one eight has been given a way in by a head of household."

Sun Meiling took her hand off her pocket.

"Where does it go back to," she said. "Tonight. That is the only question. Not Thursday. Not the ninth of next month. Tonight."

"It goes back onto the fourth floor at nine o'clock and it goes back into the chained room and it goes on the floor in the attestation book at nine minutes past," Marguerite Vale said.

"Then it goes back," Sun Meiling said.

She came all the way into the kitchen for the first time in that exchange and put her hand flat on the bag's strap.

"I have been a thing in a coat pocket that no office in this city could find, for a hundred and twenty-eight days," she said, "and I liked it, and I asked you on the fifteenth of this month to put me in ink, and I have spent every day since then being told what that costs the two women who are not in this kitchen.

"And I am not going to stand in a kitchen and let a fifth thing be put in a bag in my house so that a sixth thing does not have to happen.

"Take it back tonight and let the Thursday come. When it comes and there is a dated page in four divisions with her on it, I will be standing in this room and I will have said so with my mouth, in front of witnesses, and that is the only thing I have ever wanted out of being a row."

She took her hand off the strap and went upstairs and the whole of the house heard her do it.

Marguerite Vale took the bag up off the table. She held it for a moment with the strap in both hands, at the place on the table where it had been, and she did not say one word about the twenty-eighth of July, and she did not look at Beatrix, and she did not look at Nadia, who had written the hour of it down and her own name against it, and read it back, and read it back again.

She put her coat on.

"There is one thing I want said while I am standing in the room," she said. "Not to me. To him."

They waited.

"Fourteen days ago I carried this bag out of that table and I told a clerk I was going home, and it is true, and I did not say why, and the reason is that a broker who has been sitting at a head's kitchen table every morning for four years has begun to know what that head is worth, and a broker who knows what a thing is worth is a broker with a price on it.

"I took it home to keep it out of a room where it could be asked about at breakfast." She picked up her case. "That was the whole of my reason on the fourteenth, and it was a good reason, and it is the reason there is a page coming on Thursday, and neither of those two facts is going to be in the page and I have had fourteen days to think about which of them I mind more."

She went out and the door was not slammed.

Wren Kallo came out of the stair with her folder shut under her arm.

"You have just agreed not to ask," she said.

"I have."

"That is a term. You have given two women a term in three weeks and you have not given one man a fact in nine years." She put the folder on the settle. "It is the right decision and I am not going to tell you that it was free, because it was not, and because a thing you give up on purpose is still a thing you have given up, and I write those down as well as the other kind."

"Write it down," he said.

He stood in the kitchen after that with the table bare again for the fifteenth time and five women in it.

On that Thursday there would be a page with a broker's name on it and a figure on it and a date on it, because on the twenty-fourth he had gone into a counting room two floors under a sales floor and said two words to get it made, and in two days a woman who had protected a man for four years by carrying her book home to a flat would have a dated disagreement against her name in four divisions.

He had known the price on the twenty-fourth. She had told him the price on the twenty-fourth, standing up, in a cold room two floors under a sales floor, and he had said do it.

He went up and looked at the form on the table at the top of the stairs with his name and the date on it, and the lamp, and the saucer on the bare middle of the table with a pencil row under it that was going into ink in September because he had signed that on the twenty-sixth and it could not be told to the woman it belonged to without knocking on a door he had promised not to knock on.

Tomorrow the instrument upstairs dies. On the Thursday the page comes. In two days the first of August.

And a woman in two rooms on the north side of this city has no idea yet, and there is no lawful way to tell her, and he has just promised a second woman never to ask.

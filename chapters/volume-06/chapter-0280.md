# The Seventh Step Down

The painters' card comes off the board at the north end of the ranks wall a few days before it lands, and on the ninth of August it said the thirtieth of this month and eight thousand one hundred and forty-three, and Beatrix Halm read it at five in the morning standing in the dark.

"Five under," she said.

"Five under."

"Three under for four years and one month, four under for eleven days, five under on the thirtieth." She gave the card back. "Twenty-one days."

He told the kitchen at about nine in the morning on the ninth and there were seven of them in it and the table was bare for the twenty-seventh day.

He told it the way he had told them everything since the second, in the order he had worked it out in, and it took about eleven minutes, and the last part of it was that there were two roads and both of them were a name and he was going to go and ask her for it.

Sun Meiling wrote a figure in her notebook and shut it.

"That is the term," she said.

"Yes."

"The term is a paper."

"The term has never been a paper." He said it standing up, in a room with six women in it, on the ninth of August. "She gave me a term in the dark on the ninth of July about paper. I have kept it for thirty-one days by not going up a stair. I am going up the stair this afternoon and I am not going to take anything in my hands, and that is the first honest reading of it I have had since the summer."

Runa Sato said, "You have known that since the seventh."

"I have known it since the night of the seventh at about eleven and I did not say it until this morning, and I want it in the kitchen that I sat on it for a day and a half."

Lantern Row runs two floors above a laundry and the stair smells of soap and heat and the door at the top stands shut, and the rail is hot, and at about a quarter past six on the ninth of August he put his hand on the rail and did not knock.

A floorboard took weight above him. Nothing came to the door for a long time, long enough that he had worked out two different versions of what he was going to say and thrown both of them away.

"You can say it from down there," Ines Duarte said, through the door. "From where you are standing. Come up four steps and I go back inside and shut the door, and I would be right to. You are not going to come up four steps. So say it from down there."

He said it from the seventh step down.

He said that the composition of the premises had been read at seven on the second of August and the seventh line of the return of the third of June was blank and could be filled once, on the re-issue, before the certification on the first of September, by his hand, with his date.

He said that the only office in this city with standing over a district premises return had been hers until the first and was his after it, and that correcting the district's own return required him to certify that the line was not blank, and that certifying it required him to produce the person who returned it.

He said that there were two roads out of the seventh line and that both of them were her name, that he had worked it out in a hall at Cordwain on the sixth, and that he had spent a day and a half at a counter and in a queue proving to himself there was not a third one.

He said that he had come up a stair to ask her one question, and that the question was whether she kept an address at Ferrier Street.

He said it in about ninety seconds and he said it with nothing in his hands, and the stair was hot, and the light on the landing went off while he was doing it and came on again, and at no point did the door open.

It was a long time before she spoke.

"Say the question again," she said.

"You said on the first of August that you were not living at my address."

"I did."

"A composition return does not ask about living. It asks about keeping an address at the premises." He put his hand flat on the rail above the seventh step. "Ines. Do you keep an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen."

The stair was very quiet and the laundry under it made its ordinary noise four floors down.

"Yes," Ines Duarte said.

He had worked out about eleven different things in four days and none of them was the shape of that word.

"I have kept it since the sixth of July," she said. "I have kept an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen since the sixth of July and I have never slept in it and I have not been inside it since the seventh, and I kept it because a correction filed on the thirtieth is an answer on the record and an answer on the record is about an address and not about a woman."

She did not move away from the door.

"If I gave the address up on the seventh of July then the correction I filed on the thirtieth would have been about a person who was not there."

"You filed it knowing that."

"I filed it at twenty-five minutes past eleven at night on a ground floor with one clerk and a lamp and the window shut, and I knew it, and I have been in two rooms above a laundry for two days knowing that a man was going to work out what I had done and come up a stair about it." The door did not open. "Say the next part."

"The certificate issues on the first of September and a blank line is a bar on it."

"Yes."

"And a bar on a certificate is a bar on the certificate."

"Yes, and I taught you that in your own kitchen on the twenty-sixth of July, and you have been using it on other people ever since."

He did not have anything to say to that and he stood on the seventh step and did not say anything.

"You are going to write my name," she said.

"I have come up here to ask whether I have to."

"You have come up here to ask me for a name for a line and you have got it and you are standing in a stairwell holding it, and I want you to hear me say one thing before you go down those stairs with it, and then I am going to shut the door and you are not to come up this stair again until you have written it."

"Say it."

"They put me at a desk on the sixteenth of August." She said it levelly, on the other side of a door, in the dark. "Miss a sitting you have been called to and the division gets a fortnight from the sitting to put you at somebody else's desk with your own name on the head line."

"I told you that at six in the morning on the first, on the bottom step, and you wrote it down somewhere. You are going to want to use it as an argument that you must not write my name."

"Yes."

"Do not." Her voice did not change at all. "Do you understand what you have just made me do? For nine days I have had the only argument there is, and it has been sitting in a file in a building two miles away, and it is the reason I did not go on the second."

She moved away from the door and came back.

"If you write my name on the seventh line of a composition return in four divisions on the first of September, then on the sixteenth of August a division that has spent thirty years learning how to put a member at a desk where she is the subject will have two documents."

"It will have a composition return that says I am the seventh person of a household that is out of band on a public wall. And it will have a correction in my own hand that returned the figure at seven on the night before I lost the office. And the two of them will be read together, and I will be a woman who corrected a record about her own address and then kept the address."

"So do not write it," she said. "Not out of kindness and not because of the ninth of July and not because you think it would keep me safe.

Write it because it is the one thing in this city that is worse for me than a bar on a certificate. I am telling you which one. I am telling you because you have spent four days looking for a road that does not have my name on it, and there is not one, and I would rather you had it from me than work it out on the first of September at a counter."

He stood there.

"What happens on the first," he said.

"A certificate with a bar on it, and a woman in ink who filed four hundred words about being absent with reason and did it herself." A pause. "She wanted you to fill the line. I heard it on the fifth from a woman who cannot keep anything. Do not tell her I said it and do not tell her I am on your stairwell."

"You are glad I came."

"I have been in two rooms above a laundry since the first of August," Ines Duarte said, "waiting for you to work out what I did on the thirtieth, and you worked it out in four days, and it is the first time in five months that anybody in this city has been in a hurry to understand something I did."

She shut the door.

He stood on the seventh step for about two minutes with his hand on a hot rail, and then he went down sixty-one steps to the bottom and across the yard and up the hill, and he did not write anything down for four days.

The kitchen on the ninth of August at half past ten at night had six of them in it.

He said it in three sentences. She keeps an address and does not live there and has kept it since the sixth of July. He has asked her and she has said yes and then she has said do not. On the first of September the certificate issues with a bar on it and Sun Meiling goes into ink on a barred certificate.

Nobody spoke for about a minute.

Sun Meiling was standing at the far end of the table with her palms down, which is what she does.

"Twenty-two days," she said. "And the band comes down on the thirtieth and the certificate comes on the first."

"Yes."

"Then say the thing that has to be said in this room and say it without her name in it, because you are not going to be able to say her name and you have to say the rest of it anyway."

He said it, with the door shut.

It took him about eleven seconds, and **there was not one name in it**. It was the reason a man who had sworn seven into a chained book on the first of August could not put a single figure against the seventh line on the first of September.

It was about a woman who kept an address in two rooms above a laundry for the whole of a month so that an answer she had filed at twenty-five minutes past eleven at night would still be an answer, and it was the first sentence he had said in that kitchen in two months with nothing in it that could be produced.

Runa Sato wrote it down. She did not read it back.

"Leave that one," he said.

"That is the first one in two months I have not read back," Runa Sato said, "and it is because you have told me not to, and I am going to do what you have told me and I want it noted that it is the first time."

Beatrix Halm had her notebook open and had not written in it.

"Twenty-two days," she said. "And a bar. And Sun in ink underneath one, which is what she asked for, in her own words, on the fifth, and she did ask."

"Then it is settled and it is not good and I am not going to have anybody in this kitchen pretending for one night that it is a defeat, because it is a bar and the bar is on her and she chose where to put it."

Beatrix Halm shut the notebook.

"There is a card coming down on that wall on the thirtieth. There is a figure coming up a page in a book in this city on the first of September."

"And between those two things there is a woman who is going to be at a desk on the sixteenth of August. There is a broker who has said she will say something to a panel before the month is out. There is a woman in a division who has told five people and is about to tell a sixth. There is a keeper two floors under a sales floor who signed a page on the third and cannot un-sign it."

She stood up.

"Four hundred and four days," she said, "to the annual sitting. I have counted it out loud since the ninth of July and I am not stopping now and neither is she."

There was a cloth bag on the settle with the twelfth page inside it, written on, and Nadia Ferrous had a hand flat on the top of it.

"The blank one is still blank," she said. "The twenty-ninth. It is going into four divisions on the third and it is going to be blank, and there are three documents about this household in four divisions and one of them is a hole, and I put the hole in on purpose and I have never once regretted it and I have not once stopped wanting to fill it."

"What would you fill it with," he said.

"Nothing," Nadia Ferrous said. "That is the answer and it took me five days. A book with a hole in it is a record. A book with a hole filled in is an account, and this house has more accounts than it has records, and I am not going to be the eleventh one."

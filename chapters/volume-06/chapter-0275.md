# The Name Of A Date

The dead list is read out at ten in the morning on the fourth of a month at the counter on the ground floor of the Exchange, and there were nine accounts on it in July and there were eight on the fourth of August.

He was in the room. He had not needed to be and he was there.

The man who read it read it the way he read all of them, at the speed of a man reading addresses, and the eighth name went past and it was not his, and the room went on standing there, and he found that he had been holding his breath in a way that had nothing to do with his own account.

Then the man said the date out loud the way he always did, and the year, and the quarter, and then he said, "There is no further entry."

She was on the Exchange steps with the folded oilcloth under one arm and the next docket inside it, because the fourth of a month is the one morning of the month she has never once missed in sixty days, whatever the list says or does not say.

"Sixty days," she said.

"Sixty."

"I have carried a hundred and thirty-four dockets since the fourth of June and I have not had a name on one of them, and this morning a man read out eight accounts that are not ours and said the words **there is no further entry** in a room with about ninety people in it."

She looked down at her bundle.

"That is the first morning of my life that has been about something other than carrying."

"Go up and carry it."

"I am going to." She did not move. "Say the thing you have been not saying for three days."

"I am not going to say it to you."

"Then say it to somebody who is not me, because I have been standing on this step since half past eight waiting for you to work out that the hundred and thirty-fourth mark was bought with a figure about a household, and a hundred and thirty-four dockets are a thing a head carries, and Beatrix Halm has been carrying a guild's tail since the twenty-seventh out of a public wall."

Beatrix Halm read the entry on the ranks wall every morning of her shift, and on the fourth she was at the north end of it at about a quarter to six with the town still dark, notebook clamped under one arm, her good ear turned into a square she had walked past for nine years.

He found her at seven at the bottom of the sixty-one steps with the tram behind her.

"Out of band," she said, without lifting her eyes off the wall.

"Out of band."

"That is still true and it is going to be true on the thirtieth of August when the next card comes down, and the floor will be eight thousand one hundred and forty-three, and you will be five under instead of four, and the word on the wall will not change, because the word has been **out of band** since the thirtieth of July, and four under or five under says the same thing in a different number."

"You have read the card on the painters' desk."

"I have read every card on that wall for nine years." She put the notebook under the other arm. "Now sit down on this step and let me say a thing to you, and then you can go and be sick about it somewhere else."

He sat down on the step.

"Six months ago I came into this house with a thing wrong in my ear and a city that had decided it was a condition, and I asked for one thing, which was that somebody would use what I could feel instead of explaining it away."

Beatrix Halm looked at the Square rather than at him.

"Since then I have asked this house for a price four times. Not for a favour. For a price. A figure, against a security, held by a named holder, on a date, that can be called. I did not want it given to me and I did not want it forgiven and I did not want anybody to be kind about it."

"I know."

"Do you." She turned and looked at him. "Then here it is.

Yesterday morning a man with a pen chained to his wrist wrote a figure in a book at ten to nine, and that figure was the number of people living at my address. It is the only money in this city that has ever been spent on my hearing, and it was spent by you, out of a number a clerk made a mistake about in June, on a form in a building I have never been inside, and I was not asked."

"The guild's hold is still there."

"The guild's hold is still there because of that mark, and the hold is on a wall in a shade this Square has never had, and you told me on the twenty-seventh that I could have it left and not struck, and I chose that, and I chose it in daylight." She shut her eyes for about a second, which she did not often do.

"I have carried a public tail for nine days and I chose it. This morning I have been told what it was bought with, and I did not choose that, and I want you to understand that those are two different things and that I am a woman who keeps the difference between them in a notebook with the dates on."

"I am not going to say I am sorry, because it would be worth nothing to you."

"It would be worth nothing to me and it would be worth something to you." Beatrix Halm stood up. "You have now bought a year of a woman's tail with a figure about your household, and you did it in a chained book in about eleven seconds, and there is no instrument in this city that can take it back off me and there is no instrument in this city that can take it back off you either."

She got on the tram.

"Do not read it to me in a kitchen," she said, from the step. "Not on the first of September, not on any day. Read it to whoever signed it."

Rask's note came down at half past eleven by the internal hand, which it had never done before, because a keeper's internal hand is for figures and a keeper who has signed a page is a keeper whose internal hand is read by other people.

It was four lines on a sheet of the Vant's own paper with no letter on it.

She wrote: *The sitting has a date.*

*It is held on the sixteenth of September. It is held then because the guild's year ends and the book changes hands on the first working day after it, and I have known the month for four years and have never had the day.*

*I gave you four hundred and nineteen days on the twenty-fourth of July. Four hundred and nine as of this morning. You can check it: four hundred and nineteen less ten days is four hundred and nine, and the tenth day after the twenty-fourth of July is the fourth of August, because a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first.*

*Do not write back. There is nothing to write back about.*

He wrote back nothing, which was the first time in four years he had obeyed an instruction from that room without asking what the instruction was for.

At four o'clock he went down two floors with nothing in his hands and sat down opposite her, and she had five books on the table and a cup of something she had not drunk, and she did not turn round.

"Six women," he said.

Solveig Rask turned a chair a quarter round and turned it back.

"Yes."

"You told me on the twenty-fourth of July that after the annual sitting you are the one answerable for six women in a building you have never walked into."

"I did, in those words, and I have not corrected it since and I did not think I was going to have to."

"It is seven."

She put her hand flat on the second book.

"Seven," she said.

"It went into a chained book at ten past nine on the morning of the first, in my hand, on a production line, with the date under it. The figure of persons answerable at the premises on the day."

"I know. I told you on the third that my book does not see the Exchange's book, and I have been sitting with that for a day and a half and I have decided that I am not going to complain about it."

She squared the books. "Write seven down somewhere you are not going to lose it, because I am going to have to carry it for four hundred and nine days and I am not going to carry a six that a man in a cold room told me in July and that has been wrong since the first of August."

"Seven."

"Seven." She turned back to her page. "And when the book changes hands on the seventeenth of September I am the one answerable for seven women in a building I have never walked into, for an account of thirty years that is unsecured except for what you swore on the first of August."

She turned her chair a quarter round and turned it back.

"And there is a page under a column in this room with a figure on it and two letters at the foot of it that are yours. I have not looked at it since the twenty-fourth of July and I am not going to look at it before the sixteenth of September."

"You told me on the thirteenth that when you priced it there would be a date on it and it would be a fact and not an offer."

"And I am telling you today that I have a date for the sitting and the date is not the price. The price will have its own date and it will be a different one, and I do not have it."

"Four hundred and nine days until the sixteenth of September."

"Do not do it that way." Solveig Rask turned a chair a quarter round. "I have carried a figure every working morning since the thirteenth of July because a figure every morning is the only thing in this room that does not move, and if you start counting it down as well then two of us will be doing it and it will be a household."

She turned the chair a quarter round and turned it back.

"Go up," she said. "The account is not on the dead list and there is a guild's hold on a wall that is going to be there until somebody can produce a price, and the floor goes up on the thirtieth of this month and there is no thirty-first, and that is the next date in this city that belongs to you and it is nine days away and it is going to say eight thousand one hundred and forty-three."

Nadia Ferrous's page had gone out on the third and the reconciliation had gone out with it, and neither of them had said one word about the other, and there were four buildings in this city holding two documents about one household and no office in any of them knew they were in the same quarter.

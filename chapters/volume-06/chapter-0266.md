# The Form Behind A Pencil Row

The twenty-sixth was the day the reconciliation charge ran out its own fourteen days, and nothing came, and he noticed that it did not matter and then went and sat on a stair about it.

The charge was correctly raised on the twelfth and correctly unanswered on the twentieth, and the panel had carried it and entered his dispute with the hour on it, and a charge that has been carried by a panel is not a charge any more. It is an item. It goes on a list that a division reads at the next sitting and that a sitting does not let a member answer from the floor.

So the fourteen days ran out, and the thing the fourteen days were protecting, which was an answerable charge, was gone anyway, and there was no hour on the twenty-sixth at which anything arrived, and he sat on the market stair at half past two and let it happen.

The thing he had gone up the sixty-one steps to find out was underneath the charge.

There is one desk in the district that re-issues a schedule and it is not his to go to, so he went to the standing instruction on the landing and read the whole of it for the first time since a clerk wrote it down with the hour under it and two officers waiting behind him in a queue.

**A re-issue of a schedule of premises at a new figure takes the head's sworn answer in his own hand, dated, and the certification of the auditor of record. The two are the instrument. There is no third hand and there is no other way in.**

He had known that since the fifteenth of July. He had also known, since the sixth, that the auditor of record on that schedule was a woman at a desk two miles from this building who has an entry against her saying a member of a division may not report on a return her own name stands on.

Sun Meiling had told him the whole of it at the kitchen table on the fifteenth in about eleven seconds, and Beatrix Halm had said *so it cannot be done*, and Sun had said it could.

He had carried it since the fifteenth as a question about a woman's hand.

It was not about her hand.

The impediment is not a bar to certifying. It is a bar on the certificate, and a bar on a certificate comes off the way every impediment comes off: the division enters one and the division removes one, and the division removes it by removing the person it is on.

He got the district's own sheet out of the back of the drawer under the bread on the evening of the twenty-sixth, with four papers still in it and a fifth that had been lying flat against the wood since the first of July, and he read it at the kitchen table under the lamp with the middle of it bare and a saucer sitting on the middle of nothing.

Sun Meiling came in from the market at half past eight, saw the sheet, and read it standing up over his shoulder in about a minute and a half, and then went round the table and sat down on the far side of it with her hands flat on the wood.

"Read it again," she said, "and this time read the line about the auditor and not the line about you."

"It says a re-issue takes the head's sworn answer and the auditor's certification."

"It says the auditor of record." Sun Meiling took the sheet and turned it round so the head line faced him. "And it says here, in the last line, that the auditor of record is a household office and that the head of a household may change it in writing at any time, and that a change takes effect on the day the division acknowledges it, and that the division acknowledges on the first of the month."

"And that is the whole of it."

"That is the whole of it, and you have stood in this kitchen for eleven days asking the wrong woman for a signature, and the woman is not the difficulty." She was not smiling.

"The difficulty is that she has a bar on her certificate. Change the auditor and there is a certificate with no bar on it, and somebody in this household can sign it, and your sworn answer goes on the front of it in your own hand with a date, and my row goes in ink in September instead of staying pencil in a coat until the cloth wears through."

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"I am not going to." She put her hands flat on the wood again. "You are. You have just worked out that the price of my being a member is a woman losing an office, and you worked it out in about ninety seconds, which means you had most of it before I sat down, and I am not the one who says it out loud in a kitchen where agreeing with a woman is easier than doing it."

The kitchen was bare and it had been bare for twelve days and there were seven of them in it, and he looked round at them and there was not one who would say it for him.

"She has held that office for four years," he said. "It is the only reason a division ever had to answer to anybody in this household. It is why the report she filed on the sixth has a name on the head line and not a number in a column, and it is the only reason she can be asked to certify anything at all."

"Yes."

"And if I change it, the report of the sixth goes on being a report with nothing behind it."

"The report of the sixth is filed. A filed report is filed." Sun Meiling said it flatly. "Her review is not filed and it is not going to be, and a woman with no office and a live review is a woman a division can put anywhere. She will be at a desk in about four weeks instead of a fortnight, and she will be at somebody else's desk, because there is nothing about her that makes her untouchable."

He put his hand flat on the sheet.

"A change takes effect on the day the division acknowledges it, and the division acknowledges on the first of the month."

"So write it tonight."

"Write it tonight and it is acknowledged on the first of August." He looked at the date at the top of his own standing instruction, which was the third of July, with the hour under it. "The first of August is a valuation morning. A change acknowledged at ten to nine on the first is acknowledged in the same hour as a mark, and no division does two things to one account in the same hour."

Sun Meiling looked at him.

"So it is not the first," she said.

"Then it is not the first of September at all." He said it and heard it. "Written on the twenty-sixth it goes on the first of August. Written on the thirtieth it goes on the first of August as well, because the thirtieth is the last working day of the month."

"A month has thirty days and no thirty-first, and a change has to be in before the month is out, and the thirtieth is the day the month goes out." She put her finger on the lamp's edge. "Which is also the morning the quarterly bands go up on the ranks wall, and the morning your plate stops being three under a floor."

"Then it is the thirtieth," he said. "Four days."

"Four days."

Nadia Ferrous had her book open before either of them had finished the sentence, because that is what Nadia does, and she wrote the hour against it and read it back, and then she did the thing she has done twice in eleven years and put her own name on the line.

"Then it wants two hands," Nadia said, "and not one. I have only ever written down what a head has answered. I have never put my own name on a line a division will read."

"You are not putting it on a line," Sun Meiling said. "You are putting it on a witness line, and a witness line is read, and it has never once been read about you."

Nadia wrote her name against the witness line in her small level hand and read it back twice.

He read the change out at the table with all of them on the same side of it, and then he said the thing Sun Meiling had asked him for on the twenty-second of July and had not asked him for again since, which is that he was doing it for her.

"I am signing this because Sun Meiling has been a pencil row in a coat pocket for a hundred and twenty-six days, and she asked me in front of that room, and I said yes, and I am signing it because that is the reason.

"It is not because of the auditor's office. It is not because of the division or the valuation or the standing."

"It is because of the row," Sun Meiling said.

"It is because of the row."

He wrote his name in his own hand at the foot of it and put the date under it, which is the twenty-sixth of July, which is the day a reconciliation charge of the twelfth of July ran out its own term in a building two miles away and was not answered by anybody because it had been answered correctly on the twentieth.

The table had nothing on it except the saucer and the sheet and two cups, and the saucer went back over the pencil row in the middle of it, which was the first time since the fifteenth of July that anybody had touched the saucer at all.

Beatrix Halm came in at ten with her notebook shut under her arm and read the change standing up, twice, and then asked one question and got her answer and went out again without saying one word about it, and Wren Kallo wrote it down in her own book with the date on it, because Wren writes everything down, and Wren looked up after she had done it and said the only thing she said.

"You have just taken the only office in this household that anybody in this city has to answer to."

"I know what I have done."

"You do not. You have had six days to think about it and you spent them." Wren shut the folder.

"There is a woman in two rooms above a laundry who held that office on the ninth of July, when you agreed in the dark not to read a piece of paper to her in them. What you have signed tonight cannot be told to her on her own sill without a man knocking at a door he promised not to knock on, and there is no office in this city that will serve that paper on a woman in her own rooms, and I have looked."

He stood at the kitchen table for a while with his own name at the foot of a form dated the twenty-sixth of July and four days to the thirtieth.

She had told him on the ninth of July that she would sign when signing is what it needed.

It had not been signing. It had been the taking.

# The Round She Cannot Refuse

Petra Ilyin carried the docket round on foot because she had never once in five years had a cart, and she was at the top of the sixty-one steps at about half past six in the morning on the nineteenth of August with her bundle under one arm and a slip of paper in her hand that she had not put in the bundle.

She held it out to him.

He read it. It was a quarter round, lodged on the seventeenth, and it named a clerk and an hour and twenty-one addresses, and the twentieth address on the list was 34-6-118.

It was not a large piece of paper. It was the same size as the parcel slip at the back of the market hatch and it had been folded in four and soft along the folds, which meant it had been on a round before.

She had read the number of the round about a hundred times and never once read what was underneath it.

"That is me," she said.

"You have been carrying for a hundred and forty-nine days."

"A hundred and forty-nine since the fourth of June." She took the slip back and put it in her pocket instead of the bundle, which he noticed and which she knew he noticed.

"And seventy-five days on no roll at all. I have a bundle of somebody else's paper every morning and no piece of paper anywhere with my name in a list on it."

"And you know both those figures better than I do, because a woman in that building counts them out loud."

"You want me to say something about it."

"I want you to read the twentieth line and then I want you to read the figure under it, and then I am going to tell you what happened to me on the seventeenth."

"And you are going to want to sit down."

The figure under it was seven.

"That came off a production line in a chained book at ten past nine on the first of August," Petra Ilyin said. "In your hand. With the date under it. Persons answerable at the premises on the day."

"And on the seventeenth of August a clerk at the Vant looked at that address and needed to know how many persons answerable at those premises there were for the purpose of a round."

"There was one figure on the whole of the record, and it is yours, and it is seven, and he did not ask anybody, because there was nothing to ask."

He put his hand on the rail of the steps.

"So the round that will serve me in this city," he said, "is addressed to a figure you wrote."

"To a figure you swore." She said it without any edge at all. "I have been carrying paper for eleven years and I have never once known what any of it was for."

"And now I know what one piece of it is for, and it is you."

"The round starts on the first of October."

"The round starts on the first of October at eight in the morning and I am on it, and I have been on it since I said yes, and I did not say yes."

She sat down on the top step with the bundle against her knees.

"That is the thing. That is the whole of the thing and I have not been able to say it out loud to anybody in eleven years."

"You take a round or you do not," she said. "There is no third and there is no appeal."

"And there is not one office in this guild that will hear the word no from a sworn enforcer about a round she has been offered."

"I have watched three women find that out in five years and two of them found it out the hard way."

"And I did not say yes because there was no point at which I decided anything. A man I have never spoken to handed me a slip at about eleven in the morning and my hands took it."

"Then I walked out and walked two miles with it and got as far as your gate, and I have been standing here since six."

She turned her head and looked at him, and her face was not proud and it was not sorry.

"There is a promise on me," she said. "It was made in a room I cannot put a date to, by somebody whose face I could not swear to if you put their name in front of me this morning."

"And what it says is that I carry what I am given. It says it in my body and not in my head."

"And I have tested it about four hundred times in eleven years and it has never once let me put a docket down."

"I have been that for eleven years. I am the most reliable and the most powerless person in any room I walk into."

"And there is not one thing in this city that is the difference between the two."

He wrote it down on the back of the painters' card because there was nothing else on him.

"Thank you."

"It is not a favour and do not thank me for it, because it is a bill." She stood up and shifted the bundle.

"I always come early. That is the whole of what I am good at. I have never once been late and I have never once been able to refuse."

"And I am telling you about it four days after it happened, because that is how long it took me to walk it off my legs."

"Now I am going to ask you for the one thing I can actually get," she said, "and I have been thinking about it since the seventeenth, and I have not slept on it."

"Ask."

"Let it be me."

She said it the way a woman says a thing she has rehearsed on a two-mile walk and then found to be smaller in the air.

"You have eight women in this house. One of them is a field operative who could go to a round and come back with it under her arm."

"One of them is a broker who could have it stopped with a line in a book before the month is out, and one of them is a lift operator who writes down everything anybody says in a cage."

"And any one of them could take the twentieth line off that list in about four minutes and would never be asked twice."

"And if a stranger carries it, then a stranger stands on this step on the first of October at eight in the morning with a paper that has this address on it and holds it up."

"And about two hundred people watch, and not one of them knows me or knows you or knows that the figure underneath it came off a production line on the first of August."

"Let me do it and there will be one woman on that step who is standing there because she asked to be."

He said yes in about four seconds, because there was not a version of that question he was going to take longer over.

"That is the worst thing anybody has said to me in eleven years," Petra Ilyin said. "I asked for it and you gave it to me without one question, and I want you to hear the shape of what you have done."

"On the first of October I will stand on the step of this house holding a docket with your address on it and the figure seven under it."

"And I will be a woman on no roll in this city at all, out of a hundred and forty-nine dockets, carrying not one piece of paper with her own name in a list on it."

"And the reason I am on that step will be that your household asked me nicely."

She shifted the bundle.

"Do not come out. That is the other half and it is not a request. Do not come out onto the step on the first of October, and do not come into the yard."

"And do not be in this building between seven and nine in the morning. If you are on that step then it is a head of premises refusing a service and I have to write a second report about a man I live with."

"And I will write it, and I will write it correctly, and that is the end of the only thing I have been able to do for you since July."

"I will not be on the step."

"Say the whole thing."

"I will not be on the step, in the yard, or in this building between seven and nine in the morning on the first of October."

"Good." She got her hand off the rail. "Now I will tell you what I do at a door, because you are about to be on the other side of one in five weeks and I have never once been asked."

"I knock. I say the address and the number of the round and the hour it was lodged. Then I hand it over, and the hand that takes it is a hand."

"And I write the time on the docket, and I do not read it. Eleven years and I have not read one. That is not squeamishness. That is the whole of the promise."

She went down three steps and stopped.

"What the promise does not do is make me knock. I will go to that door at eight and I will knock, and if there is no answer I will knock again."

"And if there is no answer the second time I will put it under the door and write the hour on it, and you will find it about four days later, because that is when anybody in a building like this looks under a door."

"And I am frightened of the opposite," she said. "It took me four days to get that sentence."

"If I could put a docket down, I would not be standing on this step at half past six in the morning handing it to a man instead of putting it in a drawer for eleven years."

"And the promise is the only reason there is a woman standing on your step at all."

She went the rest of the way down without waiting for an answer, and at the bottom she turned round once.

"You will be in this house on the first of September," Petra Ilyin said. "I will not be. I have never once been in a room on the first of a month and I am not going to start."

"And that is not a complaint. It is the other promise, and it is eleven years in."

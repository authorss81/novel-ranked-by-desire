# The Fourth Document

The certification office takes the re-issue at ten in the morning and the queue is outside the door by about a quarter to, and there is no queue on the first of a month for a schedule that seven people are on.

He was the fourth person through the door and there was a clerk at the second desk who had his own copy of the pre-issue and did not have to be told anything.

He signed twice.

The first was as the head of the premises, and it was the same figure he had sworn in a chained book on the first of August at ten past nine with a pen chained to somebody else's wrist.

He wrote it in the same order and it took about four seconds, and he had told six women in a kitchen in June that a figure he put on a form was never his own arithmetic.

The second was as the auditor of record, and it was the first certification of his life.

His name went at the foot of a schedule of the fourth of May for premises 34-6-118 at the figure of seven.

Then the clerk turned the sheet round.

"There is a ground for it," she said. "Box four. Say why you certify the composition at seven and say what is on the seventh line."

"And if there is nothing on the seventh line then say that there is nothing on it, in those words, because a bar has to be described on the face of the thing and not left for somebody to find in a week."

He read box four.

It wanted three lines. He wrote six.

*The seventh returned line of the district premises return for the third of June stands blank. It was returned blank by the district office at ten past seven on the evening of the third of June and it has been counted as a person throughout. The composition of these premises was read at seven on the second of August. The auditor certifies the composition at seven and certifies that the seventh returned line is blank, which is a bar on this certificate, and certifies it in that state.*

He read it twice and put the pen down and did not sign it.

"Do you want a second opinion."

"I want you to understand what is in box four," he said. "I am not certifying a complete schedule. There is a line on it with nothing on it, and I know there is."

"And I have known since the second of August, and I am putting a statement of that in my own hand at the foot of my own name."

"And I would like you to minute that the auditor asked for the description of the bar to go on the face of the certificate rather than into a file somewhere."

The clerk wrote that down and did not argue about it.

"Do you certify it as it stands."

"I certify it as it stands."

"Then I have one more box," she said, "and it is box five, and it is the only box on this form that anybody has ever stopped at."

"And I am going to read it to you, because you have to answer it out loud and not only on the paper."

"Is there anything in your knowledge that would alter the figure of seven persons of reading age keeping an address at these premises on the day of the answer."

The room was about as warm as a corridor two floors under a sales floor.

"No," he said.

"Say it again for the minute, please."

"There is nothing in my knowledge that would alter the figure of seven." He did not look at the door. "Seven persons of reading age keep an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen and there were seven on the day of the answer and I am not able to say anything else."

The clerk wrote it and read it back and he said yes.

Then she read out the list of seven, in the order on page two, at the rate of a woman reading addresses.

She got to the sixth and said Sun Meiling, and did not pause on it, because there was nothing to pause on and there had not been for a hundred and sixty-two days.

She got to the seventh.

The seventh line of the composition was on the sheet about four inches from the bottom edge, and it had a rule under it and a rule above it and nothing whatever in the middle of it.

It was the only empty space on the whole of the document, and every other part of that certificate was full.

He signed underneath it.

"I want it noted," he said, "that the auditor asked for the description of the bar to be on the face of the certificate."

"It is noted," said the clerk. "It is the first time in this office anybody has asked for that."

"There will be four copies."

"There will be four copies by this afternoon."

Sun Meiling was at the front counter at about ten past ten with her hands flat on the counter, which is what she does.

She had been there before him and had watched the whole thing through a gap in the rail.

She had a copy, because copies go to the members, and she had it in both hands, and she read it from the top at about the pace of Runa Sato reading something back.

She got to the fourth box and read his six lines out loud in a front office with a queue behind her, in a level voice, with the hour not written on anything.

Then she read the rest, and then she put the sheet down on the counter and squared it with the edge of her hand.

"Six lines," she said.

"Six."

"You wrote that you know it is blank and you are certifying it anyway."

"Yes."

"In front of a clerk, with a minute, and asked for it to go on the face of the thing."

"Yes."

Sun Meiling looked at him across a counter in a public building and did not lower her voice and did not raise it.

"Two months ago," she said, "I asked a man in a kitchen to fill a line, and he did not, and I asked for it to be written down that I asked."

"And this morning that man wrote six lines in a clerk's office and got a minute on it, and now there is a record, in four divisions, of him swearing in his own hand that the line is empty and that he is going ahead."

"And I got what I asked for on the fifth and it cost him six lines instead of one blank, and I want you to hear me say that I know what that is."

"I am not going to be ungrateful about it, and I am also not going to be grateful about it, and those are both true and I have been holding both since the fifth."

She picked the sheet up.

"It is in ink." She looked at her own name. "It is in ink on the first of September at about ten past ten in the morning and there is a bar on it."

"And the bar is a line with nothing on it four inches from the bottom of the page, and it is going to be there for as long as this certificate exists."

"And nobody will ever be able to find out from this document who that line is for," she said. "Not a division, not in a year, not in eleven years."

"You have just made that permanent, and in four divisions, and with your name under it, and you did it because I told you not to write me and because a woman on a tram told you the same thing on a stairwell a fortnight ago."

"So there are two women in this city who have asked you not to write them down, and there is one document in four buildings that says, in your handwriting, that there is something there you would not write."

"And I do not know which of us was right, and I have stopped wanting to."

She went out into the street.

Runa Sato was in the corridor with her pencil and her notebook and she had not gone in and she had not gone out.

"Say the count," he said.

"The count is four." Runa Sato did not have to think about it, which meant she had done it before.

"Nadia's page went into four divisions on the third and it has a blank twenty-ninth page in it on purpose. The composition and the correction went on the thirtieth of July and the second of August."

"A reconciliation initialled in a counting room with no window went out on the twenty-fourth, and a broker's statement with a number on it went out yesterday and is in three buildings already."

"And a certificate issued at ten past ten this morning, in your own hand, twice, carrying a description of its own defect on the face of it."

"That is four."

"That is four," Runa Sato said, "and three days before that a woman two floors under a sales floor told you the books here come in threes and none of the three can see the others."

"And that every division in this city would be reading one of them in the same week."

She shut the notebook.

"She was counting books," Runa Sato said, "and there is a certificate with your name at the foot of it, and nobody has ever read a word of it before."

"Because a certificate is not a book and it is not in any of the three, and it is about the same household as all three of them."

The corridor was cold.

"You told me on the tenth she had put you out of that room," Runa said, "and that you would not go down those stairs again before the sixteenth of September."

"That is what she said."

"Then there is a figure on a page in a book she will not be holding in fifteen days, and a certificate that has been issued today describing its own defect."

"And a broker's statement that puts a man's seventeen days in a room he was not in."

She tucked the pencil behind her ear.

"And you cannot go down and ask a single question about any of it, and I can hear you doing the arithmetic from here, and it is not the arithmetic any of us has been doing since the seventh of July."

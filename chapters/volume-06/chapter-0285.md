# The Man Who Files In Her Division

The pre-issue for a certification schedule is done by a clerk at a desk and takes about eleven minutes, and on the twenty-second of August the clerk at Cobb Street was eleven minutes late.

He sat down on the bench in the corridor outside with a docket in his hand and waited.

At about ten past three the door of the records room at the end of that corridor was open, which it was not before and was not after, and a voice he knew was in it.

It was an ordinary afternoon and there was nothing at all unusual about it.

He did not stop walking.

He went past the door at the pace he had been walking at, which is the pace of a man who has decided in advance that he is not doing this, and he did not look in, and he put his hands in his pockets.

About forty seconds later the man who files in her division came out of that door with a lever-arch file under one arm and the particular expression of a man who has been told something in a small voice and is going to spend the rest of the afternoon finding out what to do about it.

He went past him too.

She came out last and shut the door behind her and stood against the wall for a moment with the file on her hip, and she saw him at the far end of the corridor, and she came down it.

"That is the sixth," Wren Kallo said.

"That is the sixth."

"I have been in this division nineteen years. That room is nine feet by eleven and it has a window and a chair in it, and I have been in and out of it four hundred times."

"And I have never once said a thing in it that I said anywhere else. And on an ordinary afternoon at ten past three I said one."

"I heard you."

"You did not." She said it kindly and then not kindly. "You went past at the pace you were walking at, and you did it because you have worked out over five days that you cannot be trusted in a corridor."

"And you are right, and it did not help, because it is me who cannot be trusted and not you."

"And that was true on the seventh and it is true today and it is going to be true in about four years."

"You said you could not stop it."

"I did not say that." She shifted the file. "I said I could not stop myself, which is a different thing and a worse one, and I have been sitting with the difference for fifteen days."

"And I have decided not to enjoy it."

The corridor was about forty feet long and had a window at one end and a door at the other.

"What happens now," she said.

"You tell me."

"I am going to tell you exactly one thing, and then I am going to go and do the ordinary work of my division."

"And I am not going to stand here being told things about myself by a man who cannot help it."

"His job is to put things in drawers in the order they arrive." Wren Kallo took the file off her hip and squared it. "That is the whole of his office."

"He has a number and a key and a column in a book, and he does not read what goes in the drawers, and that is not carelessness. That is the design."

"And it has been the design for longer than either of us has been in a room."

"So what I have done today, in about four minutes, in a room with the door open, is put a thing about your household into the ordinary stream of a division that reads everything."

"And it is going in as a date and a number and a drawer, and in about eleven weeks a clerk is going to move a file along a shelf because there is room in the next one up."

She looked at him without moving the file.

"Do you know what that is?"

"No."

"That is the end of unusual." She said the word exactly the way a woman says a word she has picked up off a wall.

"On the seventh of August a clerk on a district counter looked at a composition return with six names on it and a seventh line empty and told you in her own words that there was something unusual about it."

"And that unusual is not a thing that counter corrects, and she said good morning."

"She was right," Wren Kallo said. "She was the last person in this city who was going to be able to say that word about that sheet."

"And I have taken it off the sheet this afternoon at ten past three, and not one office will ever again be able to look at anything about your household and find anything to remark on."

He stood in the corridor and felt the thing arrive, which was not fear and was not anger, and which he had felt once before in this city, on a stage, at the back of a fifth house.

"You did it to protect me."

"I did it because I could not help it," Wren Kallo said. "There is a difference and you have spent five months learning it out of other people and you have had it exactly backwards for once."

"And I am not going to correct it, because correcting it would be a kindness and you do not need another one today."

"That is not fair."

"No." She put the file under her arm. "It is not fair and I have known since the seventh of July that it is not fair, and I have known since the seventh of July that I would do it anyway."

"And I told you that in a cold room and you said *yes* to it in about nine seconds, and I have been living on that yes for a fortnight the way you live on things."

"And I would like it noted that I noticed."

She went.

He got his pre-issue done at twenty past three and the clerk did not keep him and the re-issue is prepared.

At about half past four he went and found Nadia Ferrous, because there is one woman in that household who can hear a thing being decided and he wanted to be in the room when she said it.

He told her on the way. Not because she needed telling, and not because she would have trouble getting it out of him — she would have got it out of him inside a lift in about ninety seconds, which is what the cage is for.

He told her because he had found out on the seventh of August that being the last person to know something in this household was a thing he could not stand for twice.

She was at the market hatch at the Vestry and he came round the back of the booth and she was standing with her back to the boards with the day's paper in her hand.

"Say it quickly," Nadia Ferrous said, "because I have been waiting for you since the nineteenth and I did not want to have to say I knew first."

"You knew on the nineteenth."

"I knew on the nineteenth and I knew on the twentieth and I knew this morning at about eleven, and I am going to tell you how, and then you are going to tell Wren Kallo and she is going to be very pleased with herself."

She put the paper down on the crate.

"A return came through my division on the twentieth with a cross-reference in the corner of it. Two marks, one at the top corner and one at the foot."

"And the foot one is a clerk's number with a date under it, and there is nothing in the world wrong with either of them."

"And in nineteen years of looking at the shape of a record I have never once seen an addition that came in sideways like that."

"Nadia—"

"It was not sideways." She took the paper back up. "That is the part I have been sitting with since the twentieth. It came in the ordinary way."

"It had a date on it and it went into a drawer in the order things arrive, and it is now the fourth page of a file about a return of the fourth quarter."

"And there is nothing to see and nothing to say and nothing at all to remark on."

"Which means she did not hide it."

"No." She held up a finger. "She filed it. Do you understand what she has actually done?"

"A thing that is filed is a thing that is ordinary. A thing that is ordinary cannot be argued with, because you cannot object to a date and a clerk's number and a drawer."

"She has taken the worst thing anybody in this house has ever put in front of a division and she has made it the most boring object in the building."

"And boring is the only hiding place in this city that a division can never punish anyone for using it."

He sat down on the crate because his legs had made the decision.

"There was one word left," he said. "There was one word on that counter a fortnight ago and she has taken it off."

"There is no word left." Nadia Ferrous rolled the paper. "And she did it on an ordinary afternoon, and that is the part I cannot forgive her for."

"And I am not going to tell her that, because if I tell her that she will do something about it, and she is the only person in this city who can make a record look touched."

"And she has just spent that on us for free."

"Four divisions were going to read one piece of this house each and none of them were going to have two."

"Now there are six places, and one of them is a drawer in a division that has never had a drawer like it in it, and in eleven weeks a clerk is going to move a file along a shelf because there is room in the next one up."

She tied the paper.

"Go and be sick about it somewhere," Nadia Ferrous said. "I am not sorry and I am not going to pretend to be."

"And I am the youngest person in that house and I am the only one who has never once been frightened of what any of us has done."

# The Mark In The Broker's Hand

The counting room is two floors under the sales floor. There is no window in it and the floor is stone and has never been warm, and on the twenty-fourth, four days after a Thursday, Solveig Rask was at the same table with the same four books open and her sleeves turned back.

"Nobody comes down here but on a return day," he said.

"Stranger's questions come down here on Thursdays, because the weekly return comes up on a Thursday." She did not look up. "The keeper is here every day. That is the difference between a room and a business, and you have worked that out four days ago and come anyway."

He sat down opposite her and put nothing on the table, which she noticed.

"You have come for the entry," she said. "Which means somebody has shown you that there is one."

"No."

"Then you have come for nothing, and people do not come down two floors for nothing, and I am going to tell you what is on the page and you are going to decide afterwards what it is worth, because that is the only order in which anybody can be told anything in this room."

She turned the second book round without finding it.

"Your plate went onto a socket in a branch office once a week for four years, and behind that plate there is a shape I have been able to see and never able to see the content of.

"It is the thirty-first entry in a column of thirty-one over nine years. Under that column there is one page. It has a figure on it and a date and two letters at the foot, and I have left it where it was on purpose, and I looked at it twice this week and it has not moved, because you have not moved it. That is the last free thing you own."

"And the standing."

"The standing you put in on the seventh of June was called on the twentieth of July at twenty-five minutes past four in the afternoon."

The counting room had a door in it and the door was shut and the stair outside was empty. It was cold enough in there that he could hear himself think about it.

"Who called it."

"I cannot tell you, and I am going to spend this morning telling you why I cannot, because you have been in this city for four years and you keep asking questions whose answer is a person instead of a shape."

She wrote a figure on a slip and did not turn it round.

"The mark that answers a call is not a signature. It is a device cut into the shoulder of the pen-holder, and the pen is chained to a wrist, and a device is a shape. There are four hundred chained pens in this city. Every broker's device is different and every broker's device is a thing that anybody who has ever held that pen can cut again."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer to the only question you can ask me, which is can you prove it." She put the pen down. "I keep the movements. I do not keep hands. A movement is a thing that went somewhere and a dated figure in my book is a fact and not an accusation, and I have told you both of those things on the day you met me and neither of them has changed.

"You came down here on the twenty-fourth of July to find out whether a woman called your standing while you were telling a panel the truth about yourself, and I have told you that she may have, and that on the same afternoon at twenty-five minutes past four the standing went, and that the two facts have nothing to do with each other except the hour."

He sat with that for a while.

"She was at her desk."

"Marguerite Vale signed the attendance book at ten that morning for the whole floor," she said, without any weight at all.

"The attendance book is signed in the morning and it is the floor's own book and she has signed it every morning for four years, and it is worth exactly what a signature is worth. It puts her in the building at ten. It says nothing whatever about half past four, and you know that, because you have spent eleven days putting things on paper that will be read at half past four by people who were not there at ten."

"That is fair."

"It is the first fair thing anybody has said to me about a figure in about a month, and I am going to use it." She squared the four books. "So there is one thing more and it is the only thing in this room that I have not said to anybody, and I am going to say it because you signed your plate into a counted return on the sixteenth and there is nothing left for you to be invisible with.

"You asked me on the thirteenth what I price. I have not answered you, and I am not going to answer you this month."

"You said you would not price it this year."

"I said I do not price a thing I cannot describe, and that when I do there will be a date on it and it will be a fact rather than an offer." She turned her chair a quarter round and turned it back. "On the thirteenth I could not do it in one sentence. I want you to hear how close it is.

"It is thirty-one lines in a column and under the column a page with one figure on it, and the page is initialled and not signed, and the initials are the same two letters that have been at the bottom of that page for nine years, and they are yours.

"That is a thing I could put on a hand to somebody, and it is not a price, and I am not writing a figure on it this month. I am telling you how close it is because a keeper owes the man a thing that costs her to give it, and I have owed you one since the thirteenth."

The room was very cold and very quiet and there was a stone floor under both of them that had never been warm.

"How long is it."

"Four hundred and nineteen days." She said it exactly. "Four hundred and thirty on the thirteenth of this month, and I have counted it every working morning since, the way I count everything, and it does not stop for a month or for a bad night. When the broker holding that book is replaced at the annual sitting, after that date I am the one answerable for six women in a building I have never walked into."

"That is the whole of what you have been telling me since July."

"It is the whole of it, and there is one part of it that is mine and is not the guild's, and I told you that on the thirteenth as well." She did not soften it and she did not look away. "I have wanted one sentence out of that page for four years and I have never asked you for it because I am a keeper and you are an entry and a keeper who asks an entry for a sentence has stopped being a keeper.

"You have spent a plate number on a clinic return and told a panel your own sworn figure is a lie and said seven. There is not one thing left in this city that you are hard to find for."

The temperature in that room had not changed and something in it had.

"Say the ask."

"I cannot tell you whether a woman held that pen at twenty-five minutes past four on the twentieth of July and I cannot tell you with the movements." She put her hand flat on the open page.

"What I can do is reconcile. The real book reconciles on a Thursday. A reconciliation reads the account and the account's broker's own book and finds where they do not agree, and where they do not agree there has been a movement, and movements go on a page with a date on them.

"I will do it because you will ask me to and I have wanted to do it since the twentieth. And I am going to tell you the price of it now, in this room, while you are still able to walk out of it."

"Go on."

"When that reconciliation is done there will be a page with a date on it, and it will not say anybody did anything wrong, because I do not write that. It will say that a call was entered on the twentieth of July and that the entry and the broker's own account do not agree and by how much. That is all it will say. That is the only sentence I am capable of writing and it is enough.

"And it will be in a real book, dated, and it reconciles, and it will be in four divisions by the end of the week, and there is no version of you that takes it out of there afterwards, and the woman it is about will not be able to put her hand on it and have it struck."

"You are telling me it does not name her."

"I am telling you it does not need to." She looked at him. "A broker whose mark and whose account disagree by a figure on a date is a broker who cannot produce her pen. That is a question four divisions will ask out loud in an order in which she cannot answer it, and she will not be able to produce the pen, because a device cut into a pen-holder that anybody can cut again is not a witness."

He sat in the cold and looked at the page on the table where the thirty-first entry had been for nine years with two letters at the foot of it that were his.

"That is a woman's standing," he said.

"Yes."

"You are asking me to spend it, or you are telling me it will be spent."

"I am telling you what happens if you ask me to reconcile. I am not asking you for anything, and I have thought about the difference between those two sentences for an hour and a half." She squared the four books and took her hands off them.

"A keeper who asks is a woman with an opinion. A keeper who waits is a woman with a duty. I would rather be the second one and I have never managed it before and I am not certain I managed it this morning."

He thought about a woman who had let him stand on a false reason for a fortnight because the true one would have cost him a woman he had been protecting, and had told him so on the twenty-second before he could have found it out from a stranger.

Marguerite Vale had not been wrong about the instrument on the seventh of July. She had been wrong about one thing only, and she had done it on purpose, and she had told him about it on the twenty-second before he could have found out from a stranger.

"Do it," he said. "Reconcile it."

He said it once and he did not repeat it, and he did not qualify it, and he went up two floors and out into a clear morning in a city where the sun was out for the first time since the eighteenth.

There is no second time. That is what a thing costs when you buy it with somebody else's standing: it is not that it cannot be given back, it is that there is no second time to give it back in.

He walked past the Exchange and went down to the market stair with a crate and put it square at the foot of it, and Tessa Okonkwo took it from him and did not say thank you, because that is how she says it, and two hundred and three people went through the hatch in the two hours that followed.

Seven days to the first of August. A mark on a wall in a square that would go up on the last working day of the month. A verse with a door number in nine hundred mouths. A form on a desk upstairs that died on the twenty-ninth whatever anybody did with it.

And a page in a book two floors under a sales floor, with a date on it, that was going to have his own hand's mark against it and his own mouth's words about who did it.

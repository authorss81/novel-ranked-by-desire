# The Half He Carried In His Coat

Cordwain's Field counter shuts at half past three and he got there at ten to three with a strip of carbon in the inside pocket of a coat he had worn since the eleventh.

Runa Sato had the hatch up and the crate of returns half out and a queue of four behind a rope, and she saw him come and she put the crate down on the edge of the counter and did not stop working.

"Twenty-five minutes," she said.

"I know the hour."

"You do not know this hour." She went back to the crate. "Every week since the first of this month there has been a man from the assessment side at this counter at about this time, and he stands there until the last ten minutes and he asks me one question, and I say no, and he goes away. That is what this hour is now. Sit at the end and do not talk to me until I have put that crate out."

He sat at the end of the counter on the low bench with his hands on his knees and did not talk to her for twenty-three minutes.

At twenty past three she had the crate out and the hatch down to a slot and the rope empty, and she came round the end of the counter and stood in front of him with her folder shut under her arm.

"Twenty-eight days," she said. "You have had that half since the eleventh."

"Twenty-five."

"You have counted wrong, which tells me you have not looked at it once in a fortnight." She said it without heat.

"The twenty-fifth from the eleventh is fourteen days. I have had the other half since the eleventh and it has been in my bag with a return slip and a stamp pad and I have not looked at it either. That is the whole of what has happened to it in a fortnight, and I am telling you because in about four minutes I am going to make you say a thing out loud and I would like the position to be clean."

"You have got four minutes and there is a conversion window," he said.

"How do you know about the conversion window."

"Because a man from the assessment side has been asking you about it once a week for four weeks."

Runa Sato put the folder down on the counter and took out a slip of stiff paper with a figure on it and a date on it and put it flat between them.

"Hearing paper," she said. "Answered on the twenty-fourth of last month with a minute to spare. It is a hearing because there is a paper.

"It has been a hearing for thirty-one days and it is a hearing until the end of this month, because a paper runs from the month it was answered in to the end of the month after it, and a month ends on the thirtieth. After that it goes stale, and a hearing without a paper is not a hearing. It is a date somebody once mentioned."

"After the thirtieth," he said.

"After the thirtieth."

They stood there with the counter shutting behind her in the sense that the shutters on the field side were already down and the last three clerks were packing.

"Convert it," she said, "or do not."

He took the carbon out of the inside pocket and put it on the counter. It was a strip about the length of his hand and an inch and a half wide, the lower half of a two-part sheet, and the writing on it was in carbon and could not be lifted.

"I have been taking this to the keeper of the Vant's real books," he said. "I was going to take it up to her on a return day and have her price it."

Runa Sato did not touch it.

"Why."

"Because a priced hearing is a secured hearing, and a secured hearing survives anything that happens to the account, and the thing that is coming on the fourth of August takes every security standing against that account off it on the day it goes on. And a hearing without a security is a hearing that a dead list eats."

"You have worked that out on a stair over a fortnight."

"I have worked it out on a stair, in a washroom and in a counting room, and I have had it in my coat the whole time without looking at it." He put his thumb on the carbon.

"If you hand this to me I take it up to the counting room on the next return day and it comes back with a figure and a date on it, and a hearing that is secured. It is the only thing I have been able to buy anybody with this month."

"Then you have been buying badly," Runa Sato said, "and I am going to save you the trouble and I am going to do it myself."

She turned the carbon round, read it in about four seconds, and then read it again.

"Six and seven," she said. "This is the reconciliation of the returned lines of the twelfth of June. Six on the schedule, seven on the index, and the office's own figure against both, which is a printed seven and a struck six. It is a true document. It is true in the way the clinic sheet was true, which is to say it is a page that anybody could have drawn and everybody will believe."

"It cannot be got out of your hearing."

"Nothing in my hearing can be got out of anything." She squared the folder. "That is why I want it not priced."

The shutters came down behind them in sections, and the third section took twenty seconds.

"Listen to me properly," she said, "because in about a minute a woman from the counter is going to tell me the counter is closed and then she is going to offer me the conversion one more time because that is the courtesy, and I am going to say no, and I am telling you now so that you are standing next to me when it happens and not hearing about it tomorrow from Beatrix, who will tell you about it correctly and without any of the reason."

"Go on."

"Beatrix Halm has had a guild's hold over her hearing since the first of July. That hold is a security. A security is the instrument the guild reached for, and you watched on the nineteenth what it does to a woman when the security it hangs on comes off."

Runa Sato's voice did not go anywhere and it did not go up. "I have spent a month in this house learning what that is worth. Nine of us watched a man put six on a page and every one of us knew what it did to the woman whose name it was not on.

"And this counter has been offering me the identical instrument, once a week, for four weeks, pointed at me. I convert, my hearing is secured, and the thing that secures it is a standing against a household that is about to be four grains under a floor and answer to a called security of its own."

"You would be buying your safety with our risk."

"I would be buying it out of it, which is worse. I want that written down in the room you write things in, because I have never been able to say a thing like that and be believed afterwards."

She put both hands flat on the counter. "I would be taking a piece of the risk out of this household and putting it under myself where it cannot be seen, and in four years when it is called the loss comes out of a house with eight women in it and not out of me.

"That is what the guild did. That is what it is for. And I am not doing it."

The woman from the counter came along with the daybook under her arm and said the counter was closed and asked Runa Sato whether there was anything outstanding, and Runa said there was, and said thank you, and said no.

She did not explain. The woman went on up the row.

"Now you," Runa Sato said, and she pushed the carbon across the counter toward him.

"What is this."

"It is yours." She put her hand flat on it. "It has been yours since the eleventh and I have been walking about this city for a fortnight knowing that the only thing standing between my hearing and a date somebody once mentioned is in a coat pocket belonging to a man who did not open it.

"Take it back. No." She took her hand off. "Do not take it back. Put it in front of me and push it across, because a thing that has been in your coat for a fortnight and has been in my bag for a fortnight has been in neither of our hands for a fortnight, and in about an hour the ninth of a month this street becomes a street with no press in it at all, and then it stops being a reconciliation and starts being a leaf."

She did not put her hand on it.

He pushed it across.

She picked it up and squared it into the folder and put the folder under her arm and looked at the counter shutter and then at him.

"That is the whole of what I wanted," she said. "You were going to take the only thing in this house that cannot be forged and put a price on it without asking the woman it belonged to, and you did not, and you came to Cordwain at ten to three on the twenty-fifth of July and asked."

"I came to ask you to give it back to me. I decided on the stair at six."

"Say what the decision was, not what the outcome was."

"I decided not to have it priced," he said, "because you have told me not to, and because I have been asking eight women what they want since the eleventh of July and you are the only one of them who has told me the cost."

Runa Sato looked at the shuttered counter for a second.

"Say the rest of it," she said. "Because that is a lovely sentence and it is not true."

"The rest of it is that the hearing goes stale the moment the month ends," he said, "and I could have secured it, and you have told me not to, and I have agreed, and that is the first time in a month I have agreed to something a woman in this house asked me for and done the thing I wanted anyway, and I am telling you that the reason I agreed is not the reason you gave me."

"Then give me the reason you had."

"You have been in this house since before I could read," he said. "And I have been looked through by every office in this city for four years and I have never once been able to put a price on what it costs me, and you have been on no side of that and you did not put yourself on one, and I have never had that from anybody."

The Field counter shut at half past three and both halves of that reconciliation were in the same folder in the same bag, and they had never been together in a fortnight and they were never going to be together at all.

He walked back up the sixty-one steps with his hands in his pockets and nothing in either of them.

In four days a form on a desk dies of its own week whatever anybody does with it. On the night of the thirtieth a month ends and a hearing with it. Tomorrow a charge at Cobb Street runs out its own term. And on the third of August a reconciliation will put a broker's mark and a broker's account on a dated page in a real book four divisions hold by the end of the week.

Runa Sato's hearing paper is good for five more days and she has turned it down herself, out loud, in front of a man who came to buy it.

# The First Room That Agreed With Her

The answer went into the Bellhouse's card on the twenty-second at about eleven in the morning, without a date, and the lamp went on it, and by six in the evening there was a queue round the corner.

He was at Ferrier Street at six with the door open on the passage and seven of the household round the kitchen, because Marguerite Vale had not been in that room since the fourteenth and the eighth of them were in the building and one of those eight was at her counter with a stamp and a crate.

The word itself had not been said out loud in that kitchen. It was on the landing, in Lode's mouth at eleven that morning, in the mouth of a man at the door that afternoon, and in the mouths of six blocks of flats since the eighteenth, and not one person at that table had gone out and looked at it.

Agnete Lindqvist put her coat on over her cardigan at the end of the table without announcing it, which is the only way anybody in that kitchen ever does anything.

"You are not," Wren Kallo said.

"I am going to hear it."

"It is a room that agrees with whatever she sings. That is the whole of what the woman is." Wren put her pencil down. "You will go into a room of nine hundred people and it will agree with her about something in four seconds and you will have no opinion afterwards. You have kept yourself out of rooms for eleven years and I have never once called that cowardice, and I am not starting now, and I am telling you what the room does."

"I know what it does."

"Then you know that going is a decision and staying is one too, and the staying has cost you eleven years and the going costs you an afternoon, and I would like to know which of the two you think is cheaper." Wren picked her pencil up again. "That is all I have. I am not going to argue with a chaplain about a door."

Agnete went down the sixty-one steps for the first time since she came into that building. She went at the speed of a woman going to work, and she did not stop at the bottom to count them, and she did not stand there the way the others do.

He went with her as far as the foot of them and then stopped and let her go, and he went back up and washed his hands and sat on the landing with his back against the cold paint for a while, because there was nothing else in that building that evening that he was any use in.

Two of the women in that building are religious about numbers. One of them was on her way down the stair to walk into the one instrument in this city that makes them meaningless.

He was on the landing with that when Petra Ilyin came up the last four and stood at the top with her hands behind her back, which is where she has stood every time she has had something to say in eleven years.

"The sixty-one," she said.

"Yes."

"She went down the sixty-one and she did not do the thing at the bottom."

"No."

Petra came down two steps, which she has done perhaps nine times in four years, and looked at the rectangle on the landing wall with the lamp on it.

"Eleven years she has been in that room," she said, "and eleven years I have been on this stair, and we have both of us been not going out. And you know what the difference is between us and I want you to hear it from me and not work it out on your own, because it matters and I am saying it once."

"Go on."

"There is no difference," Petra Ilyin said. "That is the difference. I have been marking and she has been hiding and both of us have been in a room with the door open, and both of us have been in it for the same reason, which is that the room is a job."

She went back up before he could answer her, which is what she always does, and she was at the top of the stairs before she had finished deciding that she had said enough.

Agnete Lindqvist was in the Bellhouse by a quarter past six, in a cardigan with a coat over it, and she did not go in by the front. She went round to the side door because it was nearer, and she stood in the queue for fifty minutes in a doorway with the stage door's latch in her hand.

Then she went in and up to the gallery and sat in the front with her hands in her lap, four feet from the rail, in the seventh row.

He was at the door of the gallery at ten to eight and she turned round and saw him and did not react at all, which told him everything she had come here to learn about herself in the first hour.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat down.

"You said you would not come."

"I said I would not come to the second house." He had said the first house. "I have been on a landing for two hours with a woman who tells me what the difference between two women is."

"Petra." She almost smiled. "Petra has been the only person in that building who has ever asked me a question about it. In eleven years, and not one of them has ever put the question to the woman about the thing she is doing. They ask her about the thing she is not doing."

The lamps went down and there were nine hundred people under the rail and he could not see the bottom of the house from the gallery.

Isolde Marchetti came out and the house came with her before she had sung anything at all, and Agnete Lindqvist, who had spent eleven years learning to hold a number in a room without being moved by it, put both her hands flat on the rail and gripped it.

The verse from the eighteenth went first, because the house expected it, and the nine hundred went round with it out of habit.

Then the song.

It was not a song about him. He had got as far as deciding that he did not care either way, and then the second verse went into the tune of the first one, the way a thing is designed to go into a tune that is already in a room, and it was about a street.

A street with a stair in it, and a block on the top of the stair, and a number on the door of the block, and the number was not said out loud.

But the chorus of the first verse put six and seven in the air every time it went round, and the street was named in the second verse by its own shape and its own two names for itself, one the carriers used and one the post used, and there is one block in Ostara that has been called both of those things since before either of them was written down.

Nine hundred people in a house worked out the address in about eleven seconds.

The song did not say his name. It said the number of the door. It cost a woman four hundred pence and an afternoon of work and it put 34-6-118 into the mouths of nine hundred people who had never seen it and never would.

**The household was public. It was public as a song, in four seconds, for nothing.**

And then it was over, and the house did what a house does, and the agreement went out of nine hundred people the way it goes out of a lamp.

Agnete Lindqvist stood up in the seventh row of the gallery and leaned over the rail and said one sentence into a room that was only a room again, and nine hundred people did not turn round, and four of them heard it, and it went nowhere.

"It has gone," he said.

"It has gone," she agreed. "And I want you to understand what you are looking at, because you are a man who thinks things are made of paper and you have just watched nine hundred people be told where you live and forget it in one interval."

"Then what am I looking at."

"That I have been right for eleven years, and that right is not worth what is in that room," she said.

"I have spent eleven years keeping a number in a room. Not a count. A number I could have said out loud and did not. Tonight a woman stood in front of that room and made it agree with her, and the whole of what I have spent eleven years doing came out of my hands like water out of a cracked jug. I have no instrument. I am going to have to think about this for a long time."

She went down the stairs and out into Bellhouse Row and walked about two hundred yards in the wrong direction and stood in the middle of the street looking at a lamp.

"Do you know what I would have said if it had not stopped," she said.

"No."

"I would have said it in the third verse, which is where the door is, because I have been out of doors four times in eleven years and I know exactly how long I have for a sentence." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "A chaplain is not a person who believes in the number. A chaplain is a person who has worked out what it costs the room to say the number out loud."

"The house stops you saying it."

"The house stops you saying it. That is what it is for. It is a room that will not let a sentence finish.

"Eleven years I have been the only sentence in a room and not one person in that building knew it. Tonight nine hundred people agreed with a woman about a street, and I stood up and got four words into the air and they went into a fourth person's ear and stopped there. I have never in my life been that quiet in a room and I did not choose it and I could not have chosen it differently."

She walked back to Ferrier Street with him and up the sixty-one steps, and she stood on the landing with her hand flat on the cold rectangle and she said goodnight to him, and then she went down four steps to her own floor and did not go behind her own door.

She slept on the landing.

Wren Kallo was awake. She came out of the back room in a shirt with her folder shut under her arm and looked at the shape of Agnete Lindqvist on the floor by the stair lamp, and she went back in and got a blanket and put it over her and came back in and did not say a word.

At two in the morning he came out of the front room and Wren was still there with the folder open on her knees.

"Wren."

"I am writing it down," she said. "Not to her. There is nothing to write it on. She is on no list of any kind at any time, and I have known that for four years and it has never cost me anything to know it.

"You went out tonight. You have never gone out in four years, and you went down sixty-one steps and you sat in a house and you came back up and you did not go behind your door, and I have just put the twenty-third of July against it with the time on it, in my own book, in my own hand.

"That is the first time anyone has written a date against anything that has happened to Agnete Lindqvist in eleven years," Wren Kallo said.

"There is no form for it and there is no return it goes on. I have written a date against a thing that happened to a woman who is on nothing at all, and if anybody asks me where I got it I can produce my own book and my own hand and eleven years of pages where her name is not on any of them."

"That is not protection, is it."

"No." She shut the folder. "That is a door. A protection is a thing a person stands behind. That is a thing I have done to her, and I did it because I am the only person in this building who could, and I did not ask her, and I would do it again, and if she asks me tomorrow to burn it out of the book I am going to argue and I am probably going to lose."

She went back to her room.

He stood in the dark on the landing with a woman asleep against his warm wall and a date in a clerk's hand on a page where nothing else was, and eleven years of quiet, and one verse, and one song with a door number in it, and nine days to the first of August.

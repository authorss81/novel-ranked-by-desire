# The Chair Where She Is The Subject

The twentieth was a Thursday. The pass went along the ranks wall before the figures went up, and by half past six that afternoon the wall had been done as far as the middle in a shade that was not the one under it, and there were figures on the lower half of it he had not been in the city to see.

He left Ferrier Street at one with the plate in his coat and the certificate in the drawer and the receipt on top of it, and he walked past the Exchange without going in and left the stair that went down to the counting room alone, and took the tram to Cobb Street.

Audit intake had the charge on its list at four in the afternoon and the head of the schedule sat in a row of chairs with a form number against his name and a plate on the table in front of him that he did not have to press anywhere.

The charge was raised on the twelfth, in respect of the premises return of the twelfth of June, and the division had found that the return and the certified schedule of premises 34-6-118 did not agree.

"What figure does the schedule carry," the officer asked.

"Six."

"And the premises return of the twelfth of June, counted in the audit index on the same day."

"Seven."

"Then the reconciliation is a simple arithmetic," the officer said, "and a simple arithmetic does not need a panel."

"It needs a panel," said the officer next to her, who had not spoken until then, "because the seven on the index is a count of returned lines and not a return, and the six on the schedule is a head's sworn answer, and one of those two is a person and the other one is a document."

They asked him whether the sworn answer of the fourteenth of June still stood.

He had known they would ask. He had known it since the fifteenth, sitting on a step at the bottom of a market stair with a strip of carbon in his pocket, and he had thought about it in every washroom in the city for six days.

"It stood on the fourteenth of June," he said, "and I am disputing it now."

The room did not move.

"Say that again for the record," the officer said, "and say it slowly, because a head who disputes his own sworn answer is not a thing this division has had in nine years."

"I dispute the six on the schedule of the fourteenth of June," he said. "I wrote it. It was in my hand. I set it down at a table in a house in Ferrier Street with six women watching and I have not denied it once since. I dispute it today."

"Then state the figure you say is correct."

"Seven," he said. "The seventh returned line was left blank on the third of June at ten past seven in the evening by a district officer and six was written in the foot of the column, and four divisions later the office read seven lines and counted seven, and the seventh is a person and her name is on this schedule and her name is not on my roll."

"You will swear seven."

"I will swear what is true today and today it is seven," he said. "I am not going to swear it in a room where the only person who can certify the schedule is the person the schedule is about."

The officer wrote it. The second officer read it back twice, which is the thing that happens when two of them have written the same thing down.

"The reconciliation is then correctly raised," the first officer said, "and correctly unanswered, and I will carry it, and you may sit down."

He sat down. The word seven was in a room with a form number on it and four officers in it and it was going to a division in a building that already held a true report in a woman's own hand about the same figure.

At ten past four they called the auditor of record.

She came in with her bag over her shoulder and no coat on and she put the bag on the floor under the chair and she did not look along the row of chairs where he was sitting, and she did not look at him at all for the whole of the afternoon.

They gave her the file.

It was his file. It was the whole of it, from the composition return of the twenty-second of March to the schedule of the twenty-sixth of June and the certificate dated the twenty-seventh and the receipt signed on the twenty-eighth, and it was in date order and it was the file she had read out loud on her own floor in April before she had ever been asked to look at it.

She read it the way a person reads a thing she expects to be quoted back at her. She went slowly, and she read the fourth page first because the fourth page is where a head's sworn answer goes, and then she went back to the start and did it again in order.

She did not hurry and she was not hurried, because she was the only person in the building qualified to hurry herself.

He sat in the fourth chair with a form number against his name and listened to a woman who had walked out of a flat she was still in on the sixth of July describe his household to four officers who had never met one of them, and every figure she gave was the correct one, and he had asked her for none of it and could not have stopped any of it.

She gave it to the room with no emphasis at all, which was worse than any emphasis.

At about two hours in she stopped, and turned back about forty pages, and asked for a document from another division of the same building.

"The counter-forensics finding of the twenty-third of June," she said. "Four edits to a file, every one of them a removal, filed by a member of this division's own service. It has been on a desk for twenty-seven days."

"Yes," said the officer at the table. "It is in another division."

"Then it has not been before this panel, and this panel is sitting on a reconciliation of the return that finding was made about." Ines did not raise her voice and did not ask for anything. "I am not asking you to act on it. I am asking that its existence be noted in the record of this sitting, with the date it went in, because in twenty-seven days not one person has, and I am the auditor of record on the schedule it concerns."

It was noted. She asked them to read it back and they read it back and it went in with the date on it.

That was all. It did not answer the finding and it did not act on it and it put a date against it in a record that four divisions could read, which was not the same thing and was a great deal more than nothing had been an hour before.

Then they asked her about the charge.

She read the charge twice before she answered it and he understood what that was, because he had watched her do it to a document in April.

"I reported a discrepancy," Ines said. "The discrepancy has not been corrected. The charge was correctly raised and it is correctly unanswered."

"You are the auditor of record on the schedule."

"I am the auditor of record on the schedule and the head of the household is disputing his own sworn figure, and the only instrument that could correct the schedule is a re-issue with the head's sworn answer and the auditor's certification on it, and I am the auditor, and I may not certify a return my own name stands on, and that entry went up with my report on the sixth of this month."

She said all of that in about eleven seconds without stopping, and the second officer wrote it, and the first officer read it back.

They directed that the schedule should not be read further until the head's sworn answer of the fourteenth of June was either withdrawn by the head or certified by the auditor of record, and they carried the charge, and they entered his dispute with the hour on it.

It was half past six when they let him out.

Her report had sat fourteen days in intake, since the sixth of this month, and a counter-forensics finding had sat twenty-seven days on a desk, since the twenty-third of June and a reconciliation charge had been raised against his head on the twelfth and answered correctly at half past six on the twentieth by a woman reading a file she had been inside of since the sixth of April.

And the instrument that could settle all of it had just been named in a room with a form number on it as her hand.

He came out into the Square and the Thursday crew had gone home and two ladders were standing against the ranks wall with nothing on them above the middle.

Under the wall, in the open, with no instrument and a coat over one arm in July, a woman was singing.

He stopped on the pavement because everyone else had. The man loading the barrow had put it down. Two women arguing at the foot of the steps had stopped and were facing each other. A queue of eleven at the tram stop stood without moving while a voice went along under the wall, and every person in it nodded at nothing in particular.

A man came out of a doorway with his mouth open to shout something, turned round, and went back inside instead.

Beatrix Halm was standing at the edge of it with her notebook shut under her arm and her good ear turned toward it.

"That is Isolde Marchetti," she said. "She sings at the Bellhouse and she sings out here on Thursdays because the wall takes it."

"She is very good."

"That is not what I am telling you." Beatrix had not moved toward the Square and neither had he. "Everything she sings, the room agrees with. It agrees before it has thought about it. Two of those people have known each other for twenty years and they have just agreed with each other about something they have never discussed, and neither of them will remember it by the end of the week."

"So do not stand under it."

"If you do not want a street to agree with something in about four seconds, do not stand under it," Beatrix said. "I have been in traffic for four years and I know what four thousand people agreeing with one voice sounds like, and I am telling you that as a person who can still hear it, and I am telling you it on a night when I have just put a page about your price in a book and I would like to be believed about something."

He stepped back out of the Square and stood with his back to the wall and let it go on behind him without him.

It was not merit. It was not a ledger and it was not a cold minute and it was not anything he could hand to anybody, and it went through a street the way a voice goes through a street, and it did not cost him a thing, and it was the first thing in five years he had wanted that he could not have.

Beatrix came out from under the wall with her notebook shut and gave him his sleeve to hold while she read the road.

"The first of August is in twelve days," she said. "Petra will be at the bottom of the steps at ten to nine and the mark will go in whatever you have done in this month, and there is a head's sworn answer now in dispute and a sheet with a pencil row under a saucer and a plate number in a return at a market hatch, and you have not slept. Say the thing out loud on this corner and then go and sleep it off."

"Seven," he said. "It is seven and I said it in a room and it cannot be unsaid."

"Then it is seven," Beatrix said, "and the rest of it is the first of August, and it is twelve days, and you have got a woman to answer for a schedule and she has not said whether she will."

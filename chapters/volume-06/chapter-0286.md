# The Reason She Does Not Ask

He did not go down. He stood at the top of the stairs at about eleven on the twenty-fourth of August with his hand on the rail and no reason to be there.

A boy from the Vant's floor came up through the street four minutes later with a folded sheet and a slip, which is how the house circulates on a Thursday, and which is the reason half the men in this city know a thing a week before the woman who wrote it does.

He took it on the top step.

It was headed the way a reconciliation is headed, and it was for the week ending that morning, and it carried the mark of the counting room at the foot of it in two letters and no name.

He had never seen that mark on a page before and he understood it at once.

There was one figure on it and the figure was his own.

He read the paragraph above the figure four times and then he read the paragraph below it once.

The paragraph above said that an account of the broker's, standing open and unsecured in the books of that house since it was opened, carried nothing against it except a standing lodged against it on the seventh of June at the Exchange under the second form, secured at the figure of the head.

And that the books of the house carried that standing whole at the close of the thirtieth of July.

The paragraph below said that the figure set against that account, in the absence of any other, is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight, and that this figure is not a price.

And that no price has been entered against that account in those books and none is capable of being entered there.

There was a line under it in a smaller hand that said the figure would stand from the thirtieth of August until it was called, and that no instrument in the city could move it before it was called.

And that it could not be paid before it was called and could not be answered before it was called.

Then, at the foot of the page, in the two letters and no name.

Not a signature.

He sat on the top step of a staircase that came out of a basement with his back against a wall for about four minutes.

It took him a while to see that he had just read the whole of the fourth page of a woman's working life in about ninety seconds, and that no part of it was about a woman.

Runa Sato found him at the market hatch at half past two.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," said Runa Sato. "I have had it off the market copy and I have done it twice on the back of a docket."

"And I am going to do it a third time out loud, because you are about to do it in your head and get it wrong."

"Go on."

"Rank four is three thousand grains a week and you have been rank four since you were twenty-one."

She had the docket turned round and the arithmetic on the back of it in her own small level hand.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight divided by three thousand is two and a half weeks. Two weeks and five days of a rank-four man's plate, in a rank-four man's ordinary earnings, at the ordinary rate."

"And I have written it down and I am going to say the rest of it, and you are not going to like it."

"You cannot earn it. Not in any quarter and not in any year."

"You score at all because you are an office attendant and the Bureau is obliged to be able to say it scores you, and that is a split of an aggregate that has never been published."

"And the moment there is a witness in the room you stop scoring altogether, because you have not done one attested deed that anybody could sign for in four years."

"You have counted that before."

"I have counted that before and I have counted it since the seventh of June and I have never once put it on a table."

"And I am putting it on a table now because there is a figure in a real book with a date on it, and it is the first time in my life that the arithmetic has been somebody else's job."

She put the pencil flat on the crate.

"So there are three answers to a figure of eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight and I have written all three out, and you may have them in order."

"You cannot pay it. You cannot earn it. And you cannot answer it, because a figure standing against an account cannot be answered until it is called."

"And a called figure is answered with a production, and a production is the figure of persons answerable at the premises on the day."

"So it stands. That is the third answer and it is the only one that is true, and it is the reason the woman who wrote it went at it the way she did."

"You have been down there."

"I have not been down there since the third of August and neither have you, and I did not need to be, because she initialled it and she did not sign it."

"And there is not one other person in this city who can tell you what the difference is except a woman who does counter work and has been on both sides of it."

He looked at the sheet again.

"She initialled it."

"She initialled it," said Runa Sato, "and on the third of August she signed one with her whole name at the foot of it."

"And I have been trying for three weeks to work out why a woman signs one and initials the other, and I worked it out on the twenty-second, at about eleven at night, at a counter."

"And I have been waiting for you since."

"Tell me."

"An initialled reconciliation is a keeper doing arithmetic." Runa Sato turned the docket over and wrote the two words on the back of it, large, the way she does when she is making sure he has them.

"A signed one is a woman putting evidence in front of a division."

"She signed the third because that page was about a pen and a hand and it needed a person behind it," Runa Sato said, "and she knew what it would cost her, and she did it anyway, for a man who said *do it* once."

"The twenty-fourth is not about a pen and it is not about a hand. It is about a figure and two books."

"And she initialled it because the day she signs a page about an account, that page is evidence about the account's holder, and she would rather publish a figure that convicts nobody at all than publish one that convicts you."

The market went on around them.

"That is the reason," Runa Sato said, "and it took her nine days in a room with no window, and you had four minutes of it on the tenth and she did not give it to you then."

"And I want to say one more thing about it and then I will stop."

"If she had asked you what the account of thirty years was worth, she would have had your figure and not hers," she said.

"And a figure a man gave a division about his own account is not arithmetic. It is a man putting a number in a book about himself."

"And there is not one office in this city that reads one of those as a fact. It reads it as a charge, and it strikes it inside a week."

"And then the figure is gone and everything that was true about your account goes with it."

"So she did not ask you." Runa Sato pulled the pencil out again. "She has not asked you four times and she is not going to ask you a fifth time."

"And the day she looks at that page underneath the column is the day the figure stops being hers and becomes something a man said."

He stood there with the sheet in his hand for a long moment.

"She has not opened it."

"She gave you a date in the middle of August at which she will not have opened it, and she initialled this one on purpose, and both of those are the same decision."

"And she made it in about nine days and told you none of it." Runa Sato put the pencil behind her ear. "Put it somewhere you will not lose it, because I am not keeping it for her."

"She has carried it since the thirteenth of July and she does it every working morning and she will not let anybody help."

She turned to go and then stopped.

"There is a line on it I have not read out, because it is yours and not the arithmetic."

He turned the sheet over. There was a line at the foot, above the two letters, in the same hand.

*The figure above will be five under the band floor on the thirtieth of this month.*

That was all it said.

"A woman at the top of a staircase in a cold room," he said, "who has not opened a page for four days, worked out on a Thursday morning that a security five short of the floor is not a security."

"And she put it on a page that goes to four divisions in the same week as everything else in this household, and she did not put one word under it about what that means."

"She would not," Runa Sato said. "It is not arithmetic. It is a woman telling you something she is not allowed to say in a book, in the only direction she is allowed to say it in."

"And you have been living with arithmetic for five months and today is the first time it has said anything to you."

He read the line again.

Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. From the thirtieth it is five under a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-three, on a wall, in a shade the square has never had, in a word that has not changed since the end of July.

That is what a security that does not cover costs.

It does not fall. It simply stops being a security and becomes a price, and a price is a thing somebody has to find, and there are exactly two dates on which anybody in this city finds one.

The turn of the quarter is the first of October.

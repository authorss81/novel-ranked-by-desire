# The Pen On A Chain

She let him in past the outer door in four minutes instead of nine, and the hatch conversation was about nothing at all, and he noticed both of those things and said nothing about either.

Marguerite Vale was standing in the middle of the finest room he will ever be permitted into with her hat still on.

"Sit down," she said. "You have got until about half past four and I am going to use all of it, and tomorrow at noon I am in a room that is not this one and I do not yet know what it is."

"Tomorrow."

"The annual sitting. It is on the sixteenth, and after it the room changes hands, and I have not been in this office as its keeper for nine years without learning how a day like that works." She took her hat off and did not put it down. "That is the answer to the question you came in with, and you did not ask it, and I have got eleven years of a habit and you get one day of it."

"I came about the mark."

"Yes." She did not sit down either. "The mark is at ten to nine on the first. It is entered by the broker holding the book, and the broker holding the book on the first of October is me, because the keys change on the seventeenth and the seventeenth is after."

"Then I have come to ask you to enter it."

"You have."

"Against premises thirty-four six one one eighteen. In the ordinary way. And the answer to it will be sworn by somebody answerable at that address, and I will not be there."

"And you want my hand on the mark."

"I want a mark against the account and I want it to be a mark and not a charge and not a conversation." He put his hands flat on his knees. "A charge is struck inside a week. A mark is a movement and it reconciles and it stands. And the only office that can put one there on that morning is the one with the pen on the chain."

Marguerite Vale looked at him for a length of time that was not four seconds.

"No."

"You said no before I had finished."

"I said no because I have had it since about eleven o'clock this morning, and I have spent five hours making sure it was the right no." She took her hat off again, which she did not need to do. "Listen to the reason, because the reason is not a preference and you will not enjoy it."

"Tell me."

"The mark against an account is a movement. A movement is a fact about money that went somewhere." Marguerite Vale stood with her back to the window. "I have been the broker of that account for nine years and I have entered every kind of mark in the Vant's book eleven hundred times, and not one of them has ever been against a house I sleep in."

She said it without any weight, the way she says most things.

"And on the first of October I would enter a mark against thirty-four six one one eighteen in a real book, and it would go into four divisions inside a week because everything I touch goes into four divisions inside a week."

She came away from the window.

"And a broker who has entered one mark in nine years is a broker with a mark. Not a woman with a mark. A broker."

"In every house in this city there is somebody who has done it once, and it is the thing they know about me, and it is the thing they will say when they want me to do something."

"That is not an answer, that is a reputation."

"It is the same thing in a book." She turned her chair a quarter round and left it. "And you have known that since March, because I told you in March what I would do if I ever had to and you wrote it down in that head of yours and never once mentioned it."

He had mentioned it once, in a kitchen, in June, and she had taken it as a favour.

"Fine," he said.

"It is not fine. Listen to the rest." She sat down at last, on the arm of a chair, which is what she did in July. "If I do not enter it, somebody enters it. And the only person in this city who can enter it on the first of October is the broker holding the book, and that is me, and I am not going to, and it will follow through to the man who takes the keys on the seventeenth."

"So it does not get entered at all."

"It does not get entered at all, and that is not the same as it not happening." Marguerite Vale let the room be quiet for about four seconds. "A figure standing against an account that nothing else secures is not asked to pay. It is asked to be answered, and it stands until it is answered, and a figure that is not answered at the turn is carried to the next turn. That is not an invention. That is what a standing does."

He thought about the four days in front of him and about the thirty-first of August and about a sentence a broker had said in this room to his face, that there would be somebody standing on a step at his address who would not answer a question about an account.

He had spent four seconds of a careful woman's attention every quarter for four years keeping it off, and the turn had arrived and she had used it, and the thing she had been keeping off was now sitting against an account that nobody had answered.

"Marguerite."

"You are going to ask me for something else now." She had both hands on her knees. "You always ask me for something else, and this time I am going to give it to you, and I am going to make you pay for it, and I have thought about it since eleven o'clock and I am not ashamed of any part of it."

"Go on."

"I am going to give you an undertaking. On paper. Signed."

She took a sheet of the house's paper out of the side of the table where she keeps the things she is not going to show anybody, and put it in front of him, and turned it round so the top of it was at his side of the desk.

"Write what I dictate."

He wrote it in his own hand and turned it round.

*The undersigned, Lin Hao, of premises 34-6-118, will not ask Marguerite Vale, of this house, to enter the mark against that account on the first of October, or on any day following it.*

"Stop there and read it back to me."

He read it back to her.

"Now I am going to sign it and you are going to countersign it and it is going to be dated, and it is going into four divisions by the end of the week, because it is dated today and it is in your handwriting and it is about an account of thirty years that is not yours."

He countersigned it. His hand was steady and he did not have time to be glad about that.

"There," said Marguerite Vale. "Now you have asked a woman in your own house to do the one thing she could have done for you on the first of October, and she has refused, and both of those are on a page with your name at the top of it."

"That is a cruel piece of paper."

"It is an expensive one and you have paid for it." She folded the sheet and did not put it away. "You wanted a mark that stood and reconciled. What you have is a paper that says a man asked a woman for something she would not do, in her own rooms, in a fortnight before a turn, and there is not one office in this city that reads that as anything except the truth."

She stood up and put her hat on.

"And the truth is the only thing either of us has ever been able to use."

He stood in the middle of that room with his hat in his hands and did not put it down.

"You said once that this year is the first year you have done it for a man you like."

"I did not say that."

"You did not say it and you have been saying it for about four minutes."

Marguerite Vale looked at the door instead of at him.

"I have been standing on other people's accounts for four years, one quarter at a time, four seconds each, and I have never once enjoyed it and I have never once minded." She got her hand on the door. "This year I have minded every single one. And you are not to get anything out of that, and you are not to thank me for it, and if you ever put it in a document I will have you off this roll inside a week."

"I will not thank you."

"You will thank me by the seventeenth," she said, "and then the keys change, and whoever is standing in this room with the keys will be a person I have never met and have never taken a cup of tea with, and they will hold this book on the morning of the first of October, and they will have had my account in front of them for two days and not one hour of me."

She opened the door about four inches.

"Six weeks is what I gave you on the twentieth of July," she said. "It runs out on the first of October and there is nothing after it and we both know it, and you came here at two o'clock on the fifteenth to ask me for a pen."

"And you gave me a paper instead."

"I gave you the paper because the paper is the only thing you can carry out of this room and I cannot."

She had her hand on the door and she did not open it.

"Go home, Lin Hao. You have a book closing on the thirtieth and a woman on a step and a guild clerk who has found your household in a certificate, and you have come to the wrong office two days before the sitting to ask a woman to break nine years of her own life for you."

He went out past the hatch and got as far as the street.

Twenty days to the turn, sixteen to the sitting, and a sheet of the house's paper in his coat with his own handwriting at the top of it and a woman's signature at the foot of it, asking her to refuse him and dated, so that the refusal would be true forever.

# The Room He Is Not In

He was not in the building.

He had tried it once, at about half past three in the afternoon, and had got as far as the second landing, and a man in a grey coat had come out of a room at the top of that flight and said that the room was not on any list.

He had also said that anybody standing on that landing would be noted, and had not asked him to move and had not let him go up.

So he waited at the bottom of the street in a doorway that was not a doorway for about two hours and forty minutes.

Then, when it was over, he did not find out what had been said, and he had known he would not, and had gone anyway.

## Eleven

The room was on the first floor of a building on the north side and it had one window and a table down the middle of it and four officers.

Two of them were from the divisions that had already seen her page and one of them was from the Exchange, and none of them had met her, which she established in about ninety seconds and which she found useful.

She had asked for it herself. That was the whole of her preparation and it took her nine days and she did not use one hour of it on the wording.

"I am going to state a fact about my own office before anything else is put to me," Marguerite Vale said, standing at the table rather than sitting at it.

"I convened this. I asked for the date, I asked for the hour, and I asked that the minute go into the divisions in the ordinary way rather than being held."

"Because a thing said in a room nobody else ever hears is a thing said at a counter, and I have never once said this in a room."

"Four officers. It is recorded that I asked for four."

The senior officer wrote that down and then said that she was entitled to produce a document and to have it read.

"I am not producing a document." She put her hands flat on the table. "I am making a statement, and I am making it under my own signature."

"And I am going to say the first thing about a pen before you ask me for the pen, because if you ask me for the pen first then everything I say afterwards is an answer to the question you asked and not a thing I came to say."

"On the afternoon of the twentieth of July, at twenty-five minutes past four, a call was entered in a chained book on this floor, and a discharge was entered in the same minute in my own account, and the two do not agree."

"I have carried the reason since the twenty-second of July and I have not said it. I am saying it now."

She did not sit down.

"I did not cut the device on the pen I was holding."

Nobody in the room moved for about four seconds.

"There are four pen-holders in my office," Marguerite Vale said. "There have been four for four years. I cut them all, in the first month, myself."

"And I can put my hand on the day I cut each of them and I did. One of them is not the pen I held at twenty-five minutes past four on the twentieth of July."

"And I have known that since the twenty-second of July, and I have had nine days to work out why a broker with four devices of her own cannot produce the device she used."

"And the answer is that she cannot. That is not a mystery. That is a fact about my office."

"A device cut into the shoulder of a pen-holder can be cut again by anybody who has ever held it. I said that on the twentieth of July, in a room, to a person."

"And I have said it four or five times since, and I have never once said who."

The senior officer put his pen down.

"Mrs Vale, are you making an accusation."

"I am recording that I cannot produce an instrument," Marguerite Vale said. "That is a fact about a woman and not about a hand."

"And if you write the word *accusation* into that minute I will ask you to read it back and then I will ask you to strike it, and then we will have an hour where nothing at all happens."

"I am not accusing anybody. I have no idea who it was."

"I have been the senior broker of that house for nine years and I have never once been in a room with a device that was not mine, and on the twentieth of July I was."

"And the reason I did not notice at the time is that I did not look, because I have never once in nine years had to look."

She took her hands off the table.

"There is a second thing and it is the reason I asked for four officers instead of one, and I am putting it on the record before you ask me for it."

"Because you will ask me for it and I would rather it were mine."

"There is a number and I am going to give it to you before you ask me for it."

The senior officer asked her to repeat it.

"Eleven," Marguerite Vale said. "I have counted them four times this week."

"Eleven of them are in my own working book over four years, and every one was made with a pen-holder I did not cut."

"And I cannot tell you what one of them was, because I keep movements and not hands, and a movement in my own book is a line and a time and a figure, and that is the same whoever held the pen."

"And that is the whole of what I have. I have said that the device was not mine and I have said that there are eleven and I cannot say what they were."

"And I am not able to say one word more than that, and if anybody in this room writes a sentence that goes further than that sentence then that sentence is not mine and I will have it struck."

She looked at the four of them.

"I would like it minuted that I offered. Not that I was asked. That I came on my own motion, at my own hour, with a number on this table."

"And that no one in this room put the number here."

She wrote four lines on a sheet of the house's paper and signed at the foot of it with her own name, which took about eleven seconds and which she had done perhaps four hundred times in her life.

None of them had ever been read out in a room.

"One more thing, and then I am finished, because it is the only part of this that is about anybody who is not me."

The officer looked up.

"There is a man in this city who has put five documents about his own household into four divisions in seventeen days and has not moved a grain in five years," Marguerite Vale said.

"A reconciliation initialled on Thursday carries a figure against an unsecured account of thirty years, and the figure is the plate of the man it is about."

"And from the thirtieth of this month it is five under a band floor."

"I am not here about that figure and I am not here to speak to that account. I am here because a device came off my bench in July."

"And the reason it matters that I say so is not the figure and it is not the account."

"It matters that in about four weeks a woman who reads a book like a hand, and a woman who reads a hand like a book, are going to be asked to say which one of them is right."

"And one of them is going to be asked to say it about a man who is not in this room and who did not put me in this room."

"And I would like the minute of this afternoon to be the first document in that business and not the fourth."

She picked up her hat.

"You will want my pen." Marguerite Vale took one out of her coat and put it on the table, and then took it off again.

"No. You will not want my pen, because you cannot have it and I would like that in the minute too."

"There is nothing in this city that can lift a pen out of my office on a date, and that is the first thing I ever tried to teach anybody in March."

"And not one of them has learned that in five months."

She left the room and did not look back and did not speak to anybody on the stairs.

It was forty-one minutes.

## The Landing At The Bottom Of The Street

Petra Ilyin was standing in the doorway across the street with a bundle under her arm and she had been there most of the afternoon.

She had come down that street at four o'clock with twenty-one addresses on a slip and the twentieth one is four buildings up.

"Forty-one minutes," she said. "Seven people went in. Four officers and two clerks and her."

"I counted from the doorway because I was counting the doorway, and I know what the room is because I have been delivering to that floor since March."

"She went in with her hands empty. She came out with her coat on and her hat in her hand and she did not stop."

"And she did not speak to anybody on that landing, including me, and I would have held her parcel if she had given it to me."

"What was on the table."

"I could not see the table." Petra Ilyin shifted the bundle. "I have not been in that room. I have never been in that room."

"And I am telling you that I stood at the bottom of that street for two hours and forty minutes because I was not going to leave it, and that I will do it again on the next one."

"And that neither of us is ever going to know what was said, and that is the price of the room and you did not have to do anything to earn it."

"You simply were not in it."

The building's outer door opened and a clerk came down and put a sheet of the house's paper into a satchel and shut the door again.

"That will be copied tonight," Petra Ilyin said, "and it will go out in the ordinary way in the morning."

"And by the end of the week there will be four copies of it in four buildings with a broker's name at the foot of it and a number on the table that neither of us has heard."

She went up the street with the bundle.

He stood in the doorway until the light went off the front of the building.

There were two days left of anything in this city being his own handwriting and then none at all.

A woman had gone up those stairs with her hands empty and come down with her hat in them and had not spoken to anybody on the landing, including the woman with the bundle.

That woman had stood at the bottom of that street for two hours and forty minutes on his account and would have held her parcel.

Whatever was on that table, he was going to be holding it at six the next morning, along with the card, in the ordinary way, like everybody else in the city.

He had not earned one word of it and there was not one person he could ask.

# One Of The Nine

He stood up after the washing on the eighteenth of July and asked the room to stop, and the room stopped.

"There is a ledger," he said. "I have kept one since the first month I had this address. A hundred and eighteen lines in it. They are faces and streets and hours. They are not deeds, because it has never once told me what any of them did or whether any of them was worth it."

The room said nothing back. Eight women in it, four of them on the cut roll and four of them on nothing of any kind, and a kitchen with nothing in the middle of the table.

"Nine of those lines are turned. Turned means the person has felt something cold from my hand once or twice or four times and has started keeping track of when. That is all it means and it is the whole of what I have ever told anybody about it."

"Who," Beatrix said.

"That is what I am telling you."

"That is not what I asked and you have heard the difference."

"Nine," he said. "Out of a hundred and eighteen, across five years, in a city of a million people. Nine is not a pattern." It was not a sentence he believed and it came out level.

He put his hand flat on the bare table.

"The ninth is me."

Agnete Lindqvist put her cup down on the sill behind him and did not pick it up.

"You have given a true sentence into a room that could not have compelled one," she said. "Not one person in this kitchen can make you say anything, and I have stood in a doorway for eleven years and watched people pay money to sit in rooms where they can be made to say things. You have done the whole of my discipline in my kitchen in eleven seconds and you have not once stood in my room."

"Your discipline is that a room where you stand cannot compel testimony."

"My discipline is that it cannot. Mine cannot because of what I am. Yours cannot because you did not want to." She picked the cup up. "Both of those are the same thing and I want you to hear it, because you have spent five years believing the difference is what you have."

Runa Sato did not stand up and did not raise her voice.

"You have been sitting at this table for a week calling debts," she said. "Runa's debt. Sun's debt. Tessa's nine years. Yesterday you signed your plate number against a counter in a market stair. The first debt was the one you have been carrying since April and you have spent a week going round the outside of it."

"I did not want to open with it."

"You did not want to open with it because it is the only one that is not about somebody else's hour." Runa put her pencil on the table and left it. "Twenty-seven days I carried six into a fourth office. On the eleventh you told me it was yours. Tonight you tell six of us at once. I want to know which of those was for me and which of those was for the room."

"That was for the room," he said. "And that is the answer and I hate it."

Petra Ilyin had her hands behind her back and had had them behind her back for the whole of it.

"Eleven years I marked what other people produced and did not ask who was producing it," she said. "I want one question answered and then I will go to bed. Forty-four days I have been on no roll. You have a hundred and eighteen lines in a book and nine people on them have been marked by you. How many of the nine know they are on it?"

"None of them."

"Then it is a book that cannot be produced and in which not one of them has been told they sit," Petra said, "and I have been the other half of that description for eleven years, and it is not a comfort, and it is the closest thing to my own work I have found in this house."

Wren Kallo had not moved from the step by the door and her folder was not in her hands.

"Give me the rule," she said. "Not the feeling. The rule by which a person is turned, and what happens when there are nine."

"Someone feels cold in one hand, or in the chest, or in an old injury. One to four seconds. Most of them think they were cold."

"And the ones who think again."

"They keep track. They notice the hand, or the street, or the hour. They begin being able to find him without knowing they are doing it." He looked at her. "Nine is not a pattern. A good analyst takes nine turned lines and triangulates them to a source, and when they have done it the source has an address, and they do not need a warrant for the address because they already have the hand."

Wren's mouth went flat.

"I build the tools that read that," she said. "That is what I did before this house and it is what I still do, and I wrote the finding on the twenty-third of June about the four edits in your file, and that finding has sat on a desk for twenty-five days and it has not been acted on."

"What are you telling me."

"I am telling you that I have never once had nine." Wren came into the kitchen and put her hands flat on the table on either side of his. "In four years of doing this, the largest single set in this city is six, and six is a rumour a division files and forgets.

"Nine is not a rumour. Nine is a route. A person with nine of them can sit on a tram, read four streets out of a folder, and be on this floor inside a week. Every instrument that does it is in this city. Every one of them is on a list that the books of a woman called Solveig Rask reconcile against."

Marguerite Vale uncapped her pen, which she had not done all week, and looked at it, and capped it again.

"No," she said.

"Marguerite—"

"You have told six women you are a person who is fed by strangers and who has not spent one grain of it in five years." She had her gloves in her hand and did not put them down. "I am a broker. I price things. This month I have priced a household, a security, a room, a thirty-year account and a woman's eleven burned months.

"I am not going to price that, and I want the room to hear the reason, because the reason is not squeamishness. A thing with no deed in it has no yield, and a security with no yield is not a security. It is a rumour with a man's handwriting on it."

"So it is unpriceable," Sun said.

"It is a rumour," Marguerite said, "and a rumour is what a roll with a pencil row on it is. I have spent nineteen years finding the one asset in this city that a court cannot take off a man, and it turns out the only thing no court can touch is the thing no one will put on a page."

Nadia Ferrous wrote the whole of it down.

She wrote it with the hour against it, in the same hand and at the same speed as everything else in that book, and when she had finished she read it back once and then wrote her own name against the line, which she had begun doing on the seventh of July and had done four times since.

"I am a witness," she said. "I was not asked to be a keeper. That is not the same and I want it written that I know it."

"It is written," Beatrix said.

"It is written in Nadia's book," Beatrix said. "Beatrice Halm's notebook has bread in it and a hatch carried and a wall looked at. That is the ordinary record, and it is the only record in this house that anyone from outside can produce, because two of the women who keep it ride in traffic and one of them works a counter.

"Not one of us is in an office. That is not an accident. That is thirty years of my life."

She closed the book on her thumb.

"What he said tonight is not ordinary. I am not writing it in there. I am going to hand it to Nadia's book and I am going to let it sit where it sits."

Petra went to bed at that point and said goodnight to the room in general on the way out.

He stood in the kitchen with the lamp off the edge of the table and seven women looking at him who had not looked at him that way in five years, and the thing he had been carrying since April was in the room, and the room had done what every room does to a thing, which was to keep it.

It could not be given back. He had said it out loud to a room in which every woman keeps a record of something, one of whom now signs against every line she enters and one of whom has ridden forty thousand people a morning for four years for exactly this. The single thing he had managed in five years that no form could reach was now in three written places in one kitchen and would be in a fourth by morning.

"Twenty-five days," Wren said at the door.

"Twenty-five."

"Twenty-five days the finding has sat and today six people learned something about you that is worth more to a division than the finding." She had her folder under her arm and she did not put it down. "I do not know what you are and I have not known since April, and I have stopped needing to know, and that is the first time in four years I have stopped needing to know and I would like the room to understand how strange that is."

She went out. The step outside took her weight and did not make a sound.

He went up at eleven and lay down in the dark with the head line in his coat pocket and listened to a building with eight women in it breathe around an empty room that still had a name on the corner of a shut notebook on a shelf.

Somewhere under him a woman who was on no list at all put her hand flat on the back of a chair and left it there, and a woman in her own rooms across the city was sitting on the fourth step of a stair with the candle out, and a hundred and eighteen lines sat in a book that had never been in a room and had been in one since half past eight.

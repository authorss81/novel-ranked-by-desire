# The Shortest Thing On A Wall

Beatrix Halm came off the second shift at seven in the morning on the thirteenth of August and found him on the bottom of the sixty-one steps with his back to the yard, listening to a tram that was not his.

"Sit down," she said. "I have been awake since four doing the other kind of arithmetic and I would like to do it out loud to somebody who has not already done it."

He sat down on the second step from the bottom, which is where she puts people when she wants them lower than her.

"Thirty-three days," he said.

"That is the card and you have been counting it since the sixth, and it is not the number I have been awake for."

She turned her good ear toward the street, the way she does about four times a minute, and it has been the most unnerving and the most useful habit in this house since March.

"Say the three dates out loud. The only three that matter about my hearing."

"The guild posted its hold on the twenty-seventh of July. Your hearing was answered on the twenty-fourth of June and it has been running ever since."

"And the standing the hold is bought with was lodged on the seventh of June, at the figure of the head, and it was called on the twentieth of July, and you and I both know what answered it."

"Seven." Beatrix Halm sat down on the step above him. "In a chained book, in your hand, in about eleven seconds, on the first of August."

"Now do the part that has not been done," she said. "Do it out loud and count it as you go, because I have done it four times since the tenth."

"Each time it comes out the same and each time I want it to come out different."

The yard tram came in and went out and she turned her head with it.

"A production is the answer to a call. That is what a production is. It is not an answer to a quarter and it is not an answer to an account and it is not a thing you can hold up next year."

"So the hold on my hearing is bought with a figure of seven that has already been spent being an answer to the twentieth of July."

"From the eighth of August that figure has been doing nothing at all. It has not been standing behind anything."

"It has been a number in a book with a date on it, and the date is the first, and the first was eight days ago."

She looked at him without shifting the notebook.

"And from the thirtieth of this month the floor is eight thousand one hundred and forty-three," she said, "and your plate is eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight."

"And that is the only figure left standing anywhere near me, because the seven is spent and the plate is five short of the floor."

"So here is what a security that does not cover costs, and I have been waiting three days for a man to work it out so that I could say it instead of him."

"It costs nothing on the first of August. It costs nothing on the ninth."

"On the thirtieth it stops being a hold and starts being a price, and a price is a thing that has to be found by somebody."

"And there is exactly one kind of woman in this city who gets found. It is the kind that is already on a wall in a shade this square has never had, with a notebook, and a good ear, and eleven burned months behind her."

He had not said anything.

"That is the first cost," Beatrix Halm said, "and I have known it since the tenth."

"And I have not said it out loud because saying it out loud to you makes it a thing two people know."

"The second cost is worse and it is this. You are going to find out about it in about nine days and you are going to do something."

"You always do something. You did it in February for me and you did it again on the first of August, and both times you brought me a figure, and both times the figure came out of your own house."

"So I am going to say the condition now and I am only going to say it once, and it is not a favour and I do not want it kept."

"Go on."

"Bring me a price that has somebody else's name on it," Beatrix Halm said, "or bring me nothing. That is the whole of it."

"Not a price out of a lift operator's arithmetic. Not a figure of persons answerable at a premises."

"Not a number that gets into a book because a man in a cold room was frightened about something else."

"If you cannot get me a price with a person on it, then you do not get me a price. And I will carry it uncovered, and I will carry it in daylight, and I will write it in the book with the date on it."

He sat with it for a while, and what he felt was not the fear she had put on purpose, which was the point of it.

"Agreed," he said.

"You have agreed in about four seconds and you have not asked me a single question about it, and that is how I know you meant it, and it is also how I know you have not worked out how to do it."

"I have not."

"That is fine." She did not get up. "I did not ask you to work it out. I asked you not to bring me one, and I have got what I asked for."

"I am going to be ungrateful about it for as long as this takes, and I am telling you now so that neither of us has to do it later."

She put her hands on her knees and turned her head about thirty degrees, into the yard, and her good ear went flat against the noise of the street.

"Can I tell you what I actually want," she said, "because you have offered me four things in six weeks and none of them has been it."

"I have been too proud to write it down and I am not proud this morning."

"Tell me."

"Not a price." Beatrix Halm kept her ear turned away from him. "I asked this house for a price four times and I meant it every time and I have never once been given one, and I have stopped wanting it."

"A price is a number set against a security, with a holder named and a day named, and every time somebody has offered me one it has cost somebody else something I did not know about until afterwards."

She turned her head back.

"What I want is a date. Not a day it is called. A day it is held."

"I want a line in that notebook with a day on it and a number of days under it in your handwriting, the way Runa does it, so that when it comes I know how many I have got and I am not told afterwards."

"Nine years of that notebook and every line in it is a thing that happened, and not one of them is a day I can arrive at."

"I have a hundred and forty-three dockets of Petra's in my head every morning and not one date. I have eleven burned months in this book and I have known the count of them since March, and there is not a single day on any of them."

"You want a date. I do not have one."

"There is one and it belongs to somebody else and she lost hers at midnight on the thirtieth of July."

Beatrix Halm said it flatly, without any performance.

"Runa Sato had a hearing and it went stale on the last night of July, and it is now a date that three people once mentioned, one of them at a counter in front of a witness."

"And it is the only date in this house that has gone missing instead of arriving."

"I would trade her mine for it this morning, in a kitchen, in front of everybody," she said, "and she would refuse it, and she would refuse it for the reason she refuses everything."

"Which is that it is the identical instrument the guild reached for over me in February with her name on it."

"And I would have asked, and I am not asking, and you may tell her I said so and she may write it down, because that is what she is for and she does not charge."

He was quiet for a moment.

"You are the only person in this house who has ever asked me for something I could give," he said.

"I know." She got up. "That is why it is a pity."

"A clerk from the guild came to the gate at four o'clock yesterday morning and asked me what my hearing would take," she said. "Not what it would cost. What it would take."

"And I said not your figure, and he wrote my not your figure down, and then he said that was a fair answer and that he would put it to his house in the ordinary way."

"And the ordinary way is the quarter."

"The ordinary way is the quarter." Beatrix Halm got her bag up onto her shoulder. "He said it the way you would say it to anybody."

"A house takes that sort of thing up at the turn, and the turn is the first, and the first is the first of September."

"And he said it to me at four in the morning on the twelfth of August without knowing one single thing about this household."

She looked at him from the top of the step.

"So that is the day it is all on," she said. "A card on the ranks wall at six, a certificate with a bar on it, and a guild house taking up what my hearing would take."

"And all three of them about the same door. And I am the only one of the three who will have nothing in her hand."

She went up the hill.

He sat on the second step from the bottom for about twenty minutes with the yard at his back and did the sum, because he could not help it and because Runa Sato was not there to do it for him.

Eighteen days. Everything in this household that can be priced is priced inside eighteen days, and three of the four prices are already written.

The fourth one is a woman standing on a step being asked what she would take, by a man who had never heard of her.

He had agreed in four seconds not to bring her a price.

He had not worked out, sitting there on the second step, what he was going to bring instead, and he understood that he had given the word before he had the thing.

That was the first time in five months he had done that, and a woman with a notebook was going to find out whether he had meant it.

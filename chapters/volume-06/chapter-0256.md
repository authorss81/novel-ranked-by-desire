# Nine Years In A Line

The hatch ran from a quarter of eight and by eight the crate had been carried down four times and back up four times and two hundred and three people had gone through it in under six hours.

Tessa Okonkwo worked the counter with her sleeves above the elbow and the wood worn smooth in a curve where nine years of forearms had rested, and the counting sheet was folded in three in the inside pocket of her coat because she had come up the market stair at six that morning and taken it off his bare table, where it had lain all night and neither of them had touched it.

He said the debt to her while she was wiping down between two lines.

"I owe you nine years of a quarter of eight that were in a line in your own hand and in no return in this city."

The next person came and she served them and turned round and put her hands flat on the worn part of the wood.

"Say the whole of it and then I will answer it," she said, "because you have said it nicely and I want it plain."

"Nine years of a quarter of eight. Nine years in which nothing that happened at this counter was recorded anywhere except by you, and now the clinic is counted from the first of July and the return asks for the name of the person answerable for the hours, and the line is blank, and the printed foot of the sheet I signed in May says the head of a household answers in his own name for every member of it."

"Which makes you," Tessa said.

"Which makes me. Plate 8,138 in a line against your hours."

"Yes." She did not hesitate and she did not lower her voice and she was not careful about it. "Put it there."

He had come down the stair with the answer worked out and it had been the other one.

"You are not even going to think about it."

"I thought about it on the ninth of this month and I wrote it on the back of the count," Tessa said, and took the folded sheet out of her coat and put it on the counter face up. "I have been waiting a week to be asked.

"If a return counts my hours and the name against them is blank, then a clerk can count me and answer for me and never once learn that I am a person. That has been true of this hatch since the first of April and I decided on the ninth that it was not going to be true any more."

She turned the sheet round so the blank line faced him.

"I am not frightened of it. It is the first thing ever put in front of me that I am actually able to hold. Nine years I have kept a hatch that no one in this city could point at, and that was a defence and a good one, and I was hiding behind it." She put her thumb on the blank. "Answerable is the difference between a person and a hatch. Put your number on it."

"Once it is on a return it is on the district's copy and the office's copy and the branch book's, and every plate number in this city is on a machine somewhere with an hour against it, and a plate that appears on two lines is a plate that has been looked at."

"You have been looked at by no one for five years."

"That is the whole of what I am."

"Yes," Tessa said. "That is what I am buying with my line. Do it before the next man comes and do not make me ask you twice."

He took the pen off its string and put his plate number in the blank. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. He put the date under it in the form the return wanted, the sixteenth of July, and signed his own name under that because the return wanted a hand and not a number.

Tessa read it back aloud the way a person reads a thing that is going to be quoted in a room, and then she took the sheet and squared it to the edge of the counter and put it under the till, which was where it lived now.

"That is done," she said. "That is the first thing in nine years that is done."

The woman who came at half past nine had been coming since the first of April and had a card and a bad morning and no idea whose hands had carried the crate down four times that week. She took her turn, and she said good morning to the counter and not to either of them, which was how it had been every morning for nine years.

He watched her go up the market stair with her card in her fist and worked out what he had actually done at that counter, which was that a number with a washroom behind it was now attached to the place where she went, and that in about a week a clerk in a district office would be able to put that number and that counter in a line together, and that the line would be true.

He had spent five years being the person a form goes past. He had just bought a hatch with the price of being findable, and the woman who had sold it to him had watched him pay and had not once told him the price.

He picked up the empty crate and carried it down to the stair foot because she had asked him on the ninth of this month to help her with weight and not with paper, and she came down after him and stood at the foot of it with her arms crossed and watched him set it square.

"You are going to regret this within a month," she said.

"I know within a week."

"That is not the same and I wanted you to have the better of it." She unfolded her arms. "A plate on a clinic line is findable. You have spent five years being a man a form goes past. From the eighteenth of this month there is a form in this city with your number in it that a person can go and look at, and there is nothing I can do about that and I would do it again before the next man comes."

At the foot of the market stair Lode was waiting with a bucket, which she never was at that hour, and she said it before he had got the crate down.

"Your plate's on a return."

"It is."

"Then they can find you now." Lode took the crate off him and set it down herself. "I have been on this landing six years and the whole of what I know about you is that there is a man on the fourth floor whose number never comes up when a form goes past. That is a strange thing to be and it has been the only thing about this building that made any sense to me, and now it is finished."

She picked the bucket up again.

"I am not asking why. I am telling you that everything else in this building is going to hear about it inside a week and that the women upstairs are the last people in Ostara who will be told anything by a man on this landing, and you have just made yourself findable for a hatch." She went up. "I hope it was worth a counter."

He went up the sixty-one steps and stopped twice.

At the table Sun's pencil row was still under a saucer and the bare wood was bare. Beatrix had the notebook open and had written a line and had shut it again before he came in.

"I wrote it down," Beatrix said, without opening the book. "A plate number on a counted return. That is the entry and that is all of it, and I am not going to read it back to the room because you have not asked me to and I do not enter a thing twice.

"Do not come in here and be grateful," she said. "You have bought something today and it cost a thing that was not yours to spend, and the woman it was spent for knew that when she said yes, and you are going to sit down and eat and not say the word thank you, because you have noticed that it goes into every book in this house and it goes into hers."

Wren was not there. Petra was, with nothing in her hands, forty-two days on no roll.

"You went down to the hatch at a quarter of eight and came back with a look on you," Petra said.

"I put my plate number against nine years of her hours."

Petra stood absolutely still with her hands behind her back, the way she stood when a thing she had no standing in was being decided in a room she was in.

"That is the first thing anybody in this house has done since May that I have not been able to put in a column," she said. "Marking is a column. Answering is a column. What you have done is not a column, it is a signature, and I have watched you put three of them into books in a month and I do not know what a man is supposed to be afterwards."

He got out Tessa's day book and put it on the table open to the month.

She had his initials on the first line of the month with a date against them and no number, and the date was the ninth.

"August," he said. "What happens in August."

"A fresh first line on the first, and you stand in it."

"Not with no number."

Tessa was not there. He said it to the table and the table heard it.

"August will get a first line with a number on it," Sun said, "or it does not get written at all, and I would like it on the record that I said that before you did."

He stood on the landing at eleven with his hand flat on the pale rectangle, which was nine inches by fourteen and cold in the middle, and the nail above it held nothing, and the paint had not been laid over since the twenty-ninth of the month before last and no crew would ever paint a stair.

Below him a woman was closing up a hatch that had run nine years from a quarter of eight and had been in no return at all until this morning and was now in three, with a washroom attendant's plate number at the foot of it in his own handwriting.

He had gone into this building five years ago with a plate and no history and nothing anybody could ask him about, and every month since he had put one more thing against his own name on purpose, and on the sixteenth of July he put the first one that could not be got off, and he had done it for a counter worn smooth by nine years of forearms, and he was not sorry, and that was new.

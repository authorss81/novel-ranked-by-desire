# The Room At The Top Of The Stair

The room at the top of the stair at Ferrier Street is eight feet by nine and it has a window that gives on the yard and a door that does not lock, and it has been hers by a usage that no form in this city records, and on the eighth of August she came down the outside stair at about nine at night with her coat on and told him to come up.

He had known for four days that she was going to do something about the page, and the something had been getting larger in his head every day, and when she came down the stair at nine it was not that.

"Shut the door," Wren Kallo said. "Not for the house's sake. Because there is a thing to say and it is not going to be said into a stairwell."

The room was cold and it had a chair in it and a deal table and forty-one books on the floor in four stacks, and she put her coat over the back of the chair and did not take her file off her arm, and he stood in the middle of eight feet by nine and did not know what to do with his hands.

"I have been in your division's records since April," she said, "and before April I had been in a different division's records for nine years, and I want to say a thing about what I saw, and then there is something I am going to ask you for and the answer is going to be no, and I would like the no to arrive before the question so that neither of us has to be brave."

"Wren—"

"You have been reading me for two months as a woman with a finding on a desk and a tool I built and a division I work in, and you have been right about that about nine times out of ten and it has been the most useful thing in this house. I have never once read you."

"There has not been a moment in two months in which I looked at you and worked out what you were, because you are the most careful man I have ever stood next to and I have been letting you be careful."

"Say the thing."

"I read you yesterday morning." She did not sit down. "You went into a counter at ten past nine and you came out of it at eleven twenty and you stood in the street with a file of nothing, and then you walked across the cobbles and told me what you wanted, and then you sat in a kitchen until midnight and told six women that both roads out of the seventh line are her name."

"Yes."

"You knew it in a corridor at Cordwain." Wren Kallo said it without any weight at all. "You worked it out in about nine seconds in front of a woman who counts out loud. And then you went into a queue for an hour and ten minutes to find out whether there was a third road, and you told me last night that there were two and both were the same, and you said it as though that were the finding."

She did not take it back.

"It was not the finding. You went to that counter so that you would not have to be on your own with it for an hour and ten minutes. That is what you did. You are a man who cannot be alone with a thing, so you stood in a line."

"I could have gone to her."

"You could have gone to her within nine seconds of getting out of that queue and you did not, and you are not going to, and you knew that you were not going to about a minute and ten minutes after you came out of it."

"That is what I saw. You are not a man who is protecting a woman from a record. You are a man who has found out that the only thing you can give her is a piece of paper, and that you promised her on the ninth of July that you would never give her one, and you have spent five days proving to yourself at a public counter that there is no other door, and you were not looking for a door."

"You were looking for somebody else to have opened it first."

He did not say anything.

"That is what my gift is for," Wren Kallo said. "I have spent nineteen years looking at the shape of an edit and knowing what was taken out of a record, and there has not been one moment in two months in which anybody looked at me, and there has not been one moment in two months in which anybody let me look at them, and that is the arrangement everybody makes with the woman who can read them."

She put her hands flat on the file.

"I have made it too and I am good at it, and I am telling you tonight that I have read you and that you are not what I thought."

"What did you think."

"I thought you were hoarding." She almost said something else and stopped and started again. "You are not hoarding. You have spent everything you had on four documents in thirteen days and you gave away a woman's standing in four divisions in a cold room and you put a figure about your own household into a chained book, and none of that is a man keeping things."

"That is a man emptying a drawer into the street because somebody he loves is going to be hurt in a month and he cannot stop it with his hands."

"There are two of them," he said.

"Two." Wren Kallo agreed. "One is behind a counter at the Vestry and one is on a tram, and both of them have told you no this month and you have put both of their noes in your own pocket and gone on doing it, and that is not honour and it is not a plan."

"That is a man who decided in advance that he is the instrument and told nobody, and everybody in that building has been living in a house run by a man who thinks of himself as the bill."

She stopped, because she had said more than she had come to say, and she stood in the cold with the file under her arm and did not take it back.

"You came down the stair to ask me for the seventh line," he said.

"I came down the stair to tell you what I want and then to say no to it, in that order, because you have had a week of people saying no to things they could have had and you have not once been on the other side of it and I wanted to be on the other side of it with you for once."

"Say the want again."

"I said it at the bottom of those steps yesterday and I am not going to say it twice, and you do not get it twice because you are not going to be able to do it anyway." Wren Kallo put the file down on the table and did not pick it up. "I want the household. Not the roll. The roll is a piece of paper and I have been on four pieces of paper in nineteen years and every one of them was my own division reading me."

"I want to be inside the thing. I want it to be mine to leave, and worth nothing to me if I leave, and there is not one instrument in this city that does that, and the seventh line of a composition return is the only line left in it, and that is not a want. That is an inventory."

"There is one reason you cannot have it and it is not the line."

"Then say the reason, because I have had it in my hand since the third and I have been carrying it round this building for four days like a woman carrying a parcel that belongs to somebody else."

"Because you cannot keep a secret," he said. "You have told five people about a finding in April and a sixth is about to happen."

"If you are inside this household then for the rest of your life every thing this household does is a thing you carry out of a door and cannot hold, and on the third a page of mine went into four divisions because you were in a room with a man and you cannot be in a room with a man and not tell him."

"So you would be the standing reason that nothing in that building ever stays private."

"Yes." Wren Kallo did not soften it. "That is the no. That is the whole of the no and it is mine and I have been carrying it for four days, and I would like it noted that I said the want first and I said it in daylight on a step with two hundred people going past, and then I said the no in a cold room with one door, in that order, and that neither of them was for them."

She picked the file up.

"And now sit down," she said, "because you have been standing in that room for eleven minutes like a man at a counter and I have been here since nine and I have not had anybody in here in four months and I am not going to have a conversation."

She was not a woman who did anything in the middle of a thing.

That was the first thing he worked out about it and it took him about a minute, and afterwards it was the thing he could not put down. She was entirely in the room and entirely outside it, and she had been outside it in front of four divisions and in front of a man at a bar in a good coat.

She had spent nineteen years learning how to be in a room with a person and to be, at the same time, somewhere they could not follow her.

She had told him what she wanted on a step the afternoon before and told him what she could not have in a cold room, and both of those had been true, and she held both of them without any effort at all, the way a woman holds a lever-arch file.

"Stay on that side," she said. "Do not come round the table. I want to be able to see you and I do not want you nearer than the table."

He stayed on that side.

She came to him and it was not fast and it was not a performance, and she did not make a single sound she had not decided to make.

She took her own coat off the chair and put it on the floor and put her hair up off her neck with one hand, the way a woman does it when she has done it four thousand times in front of a mirror in an office and never once in front of a man. Then she stood in the middle of eight feet by nine with her arms at her sides and made him come to her.

"Nineteen years," she said. "You are going to say something about that and you are not going to say it kindly, and I want you to say it while you are doing this and not afterwards, because afterwards is where men put it so that it does not count."

"I am not going to say anything about nineteen years."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say that you have told five people and you are going to tell a sixth, and that the sixth is going to be in a room with you on an ordinary afternoon, and there is nothing either of us can do about it." He got his hand into her hair. "That is not about nineteen years. It is about you."

"That is the only true thing anybody in this building has said to me about it." Wren Kallo pulled him down. "Say it again later and I will tell you to stop."

It went on for a long time in a cold room with a door that does not lock, and she kept it in front of her the whole way through, not because she was careful but because that is how she is built. She was entirely there and she was not anywhere else, and she did not shut it down once, and she did not let it take her out of the room either.

At some point she stopped him and made him look at her.

"Say the thing you worked out in a corridor at Cordwain on the sixth," she said. "Not the finding. The other thing. The one you have not said to anybody and you are going to say to me, and if you say it now then it is in this room and I am the only person in it and I cannot take it out."

"Because I have been telling people things for nineteen years and I have never once been able to take one back."

He said it.

It took him about forty seconds and it was not a figure and it was not an instrument and it had nothing in it that could be produced, and when he had finished she put her forehead against his collarbone for about ten seconds without saying anything at all.

"Right," Wren Kallo said.

And then she said the other half of it, which she had clearly had ready since the night before.

"You cannot fill that line," she said. "And you are not going to be able to fill it on the first of September, and if you do not fill it then a bar goes on the certificate on the first of September and Sun Meiling goes into ink underneath it, and she asked for that on the fifth in front of six people and told you she would be ungrateful about it for the rest of her life."

"And you have known all of that since the fifth."

"So there is nothing left to decide after the sixth." Wren Kallo lifted her head. "You decided it in a hall at Cordwain on the sixth, and everything since has been a woman in this house being kind to you about a thing you had already done."

"I have watched you be kind back. I have watched Runa Sato count it out loud to take the room's mind off it. I have watched Nadia Ferrous put an eight on a page at one in the morning so that somebody would have written down the true figure while you were writing the other one."

"She did that for you."

"She did that for you and she has been telling everybody she did it for me since the third, and she does not care, and I am going to sit here and be told that I am the reason a page of this household is in four divisions until the day I die."

She pulled away from him and got her shirt over her shoulders and did not put anything else on, and she sat down on the edge of the deal table with her feet on the floor.

"So no. I am not telling you to fill it and I am not telling you not to. You have had six days and you have not filled it and you are not going to, and the only thing I want said in this room tonight is that I know what you are, because there are two women in this city who know what you are, and one of them has filed it and one of them will not tell me which."

He looked up.

"Which one told you."

"That is the price of the room," Wren Kallo said, "and it is one question, and it is the only one I am going to take off you tonight, and I have asked it because I have known since the twenty-fourth of July that you did not know it was coming, and I have watched a man who did not know it was coming spend two days proving that there were two roads."

She waited.

"Who told you."

Wren Kallo put her hand flat on the file on the table.

"That is the question I am not going to answer," she said, "and you did not think that when you asked it."

"That is the last thing I will teach you tonight, and I am teaching it for nothing. In about eight days you will be standing in a room being read by a woman who can hold a record in her head and knows when it has been altered. She will ask you that question, and you will not be able to do a thing about it."

The yard tram came in at about half past eleven and went out again.

"Get out of my room," Wren Kallo said. "And do not come up the outside stair, because that stair is the only thing in this building that is mine and I have just given you the room and I am not giving you the stair as well."

# The Ear Turned Into The Street

She was at the kitchen door at about eleven with her coat still on and the notebook in her hand, and she did not come in.

"The quarter turned at midnight."

"It is not midnight."

"It turned at midnight and I could not tell you a number for it, and I want you to say something about that before we go anywhere, because you are going to try to make it a figure and I want you to say that you cannot." Beatrix Halm turned her head about thirty degrees into the dark of the yard. "Say it."

"I cannot."

"Good." She shifted the notebook. "Then come with me and I will not have to explain it to anybody in the morning."

Her rooms were two over a shuttered shop on the tram line. It took eleven minutes to walk there and she did not fill any of the eleven, which he noticed that night and made sense of much later.

She shut the door and did not turn a light on and put the notebook on the sill of the window with the street going past it about eleven feet away.

"Now sit down," Beatrix Halm said, "and take your boots off, and I am going to tell you what I want and I am not going to be coy about it, because I have had a month of being coy with a man who cannot be quick about anything."

"Say it."

"I want you to read this."

She put the notebook into his hands in the dark.

"Not the lines under the last one. Those are mine and they are arithmetic." Her hand stayed on his for about a second. "The seven dates. In your order. Out loud, from the top, and you do not get to look."

"That is a test."

"That is the thing I have wanted for nine years and have never once asked anybody for, and I am asking you at eleven o'clock at night on the last night of a quarter." She took her hand away. "Do you know why."

"Because you read everything else."

"Because I read everything else." Beatrix Halm turned her good ear flat against the street for a moment and then let it go. "Nine years of a wall at the north end of that square and nine years of reading a room when I get off a tram, and every person I have ever spoken to has wanted to know what the city sounds like this afternoon."

"And I want the dates."

"And you want the dates, and you have wanted them since the twelfth, and you have not once asked me what I do with a room." She put her hand flat on the front of his shirt and pushed him back about two steps until his shoulders were against the shutter. "So here is the whole of what I want and then we are going to stop talking about it."

"Say the whole of it."

"Read me my own page," said Beatrix Halm. "That is all of it. That is nine years and it is thirty-one words in your handwriting and I want to hear a man say them back to me in a dark room, and I want him to get one of them wrong, and I do not care which one."

"Which one should I get wrong."

"That is mine and not yours and I am not telling you."

He got her coat off her and she let him, and her hands were cold from the tram and she put them under his shirt against his ribs until they were not.

"Now," she said. "Not gentle."

"Not gentle."

"I said not gentle on the twelfth on a step and I am saying it again in here, and the reason is a doctor."

She pulled his shirt out of his belt with one hand and did not look down.

"Four years I was told what I hear is a condition. He said the word *condition* three times in eleven minutes and wrote it on a card."

"And there is a card in a drawer in this city with my name at the top of it saying that the ear is the symptom."

"Then do not be gentle."

"Do not be gentle," Beatrix Halm said, "and do not be careful either, and I have thought about the difference for about a month and it is not the one everybody thinks."

"What is it."

"Careful is about me." She got his belt open. "Careful is a man watching a woman. I have been watched every day of those nine years and I have never once been read, and that is not the same thing, and it has taken me nine years to be able to say it in one sentence."

She undressed herself in about nine seconds with the efficiency of a woman who has changed out of a wet coat on a tram platform, and then she took his clothes off him one at a time and did not hurry any of it, and she talked the whole way through in the low voice she uses at the end of a shift.

"I can feel the turn," she said. "That is what I am not going to be able to explain. It is not a noise. The quarter went at midnight and about four minutes afterwards every tram in this city took the corner by the shop differently, and they have all done it since the first of July and I have been the only person in this city who has heard it and I have told it to a man in a kitchen and there is no instrument for it."

"You have told a man."

"I have told a man in a kitchen and I am not going to count it." She got her hand round the back of his neck and pulled his mouth against hers and then took it away again. "Say the first one."

"The seventh of June. Lodged down at the Exchange, on the second form, at whatever you were worth that morning."

"That is right." She let him go and lay back on the bed on her forearms. "Go on."

"The twentieth of July. Called, and answered by a production at a valuation."

"Right."

"The first of August. A production sworn in a chained book, seven, in your hand — "

"That is mine." She said it fast. "That one is mine and you are not going to get it wrong, and if you get that one wrong I will put you out of this room and you will not get back in."

"The eighth of August. The security stops answering anything."

"Right."

"The thirtieth of — "

"Month."

"September. It stops being a hold and becomes a price."

"Go on."

"The first of October. Seven persons, sworn by somebody answerable at the premises, at ten to nine."

She sat up off him.

"That is all of them," said Beatrix Halm, "and you got six out of seven, and you were not reading."

"I was not looking."

"You were not looking, and you have just told me that on the thirtieth of September a security of seven turns into a price, and it did not do that."

She reached past him for the notebook on the sill and found the page by touch, which she can do.

"That was the thirtieth of August, and I was the woman who wrote it into a book, and you have had it right in your head for eighteen days."

"Then what is the thirtieth of September."

"Nothing yet." Beatrix Halm turned the notebook round in the dark. "In about nine hours a district office will put a quarter of a street on a cart, and the card comes off the wall at ten to six, and after tonight the floor is eight thousand one hundred and forty-four and the word on the wall still has not moved since the end of July."

"Say the other one again and I will tell you which of your two thirtieths is written on this page."

"The thirtieth of August. The band floor goes up and the security stops covering."

"That is the right one." She wrote something with her thumb-nail and then found the pencil and wrote it again properly. "And it is going in the margin in my own hand and you are not going to initial it."

He took the correction without arguing about it.

"You do not have to write it in the margin."

"I am going to write it in the margin," said Beatrix Halm, "in my own hand, and you are not going to put one letter next to it."

She had the pencil against the page.

"In about four years there will be a piece of paper in this city with your handwriting on it and my handwriting on it, and if there is one correction on it that is mine then every other line on it is mine as well."

"And I am not going to let that happen on a page that says eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight next to the seventh of June."

She put the notebook down.

She came back to him without hurrying about it and put both her hands flat against his chest and pushed once, hard enough to move him about four inches, and stayed on him.

"Right," she said. "Now you have got one wrong and you know which one and I have got a margin, and I have not been read by anybody in nine years and I have been the most accurate woman in every room I have walked into in all of them, and I am going to be the one who is accurate in here as well."

"You are—"

"I am going to be accurate," Beatrix Halm said, "and I am going to be quick about the parts you are slow at, and I am not going to be tender, and I am going to ask you for things, and I have wanted to ask a man for things in a bed since about March and I have been doing it in a notebook at four in the morning and a notebook does not answer and that is the entire reason I am asking you and not asking you."

"Ask me."

"I am asking you for all of it and I am asking you for it now."

She took him apart with her hands the way she takes a tram apart, knowing exactly where each piece goes, and she did it in the dark and neither of them turned a light on, and when she got to the place where she wanted him she put her palm flat on him and left it there and made him count, and he counted, and she told him he was counting her and not listening to her and made him start again.

"There," she said. "That. You have been doing it since the thirteenth of August and you did it to a woman in a laboratory for three years and you are doing it to me now and you did not know you were doing it."

"Tell me what to do."

"Listen to the street and tell me what it is doing." She got her hand under his hair at the back of his head and turned his face. "The turn is in it. It has been in it since midnight and it is going to be in it until about four and you have not heard one single thing since we started."

He listened to the street.

"The trams," he said.

"The trams take the corner by the shop differently after a quarter turns," said Beatrix Halm. "Every one of them has done it since the first of July and not one of them has ever mentioned it, and you are the first person who has ever asked me a question about what I hear instead of telling me what it is."

She was not fast.

That was the first thing he found out about her in a bed, and it took him about nine minutes to stop trying to be careful about it.

He kept waiting for the part where a man is careful — where he slows down and watches a woman's face and asks her twice — and she took his hands off her and put them where she wanted them and said nothing at all, and the not-saying took up more of that room than a question would have done.

"You are doing it now," she said.

"Doing what."

"Being careful." She got her weight off his forearm and onto his hip, unhurried, the way she moves between a counter and a door. "I gave you the difference about two hours ago and I am not going to give it to you twice. Nine years of being watched is the whole of what I am describing. Lie here and be read, or get up and go home."

"You want me to be worse than careful."

"I want you to be accurate." She said it with no weight in it. "In about four months somebody is going to be sitting at a counter with your name at the top of a page in front of them and you are not going to be able to do the careful thing. I am not going to be in a room with a man who does it."

He laughed. He had not expected to and it came out of him at about the wrong volume, and she waited for it to finish instead of talking across it.

"That is a strange way to ask a man for it."

"That is the only way anybody has ever put it to me, and I have been putting it to myself since about March." She got her hand flat on his chest and pushed until his shoulders went back into the pillow. "Now stop talking. I have been talking since the twelfth of September and I would like about eleven minutes of a room where I am not explaining one part of it."

He was slow about that. It took her four minutes to get him to stop asking and about nine more before he had stopped apologising for the asking.

She put him on his back and then on his front and did not consult him about either, and when he reached for her she moved about four inches out of the way, which left him on her and not on what he had been reaching for, and she let him arrive at that by himself.

He put his mouth against the back of her neck and she let him have about two seconds of it and then took his chin between two fingers and turned his face away.

"Eyes on the window," she said. "And the numbers are in your ears. Tell me what the street is doing and I will tell you when."

"That is not what you just asked for."

"It is exactly what I just asked for, and it is what I do all day, and you want to know what it is like to be inside nine years of my head, so here it is." She shifted her weight back onto him and put his hands where she wanted them and started again, unhurried, completely on purpose. "Street. Now. Out loud. Keep going."

He told her about the street.

He got the trams and the water and a shutter going up two streets down, and she let him run about four minutes before she stopped him with two fingers on his jaw.

"You are counting."

"I am listening."

"You are counting me." She turned his head until he was looking at her. "That is what you have done since the thirteenth of August, and it is what you did for three years to a woman in a laboratory, and you did not know then either. Start again, and this time say what you can hear instead of what you have worked out."

He said what he could hear.

It was not much and all of it was true, a tram that had taken the corner and a man on the far side of the street who had shut a door and had not gone away, and she worked him about nine minutes on it and made him go back over the tram twice, and he went back over it, and somewhere inside the third time through it he stopped translating and began to hear, and the difference in his hands was visible from above her.

"There," she said. "That. Do not lose it."

At about twenty past twelve she said his name twice. The first one was the one she uses at a counter. The second one came out of her from lower down, and she did not apologise for it and did not explain it.

At about half past one she stopped.

"Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight," she said, in a completely flat voice, the way a woman reads a figure off a board.

He had it. He had had it since the thirtieth of August.

"Seven," she said. "Now say seven."

"Seven."

"Now the other one. Again. And do not put a face on it. It is a figure and it has stood in a column since the seventh of June and it is not a thing to make a face about."

He said it again.

"Five times," she said. "You have done it four."

He said it a fifth time and she took the number off him and put it down on the sheet beside them and went back to the street.

At about three she began deciding when she was going to stop, and letting him know, which she can do because she can hear forty thousand people moving and a body in a room is four of them. She told him about nine seconds before every time and he got it wrong twice.

"Again," she said, at about six minutes to four.

"I have not said anything."

"You have not said anything and I have said nothing, and that was the correct thing to do, and I want you to notice that you got it right." Her hand went flat on him and stayed there. "Nine seconds. I am not going to this time."

"What does that mean."

"It means I am going past it," said Beatrix Halm, "and I am telling you now because you would rather know than guess, and I am not being tender about it, I am being accurate about it, and you may count if you want to. I have never once been a woman who could not be counted."

He counted, and he wrote the hour down in his own head afterward because he has been counting days all month and a man who does one thing all month does not stop.

He came out four minutes past it, which is poor work from a man whose whole life is arithmetic, and she told him so afterwards in about four words and would not tell him what they were.

He was about to make something out of that and she put her hand flat over his mouth.

"Not yet," she said. "You always do that. You take the worst thing anybody has said to you in a room and turn it into a thing you are going to carry, and I have watched you do it since the third of August and I am not going to be in a bed while you do it."

She lifted the hand off his mouth.

"Tonight it is going to be a woman who went on past you and said the number out loud. Four minutes. A figure. Go to sleep."

And she lay with her head on his chest and her ear against his ribs and let the street go on for about a minute, the way a woman does it when she has been listening to something for nine years and has decided to stop for the length of a tram.

"Now the other thing," she said. "And I am going to say it before I get warm, so listen."

"Go on."

"I am going to be at a counter at about ten in the morning." Beatrix Halm did not lift her head. "Alone. With this notebook and nothing else, because a price with my name on it is the only thing that gets a hearing moved and I have not got one and I am not getting one out of your house."

"I will not come."

"You were going to offer."

She lifted her head about an inch.

"I want you to hear the shape of it. If you are on the step with me at ten in the morning then a woman with a notebook is being brought to a guild counter by the man whose household is on a certificate."

"And I stop being a woman defrauded of a date and I become a woman being helped, and the guild will say *noted* and put the help in the file instead of the answer."

"So I will not."

"You will not, and I want it in words, and not because it is good manners." She got up onto one elbow. "Say the whole of it."

"I will not be at the counter, in the queue, or within about eleven yards of you between nine and eleven in the morning."

"Nine and eleven is not the whole of the day."

"Nine until the hearing is answered or refused, whichever is later."

"Which is not a time and is the only acceptable answer anybody has ever given me," said Beatrix Halm, "and I am not being tender about it, and I want the tea."

She got up and got the tea and brought it back and sat on the end of the bed with it in both hands and did not drink it.

"Twenty-one minutes past four," she said. "That is when the corner goes back."

He lay there and listened to it go back.

And then she put the notebook down on the sill, found the page by touch, and wrote the thirtieth of September in the margin in her own hand, twice, so that it could not be rubbed out, and turned the good ear over onto the pillow and went to sleep in about nine seconds, which she can do after a double shift.

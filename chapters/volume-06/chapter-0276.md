# The Space Under The Signature

Almost nothing in this city opens at a weekend, and on the fifth of August there were seven women in a kitchen with a bare table in it and a saucer in the middle of the table for the twenty-third day.

Sun Meiling put her hand on the chair and did not sit down.

"Say it back," she said. "In your own words. The one you read out at that table on the twenty-sixth."

He said it back in his own words, because she had asked for his own words, and it came out the same. The head answers. The auditor certifies. Two hands, both of them in this household now, and there is no third party in this city to send for.

"Good. Now the other one." Sun Meiling turned the page over without looking at it, which she can do. "You have been the auditor of record since yesterday at ten to nine, and I have had five days to think about what that means and I have got there in about ninety seconds and I would like everybody in this room to understand that I did not enjoy getting there."

"Say it."

"I am going to be in ink on the first of September." She put both hands flat on the wood. "My row has been pencil in a coat pocket since the twenty-second of March. It is one hundred and thirty-five days old today and it is under a saucer and it is in a coat and it is stitched shut with twenty-two stitches that a woman put in it in the dark on purpose."

"On the first of September it is ink, because there is a re-issue of that schedule on the first at the figure of seven and a schedule names the members of the household and I am going to be one of them."

The kitchen was very quiet and there were seven women in it.

"So," Sun Meiling said, "and the re-issue certifies. And the certification is the auditor of record. And the auditor of record is him."

"Yes."

"And a certification is not a return." She looked at Beatrix Halm. "You look at me like that whenever I say something you already know."

"You look at me like that every time you say something that is going to cost somebody in this room," Beatrix Halm said.

"Then look at me like that, because here is the second half." Sun Meiling put one finger on the table. "He swears the composition. He is the head and the composition is his answer and it is his hand and it is his date. And then he certifies the schedule. Same man. Same hand. Same morning."

"And there is a thing about that which I would like said out loud in this room by somebody who is not me, and it has taken me five days and it is going to be said out loud by him or not at all."

"It is not a problem," Nadia Ferrous said from the settle.

"It is enormous." Sun Meiling turned round. "It is enormous because a head who certifies his own sworn answer is a man standing behind his own instrument, and there is not one office in this city that will read a certification and ask what the certifier thinks about the figure underneath it."

"There will be a form. There will be a date. And there will be no second hand anywhere in it, and I have spent nineteen years being an arrangement inside somebody else's arithmetic and I am not going to be an arrangement inside his."

Nobody in the kitchen had an answer to that, and she had not expected one.

"Go on," Runa Sato said. "You have not got to the part that matters."

"I got to it at about two o'clock yesterday morning," Sun Meiling said, "and I have been sitting on it since, and I would have said it at the table on the second if I had known it on the second."

"Say it."

"The certificate issues on the first of September at the figure of seven. Seven persons of reading age keeping an address at these premises. And the seventh line of that certificate is the line an officer of a district office let stand empty on the evening of the third of June, at ten past seven, and it is blank, and there is no name on it anywhere in this city."

She put her hands flat again.

"And on the morning of the second a woman at Cobb Street told a room of three officers and a clerk that the head may fill it. Once. His own hand. His own date. On the re-issue. Before the certification."

Runa Sato had her pencil out. She had it out before Sun Meiling got to the end of the sentence.

"Go on," Runa said.

"After the first of September, if that line is still empty when the certification is made, the certificate goes out with an empty line on it, and an empty line is a bar, and a bar sits on the certificate and does not stop the certificate being made."

"So my row goes into ink on the first of September either way. There is no version of the first on which I stay out of it, and the line is not about me."

She stopped, and Runa Sato wrote it down, and read it back, and neither of them had to be asked to do that twice.

"Say the part where you worked out whose name it is," Agnete Lindqvist said from the landing door. She had come down four steps and was standing on them, which is as far as she had gone in a room in eleven years.

Sun Meiling did not answer for about four seconds, which for her is a long time.

"There are six named lines on that certificate," she said. "They are the six members of the schedule of the fourth of May. Five of them are women in this building and one of them was entered as absent with reason in four hundred words that I filed myself on the sixth of July. So the sixth is me and the sixth is a line with my name on it and a reason against it that I wrote myself."

"The seventh," he said.

"The seventh is the only person of reading age keeping an address at these premises whose name is not on a certificate of this household anywhere in this city." Sun Meiling looked at him. "There is one."

"And she put a correction in at Cobb Street on the thirtieth at eleven at night, on purpose, on the last day she held that office, in a form she could not have used on any other day of that month, and the correction returns the figure at seven and leaves the seventh line of that district return empty."

The kitchen had seven women in it and one of the seven was not there and had been gone since the sixth of July.

"She did it to stop the district closing that line," Sun Meiling said. "And she did it by making herself the only name in this household that cannot be written down anywhere. And the only hand in this city that can fill that line on the first of September is the one that swore the figure."

There was a long stretch of nothing at all.

"Fill it," Sun Meiling said.

"Sun—"

"Fill it." She was not shouting and she was not calm.

"Do not stand in that kitchen and do the thing your face is doing.

"I have been a pencil row in a coat pocket for one hundred and thirty-five days, and I asked you for it on the fifteenth of July in front of a room and you said yes, and then it turned out that yes cost a woman an office. I sat down and worked out what that meant in about ninety seconds. So do not make me say this twice."

"The term."

"The term." Sun Meiling put one finger on the table again.

She gave you a term in the dark on the ninth of July and you have kept it for twenty-seven days, and it is the only thing in this house that has never once been tested.

"And there is no office in this city that can serve that paper on her in her own rooms, and there is no man who can knock on that door, and there is no way to put any of this in front of her that is not putting a sheet of paper in front of her.

"Yes."

"And you are going to leave the line blank," Sun Meiling said, "and I am going into ink on a certificate with a bar on it, and I am not going to be ungrateful about that for the rest of my life. I want that written down somewhere with a date on it."

"I want a woman in this city to be able to read at some point that Sun Meiling asked a man to break a promise in a kitchen and he did not, and that she asked."

"Nadia," Runa Sato said.

Nadia Ferrous had the cloth bag on the settle and her hand flat on it and she had not opened it.

"Two hundred and three people went through that hatch on the seventeenth of July," she said, "and two hundred and three on the eighteenth, and it is two hundred and forty-something today, and it does not matter."

"What matters is that there are four divisions in this city. I have known that since the fifteenth of July and I have not thought about it once until this minute, and I have just thought about it, and I would like it said in this kitchen in my own words before one of you gets there first."

"Say it."

"There is a page of mine going into four divisions since the third of August," Nadia Ferrous said, "and it has eight names on it, and one of the eight is a Bureau counter-forensics officer who is on nothing at any time and who has told four people in this city about a thing she found in April, and her division reads everything, because that is what her division does."

She lifted her hand off the bag.

"I did that on the thirtieth at one in the morning without asking anybody. I have said that out loud twice this week and I am not taking it back this morning, and I am going to say the other half of it now, because I have been not saying the other half since the third and I am the youngest person in this room and I am the only one who is not frightened of what I have done."

She stood up off the settle.

"I would like to know whether Wren Kallo is a member of this household from the third of August or not," she said. "In four divisions. On paper. In the only room in this city that has ever let me say anything, which is a lift, and I have been saying things into it for two years and not one of them has ever been heard."

The table was bare and there was a saucer on it and the seven of them were in the room and one of them was on a tram in another city.

Sun Meiling sat down.

"Sit down, all of you," she said. "Not because there is going to be a speech. Because there is going to be a figure, and I am not having one more figure said out loud in this room standing up."

They sat down.

"Six members on page two," Sun Meiling said, "and one blank line, and one bar, and thirty days, and no thirty-first. Say it back to me, Runa, all of it, in order, because you are the only person in this room who does this and I am not going to do it twice."

Runa Sato read it back. Six members on page two and one blank line and one bar and thirty days and no thirty-first, in that order, with the hour under it, and then she wrote it in the notebook and put the notebook flat on the table and looked at the seven of them and at the door.

"There is one more," Runa Sato said. "And I have been counting out loud in this house since the seventh of June, on the understanding that the total was never to sit in one skull and be that woman's problem, and I have been the one saying it, and on the second of August a division read this household's composition at seven and did not ask me."

She put the pencil down.

"There are eight women in this building and one of them is behind a counter at the Vestry and one of them is somewhere on a tram. That is eight and not seven. And the reason the answer is seven is that a man in a district office left a line empty in June, and this city has been counting his emptiness ever since."

"Twenty-nine days," she said. "That is what is left. And one of them has to be a name."

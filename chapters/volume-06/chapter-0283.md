# A Term With A Date On It

The desk on the sixteenth of August was on the ground floor at Cobb Street, and it was a room with three officers, a clerk, a window that had been painted shut, and a member of the certification schedule of the fourth of May sitting at it with her own name on the head line of the paper in front of her.

He was there because the record is about his premises, and the head of the premises attends a sitting that is about his premises.

She had come on her own. She was wearing the coat she had worn on the second and it was not a good coat, and she had a pencil and no folder, and she had not looked at him once in the first four minutes.

"You will want to know why she is not here," the senior officer said, before anybody sat down properly. "Miss Duarte was called to a sitting on the second of August at ten in the morning and did not attend it."

"Fourteen days from that sitting puts her at a desk where she is the subject, and she has come, and the fourteen days were hers and not the office's."

"Nobody said that to me on the second," he said.

"No one said it to you on the second because you were not the person it was done to," said the officer.

"And you have been in this room for three minutes and you have already said the thing that makes a man look like the office. Sit down."

He took the chair in front of the desk.

They began at the beginning.

She read it herself. She had a hand on the copy in front of her and she went down the dates without being asked and without a pause, in order, the way she had read a household's whole record aloud in a room at the third office of the Bureau for four hours on the twentieth of July.

The only thing that was different about it was the subject line.

"The composition of premises 34-6-118 was read at seven persons of reading age keeping an address at those premises, on the second of August, on the strength of a correction put in at Cobb Street on the last night of July by the then auditor of record of this schedule, at twenty-five minutes past eleven."

"The correction returned the figure of persons at seven and entered the seventh returned line of the district return of the third of June as blank and counted as a person."

She let it stand.

"Stop there," said the senior officer, "and let me put the next one."

"That is the second," Ines Duarte said.

"That is the second in time. Put the first."

"There is no first."

"There is a composition return of the fourth quarter that carries this address and carries your name on one of its lines, and it is dated the twelfth of June."

The officer turned a sheet round so that it faced the room.

"And there is a correction filed by you on the thirtieth of July that alters the figure of persons at this address, and it is dated the thirtieth of July."

"And I am going to read them in the order the file gives them, which is the composition return first, and then the correction, because that is the order a reader takes."

He was on his feet before he knew he had stood up.

"Stop," he said. "That order says she corrected her own household and then the household turned out to include her."

"That order says what the file says," said the officer, "and it says nothing about anybody, because a file says nothing about anybody. It says dates. Sit down, Mr Lin."

He was down again before the sentence had finished.

"It took about four seconds," Ines Duarte said, "and I have been waiting six days for one of you to do it, and I did not think it would be the one who did not want to."

"And I am obliged to say that you were the best candidate."

"You filed on the thirtieth," he said.

"I filed on the thirtieth because it was the last hour a counter took from a member and the last day I held the office, and I have said so in four rooms since."

She put her hand flat on the copy.

"It is not mine to correct now. I no longer hold the office that could receive it."

"So I am going to do the second thing instead, in front of three officers and a clerk, and I would like the clerk to have a pen in his hand before I start."

"Because in about nine minutes a piece of paper is going to come into this room that nobody here will be able to take out again."

The clerk had a pen in his hand.

"Read me the twelfth of June," Ines Duarte said.

The officer read it. A composition return of the fourth quarter, dated the twelfth of June, carrying the address, carrying her name, carrying the words *address at premises held*.

"That is yours," he said, and gave it to her. "It is before everything else in this file by eighteen days."

"You held that address on the twelfth of June, in your own hand, in a return of your own office, before the district let the seventh line stand empty."

"You held it before you knew the figure was going to be seven, and before you did the thing you did on the thirtieth of July at twenty-five minutes past eleven at night."

"That is mine," she said, "and put it in the file in those words, and put it in the file as a thing I asked for and not as a thing any of you thought of."

"And the order of the file?" said the officer.

"Chronological," said Ines Duarte. "The address first, then the correction. Because that is the order it happened in."

"And because a woman who kept an address for eleven years and then corrected a record about it is an auditor doing her job."

"And a woman who corrected a record about her household and then held the address is something else entirely, and she is the woman you would have written a minute about if I had let you read it the other way round."

The room said nothing back.

"So the twelfth of June goes in the file in my words," she said, "and it goes in the file with a date on it, and from this afternoon there are four copies."

"And inside four days there is a division in this city that knows that Ines Duarte kept an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen on the twelfth of June, in writing, in her own hand, in a return of her own office."

She took her hand off the paper.

"I spent eleven days telling a man not to write my name on a line," she said, "and I have just put it on a document myself, in a room, in front of witnesses."

"And I did it because I am tired of things that have never been tested, and I would like it noted that I chose between two bad things on my own and did not ask anybody's advice."

"And that the man who has been trying to protect me from exactly this has been sitting in this room for twenty minutes unable to open his mouth."

The senior officer looked at her for a while.

"The ground of that entry will be taken," he said. "Anything further?"

"There is one more and it is not mine to give and I am going to give it anyway," she said.

"Because a member at a desk where she is the subject may put anything to the head of the premises that concerns the subject of the desk, and it goes in the file."

"And I am the subject of this desk, and that is the only power I have ever had in a room like this one, and I am going to use all of it while I have got it."

The clerk's pen was still up.

"Mr Lin," said Ines Duarte, "in your own words, and not as a formula: what did you swear to me in a room on the ninth of July at about eleven at night?"

He gave it to her, flat, the way he gives a thing to a clerk.

"That no paper is read to you in your own rooms," he said. "That was the term. I have kept it every day since, and there is no office in this city that can serve you a sheet where you sleep."

The clerk wrote it down. She read it back over his shoulder before he had finished the last word, which she can do, and then the senior officer read it back from the sheet in the way an officer does.

And it was in the file.

"Thank you," she said.

"That is now a standing undertaking of the head of the premises, made in a room, on a date, before three officers and a clerk," he said. "You understand what that is."

"Before today that was a thing between two people. As of about four minutes ago it is on a record in a division that does not count things and a division that does."

"And any woman in any of the four of them may read that a man in this city has sworn not to put a paper in front of another woman where she lives, and may put two and two together about where she lives."

"I understand it," he said. "I understood it at about the point where you said it out loud."

"I know you did." Ines Duarte folded her hands on the table. "You cannot get it back. I could not get it back either, and I put it there on purpose."

"And if I had not put it there, then in about four years somebody would have come into this room and put a paper in front of me in my own rooms, and there would have been nothing whatever on the record that you had ever undertaken not to let them."

She sat back.

"You asked me on the ninth what happens on the first," she said. "I told you, and it is worse than I told you."

"Because now there is a file that says I sleep somewhere, and it does not say where, and a man in a division I have never been in is going to try to narrow that down for a living."

She stopped.

"And you will not be able to help him, and that is the price, and I have paid it out loud, and now you can carry it."

The senior officer asked him then the only question the room had left, which was whether he confirmed the figure of seven as the figure of persons of reading age keeping an address at those premises on the day of the answer.

He said that he did.

"You said it quickly," said the officer.

"I said it quickly because it is true," he said, "and because in fourteen days I am going to certify the same figure on a document that has a blank line on it."

"And I have known since the ninth of August that it is going to have a blank line on it."

The clerk wrote that down too. Nobody stopped him.

Outside on the steps at about half past four, with the light going, she stopped and turned round, and he thought for a moment that she was going to thank him.

"Which of the two of us told you that you were the seventh line," she said.

He had been ready for it since the eighth of August and he had rehearsed three answers and none of them worked.

"I had it on my own," he said. "I worked it out on a wall in the Square on the second of August at about half past nine in the evening, alone."

"And you have been able to prove that since the ninth, because you have been able to prove that nothing was in any file before the ninth."

"And I want you to know that I have known for a week that you were going to make me say it in a room."

She looked at him for about four seconds.

"Yes," she said, "and that is exactly the answer of a man who worked it out, and it is the only answer I could have wanted."

"And it is the only question I have ever asked you that I was going to be satisfied with."

She went down the steps.

He stood on them in the dark for about four minutes with a document in a file in a division in this city saying that he had promised a woman not to put a paper where she sleeps.

There was nothing in him that could take it back, and the woman it was about had put it there herself, on purpose, to save a thing he had not known was in danger.

Thirty-eight days he had kept it by never going up a stair.

It had taken four minutes in a room and it could never be kept again by not going up a stair.

# The Price She Was Never Shown

Beatrix Halm had the last debt and it was not the account's, and he had been carrying it for a fortnight because it was the only one he could not begin without telling her what he had cost her.

She came in off the yard tram at six with her gloves folded and her season squared in her coat and put her notebook on the bare table and did not open it.

"Sun's row is under a saucer," she said. "Nadia has written it with the hour on it and signed her own name against it. Runa has gone out of a door and not slammed it. And you have been walking round this kitchen since the eleventh looking at a piece of paper in a drawer."

"Yes."

"Then say it before I write anything down, because I decide what goes in there and I would rather I did not have to decide blind."

He said it at the table with the whole room listening, because he had learned on the eleventh that a debt said at the bottom of a stair in the rain still came back into the house by morning.

"On the third of July, at the north desk, before the queue built, your broker put one thing in front of me and she was permitted to put exactly one thing in front of me and she put it down and asked me to say the word by the twelfth."

Beatrix sat down on the edge of the chair with her gloves still folded in her hand.

"It was one thing or nothing," he said. "Unmark that docket and the run breaks at the hundred and thirty-fourth place. The account is listed dead on the fourth of August, and the rule is that every security standing against an account comes off it the same day.

"One of those securities is what the guild holds over your hearing. And on that same afternoon she strikes the guild's fourth page and burns it in front of me, and there is nothing on it after that."

"Go on."

"I said no. In a queue, with two officers behind me, and the clerk wrote the refusal into the real book with the hour under it. Then I came up these stairs the same afternoon and told you what was left on the table, and what was left on the table was nothing."

"You did."

"I did. And what I did not tell you, for sixteen days, was that there was a price, and that the price was your name on it, and that it was on the table in front of me at nine in the morning with a broker's pen in her hand."

The room had gone entirely still in the way it does when a person says a thing that moves every object in it.

Beatrix put her gloves on the table and squared them.

"How much," she said.

"Everything, at once, or nothing at all. My plate comes back to me on the fourth of August. The account is struck out. The page goes into the fire.

"And Beatrice Halm stops being a member of a household standing under a called security for the rest of her life." He had got it flat by then, which had taken him three attempts. "She was not permitted to break it up. There is no smaller version, and that is what she said when I asked her whether there was one."

"And after the twelfth."

"After the twelfth she is not permitted to put it in front of me again in the life of the account. It expired at seven on the twelfth. A boy came up those steps with four words in her hand about the first of August and the plate, and those four words were not the offer, and that is how I know it is gone."

Beatrix took out her notebook and opened it and wrote for about forty seconds in her small level hand, and read it back to herself before she said any of it to the room.

"Offer made the third of July, refused by the head in writing, expiring the twelfth. Unmarked August docket. Dead list the fourth of August. Every security against the account comes off that day, including the guild's hold on the hearing of Beatrice Halm, whose hearing is therefore adjourned and not released, at the cost of one year of her season and eleven burned months she entered in her own hand in the year before the Veil broke."

"Beatrix—"

"That is the entry," she said. "That is what happened and it is what it cost and I am going to put the date on it, which is the nineteenth of July, and I am going to read it back."

She read it back.

Then she shut the book on her thumb and looked at him for the first time since he had started.

"Now the other thing," she said. "Not the price. The question under the price, and you are going to answer it now or I take the book upstairs and never open it in this kitchen again."

"I will answer it."

"Would you have said yes."

He had thought about it for sixteen days in washrooms and on stair rails and in a room in a guild where a woman with four books had told him that a figure on a dated page is a fact.

"No," he said, and then, because it was not true, "I do not know."

"No, or you do not know."

"I do not know," he said. "I want you to have the true one. If you ask Marguerite Vale what she thinks I would have said, she will give you a woman's answer, and she will be right about the woman and wrong about the man.

"I have been three grains under a floor for five years and I have never once let a thing go. I would like to tell you I would have let that go. I cannot, because I do not know what I would have done at nine in the morning on the third, with two officers behind me, and a year's encumbrance on a woman I—"

He stopped there, which was the fourth time that week he had stopped in front of that particular word.

Beatrix Halm sat on the edge of the chair and did the thing she had taught herself to do over four years of listening to a city out of one ear, which was to let a silence finish on its own terms.

Then she opened the book again, tore nothing, and turned it round so the entry faced him.

"Take this page to Nadia Ferrous," she said. "Not a copy. The page. It goes in the book that is not in any office and that eight women hold between them, and after tonight there will be nine of us who know what this house owes and who is paying it."

"That is the only record in the city that cannot be produced and you said so two nights ago."

"It can be produced," Beatrix said. "By nine women at a kitchen table, at any hour of any night. No office in this city can ask for it and no clerk can serve a form on it. A thing like that is not a record. It is a hold.

"I have kept a record for four years because I thought a record was safer. I was wrong. A page in a hand somebody else keeps is worth ten in mine."

She tore the page out along the perforation she had kept for thirty years for exactly this.

Wren took it in at the door on her way to go and read it standing and gave it back without a word, and her mouth did the thing it did when she was not going to say a thing, and she said it anyway.

"Twenty-six days on Hadewig's desk. That is the twenty-sixth. She pulled the index entry first and the correction second, and a division that pulls first has already decided and is taking its time about telling you."

Petra came down at nine with nothing in her hands and forty-five days behind her and stood at the bottom of the table.

"Two," she said.

He looked at her.

"There are two nights left in this month that I know of," Petra said, "the first of August and the fourth of August, and there is a mark to go in at ten to nine on the first, and I will be at the bottom of these steps for it, and I want to hear you say out loud, once, in this kitchen, what it is that the mark is keeping."

"It is keeping Beatrix in a hearing she cannot win," he said, "and it is keeping the clinic counted, and it is keeping a six on a certificate that a woman in another part of this city says is wrong."

"That is a list," Petra said, "and a list is not an answer, and I have been on no roll for forty-five days and I am asking because in thirty days somebody is going to make me answer for something and I would like to know what the word looks like."

"It is keeping me," he said. "The account is standing under a called security that I lodged in June, and it stays standing because I am still here and still able to answer for it, and the day I stop being able to answer for it is the day it goes."

Petra nodded once and went back up.

Beatrix put her season in her coat and squared the coat and stopped at the door.

"I am going to say one thing and then I am going to the tram," she said. "You have now said out loud, in a room of eight women, that a price with my name on it was put in front of you and you let it die. I am not going to forgive you and I am not going to be angry and both of those are on purpose."

"I know."

"I do not think you know," Beatrix said. "But you will find out at about eleven o'clock tonight when you go up that stair, and I want you to know in advance that I will know what time it is."

She went down the sixty-one steps. She did it at the bottom, standing still, without moving her lips, because he had taught her that in April and she had kept it.

The notebook lay on the table without its page. The pencil row lay under the saucer. Nadia's book was open at the entry with the hour against it.

Tomorrow was the twentieth, and a fortnight had run out of the sixth, and the only woman in Ostara who could have told him what that would cost was in two rooms above a laundry, and on the seventeenth, on a stair, in her stockings, she had told him that he had been standing on the right side of a line since the ninth and that on the twentieth the line would be gone.

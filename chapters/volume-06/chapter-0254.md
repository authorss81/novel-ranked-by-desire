# The Table Bare

Marguerite Vale knew before nine that he had been in the counting room on the return day, and she came up the sixty-one steps herself instead of sending a boy, and she had her gloves on and no pen.

The valuation copy of the first of July was in her bag. The dead list copy of the fourth was in her bag. The dead list copy of the fourth of August was in her bag.

She did not sit down.

"A broker of record stands answerable for what is in her book," she said. "Yesterday a stranger put a question into a book that reconciles. It came out of this house. There were four clerks between that room and that door and every one of them saw me not in it, and by the middle of next week one of them will say so to somebody, and then it will be in a book."

"Marguerite—"

"Take the account back where it lives." She put the bag on the table and unbuttoned it. "It is not yours. It was never yours. It is the Vant's, and the Vant's is not a flat on Ferrier Street, and I have let it sit on a kitchen table since the nineteenth of June because a man asked me to price his debts and I wanted to see whether he would pay for them."

She took out the two copies and put them square, and then took out a sheet with five lines on it in pencil in his own hand and squared that on top.

The table went from one sheet to four in about eleven seconds. The kitchen was very quiet.

"You do not owe me a figure," she said. "You will get one. That is what happened. What you have done is put the pricing of this house into the hands of a woman who keeps four guilds' books and has never met one of you and has wanted what I have spent nineteen years looking for, and who will hand it to the annual sitting with a date on it."

She set her gloves on the table beside them, which she had never once done at Ferrier Street.

"Take them."

He did not touch them for a while.

"What happens to the first of August," he said.

"Nothing. The docket is still the docket. I still mark it at ten to nine with the pen chained to my wrist, and the bundle comes to the bottom of the steps from a woman on no roll, and the mark carries the account through that day."

She said it in the voice she used for a figure she had checked twice. "The mark is the last thing I will be able to put in front of the head of this household for the life of the account. The twelfth of July took the offer. Yesterday took the pricing. Both are done and I did not raise my voice for either."

She turned the sheet of five pencil lines round so it faced him.

"I am going to say the number once so that you cannot stand in this kitchen later and tell anybody I kept it from you."

She said it. It was not large. It was very close to the figure a stranger had given him the day before with two figures and a refusal and a stack of six slips, and the two of them being within a small distance of each other was the worst part, because it meant the number had never been in dispute.

Only the book had been.

"A broker prices what is offered to her," Marguerite said. "Mine answers to me and it is read at an annual sitting by nine men and by a woman in four years who has better arithmetic than mine. Hers answers to her, and hers is dated, and a dated figure in a book that reconciles does not go anywhere. I have given you mine because I would rather you had the cheaper number and knew what I am."

She buttoned the empty bag.

"Nineteen years," she said. "I have been inside this guild since I was twenty-two looking for one asset in Ostara that a court cannot take off me, and a house is that, and you have gone and handed the pricing of it to a woman who has four books and no reason to be generous and eleven years of my answer in her head. Do that again in four years and I will not be here to be angry about it."

She put her gloves back on and picked up the bag and went down the sixty-one steps without waiting at the landing rail.

He went after her as far as the twentieth and caught her at the turn where the stair takes the light.

"Marguerite."

"I am not stopping, and if I stop I will say something I have said to no one in nineteen years, and you have not earned it by going round my back with a stranger on a return day."

"You have not answered a question," he said. "You gave me a number and you did not tell me what it costs you."

She stopped with her hand on the rail.

"I have carried the pricing of this household since the nineteenth of June," she said, "and I have not charged you for one minute of it, and I would have gone on carrying it until the account closed or I died, whichever came first, because a broker who is owed something by a client is a broker who cannot leave."

She went down three more steps before she finished.

"Take the six off your own schedule and give me the account at six, and there is a woman in four years holding my book with a name at the top of it that a stranger chose on a Thursday." Her voice stayed level the whole way.

"That is what it costs. You will not be able to put it down anywhere, because this guild prints no instrument by which a broker may hold a client's pricing. I have looked for one since I was twenty-two. It is not printed, because a guild that printed it would be admitting that brokers can be bought."

He stayed on the twentieth step a while. Then he went back up and told the room that the broker had gone and that the account was not coming back that morning, and Nadia wrote the hour against that too.

Beatrix had her notebook open and had not written anything in it, which she never did without deciding first.

"Then there is nothing on the table to disagree with," she said.

"That is what I said."

"I heard you. I meant it as the other thing." She turned the notebook round without shutting it. "Since March that table has been where this house could look at itself.

"You have taken a measure of six women off it in a day and a half. There is a drawer under the bread, a wall with a rectangle on it, and a coat pocket with a pencil row in it. That is the whole inventory of a house that exists on three papers in three offices."

Nadia Ferrous came in with her book and wrote the day down with the hour against it, hour by hour, the way she had kept it for eleven years.

She wrote: eight o'clock, the broker came up and took three papers off the table. Nine o'clock, the table cleared. Nine twenty, she read out what it said.

Then she read the line back and stopped with the pen on it.

"I am entering this against myself," Nadia said. "That is what I did on the seventh for the empty room and I have done it twice since, and I am going to do it now, because a thing this house has lost was lost by an act and an act wants a name against it and the name that was there is not available this morning."

She wrote her own name against the line and put the hour beside it.

Runa came up from Cordwain at half past three and put her folder on the bare table and left it there, which she had never done, because a folder on a table is a thing a person can pick up, and a thing on a table is not a statement.

Petra met him at the foot of the stairs at six with nothing in her hands and forty days on no roll behind her.

"Thirty-nine days yesterday, forty today," she said. "I have been marking things for eleven years that were not mine to mark, and for forty days I have been marking nothing, and this morning I stood at the bottom of sixty-one steps and worked out that the first of August is eighteen days off and there is not one sheet of paper in this house that says what any of us owes anybody."

"There is a charge under the bread."

"Then there is a charge under the bread," Petra said, "and on the first of August I will stand at the bottom of these steps at ten to nine and ask what is on it, and if the answer is that the paper is in a building across the city, then that is what I will write in whatever I am holding."

Wren came in after supper with her folder shut and her hands inkless for the second time in a week and stood at the end of the table that had nothing on it.

"Twenty-one days," she said. "Four edits, all of them removals. It has not been acted on and it is not going to be acted on, because what it asks a division to do is remove something, and no division removes anything in the back half of a week without a season behind it." She put her hand flat on the bare wood. "You went and got a price from her."

"Yes."

"Then it is on her books now and not on yours, and in four years the division that has to answer for this house is the one she sits in," Wren said, "and I count that as the most expensive hour anybody in this building has spent this year, and I include the plate you put in a chained book in June."

He mopped the landing after the household had gone down, working round the nail and the rectangle with the care he always took, and the rectangle was nine by fourteen with its cold middle and nothing had ever been laid over it, and the table in the room behind him had held this house on it since the twenty-second of March and was holding nothing at all.

At nine he went to the drawer under the bread and put his hand in it to find the charge to look at it again before bed.

Four papers were in there that he knew about. The receipt on top, signed in his own hand on the twenty-eighth, with a printed line under the signature for an object or a remark and nothing entered on it.

The certificate under that, dated the twenty-seventh, carrying six. The valuation copy at the bottom, carried out and back the same day and now written in a stranger's figures instead of his broker's. And the charge from audit intake, with a form number and a date and a sum in a hand he did not know.

That was everything the house had on itself and it was all in a drawer and none of it was the house.

The table stood bare in the middle of the room and the lamp was off the edge of it, and the drawer had been shut since the ninth, and on the twelfth the whole of it had gone out of the world without a word, and on the fourteenth a woman had walked out of this kitchen carrying the only account of the house that had ever been anywhere else.

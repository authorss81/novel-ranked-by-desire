# The Verse She Did Not Choose

The twenty-first came in with rain that stopped at nine, and Lode found him on the fourth floor with a bucket and gave him the news in the way a man gives news to somebody he is sorry for.

"Last house at the Bellhouse," Lode said. "About half eight. Four hundred in and a couple of hundred on the Row outside it in the wet. It was a verse."

"About what."

"About a man who wrote six on a paper and a house that is seven." Lode wiped his hands down his thighs. "It has a street in it that fits six different blocks of flats in this city and it has no name in it anywhere. Every one of those six buildings has decided by breakfast that it is about them."

He finished the fourth floor before he did anything else, because forty men would be in on it by eight and a practice is not a thing you break on a morning. By the time he had washed his hands the want had settled into a shape he did not like. He wanted the name of the person who paid for it, and he wanted it the way a man wants a name he has no right to.

Bellhouse Row runs behind the yard tram's last stop and the room is over a haberdasher's that shut in the spring. The front of it is eight feet of brick with a card in a frame and a lamp burning on the card through the middle of the day, and the card said LAST HOUSE EIGHT and underneath, in the same painted hand, IN REPERTORY.

He stood under it for a while. There was already a queue at six for a house that would not sing that verse for another two hours.

It had been sung four nights running. The card carried no date and the box office took commissions at twenty pence, and he had both of those facts from Lode by noon.

Inside, the afternoon house was eleven people in nine hundred seats and he took a seat at the back of it and let it happen to him.

It took four seconds. She came out and the room came with her, not noisily, the way a room does when everybody in it turns a fraction at once. Two women who had not spoken on the Row were sitting with their knees turned the same way.

Nothing in the building argued with anything and nothing had been said that could be argued with. Then she sang the verse. It had a tune every person in that room already had, a nursery count with the numbers swapped, the chorus went round twice, and it was beautiful and false in nine places out of ten.

*There is a house at the top of the stair*
*where the man wrote six and the rooms say seven there,*
*he swore it in the morning with the sun on the stair,*
*and every floor of it is full and he has not written one of them.*

He had written six. He had never sworn it anywhere, and had withdrawn it on the twentieth at half past four in a room with a form number on it, and the verse had said *swore it in the morning* and the room had agreed.

It went through him and cost nothing, the way heat comes off a stove without your touching it. Five years of earning things he could not hand to anybody, and this went through a wall of brick and a street into nine hundred people in about the length of a bar line.

Beatrix Halm had stood in the Square the previous evening with her notebook shut under her arm and told him, in those exact terms, not to stand under it.

He had walked into it anyway. He had bought a seat at the back with twenty pence.

The house emptied by twenty past four and the front of the building went back to its brick. He was on the Row when the stage door came off its latch, and the woman who came out of it was about forty, in a coat open over a work dress, with the tiredness of a person who has been making a room agree with her since two o'clock.

"Isolde Marchetti," she said. "You are the man from the block that fits six ways, and you have been in my house twice this afternoon, and I want that said first. If you start with a question I am going to answer the question and not the man."

"I heard the verse."

"You heard the verse." She put her hands in her coat pockets and leaned on the brick. "Then you know what it is for. I do not sing it because I think it. I sing it because it is in the card and I am paid by the house and not by the man who wrote it."

"How is it paid."

"By the house. Twenty pence a verse to the box office, and the box office keeps no names, because a room that keeps names for verses is a room that stops having verses in it. That is not a scandal. It is a trade. If you want the name you have come to the wrong kind of building and there is no other kind."

He had known that at the door and had come anyway.

"Then tell me the date it was brought in."

"The eighteenth of this month," she said. "A man in a good coat, at about two in the afternoon, at the office end of the bar. He read it out to me from the floor and would not give me a name and would not give me a street.

"He said two things. Say it exactly. And do not change the number." She took one hand out of her pocket. "That is what frightened me and I have carried it about for three days and I want you to have it, because you are the first person I have wanted to hand it to.

"A man who buys a verse wants it changed. He wants it turned up or turned down or turned so it reads better on somebody else. That man did not want it changed. He wanted the six and the seven exactly where they were, on a tune everybody has had since they were children, in a street that fits six blocks."

"He wanted it to be unarguable," he said.

"He wanted it to be a thing you can nod at." The Row was filling up behind her. "And it is. It has been in the card four nights and it will be in the card until it is taken down, and the card has no date on it, and the only person in this city who can take a verse down is the woman who sang it."

"You could take it down tonight."

"I could go up that stair and take the card down and sing nothing for forty minutes, and they would go home, and it would be in the card again by the end of the week, because a standing commission is only stopped by a standing refusal." She said it evenly. "Then I would be a woman who took a commission down, and the Bellhouse lives with that about once and not twice."

"Then what do you do about it."

"I have booked myself for the second house on the twenty-third and I am going to sing something else." She looked up at him. "That is the only lever anybody has ever had over me and I have had it for twenty years and never pulled it once, and I am pulling it in two days because a man in a good coat has paid twenty pence to make a street agree with a lie about you,

"and because I do not intend to be the instrument that does it twice."

"You will not tell me what it is."

"I will not, and if I tell you then you will have an afternoon to think about it, and then you will come up here and tell me it is too dangerous for the household, and then I will go up that stair that night and sing the version I have had since the eighteenth, which is the safe one, which is the one that costs you and does not cost me.

"I am not going to be managed by a man being frightened for me. I am going to be managed by the room, and the room is the only thing in this city I have never once been able to lie to."

He had eight women in a building on Ferrier Street and one of them could still hear a crowd, and every one of them would have gone up those stairs inside a week if he had asked. He worked out, standing on a wet Row, that he could have taken the card down in about four minutes, and that the reason he was not going to was not the household.

It was that he was the man in the verse. There was one room left in this city that did not have him in it.

"All right," he said.

"Go home and sleep," Isolde Marchetti said. "You have not slept since the twentieth and I am not saying that kindly. Two days."

She went back in by the stage door and the latch went off, and he stood there while the queue came along the Row past him to a house that was going to make nine hundred people agree with a false thing about him for the fifth night running.

Then he went to the door and bought a ticket and went in and sat at the back and let it happen again.

That was the room. He had one left that no office could serve a form on and no verse could reach, and he had given it away in Bellhouse Row for twenty pence, and there is no instrument in this city for taking a plate off a form and there is no instrument at all for taking a thing back once you have sat down and let it be about you.

The house let him out at half past nine and she was not in the front, and he had not expected her to be.

At the side of the Bellhouse there was a stair of nine steps with the paint worn off the treads in a stripe in the middle, and a door at the top with a disconnected bell. She opened it before he knocked, and the room behind it was the smallest and hottest room in the building.

A cot against the wall with a blanket thrown on it and not folded. A deal table with a jug, a basin and a hairbrush. A rail with two things hanging on it out of forty. A fire door at the back with a bar across it, painted so many times it had stopped being a bar. A window on the back of the Row and the wet awning three feet below it.

On the wall opposite the cot, a mirror with eleven bulbs round it, and eight of the eleven were on.

She took her coat off, hung it on the rail with her dress, and stood in the middle of the floor in a shift and a pair of house shoes.

"Right," she said. "Do the thing."

"Which thing."

"Listen." She put two fingers flat on her sternum. "Every room I have stood in since I was nineteen. Rooms with two people in them. Rooms with nobody in them. This one. Whatever I have in me goes out and the room takes it and agrees, and it is not a thing I do, it is a thing that happens to me, and the only part I control is the words. So watch."

She said it into the mirror, flatly, in the voice she had used four times that afternoon.

*There is a house at the top of the stair where the man wrote six and the rooms say seven there.*

Eight bulbs, a basin, a cot, a bar of painted iron, and a woman facing a glass.

**Nothing happened.**

No change in the room's pressure, no settling. There was a hot small room with a mirror in it and a woman saying a line, and the line was a line and nothing in the air took it up.

She said it again, louder, and the rain on the awning took it.

Then she stood quite still with her hand on the frame of the cot, and he saw it happen to her face, which was not fear.

"How long," he said.

"Twenty years is the honest answer and I do not know it in years." She turned round, and her eyes were bright and her voice had gone thin. "It has never not happened. I stopped testing it at twenty-four. Nine rooms, an empty church, my own kitchen at four in the morning. It always happened, and I made my living out of it, and I have never once been in a room where it did not.

"And it is not doing it now and I do not know what to do with my hands."

"It will not do it here."

"It will not do it here because I am not doing it," she said, and stopped, and worked it out in front of him with her hand still on the cot. "No. That is not it. If I sang that in this room tonight a hundred people would nod at it in an hour.

"I decided something about eleven seconds ago while you were working out what it meant." She let go of the cot. "This room is mine for four seconds and I am not spending it on being able to make rooms agree with me. You walked in off a wet street and asked me for a date, and a woman who asks for a date is a woman doing something on purpose, and there is not one of those in a room with me anywhere in this city."

She crossed the floor and took the front of his coat in both hands.

"I am going to be glad," she said, "and I am saying that before it happens.

"I am forty. I have had nine hundred people agree with me four times this week and I have not been touched by anybody in a way I chose since I was nineteen. I am not going to let this be a thing that happens to me as well."

"Nobody is making it happen to you," he said.

"No. That is the entire reason."

She took his coat off him and dropped it on the cot and put her hands flat against his chest and pushed him back half a step, and looked up at him along her own arms to be sure he was taller than she had guessed, and kissed him.

It was not like being sung to. There was no pressure in it and no room leaning in. It was one woman on a stair deciding to do a thing with her mouth, and her mouth was warm and unhurried and expert.

After about nine seconds she took it off him and said, "Right, you have been on your feet since five, sit down," and pushed him onto the cot and got a knee on the edge of it and stopped with both hands on his shoulders and considered him.

"How long have you," she said.

"Four years," he said, "and I am going nowhere and I have nowhere to be at ten to ten on the twenty-third."

"Then I am not going to hurry and I am not going to be short with you either. If you want me to be one of those, say so now." She said it looking straight at him and not modestly. "I have not been with anybody in two years because I could not find the thing where I chose it, and I have just found it, and it is standing on my floor with wet shoulders."

"I want all of it," he said.

"Good." She pulled her shift over her head and let it drop, and she was pale and strong and soft in the middle, with a hard line across one shoulder from a strap she had carried for thirty years. "Then come here and let me have you properly, and I am going to talk to you the whole way, because that is also how I do it, and because there is no one else in this room to be overheard by."

He stood and took his shirt off and she took the rest of her clothes off and got him flat on the cot in a room eight feet by seven with eight bulbs lit out of eleven.

She went down onto him slowly, set her hands on his chest and rode up and down twice with no hurry in it at all, getting the length of him, and looked down at his face with her hair forward on both sides of it.

"There," she said. "Nothing happened. Eleven bulbs and a bar of iron and a woman telling you what she is doing, and the room has not taken one thing off either of us."

She said his name once, deliberately, so that he would know it was a name and not something a room said, and then she stopped talking for a while.

He got his hands into her hair and she pushed up onto her elbows and told him to do that again, and he did, and she swore at him happily about it and told him exactly what she wanted and then told him she had wanted it longer than that and had not said so to anybody.

The cot took it. The bar on the fire door had been painted so often that the wall it was set into had a small bow in it, and the rain came harder on three feet of awning and neither of them heard it.

She came with her mouth open and her hand flat on his sternum, the way he had watched her put two fingers there before she sang. It went on a long time. He stayed buried and let it, twice told to.

Then she pushed him over onto his back, lay down along him, and took her own time with her chin on his chest and listened to him.

"That was thirty years," she said at last. "You know what that is. I have been making rooms agree with me since I was nineteen and tonight the only person in this room who agreed with me was me."

"That was you," he said. "Not the room."

"That was me." She lifted her head. "Say it again, and I will believe you the second time as much as the first."

He said it again.

She washed at the basin in a hard splash of cold water and came back with her hair wet down her back and sat on the edge of the cot with her feet on the floor and her back to the mirror.

"Now the part that costs," she said, "and I am going to be honest with you, because you asked me for a date on a wet street and that is worth being honest back.

"You could have taken the card down tonight. Four minutes. You are a head of a household with eight women in it and one of them hears crowds. You have had it in your head since I said it and you are not going to, and I have thought about nothing else for three days."

"Because I am the man in it," he said. "There was one room left in this city that did not have me in it, and I gave it away tonight for twenty pence, and I would have given it away for a glass of water, and I am never going to be able to tell anybody in my house that I did it for their sake."

She nodded once, and it was not a small nod.

"So on the twenty-third I sing in front of nine hundred people, and what I sing is mine and not yours, and you are not coming, and you will not be told what it is before or after."

"I will come."

"You will not." She said it without doubt. "The second you walk into that house it is about you, and it stops being mine inside four seconds, and I have had it for four seconds and I want the whole of it.

"You will be somewhere else in this city on the night of the twenty-third and wait for it to come out the way you waited for a mark to go into a book. That is your trade and I am not criticising it."

He took her at her word, because she was right, and because a woman telling you what you are like is worth more than a woman telling you what she would prefer.

The bell on the door was dead and the Row below was emptying. She walked him to the top of the stair and did not come down with him.

"Twenty pence," she said. "That is what it costs. Three days with that number and it is the only thing in the whole of it that has made me angry. I sing to nine hundred people and they give me a shilling. Somebody paid twenty pence at a bar on the eighteenth of this month to take a man's name off a man and put a street on him, and twenty pence is cheap enough to look like an accident.

"It will not have been an accident, because the cheapest things in this city are the ones somebody thought about on the way."

She shut the door on him and he went down nine steps into the wet.

The card in the frame on the front of the Bellhouse had a lamp on it and said LAST HOUSE EIGHT and underneath said IN REPERTORY, and it did not say who had bought it. He stood under it long enough that a man coming out of the queue asked him whether he was all right, and he said yes, and meant the opposite.

And in two days she was going to stand in front of nine hundred people and sing something she had chosen, and he was not going to be there, and there is not one instrument in this city that would tell him what it was going to be.

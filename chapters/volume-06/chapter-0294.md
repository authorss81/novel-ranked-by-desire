# A Day It Is Held

She came off the second shift at seven in the evening on the twelfth of September and found him on the bottom of the sixty-one steps, which is where she found him on the thirteenth of August, and which had become a thing about thirty days old.

"Sit down," Beatrix Halm said. "I have been on since six this morning and I can hear the yard from here and I want the yard."

He took the step she uses for people she wants talking upwards at her, which is the second from the bottom, and stayed on it.

"Show me the book."

She gave him the notebook without a word. She has carried it for nine years and she has never once put it down in front of a man, and she put it down on the step and opened it flat on her knees.

Four lines.

"The twenty-seventh of July," she said. "The twenty-fourth of June. The twelfth of August. The second of August." She turned her good ear toward the street. "Those are four, and they are the only four, and every one of them is a thing that happened to somebody else."

"They are the days things happened."

"They are the days things happened to somebody else." She closed the notebook. "I asked you for a day. I said it on the thirteenth of August and you agreed to it in four seconds and then you did not do anything about it for a month, and I want to say that I have noticed and I want to say it now, before you hand me the thing."

He did not have anything in his hands.

He had the whole of the twenty-ninth of August worked out on the top step of a tram and he had it because he could not help himself, and the woman who had asked him for a date had said *and you have not worked out what you will bring instead* and had been right.

"The first of October," he said.

Beatrix Halm put the notebook on the step between them and did not open it.

"Finish it."

"The guild posted its hold on your hearing on the twenty-seventh of July. A clerk came to your gate on the twelfth of August at four in the morning and asked you what your hearing would take, and you said not your figure, and he wrote down not your figure, and he told you the ordinary way is the quarter."

"The ordinary way is the turn."

"The turn is **the first of October**."

He said it out loud on a step in a street, which he had not intended to do and which turned out to be the only way to say a date like that.

"A guild house picks that kind of answer up when the quarter turns, and the quarter turns on the first, and on the first of October the guild will name a price on that hearing."

"And it will not be a figure out of my house, because I agreed to you in four seconds and I have kept it for a month and I am not going to break it in the last week."

She wrote.

"How many days."

"Nineteen," he said. "From the twelfth of September to the first of October, counted the way Runa counts them, which is elapsed. On the nineteenth it is twelve. On the twenty-fourth it is seven. On the twenty-ninth it is two."

She wrote that under it as well, three figures in a column, and then she turned back three pages and started again further up, which he watched her do.

"Seven of June," she said, writing. "Lodged at the Exchange under the second form. What was it secured at."

"The figure of the head. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight."

"That is a plate."

"That is a plate."

"Twenty of July. Called."

"Called at a valuation and answered by a production, and the production was a figure of persons answerable at my premises on the day, and I swore it myself on the first of August, and it is spent."

"Eight of August."

"From the eighth of August it has been doing nothing at all. It is not behind the next quarter. It is not behind anything."

"Thirtieth of August."

"From the thirtieth it stopped being a hold and became a price. That was your sentence and you said it on the steps on the thirteenth of August and it has been in a kitchen every day since and not one person has done anything about it."

"And the first of October."

"The day it will be taken up."

She wrote all seven and then sat with her hand flat on the open page.

"That is a calendar," she said. "Nine years of a notebook and every line in it has been a thing that happened, and I have just written down the shape of a fortnight that has not happened yet."

"Yes."

"Say the rest of that sentence, because you have not finished it."

"They are all yours now," he said. "From tonight, every date in that book came out of my mouth. If I am wrong about one of them, you are carrying a date a man gave you, and there is not one person in this city who could prove I did not know what I was doing."

Beatrix Halm closed the notebook and put it in her coat and then took it out again and looked at it.

"Good," she said.

"That is the cost."

"That is the cost and I am paying it." She turned her head about thirty degrees and put her good ear flat against the noise of the street for a moment and then brought it back. "Do you know what the first one cost?"

"Tell me."

"Nine years," she said. "Nine years I have had a notebook and eleven burned months in a hearing and not one date out of them. And it cost eleven minutes to get it and four dates."

"Then why did you wait."

"Because I asked you for it." She shut the book properly and squared it. "I asked you on the thirteenth of August, in those words, and you said yes in four seconds, and I have spent a month being furious about four seconds. That is my own arithmetic and it is not fair and I do not want it corrected."

She looked up the hill and then looked back at him.

"You agreed to bring me a price with somebody else's name on it. Say it again so I can hear whether it has gone thin in a month."

"You said bring me a price with somebody else's name on it, or bring me nothing."

"I did."

"You have nothing."

"I have seven dates," said Beatrix Halm, "and none of them came out of your household, and that is the first thing anybody has put in my hand since March."

The yard tram came in and went out.

"And the condition stands," she said. "If you come down here on the first with a figure of persons out of your own door I will put it in front of you and take it back and we will both know what you are."

"I will not."

"I believe you and I have believed you since the thirteenth of August and believing you has cost me a month." She got up off the step and brushed the back of her coat. "Now sit there for a minute, because there is one more thing and I have been putting it off since the ninth."

"A clerk came on the ninth."

"A clerk came on the ninth and he was pleasant and he asked me the same question in different words, which is what a man does when the house has not taken the first answer up." She stood in the street with her notebook against her chest. "And then he said something new, and he said it as a helpful man and not as a threatening one, which is how you know it was designed."

"Go on."

"He said that on the turn the guild has to name a price, and a price has to have a holder and a day and a security, and that the guild is not going to name a security out of a woman's account."

"The security is a production of persons at my premises."

"Yes." Beatrix Halm turned round on the step. "That is what he said, and he said it slowly, and he said it twice, and then he wished me a good evening."

The tram came through and the bell went and neither of them heard the end of it.

"So they have gone and found it themselves," he said.

"They have gone and found it themselves, and they have got a certificate with a bar on it, and a bar on a certificate is still a figure." She was not shouting and she did not need to. "And they did not have to ask me a single question to get it, and they are going to put it on the turn at eight in the morning, and I am going to be standing at a counter with a notebook and nothing in my hand."

"You will not be at a counter."

"I will be at a counter, because that is what the turn is, and I will not be at a counter at eight in the morning." She shifted the notebook. "And when they name it, the number they name will be the number of persons keeping an address at thirty-four six one one eighteen on that morning, and it will be your number, and I will have to look at it and not take it."

He sat down on that step and did not answer for a while.

"Say it," said Beatrix Halm.

"Seven."

"Seven." She had her head turned about thirty degrees again and the good ear flat against the street. "Then tell me the rest of it, because you have been holding something behind your teeth since I said the word security, and I have heard you hold it twice tonight."

He put his hands on the step.

"That figure has to be sworn on the morning by a person answerable at that address, and I have given my word that I will not be inside that building between seven and nine, and a woman on a step has promised to knock twice and leave the paper under my own door unread."

"Then somebody else swears it."

"Somebody else swears it, or the answer is not made at all, and then the mark stands at the figure of the head, and the whole of the difference is charged to a woman who has been carrying an account since before you were in this city."

Beatrix Halm took the notebook out again and opened it and wrote one line at the bottom of the seventh of June, and then she turned the book round on the step so that he could read it upside down.

*1 x. a.m. — the figure of persons at 34-6-118 on the morning — sworn by — ?*

"That is in my book now," she said, "in my own hand, under your dates, and you are going to tell me that question mark by the nineteenth or I am going to write your name in it myself and you will never be able to look at it again without thinking about me."

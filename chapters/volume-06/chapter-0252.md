# The Charge Against The Schedule

The twelfth was the day the broker's standing ran out and he had spent the morning watching a door for something that was not coming.

He got up at five and down the sixty-one steps at twenty past and worked the washrooms while the city came up around the building. The boiler was quiet. Plate 8,138, floor 8,141, tolerance three. Nothing spent, nothing scored, no classification, and a hundred and eighteen lines in a ledger with nine of them turned.

He told himself he was not waiting. He was checking the stair.

At the table before eight Beatrix put her notebook down and turned it round without shutting it first, which she did when she wanted him to look.

"Today is the twelfth," she said.

"It is."

"Is something supposed to come today."

"Nothing is coming today."

"Then why were you at the landing rail twice before you sat down," Beatrix said. "You looked at the door eleven times between the bread and the tea and I count things because that is what I am for."

"I said no to something on the third of July," he said, "and I would like very much to see it arrive anyway, and that is not a thing I am proud of and I am telling you because you asked and because I have not lied to you since the fourteenth of June except once and that lie was on a page and cost me more than this will."

Beatrix wrote something in her small level hand and shut the book on it.

"Then the twelfth is an ordinary day with nothing on it," she said, "and you will want your tea, and I am not going to be the one who asks you what the something was, because you will tell me when it is cheaper for you."

"That is fair."

"It is also a thing you have now done four times since the seventh of June." She put her gloves in her pocket. "I keep the count. Do not make me ask."

Runa left for Cordwain at ten to seven and he walked her to the bottom of the steps because it was the stair and because he had made it a place. She did not stop this time. She said good morning like a woman counting, and went up the market with her folder shut, and he came back up sixty-one steps with his hands empty.

Nineteen days. Wren Kallo's counter-forensics finding had sat on Hadewig Mers's desk for nineteen days with four edits in it and every one of them a removal, and the division had said it would answer in its own time. Six days. The true report had sat in audit intake in Ines Duarte's own hand for six days with the date under it and an entry going up beside it naming her twice.

He worked the second landing after noon and did the whole of it twice.

At half past four a boy from the Vestry post came up the sixty-one steps with a flat envelope with a form number on the corner of it, and the number was one of audit intake's, and it went round the table before anybody had taken off its coat.

He opened it at the table.

It was a reconciliation charge. Audit intake had reconciled the premises return of the twelfth of June against the certified schedule of premises 34-6-118 and had found that the return and the schedule did not agree, and a reconciliation that does not agree is charged to the head of the schedule, and the head of the schedule was him.

The amount was in the corner in a hand he did not know. It was not a large sum. It was the first figure anybody in this city had ever sent him for anything that had happened inside his own house.

It had to be answered inside fourteen days. Fourteen days from the twelfth was the twenty-sixth of July, and if it was not answered by then the schedule went to a review.

Sun read it standing up over his shoulder.

"A review of the schedule," she said. "That re-runs the figure."

"Yes."

"And it will run it at six," Sun said, "because six is what is on the certificate, and the certificate is what the review reads, and that building reads a correction when a correction arrives in the right form and not one minute before."

He turned the sheet over and read the back of it as well.

"Six is the index being wrong," he said. "Seven is the index being right."

"Seven is four divisions away and a blank line on the third of June," Sun said. "You have to want the wrong thing to be wrong, and wanting it does not put it in a column. That is the whole of my gift and it is worth nothing on a landing."

She went back to her coat and checked a stitch with her thumb.

He left the charge on the table and did not pay it, and told the room why, which was that a paid reconciliation is an answered reconciliation, and an answered reconciliation is a schedule that agrees with itself, and the schedule says six, and the index says seven, and the index is four divisions nearer the truth than the paper is.

Runa did not look up from her pencil. Beatrix shut her book. Marguerite sat with her gloves folded and her pen capped and said nothing at all, which from Marguerite was an opinion with a hand on it.

Petra came round the table and read the envelope upside down without touching it, the way an officer reads a thing she has no standing to touch.

"A charge is not a demand for money," she said. "A demand for money can be refused and the refusal is the end of it. A charge is a question the office has asked itself, and it goes up, and it comes back with a signature on it, and the only thing you can do with it is answer it or let the schedule be read again in front of people who have never met you."

"What does it come to," Sun said.

"He does not know," Beatrix said. "That is the second time somebody in this kitchen has asked for a figure and got a silence, and there were three of us here in April who could answer anything."

The sentence went round the table and found the one chair it belonged to empty, and not one of them said where the woman in it was, because all of them knew and two of them had promised not to say it out loud.

"So there is a number on that paper," he said, "and the only person in this city who can tell me whether it is right has two rooms above a laundry and a standing instruction that came with them, which is that I read her nothing in there. I have kept it for three days. Tonight is the first night it has cost me something I could have bought with money."

Marguerite uncapped her pen, which she had not done all evening, and then capped it again.

"There is another book in this city," she said. "Mine is a broker's. A broker prices what is put in front of her. What is put in front of her this evening is a reconciliation charge against a schedule she stands behind. If she prices that she is quoting on her own security, and a broker who quotes on her own security is not a broker. She is a party."

"And the other book."

"The other book is not hers," Marguerite said, "and I have not been in it for eleven years, and the woman who keeps it has never met one of you, and if you go through that door you will get a number and you will lose the only person in this city who answers for what happens to you."

She slid the envelope into the drawer under the bread, on top of the receipt.

At seven a second boy came up, from the Exchange this time, and he was a guild boy with the black cord on his sleeve, and he handed over a folded slip and was gone down the stairs without waiting for anything.

Marguerite's hand. Four words and a date under them and no signature beyond the initial.

*The account runs to the hundred and thirty-fourth mark on the first of August. Bring the plate.*

He read it twice.

Then he read it a third time with the thing he had been waiting for set beside it, and the thing he had been waiting for did not arrive and was not going to, and the four words in Marguerite's hand were the proof, because on or before the twelfth of July she had been permitted to put one more thing in front of him and not one word of it.

It was gone.

Not refused. Refused was on the third, in a queue, in daylight, in his own name. This was the other thing, the one that arrived without anybody deciding it. The standing had been spent, and the day had been spent with it, and no office in the city would send it again, and the woman who could have sent it was not permitted to for as long as the account stood, which was thirty years from the year the Veil broke and nineteen of them already paid.

Beatrix read it over his shoulder in the kitchen and did not ask.

"August the first," she said. "That is the docket that carries your plate and my hearing on it, and she is telling you to bring the plate eleven days early, and she has never once in twenty years sent a word that was not a form."

"She has sent one word tonight that is not a form." He put the slip down square on the bare table. "It was not one on or before the twelfth. That was the whole of it. It is gone and I cannot buy it and I cannot ask her and I will not know until the examination in about four years whether I was right to let it die."

The room did not say anything to that, and the rightness of it was not going to be settled in a kitchen, and he had said it out loud anyway because it was the sixth of July's second wound and there was a form number on it now.

Agnete came out of the back room with her cup and stood at the end of the table with the door open behind her.

"The eleventh of July a woman gave you something on a stair," she said. "Tonight the twelfth went out of the world on a slip of paper from the Exchange with four words on it. Two days. Both of them voluntary and both of them yours. Eleven years I did not choose a single thing and I could tell you to the hour what each one cost."

She drank and went back and left the door open.

He went up at eleven and stood on the landing with the plate in his hand because Marguerite had said bring it and he did not know where else to put a thing he had been told to carry.

The rectangle was cold in the middle. The nail held nothing.

Below him the kitchen light went off room by room. The charge lay in the drawer under the bread on top of a receipt signed in his own hand, with a printed line under the signature for an object or a remark, and it had no remark on it.

He had left that line blank in front of the whole household in June because a district officer had left a blank line on a return in May, and now an office in a different building had printed the same shape for him to fill or leave, and he had left it again.

The fourteenth day counted from the sixth of July was fourteen days off, and there was one person in Ostara who could have answered that charge correctly, and he had promised her a landing and no paper, and the twelfth had gone out of the world without a word.

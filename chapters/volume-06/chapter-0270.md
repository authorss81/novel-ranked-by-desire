# Eight Thousand One Hundred And Forty-Two

A quarterly posting does not wait for nine o'clock, and they were on the ranks wall at first light with a different crew, a different shade and a card that came down the day before.

The last working day of a quarter is the last working day of a month, and a month is thirty days and has no thirty-first, and the office that keeps the bands has a card that says so on it, and it came down on the wall at six in the morning on the thirtieth of July by four men in a blue that had never been on that square before.

The foreman read the wall before he painted it, which is the rule of the trade and has been since before anybody alive was on it, and he did not hurry over any part of it, and about two hundred people stood in the Square in a blue morning and listened to a number go past.

The floors go up every quarter. The plates do not.

On the bottom band, six inches from the kerb, the entry for that address read:

34-6-118. Plate 8,138. Band floor 8,142. Tolerance three. Out of band.

Three had been within. 8,141 was the floor that went up on the first of July and it had said *within* all month in a shade any person could read from a tram step, and this morning the number in front of the address was one higher and the word had changed.

He stood about thirty feet off with his hands in his pockets while a man on a ladder read his address and his plate and his floor and the word out of that out loud to a square in July.

A woman of forty came up beside him with a cup of tea and looked at the wall and said, "That is not one of the shops."

"No."

"You all right?" She said it the way people say it to a stranger they have decided to be kind to, and then she went back to her cart.

He had been three under a floor since he came to this city and done nothing about it for four years and one month, and on the thirteenth of July a woman two floors under a sales floor had told him exactly what that was, in the correct order, on four figures and a card.

She had said the day you are four under, the standing you put in on the seventh of June is called at a valuation.

Seventeen days. She had told him it would take four quarters and it had taken seventeen days from the day she told him.

And the standing had been called already, on the twentieth of this month, at twenty-five minutes past four in the afternoon, by a device cut into a pen-holder, and there was a page coming on the Thursday with a broker's name on it, and the last Thursday in July was the twenty-seventh and the next one is the third of August.

A valuation is a mark at ten to nine on the first of the month, made with a pen on a chain round a woman's wrist in the chained real book at the Exchange. A called standing is answered at a valuation.

Tomorrow morning.

Not at some valuation in the autumn. Not at the annual sitting. Tomorrow morning, at ten to nine, at the bottom of sixty-one steps with a bundle and a woman who has been on no roll for fifty-six days, and in about eleven seconds a hundred and thirty-fourth mark goes into a chained book and after it the standing he lodged on the seventh of June is answered out of the same account by the same hand.

She had said that on the thirteenth of July and it had taken her four sentences and he had written four figures on a slip and taken the slip home and never once joined the two things together.

Beatrix Halm came up off the yard tram at twenty past eight and read the entry from about forty feet with her good ear turned, and did not come closer, and did not say anything for about a minute.

"Out of band," she said.

"Yes."

"That is a printed thing," Beatrix said. "That is not a thing somebody did to you. That is a floor that went up a number and a plate that stayed where it has been for five years and it is the first time in your life that a wall in a public square has said a true sentence about you, and there is not one office in this city that can take it off, because the office that owns that number is the office that put it there."

Sun Meiling was next to her with a bag of bread she had bought at six and had not been able to sell because everybody in the market was looking at the same card.

"Is that bad," Sun Meiling said. "Actually. I want it in that order. Is it bad, and then what does it do."

"It is bad. Every security standing against the account comes off it the day it goes on the dead list, and the dead list is the fourth of August, and an account goes on the dead list when the run breaks, and the run breaks when a docket reaches its date without a mark in it."

"So if the mark goes in tomorrow at ten to nine, we are not on the dead list on the fourth."

"No. If the mark goes in, the account lives and the guild's hold on my hearing stays exactly where it is."

"Then it is not bad," Sun Meiling said. "It is a wall."

Sun Meiling looked up at the number for a while with the bread bag in her hands.

"It was bad at six o'clock this morning," she said, "and it is not bad now, and I am going to have to be honest with myself about that and I am not going to do it on a wall in a Square at half past eight."

Petra Ilyin came down at nine and stood at the foot of the steps with her hands behind her back and her eyes on the word.

"Fifty-six," she said, to the wall.

He turned to her.

"Fifty-six days on no roll," Petra Ilyin said, "and this morning a man read out my address on a wall in front of two hundred people and none of them knew it was the address of the woman on no roll, and I have been standing at the bottom of these steps since the fourth of June looking at this street and this is the first week I have been in it as somebody who is written about."

Agnete Lindqvist was in the Square, in daylight, for the second time in eleven years, standing about twelve feet off the wall with her hands in a cardigan pocket, and she had walked down sixty-one steps that morning in front of people.

"Eleven years indoors," she said, when he got to her. "Seven days ago I sat in a house and sang the answer into it and nothing happened at all. This morning I have been outside in a Square for fifty minutes and my hands are shaking and I am not going back in yet, and I would like to stand here a little longer, and you are not to walk me home."

He went and found Beatrix at the north end.

"I need you to listen to a date and not to anything else," he said.

"Go on."

"On the first of August, at ten to nine, a valuation is carried out, and that is the mark, and the hundred and thirty-fourth mark answers a called standing. I knew about the mark and I knew about the standing and I have not put them in the same sentence since the thirteenth of July, and this morning a floor went up a number and there is no more room under it than there was, and tomorrow at ten to nine both of those things happen with one pen."

Beatrix Halm shut her notebook.

"Then tomorrow morning you stand at the bottom of those steps," she said, "because Petra will be at the bottom of those steps at ten to nine with a bundle and she has asked you three times this month and you have not once said yes."

"Yes."

"Say it now, then, out loud, on this corner, so that it is a thing you said in a place."

He said it, on the corner, at about twenty past nine, in the Square, with a wall behind him in a new blue.

Then the post came.

It came at twenty to eleven on the thirtieth, folded in three, and it was not a band card and it was not a return and it had a division's office on it and a heading he had not seen before, and it had been addressed to the head of premises 34-6-118 and delivered to Ferrier Street because that is the only address any division has for that building.

It said that the composition of the household at that address was recorded as being out of band as from the thirtieth of July, and that a re-issue of the schedule had been delivered to the auditor of record on the twenty-sixth of July and acknowledged on the first of August, and that the division would hear the composition on the **second of August at ten in the morning**.

It said that the former auditor of record was required to attend, and that her attendance was required because a schedule could not be read further until the impediment standing against her certificate was answered in person.

It was four lines and a date and an hour and it was the first piece of paper that had come to that address since the sixth of July with a woman's name in it that had not been put there by anybody in the building.

The second of August at ten. Two days from now.

She had not written it and had not asked for it.

She was not going to be in that room on the second because somebody in a building two miles away had read a number off a wall and put her attendance down on a form. She would walk in on her own terms or she would not walk in at all, and he would not be able to affect either.

The term she had given him in the dark on the ninth of July held, and had held for twenty-one days, and was still the most expensive thing he owned.

He stood in the kitchen with the post in his hand and the table bare for the seventeenth day and the saucer in the middle of it.

Nadia Ferrous came off the night side at half past six that morning, before he had been down, and had left the cloth bag on the kitchen table with eleven pages in it.

Ten of them were written on, in her own hand, with the night and the hour against each, and they ran from the nineteenth of this month to the twenty-eighth, one a night, without a gap.

The eleventh was blank from the top of the page to the bottom. She had said on the night before that she was not going to skip a day and go round to the next one, and she had not skipped a day. She had gone into the book with the twenty-ninth of July sitting there empty and done nothing to it, and that empty page was the record, and it was going into four divisions' worth of reading in about three days whether she wrote on it or not.

The thirtieth said a floor had gone up a number and a wall had said his address out loud in front of two hundred people, and it said that he had taken nobody's advice and warned not one of them and had gone down sixty-one steps in daylight in front of the women in that building, and it said that, and it stopped there, and there was nothing in it she had not seen.

Runa Sato's paper was good for the last day of the month and this was the last day of the month, and at midnight it stopped being a hearing and became a date somebody once mentioned, and she had done that to herself at a counter with a woman watching, at ten to three, on the twenty-fifth.

The instrument on the north desk had died of its own week on the twenty-ninth. The band was out. A broker's page was coming on the Thursday, which is the third of August, because the last Thursday in this month was the twenty-seventh and there is no other one.

**Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight**, on a plate, on a wall, in a blue this square had not had before, against a floor of eight thousand one hundred and forty-two, in a morning that had taken four years and one month to arrive and arrived in about nine seconds on a ladder.

At ten to nine tomorrow morning he would be at the bottom of sixty-one steps with eight women in that building and a bundle on the stair, and a woman would take a pen off a chain and write the hundred and thirty-fourth mark, and then answer the standing he had put in on the seventh of June out of the same account with the same hand in about eleven seconds.

Four divisions would have a copy of both by the third of August.

# The One He Named First

He had one day left to say what he owed before somebody else said it for him, and the first name on the list was the one he had written in his own hand.

The twelfth was tomorrow. On or before the twelfth the broker of record could put one thing in front of the head of a household that was hers to give, and after it she was not permitted to put it in front of him again in the life of the account. He had said no on the third, in a queue, with two officers waiting behind him and the pen chained to the desk.

So the window was the same width as the debts. Five of them stood, and six women were standing at the end of five of them, and the sixth thing on his list was himself.

He went up the washrooms one floor at a time. The boiler on the fourth chased at nine, knocked once, and settled. Plate 8,138 against a floor of 8,141 and a tolerance of three. A hundred and eighteen lines in the ledger and nine of them turned and the ninth still himself. Nothing had been spent in five years, and the last four days had not changed a figure on any page in this city.

Lode came up the back stair with a bucket at half past nine and said the landing had been talking since breakfast.

"About what."

"About tomorrow." Lode set the bucket down. "Two divisions hold you and neither one has said anything and the twelfth is tomorrow. I am not asking. I am telling you that I have listened to it going round and I would rather it went round from you."

She went down again with the bucket. He carried his own down after her.

Runa left Ferrier Street at ten to seven for the Field division in Cordwain, which opened at eight and closed its counter to anything but returns at half past three. He walked her down the sixty-one steps because there was something he had to say to her on the way and not at the table.

At the bottom he stopped, and she stopped, and the market came up wet around them.

"I am going to tell you a thing on this stair and not at the table," he said. "Listen to why first. At the table a thing becomes a record, and Nadia writes it down, and from then on it is in this house for good."

"Say the thing," Runa said.

"The six on the sworn page is mine. I wrote it on the fourteenth of June in my own hand on a table in this building, and the index holds seven, and the difference between those two figures is the reason your answer went into a fourth office instead of a hearing you could win."

The market went on past them. A tram came up through the wet and the water came off its roof in one long sheet.

"I have carried it twenty-seven days," Runa said. "I put a man's figure into a fourth office with my own name under it in my own hand and I did not know it was a lie until you said the word just now on a step."

"Yes."

"Twenty-seven days is six of June to the eleventh of July. I have been counting that since about the twenty-second." She did not raise her voice. "Who else knows?"

"Marguerite. Sun, because she was at the table. Beatrix, in her own hand, that I had given you a document to answer for and would not tell her what was on it. Ines, who read it standing up in about four seconds and asked me one question and has not said another word about it in this house."

"Four." Runa put her hands in her coat pockets. "You want it to be five."

"I want it said before somebody else says it. That is the whole of what I am doing this month."

She took her hands out again and looked at him, and the anger came up in her face and stayed there and did not go anywhere.

"What do you want me to do with it."

"Nothing." He said it flatly because a flat thing is the only kind that can be trusted. "That is the favour I am asking and it is the first favour I have ever asked anybody for in this house. I am not asking you to file it. If you file it the certified figure dies, and the household prices at five, and I will not spend you for that."

"That is not a favour." Runa's voice did not rise. "That is a debt you have handed me and told me I am allowed to keep. You have given the only person in this city who can act on it the truth and asked her to sit down with it."

"Yes."

She took her folder out from under her arm and opened it on the step, and took out a small sheet of grey carbon with a printed head and no number on it. A Field division reconciliation slip has two halves and a thin leaf between them and neither half is a document until the other half exists.

She wrote on the top half with a pencil she had not put down since June. The schedule's figure. The index's figure. The date of the sworn page. And under those four lines one sentence in her own hand, which was that the figure on the schedule is disputed by the head of the household at the foot of premises 34-6-118 stairs on the eleventh of July.

She dated it. She tore it along the perforation. She kept the top half and put the bottom half into his hand.

"Neither of us can produce that alone," she said. "It is worth nothing to a division until the two halves are together, and the two halves are never together unless we both stand in the same room and both want it to be. It is the only kind of paper in this city I can make that a person cannot take off you."

He looked at it. Four lines and a sentence in pencil on grey carbon, in the only hand in Ostara that had any business disputing his figure.

"I did not ask you for that."

"You did not ask me for anything," Runa said. "That is what I am telling you and you have not heard it. Put it in your coat. Do not put it on that table. And do not ask me again, because the second time I will give you an answer you cannot un-put-in-your-coat."

She went off up the market toward the tram with her folder shut and her pencil in her hand and her boots striking square, and he stood at the bottom of the sixty-one steps with half a reconciliation slip in his pocket that no division in Ostara could produce without the woman who had made it.

At the house the three papers lay in the drawer under the bread with the receipt on top and the certificate under it and the valuation copy at the bottom, and the broker's account lay square in the middle of the kitchen table where it had lain since the nineteenth of June, and five lines stood in his own hand on the sheet Marguerite had given him on the eighth.

He did not read them. He put them back with the three.

Agnete was in the back doorway with the door open behind her and her cup in both hands.

"You went down the stair with Runa Sato at ten to seven and stopped at the bottom," she said.

"You were at the window."

"I was at the window." She drank. "One true sentence was given on the first of this month in a pale room and it sat nowhere. Whatever she has taken off you this morning is not that, because nothing compelled either of you and nothing was written in a building. But it is on a stair in the rain and it is now two people instead of one, and that is the whole of what my discipline is and it is not mine."

She went through to the back room and left the door standing open.

Petra came down at eight with the bundle squared under her arm, thirty-seven days on no roll and counting, and took the top of the sixty-one steps with her hands behind her back and did not go down them.

"You took something off that stair," Petra said. "It was not paper, because paper is larger. I have been an officer for eleven years and I know the weight of what a person is holding when they come back up."

"Yes."

"Then you will tell me before the first of August, and not after," she said. "On the first there is a mark to go in at nine and I will be standing where the steps meet the market with the bundle, and I will not hold it for a man who has gone round my back twice in a month."

She went down. He heard her count them without moving her lips.

He worked the second landing and the third after lunch and came home at six to a kitchen that ate standing up round a table with a broker's account still squared on one end of it, because there were seven women on the cut roll and six chairs, and the seventh chair had been a decision taken in April that the house was not going to revisit.

Beatrix read the day out of her notebook in her small level hand and shut it. Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. A stair at the bottom of sixty-one steps where two people had stood still in the rain for four minutes.

"That is a thing I cannot read off the weather," Beatrix said. "What was said."

"Something I owed her."

"Then it goes in the ordinary record or it does not go anywhere," she said. "There is no third place, and the ordinary record is mine."

He went up at half past seven with the valuation copy rolled in his hand.

Lantern Row ran two floors above a laundry and the stair smelled of soap and heat and the door at the top stood shut. He had agreed in the dark on the ninth of July to read nothing to her in her own rooms, and he had meant it, and paper was the only thing he had ever held a house together with.

He stood at the bottom of her stairs with it rolled in his fist and put his other hand on the rail.

The rail was hot. Above him a key turned, and a floorboard took weight, and the door at the top did not open.

He put the copy inside his coat and went back down and home, and the pricer of every debt he owed was a floor above a laundry in eight feet of rooms she had paid for out of what they would allow her to answer, and tomorrow was the twelfth.

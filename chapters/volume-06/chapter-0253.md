# The Keeper Of The Real Books

An unpriced security cannot be carried a year running, and the assessment side would not carry one, and the first of August was nineteen days off, so the debts had to have figures on them before then or the account went on the dead list on the fourth and took every security standing against it with it.

He had one pricer and she had set a term. So he went to the other book.

The Exchange took stranger's questions on Thursdays, when the weekly return came up, and the counting room was two floors under the sales floor and had no window and a stone floor that had never once been warm. Four books lay open on a table with the spines unwritten. The room smelled of ink and cold paper.

The woman at the table was forty-two and had her sleeves turned back and was not Marguerite Vale and had never met him.

"You have brought five lines in pencil and a form number off an envelope," Solveig Rask said, before he had got the door shut. "You have written them badly, and you have written them in the order you feel them in rather than the order they were made. Sit down. We will do it the other way and it will take an hour and it will be worth the hour."

"Solveig Rask."

"You have had that name off a woman who resents me for four years," she said. "I keep the real books. Not the guild's ledger, which is a beautiful lie and earns everybody in it a living. Mine are the movements. Everything that actually went somewhere is in mine, once, dated, reconciled. Ask me for a figure on what a thing is worth and I will not have it. Ask me what it did and I will have that, and it is nearly the same question and it is not."

He put the five lines on the table and she turned them round and read them in her own order, which was the examination, the open security, the thirty-year account, the certified figure, the counted clinic.

"The examination," she said. "Bought on the eighteenth of May off a broker's own entitlement, which is the only thing a broker can spend, entered once a year against a standing, open-ended. It has a movement, because an entitlement came out of a woman's account on a day in May.

It costs nothing to carry and it can never be sold and it cannot be answered by anyone but the holder. That is not a price. That is a chain with a person at each end of it, and in about four years the person at the far end is me."

"And the open security."

"That one I can price, because you moved it on the seventh of June and I saw it go." She turned to a second book without finding a page. "You lodged a standing against a broker's account in a chained book into a public quarterly return. You cannot withdraw it. The holder of the book may call it at any valuation.

"So it is not worth its face. It is worth the whole of the standing every week for as long as it stands, and your standing is a plate at 8,138 against a floor of 8,141 with a tolerance of three."

He had not expected the number. He had expected a shape.

"You are three grains under the floor of the band you hold," Rask said. "The floor moves against you every quarter, because the floors go up and the plates do not. On an ordinary quarter you go further under.

"The day you are four under, the standing you put in on the seventh of June is called at a valuation, and the account takes the loss. The mark that answers it is not mine to enter. It is the broker's. It is a pen on a chain round a woman's wrist, and she will enter it, because it is her job."

"So it is not a figure," he said. "It is a week I have not fallen."

"It is a week you have not fallen. That is the only arithmetic in this room that will ever frighten you, and not one person in your house can do it, because it needs four quarters of your plate returns and you have never kept a copy of one."

"And the thirty-year account."

"Live, secured, marked monthly, described as six names in your broker's hand. It has a movement every month at ten to nine from a woman on the stairs, which is the cheapest thing in this city and the only line I have ever entered that costs nothing and settles something."

She wrote one figure on a slip and turned it round. "That is what the account costs to carry for a year. It is not large. It is the whole of your risk and it is written on the other side."

"And the certified figure."

That one she would not touch. She put two fingers flat on the line and looked at him over them.

"A dispute is not a movement," she said. "It is the absence of one. Six men can swear a figure and none of them moves anything and the real books have nothing to write about, and you have a sworn page that says six and an index that says seven and no transaction between them of any kind, because two pieces of paper disagreeing is not a trade."

"That is the truest of the five."

"It is the one I cannot put a figure on and it is the one that will cost you the most." Rask said it without relish and without sympathy, the way a person states the position of a column. "I am not withholding it because it suits me.

"A thing that has never been entered is a thing that has not happened. Until a transaction happens I have no book to read it out of. Make something move and it will price in a week. Until then it is the only debt you have that is free, and free is not the same as small."

"And the clinic."

"Counted from the first of July, hours against a return, monthly, at a rate." She wrote a second figure. "That is your clinic, priced, and it is small, and it is the only one of your five that goes up. Hours go up when a certificate puts a household over a clinic, and your clinic is the best-attended one on that stair and it was not counted before the first of this month, so somebody put it there."

He looked at the two figures on the slips and at the one line she had refused and at the three she had given in words.

"That is four of the five," he said. "You have not given me the thing that matters."

"I have given you four of five and a refusal, and the refusal is the honest part of my morning," Solveig Rask said. "Now sit back, because you have been asking me for figures and you have not once asked me what I price, and a woman who values four things without asking what they stand behind is not doing arithmetic. She is shopping."

He sat back.

"Your plate goes onto a socket in a branch office once a week," she said, "and there is a set of movements behind that plate that I have been able to see the shape of for four years and have never been able to see the content of, and yours is the thirty-first entry in a column of thirty-one over nine years, and under that column there was one page with one figure on it, dated and initialled, and I left it in on purpose."

She turned the chair so she was facing the wall.

"That is not a reading I have shown your broker and I am not going to. It is the only thing I have ever wanted and it has nothing to do with the guild."

She turned back. "I will not price it this month and I will not price it this year. I do not price a thing I cannot describe.

"When I do price it there will be a date on the page and a figure under it and you will not be able to refuse it, because a dated figure in a real book is not an offer. It is a fact. Offers expire. Facts do not."

"And when will that be?"

"That depends on you and not on me," she said, "and I will say one more thing and then we will finish the hour.

"In about four years the examination passes to me, because the broker holding the book is replaced at the annual sitting. On the day it passes I am the person answerable for six women in a building I have never been inside. So the four hundred and thirty days I have been giving this account are not mine. They are the guild's. And in four years they will be mine."

She squared the four slips into a stack and pushed them across.

"Your debts cost what I have written and one of them is free, and the free one is the only one that will hurt you, and you have come to the wrong woman for it," she said. "I did not say the right woman was easy."

She wrote a sixth slip, which was the smallest of the five, and squared it on the stack.

"That one is the hour," she said. "A stranger's question taken on a return day is entered against the account it concerns, because that is what a question in this room is. It is a movement. It is a very small one and it is dated and it is in the book and it reconciles."

"That will cost the account."

"It will cost the account eleven minutes of a keeper's time on the return day of the week, and it will do it whether you pay it or not, because the book does not take refusals." She pushed the sixth slip across on top of the others. "You came here to find out what a debt costs. The first thing your debt did this morning was cost you the price of asking."

He read the stack. Five lines, one of them dated the fifteenth of June without being entered, and one of them the hour he had just spent, and neither of those two had been there when he walked in.

He came up out of the counting room at half past eleven with two figures on a slip and one sentence he could not put down anywhere, and the assessment side that had valued a security by what it could be made to yield had said the same thing as the assessment side on the north desk in the same words, and the only difference between the two women was which book the sentence was written in.

The Square was in front of the building and the Thursday pass was still on the ranks wall. They had been at it since nine, the figures go up at ten, and they had not reached the middle of the wall when he came out at half past eleven, so that from the foot of the ladder there was a stretch of bare tile with nothing on it at all.

Then a pot came along and a figure went back on, in the shade, out of a numbered hand, and he stood at the foot of the ladder and understood that in about four years the woman who decided which figure went on would have his name at the top of the page as well as the bottom, and that she had had it at the bottom for four years already, and that neither of those is a thing a man can refuse.

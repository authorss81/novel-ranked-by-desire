# The Woman Who Asked To Be Counted

The painters came at six and Beatrix Halm was at the north end of the ranks wall at about a quarter to six with her notebook clamped under one arm and her good ear turned into the square she has been reading for nine years.

She watched two of them carry a board up the ladder and set it against the stone in front of her.

She read it before they had the brush out.

"Eight thousand one hundred and forty-three," she said. "Thirty-third day. And the word is the word."

The second man looked down at her and then went back to his ladder, because it is a public square and a woman reading a wall is not an event.

That is the only mercy this city has ever offered anybody.

He got there at ten past six. He was four under at six o'clock and five under at six o'clock, and there was not one thing anybody could do about it.

The word on the wall had been **out of band** since the thirtieth of July and still was and always would be, because a number is a number and a word is a word.

The broker's statement came down with the ordinary post at about half past seven.

Four lines on a sheet of the Vant's paper, her signature at the foot of it, and a number in the third line that was not his, and that was eleven.

He read it standing at the wicket in his coat with the yard going behind him.

Four lines on the sheet. She had read them about forty times since seven in the morning.

The first line said that she did not cut the device on the pen she was holding.

The third line said that eleven entries in her own working book over four years were made with a pen-holder she did not cut, and that she could not tell them what any of the eleven were.

Eleven. Four years. A woman with four pen-holders who cut every one of them herself in her first month, and had carried one of them to a chained book without ever once looking at the shoulder of it.

And on the same page, in the same four lines, a woman who did not name her had told a room that a man who is not in the room had put five documents into four divisions in seventeen days.

He had asked her on the twenty-seventh not to come to her rooms.

He had gone and stood at the bottom of the street anyway.

"They are both true and only one of them is about you," Runa Sato said, at the kitchen table at about nine, reading it upside down over his arm.

"That is the first thing I have worked out from that page and I worked it out in about nine seconds and I have not written it down."

"And I am not going to, because I have got about four days of counting left in me before the first and I am saving all of it."

Sun Meiling put her hand flat on the table.

"He has read it," she said.

"He has read it."

"Then say the other thing in this room," Sun Meiling said, "because it is the eighth and I have had the whole day and I have not spent one hour of it on being calm."

"And I am going to be perfectly calm at about eleven o'clock tonight, and I would like everybody to be out of this room by ten."

There were six of them at that table.

"I am going to ask you for something tonight," she said, "and it is not on the seventh line and it is not about the bar."

"And it is not a thing I am going to be grateful to you for afterwards, and I want all six of you to hear that from me now rather than work it out."

"I am not going to spend the last night of being a pencil row being sorry."

She took her hand off the table.

"Go out," she said. "All of you. Not the building. This room."

They went.

## The Last Night Of A Pencil Row

She came up at about eleven and she did not knock and she had her coat over her arm and she shut the door with her heel.

"Nineteen years," Sun Meiling said. "I want to say the number out loud in this room and then I do not want to hear it again."

"For nineteen years I have been a line inside somebody else's working. I was in a laboratory for nine of them and I was the reason a man scored six and went out of public record."

"And there has not been one day since that anybody put me in a room where the count was mine."

She put the coat over the back of the chair and did not sit down.

"Tonight the count is mine. Not the certificate's. Mine."

"I asked you on the fifteenth of July in front of this house to be counted, and you said yes, and it turned out that yes cost a woman an office."

"And I have spent a fortnight being ungrateful about the bar instead of being in it, and that ends at midnight."

"Sun—"

"You are going to tell me about tomorrow," Sun Meiling said. "In about four minutes you are going to start, and I have thought about it for two days and I know what you are going to say and I have already answered it."

"So let me get it out of the way now, so that the rest of the night is ours and not a conversation about paper."

She came and put her hand on the front of his shirt.

"I have written out what I want the ground of the certification to say. I did it on the twenty-eighth, after you had gone to the broker's."

"And it is four lines and it is good and it is true, and I am going to read you two sentences of it and then I am going to forbid you to use any of it."

"Go on."

"The seventh line stands blank. The seventh person holds an address at these premises and does not sleep at them and has asked that her name not be written."

She took her hand away.

"Do not write that," she said. "Not one word of it, not on the form, not in the margin, not underneath your signature."

"If it goes in, then a woman asked not to be named becomes a thing that happened in a division, and it is in there for good."

"And the next person who reads that certificate learns that there is a name in this house that a person was frightened of."

"And I did not ask to be frightened of in a division. I asked in a kitchen, in front of six women, and I want it to stay in a kitchen where it is mine and I am the only one who has to hold it."

"Then what do you want me to write."

"The truth, short," Sun Meiling said. "The seventh line stands blank. That is the whole of it."

"It stands blank and it is a bar, and you certify it anyway and you put your name under it."

"I do not want to be protected in writing. I want to be counted in writing, and there is a difference, and I am the only person in this room who can tell you where it is."

She pulled his shirt out of his belt.

"Now sit down. You have been standing up since nine o'clock explaining a certificate to six women, and I have been standing here for four minutes waiting to stop hearing about it."

"And I have not been touched by anybody for eleven days and I have thought about almost nothing else since the sixth."

He sat where she pointed.

"Take that off," she said. "And do not be gentle, because I have spent a fortnight being handled carefully by six people who were frightened of hurting me."

"I am not fragile, and I am not brave today either. I am ordinary, and I have asked for this, and if you make a ceremony of it I will get up and go to bed."

"And you will not get a second night out of me."

He took it off.

"Say my name," Sun Meiling said.

"Sun."

"Again, later, and I will tell you to stop."

She had her hands in his hair before he had finished it, and she was not fast and she was not a performance and she did not close her eyes once for a long time.

At some point she made him stop and made him look at her.

"Nine seconds," she said. "You wrote seven on that line in nine seconds on the first of August and you have been sick about it since."

"I want you to know that I know exactly how long it takes to do a thing like that, and I am not going to make you do anything at all tonight."

"Everything that happens tonight is mine. You are not the instrument and you are not the bill."

"And nobody in this building is going to be kind to you about it afterwards, because there is nothing here to be kind about."

"It is the thirtieth and it is two days before the first of September, and I want you in this room with me because I am about to be ink."

It went on a long time and she did not shut it down and she did not let it take her anywhere else.

The room at the top of the building was dark and hot and neither of them looked at a clock.

Afterwards she lay with her forehead against his collarbone for about a minute and a half and said nothing at all, which is the thing she is best at.

"There is one more thing," she said, "and it is not a favour and you are not going to argue with me about it."

"Go on."

"Your hand." She reached up and put his palm flat against the muscle at the base of her throat, where she had been carrying something since about eleven o'clock in the morning.

"Keep it there. And do not ask me whether I want it and do not ask me what it costs me, because I know what it costs and I have known since March."

"And the last thing I want in this room is a man being careful with me."

He kept it there.

She felt it arrive, and she made a short sound she had not planned and put her hand over his wrist.

Then she was warm all the way through in a way she had not been since about the third of August, and something that had been sitting behind her sternum since eleven o'clock that morning went out of her without asking.

She lay very still for about four seconds.

"There it is," Sun Meiling said. "That is what it feels like when somebody finishes a thing for you."

"And I have been owed this since eleven o'clock this morning, because I have been sitting in a room being calm about a certificate since eight."

"And nobody in this house was going to give it to me tonight, because they were all thinking about tomorrow."

She lifted her head.

"Do not ever do that to me to make me grateful to you," she said. "I have a bar on a certificate tomorrow and I am not going to be grateful to you about it."

"And if you spend the next ten days being sorry at me in a kitchen I will leave, and I have said that out loud now so that it does not have to happen between us later."

She got up and put her shirt on and did not put anything else on and stood in the middle of a dark room with her arms at her sides.

"Two days," she said. "And at about ten o'clock in the morning I am going to stand at a counter and a man is going to read my name off a list of seven."

"And there will be a bar on the page under my own address, and there will be a certificate, and my name will be in ink for the rest of my life."

"And I asked for it," Sun Meiling said, "in this room, in front of you, on the fifth, and he did not do it for me, and I am not going to be ungrateful about that either."

"I want both of those in the same sentence and I have wanted them in the same sentence for eight days."

She put her hand on the door.

"You certify it tomorrow," she said. "Whatever ground you write, you write it yourself, and you do not ask me and you do not ask the woman on the tram."

"And if you write one word in it about anybody asking for anything I will know, and you will have spent a morning's work for nothing, and I will not be able to forgive you for it."

"And I have told you so in advance so that you cannot say you did not know."

She went down the stair and did not close the door behind her, and he sat on the edge of a bed in a hot room with a cold hand and a warm room around it.

At about half past ten the yard tram came in and went out and he worked out, sitting there, that a certificate issued in the morning would be in four divisions inside a week.

And that a statement signed by a broker would already be in three of them, and that Nadia Ferrous's page had been in all four since the third.

And that a composition return read at seven on the second of August was in one of them and would be in the rest inside a week.

Four documents.

On the third of August a woman two floors under a sales floor had told him that the books in this city came in threes, that none of the three could see the others, and that in the last week of August every division in the city would read one of them.

And she had been exact about the books.

He had a certificate to certify at ten in the morning, and it would be the fourth of them, and it would carry his own name at the foot of it.

And there was not one person in this city who would ever be able to tell which of the four was the man.

# A Landlord's Man And A Public Sheet

Two thousand six hundred and ninety-five was the twenty-sixth of July and the third of August is day two thousand seven hundred and two, and two thousand seven hundred and two less two thousand six hundred and ninety-five is seven.

July runs to its thirtieth and there is no thirty-first. Twenty-sixth to thirtieth is four, the first of August is one, and the third is two more, and four and one and two is seven.

## A Room And Nine Pounds

The case that particular hot still August morning was all about one very small damp rented room off a street that runs down to the laundry, and about nine pounds for work done in it in the ninth month of last year.

The man who does that work had a piece of pencil paper in his own hand with the bottom two inches filled, and the woman who lets the room had engaged a man to speak for her who goes round about six sets of stairs with a rent book and has done it for about nine years without ever being thanked.

Lin Hao was not inside the room at all. He stood out in the passage behind the magistrate’s counter with his hands in his pockets, because a man in a white plate with no figure cut into it is not a party to anything at a counter, and the clerk knew it, and the clerk had been right about every part of it in about four seconds in May.

The landlord’s man put a printed sheet face up on the counter, squared it with two fingers, and put his finger on the bottom four inches of it, where four sentences stand above a name and two inches of nothing.

He had kept it folded inside his rent book since the afternoon the thirtieth sheet went out, interleaved between June collections so the creases would hold, and smoothed flat each evening out of habit, when the thirtieth sheet went out at four, and he knew the bottom of it by heart, because a man who goes round stairs learns the papers people hold against him the way a broker learns books.

“This office itself says none of these papers is a deed,” he said. “That is the third line of the second column, entered in September and put on the page at four on the thirtieth of September, and it has been read out at a rail in a passage off Cordwain every morning for nine years. My point is that the office itself says it, and the point is correct, and the paper this man holds is one of those papers.”

The clerk read where it lay, which is what a counter is for, and did not improve one word of it.

## The Point Is Correct

The man who does the work stood at the window with his piece of pencil paper in his hand and said nothing, because there was nothing in that room that belonged to him except waiting, and the woman who lets the room was not there either.

A person put out of a room by a public sheet is never in the room when it is done, and the clerk would not have let her speak if she had been, because his one question had gone at eleven about a rail and there was not another coming.

Lin Hao listened from the passage with the folded sheet in his coat against his ribs, and both halves of it were still true, and one of them was that a correct point had just put a man out of a room.

The landlord’s man was right. The office’s own receipt says none of the pieces of paper received is a deed, and it says it in four words with a man’s name under it, and the receipt has been on a rail in Cordwain since the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, and about nine people a week read it to the bottom.

And the man with the pencil paper was right too, in the way about four hundred people in kitchens are right, because he had done the work in the ninth month of last year and made the copy at a rail on the fourth of February and carried both in his own hand, and no finding about paper undoes a week of work.

The clerk wrote it in the day book in about nine seconds, in his own hand, as a note beside a claim and not as a ruling, because a ruling must come from somebody entitled to give one and he would not devise another matter that same morning.

He wrote that the office’s receipt was put in front of him in a case about a room, and that the point made with it is correct, and that the paper the man holds is one of those papers, and he put the day against all three, because a counter that does not write the day writes nothing.

“The receipt stands,” he said. “This office entered it and this office keeps it, and it says what it says. Whether what happened to you happened is not a thing this counter decides on paper, and a copy gives you when it was copied and only a person can give you when it was done.”

The landlord’s man nodded slowly, as though he had long expected nothing else at all, and did not press it, because he has spent nine years going up stairs nobody has thanked him for and has never once been inside this building, and he knows what a counter can do and what it cannot, and he had put the point correctly and left it there.

*Two thousand seven hundred and two days. Less two thousand six hundred and ninety-five, the twenty-sixth of July of the eighth year, is seven, and the office’s own receipt was put in front of a magistrate in a case about a room and the point made with it was correct.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and twenty-three days, which is four months and three days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up for every one of the last thirty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and three days, which is ten months and three days after them.*

## The Person It Is About Is Not There

He walked out slowly after them both into the passage and stood about nine feet off the rail while the man who runs the desk under the window read the bottom of the page out to three strangers, in the ordinary way, without being asked, and stopped at the striking, and the landlord’s man listened the whole way through although he had just used it in a case, because a man who uses a page listens to it read out to make sure it still says what he used.

“You used it correctly,” said Lin Hao, quietly, in the passage, because it needed saying and there was nobody else to say it to, and the landlord’s man is right about every fact and is not anybody’s enemy and every one of them in that lane off Cordwain knows it.

“I used what is on the page,” said the landlord’s man. “I go round stairs with a rent book and I have done it for about nine years, and I have never once thanked a counter and I am not going to start. Your receipt says none of them is a deed. That is true. It is the truest thing any building in this city has ever published about people who do work in rooms.”

He went out calmly with the rent book tucked under his arm and the man who does the work went the other way with his piece of pencil paper folded along the crease he had made in it, and neither of them once looked back, because a passage off Cordwain is a place people pass through on their way to somewhere else.

Lin Hao went up four flights at about half past twelve and told Perla Quist in the order it had happened, with the nine sheets squared between them, and she listened without writing, because an ordering at a counter about a room is not a day she keeps, although the day it was said is.

He recounted the room and the nine pounds with the rent book and the printed sheet squared by two fingers, and the clerk’s words and the day book and the passage afterwards, because she keeps days and a day without its particulars is not a day she can hold.

“So the receipt comes back,” she said, when he had finished.

“It comes back,” said Lin Hao. “What I entered in September and put on the page at four on the thirtieth of September is now read back at people by men entitled to read it, in cases about rooms, and the point is correct every time, and the person it is about is never in the room.”

She put the pencil down across the bench and looked at him for about four seconds, and he saw that she was angry, not at him and not at the landlord’s man, but at the shape of a city where the truest thing any building has published is that nothing happened that can be shown, and where a man who is right about every fact can put a person out of a room with it.

He went back down the four flights with the rent book going out of the lane ahead of him and did the floors of three washrooms and the stairwell and the bin room, and went on thinking about a room off a stair off a lane that he has never been inside of, and about the woman who was not in it. A paper saying that none of these is a deed can empty a house without a single person in the room ever knowing that it is being emptied.

The office set out to be the one place a claim can be found, and then to enter every paper with the person who brought it, and now the receipt it entered is carried by hand into cases about rooms by men who are right to carry it.

About four hundred people keep writing in the bottom two inches of their own paper, and they are right to do it, and none of them can ever prove to anybody that they are right, and that is all any one of those pieces of paper holds.

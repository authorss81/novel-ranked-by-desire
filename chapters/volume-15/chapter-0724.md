# The Seventy-Third Form

Two thousand six hundred and sixty-nine was the thirtieth of June and the seventh of July is day two thousand six hundred and seventy-six, and two thousand six hundred and seventy-six less two thousand six hundred and sixty-nine is seven.

June runs to its thirtieth and there is no thirty-first. The thirtieth to the thirtieth is nothing, the first of July is one, and the seventh is six more, and one and six is seven.

## Paste And A Board By A Kerb

The seventy-second came off at about half past six in the morning with paste still wet along its top, and the boy climbed to the new joint with the brush and halted at its foot, as he halts every morning, because paint will not take where a mason has dressed the face.

The seventy-third went over it at about seven. A month’s form on office paper with two ruled squares at its foot, and nothing in either, and both to stand empty at four that afternoon and at four on every afternoon after for as long as the boy will carry a jar up that hill.

He squared it with the side of his hand and stepped back to look at it, and there were about nine people on the hill below him going up and down, and none of them stopped, because a board by a kerb is a thing people pass on their way to somewhere else.

He had put boards up by that kerb for seven years and he had not been wrong about anything in them, and in the seventh year he had said once, for about nine seconds, what the two squares at the foot of a form are for, and had not said it since, and nobody had asked him anything in all of that time, including the man in a white plate who stands four feet off and does not ask.

The jar pulled at his arm and the brush had worn to bare wood where his thumb grips it, day after month after year, and he set both down square by the kerb while the paste dried, the way he does every month, because a board that slips in its first hour slips for the whole month and he has never let one slip.

A woman with a short-handled broom came past on the lower slope with her head bent to the work, sweeping the four inches clean the way she does every morning, and she did not look up at the board and did not look at him, and he did not speak to her, because she has never once said she has seen a person put anything on those inches and nobody has ever asked her.

## Somebody Who Is Not Lin Hao

She came up the hill at about half past seven with two cans of hot water on a yoke, going to the washrooms at the top, and she put the yoke down by the kerb to rest her shoulders and looked at the new form the way people look at a thing that has just gone up.

“You put that up,” she said.

“Yes,” said the boy, to the board.

“Every month.”

“Yes.”

She shifted the yoke and looked down the hill at the render and the four inches of dressed stone at the foot of nine feet of new render, where about nine people a week go down low enough to read.

“My brother says there are two words down there nobody will say out loud,” she said. “And my brother says you have got them in your coat.”

The boy did not turn round. He put the brush in the jar and wiped his hands on his apron, and his shoulders came up once, the way a person’s shoulders come up when they have been asked the one thing they did not want asked.

“Have you,” she said. “Got them in your coat.”

That was the question. It was the first time in seven years anybody had asked him anything at that corner, and it was the one question nobody in this city wants answered.

The only copy of those two words is a rubbing taken with chalk on the back of a month’s form on the twenty-second of December, and it went into the inside of his coat because a rail is a place a thing is read once and a corner is a place a thing is said, and about nine people have been in a room where it was read out.

He did not answer. He picked the jar up and picked the old form up over his shoulder and started down the hill with the paste brush in his hand, and she called after him once and then let him go, because a boy going down a hill with a jar is not a thing anybody stops.

*Two thousand six hundred and seventy-six days. Less two thousand six hundred and sixty-nine, the thirtieth of June of the eighth year, is seven, and the seventy-third form went up on a board by a kerb with two ruled squares at the foot of it and nothing whatever in either.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is ninety-seven days, which is three months and seven days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up on about two thousand counters for every one of the last seven of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is two hundred and seventy-seven days, which is nine months and seven days after them, and the third line of a second column went onto a page at four that afternoon.*

## What He Carries

Lin Hao heard it at about eleven in a passage off Cordwain, from the counterman, who had it from a man who carries coal up the hill, who had it from the woman with the yoke, and it had taken about four hours to come down the hill, the way everything from that corner comes down.

“A woman asked him what he has in his coat,” said the counterman. “She sells hot water at the bottom of the hill and she is not an officer and not a clerk and not anybody with a form, and she asked him straight out, and he picked up his jar and went down the hill.”

Lin Hao stood about nine feet back from the rail with his hands in his pockets and felt something cold go through him, because he had stood four feet off that boy on the seventh of April and had not asked him anything, and had not come up the hill to ask him anything, and was not going to, and now somebody else had asked, and the asking could not be taken back.

“Did anybody else hear.”

“The coal man heard,” said the counterman. “And two others on the hill. By tonight it is in four kitchens. By the end of the month it is in forty. There is nothing in this city that stops a person saying a boy was asked about a coat.”

He went up four flights at about noon and told Perla Quist with the nine sheets out between them, in the order it had happened, and she listened without writing anything down, which she does not do unless the thing being told has nowhere to go.

He told her about the yoke and the cans and the hour, and about the coal man and the two others, and about the way the boy had picked up his jar and gone down the hill without turning round, because she keeps a column of days and a day without its hour is not a day she can hold.

“She is not an officer and she is not a clerk,” he said. “She sells hot water at the bottom of the hill. She has no form and no right and no reason beyond a brother who talks in a kitchen, and she asked him straight out.”

“And he has carried that rubbing since the twenty-second of December,” said Perla Quist, “and about nine people have been in a room where it was read out, and one of them cannot read, and he has never once taken it out of his coat at that corner, and now a woman with a yoke has asked him what is in it.”

“He did not answer,” she said, when he had finished.

“No.”

“Good,” said Perla Quist. “A thing that is not said out loud is the only thing in this city that cannot be copied, and he knows it, and a man in a rank-one coat has a piece of paper in the inside of his coat that four hundred people could read and has not read it to anybody, and the boy knows that too.”

She squared the ruler against the edge of the bench although she had not taken the pencil up.

“If he answers, then the rubbing is a thing said at a corner, and a thing said at a corner goes round a passage in four minutes, and in about four months a woman who cannot read is asked what a heading is and says what a man at a rail said, correctly, and it is wrong. If he does not answer, then the claim about two words stays unanswerable, and unanswerable is the only thing keeping about four hundred kitchens writing what they believe.”

He nodded and went back down the four flights with nothing in his hands, and the seventy-third form stood on a board by a kerb with two empty squares at the foot of it, and a boy with a paste brush had been asked the one question nobody wants answered, and had not answered it yet at all even once.

He scrubbed the floors that afternoon with the unanswered question circling alongside the bucket work, counting the stairs as he went to keep his hands steady, and rehearsing nothing to say in case anyone stopped him.

At about half past five he stood in the bin room with a bucket in his hand and the washroom door open behind him and understood that the asking was the change, not any answer, because for seven years nobody had asked and now somebody had, and a corner where a boy can be asked is a different corner from one where he cannot.

The day was the seventh of July and the seventh is not the eighth, and no paper in this city turns on what happens three months from now, and there was still time, although he did not know what time was for.

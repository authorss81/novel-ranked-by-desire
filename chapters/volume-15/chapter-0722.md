# Two Words Nobody Has Written Down

Two thousand six hundred and fifty-six was the seventeenth of June and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand six hundred and sixty-three, and two thousand six hundred and sixty-three less two thousand six hundred and fifty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## What Wenna Raut Will Not Enter

The pencil was still there on the morning of the twenty-fourth, under the striking, in the two inches of nothing, and the counterman had put the ruler back across the bottom edge every night for seven days and had read the four sentences out past it every morning without once reading the pencil itself.

Lin Hao came into the room off a third court at about eleven with rain on his coat and put his hands in his pockets and told Wenna Raut the whole of it in the order it had happened, because she may only enter what she is given and he wanted her to have it exactly.

She listened without writing anything down, which she does not do unless the thing being told has nowhere to go.

“Say what you want,” she said, when he had finished.

“I want to answer it.”

“You cannot,” said Wenna Raut. “There is no form in this city on which the office may ask a person what they have in a coat, and the only copy of those two words in this city is a rubbing in the inside of a boy’s coat, and about nine people have been in a room where it was read out, and one of them cannot read.”

She squared the book against the edge of the table although she had not opened it.

“You have a claim in pencil about two words nobody has written down. The office holds a page with four sentences at the foot of it and three lines in a second column and a name under the striking, and none of those is either of the two words, and no paper in this room says they are.”

He had known all of it before he came up the stair and hearing it in her mouth made it worse, because she is the one person in this city who enters exactly what she is given and cannot improve one word of it, and what she had just said was that there was nothing to give her.

“What if I bring the boy here.”

“You will not,” said Wenna Raut. “You do not ask him. Nobody has asked him anything in seven years and you are not going to be the first, and if you were, then a boy with a paste brush would be a person this office had asked about a thing somebody else had written down, and this office has never in nine years done that.”

He stood at the end of the table for a while after that and looked at the standing sheet face up under the window, with the head unmoved at the top of it and about four hundred lines under the head with a date and an hour against every one and no figure and no name, and the second column with three lines in it and nothing going into it until the quarter turned.

“What would you enter if I gave you the rubbing.”

“I would enter that a rubbing was received, with a date against it, and with your words for the person who brought it,” said Wenna Raut. “And the person who brought it would be you, and the words would be yours, and the rubbing would be a thing you had carried in your own coat since December, and every person who reads that row would know where it came from, and then about forty people would say the office had kept a stone.”

He laughed, once, without any pleasure in it, because she had said the whole of the cost in about eleven seconds and had not improved one word of it.

“And the boy.”

“The boy has never been through that door and there is no row for him,” she said. “A row may be added to and may not be taken from, and I am not going to be the clerk who puts a boy into a book in a building with no door onto a street.”

## The Coat

He had the folded sheet in the inside of his own coat all through that morning and it sat against his ribs the way it had sat since December, and he thought about taking it out and putting it on the table face up, and did not.

The sheet in his coat was a docket with a date on it that was older than the yard it came out of, and a woman off a street two streets wide had read the date out loud twice in April and neither of them had written it down, and the answer had been a fact about a building and not about a man.

It had closed a thread and it had not touched the words, and now the words were the whole of what was open, and the docket was no use against them because a docket says a thing left here and went somewhere and does not say what was cut into it at a bench.

He went down to the two cardboard boxes under the window at about half past eleven and put two fingers on the lid of the top one and did not lift it, because the drawer instruction of the twenty-first of October still stood and the new instruction of the tenth of June did not reach backwards into those boxes and he had written it that way on purpose.

Four hundred and nine pieces of rag paper in there, entered as nothing, and not one line in any book describing any of them, and from the tenth of June every new piece through that front door went in with a date and with words the person used, and the first row under it said off the third court, the one with the stair at the back, which was not an address and was not going to be corrected.

None of that reached a rubbing in the inside of a boy’s coat, because a boy’s coat has never been through that front door and never will be, and there is no paper anywhere that asks a person to open a coat.

*Two thousand six hundred and sixty-three days. Less two thousand six hundred and fifty-six, the seventeenth of June of the eighth year, is seven, and the pencil stayed under the striking for every one of them and the counterman read past it every morning.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is eighty-four days, which is two months and twenty-four days after them, and eleven papers came through a front door in eight days of them and then the door went quiet.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is two hundred and sixty-four days, which is eight months and twenty-four days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## The Hour After Four

He went down to the passage at about half past four and stood about nine feet back while the counterman read the bottom of the page out to three strangers, in order, without being asked, and paid nothing for it, and stopped at the striking the way he has always stopped.

One of the three bent at the knee afterwards and read the pencil and said nothing for about two seconds and then said, “So the office knew.”

The counterman said, “The office reads what is on the page.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have got,” said the counterman, “and I have had it for nine years and it has never once been thanked.”

Lin Hao watched the man go out with the other two and understood that the claim was unanswerable in a way that had nothing to do with whether it was true, because to answer it he would have to do one of two things and both were closed.

He would have to say what the two words were, and the only copy was a rubbing taken with chalk on the back of a month’s form on the twenty-second of December, and it had gone into the inside of a boy’s coat because a rail is a place a thing is read once and a corner is a place a thing is said, and he had not said those words to anybody in about four years.

Or he would have to climb to look at the stone, having been forbidden before witnesses last year from returning while it lasts, an agreement spoken aloud before any paper changed hands, which he has honoured for about a year.

He wanted, for about nine seconds on the way back up off Cordwain, to take the hill in the dark and read the four inches himself and put an end to it, whatever an end would mean, and then he put it aside because a man in a white plate seen at a wall in the night is an answer to a claim about a building keeping a stone.

He stopped at the laundry on the way and found Perla Quist at the bench with the nine sheets squared and the ruler across them, and told her the pencil was still there and that one man had read it and said the office knew, and she listened the way she listens to a day, without writing.

“If it is still there on the thirtieth it goes down with the twenty-ninth,” she said. “And somebody will say the office took it down to hide it, and somebody else will say the office left it up to answer it, and both of them will be wrong, and there is nothing on nine sheets under a third table that can hold either of them.”

He nodded and went on up the stair to do the floors, because there was nothing else in that hour that belonged to him.

The sheet standing face up on that rail is the twenty-ninth, and the thirtieth of June is six days off, and at four on that afternoon the twenty-ninth comes down and the thirtieth goes out, with eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it unmoved, and the pencil under the striking goes wherever the twenty-ninth goes when it comes down.

He wrote nothing down that evening. He did the floors of three washrooms and the stairwell and the bin room, and at about half past five he stood at the table in the room off a third court and looked at the bound Register with four hundred and thirty rows in it and understood that not one row in it could hold a rubbing from the inside of a coat.

The two closed doors had held all morning, because both of them were about him. The third had not held at all, and nobody had thought about it. A sentence a stranger reads out of a page in a passage is not on the page any longer, and the twenty-ninth comes down at four on the thirtieth, and a page can be taken down at four o’clock and a sentence cannot.

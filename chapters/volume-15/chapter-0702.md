# Nothing Is A Deed

Two thousand five hundred and twenty was the first of February and the eighth is day two thousand five hundred and twenty-seven, and two thousand five hundred and twenty-seven less two thousand five hundred and twenty is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

## The Passage Before Eleven

The rail is chest high on a bracket, and it carries one sheet face up, and about a hundred people a month knock the corner of it on the way past without stopping. About nine people a week get down as far as the bottom two inches of it. Neither figure has moved since the spring and neither of them is going to move this morning.

The man behind the counter had his own copy face up on the wood, taken off the rail at opening and put back on it before he went, which is the arrangement and has been for nine years.

He had run that desk for nine years. In the seventh of them he gave away the book that lives under that counter, which is ruled in two wide columns and a third one no wider than a thumb, and which has carried about thirty names with a day set against every one of them since before there was an office to put them in.

He gave it to a woman with a pencil off a street two streets wide. He was not paid for it. He is not paid for anything. There is nobody living he could point at who has ever thanked him for it, and he arranged that himself on a day nobody in this passage mentions.

He had a cloth for the wood and a pencil that he squared against the edge of the counter before he said anything.

“You have not been down here since the twelfth of May,” he said.

“I have not.”

“You have been in a coat with a piece of paper in the inside of it both times you have been in this passage since May,” said the man at the desk, “and I am not going to ask you about it, and I have got about four minutes.”

## The Man Who Came Out Of That Office A Fortnight Ago

The man from the office came down the passage at about twenty past ten and he was not the man the desk was expecting, or he was, and the desk did not say which.

He had a piece of pencil paper in his hand and the bottom two inches of it were in his own writing and there was a thing crossed out in the middle of it and the crossing had been done with the same pencil and then gone over twice.

“That office cannot tell you what it means,” he said.

“No,” said the man at the desk.

“That is the third time somebody in this city has said no to me about a line on a page, and two of them were in the same room.”

“Then you had a morning,” said the man at the desk, “and I have about four people at this rail and about nine of them a week get to the foot of it, and you are one of the seven this week who does not, and I am going to read it out and you can stand there and hear whether the page agrees with the building.”

He squared the pencil against the counter.

## He Reads It Out To Strangers

He got to the head first, because he always got to the head first, and he read the figure in a flat voice that nine years had worn the performance out of.

“Eight hundred and fifty-two,” he said. “Then four hundred and nineteen lines, all of them somebody’s account of a street dug in the cold. A day on each. An hour on each day. Nothing on the hours and nobody’s name anywhere. That is the top of it and that is most of it.”

Four people at the rail did not look up and two of them had come in with a bundle and one of the bundles was a bundle of cords.

“Second column,” said the man at the desk. “Carried by. Three lines. One of them is about a bundle and one of them is older than the office and neither of those is anybody's business. The third one is from the twenty-first of October and it is in the office's own hand and there is a man's name under it.”

He read the third line as it was written.

“Four hundred and nine. None is a deed. Not one claim.”

Nobody said anything for about four seconds and then a man further up the passage said, well, that is a thing to put on a page.

“It is a thing to put on a page,” said the man at the desk, “and it is a man's name under it, and about nine of you a week get this far down and none of you has ever asked me to stop.”

## The Sentence Said Back Correctly

The man from the office had not said anything at all while any of that was going on, and he was the one who had asked, and he stood about four feet off with his hands in his pockets and let a building in this city say a thing he had written, in this city's own hand, about him, to strangers, at about ten past ten in the morning.

Then he turned round and said it back.

“None of them is a deed,” he said.

That was all he said. He said it in the ordinary way, the way about nine people a week say it, the way a person repeats a line from a page that has been in front of them and that they have read in their own kitchen with a pencil.

The man at the desk put the pencil down and looked at him for about four seconds and did not ask him why he had said it in that voice, and that was the correct thing to do and both of them knew it.

“You have got a copy of that at home,” said the man at the desk.

“I have.”

“Then you are one of about four hundred people in this city who can read every line of it,” said the man at the desk, “and about nine hundred can read any of it, and about eleven of us know where the other three hundred and ninety-nine copies are and not one of the eleven has been to any of them, including me, and I have been at this counter for nine years.”

Nobody at the rail thanked him for the reading, because nobody at that rail has ever thanked anybody for anything, and he is not paid for it and has never been paid for it and has never once asked to be.

He went back into the passage with the pencil in his hand and put the page face up on the wood, and the four people at the rail drifted off up the passage one at a time over about two minutes, the way they do.

## Nine People A Week And What They Are Given

“There is a thing you should know about me,” said the man at the desk, to the back of a white plate going up the passage, and did not raise it.

“I have got about two minutes and I do not need them.”

“I have read that third line out to somebody every morning since the thirtieth of December,” he said, “because it is on the page and I read what is on the page. Nobody has ever asked me what it means and neither have you, and this morning I read it to you before you asked me to.”

“That is the whole of what I have got to say about it. I have been saying it out loud to about nine people a week for nine years and not one of them has ever said thank you, including the one who came in on the first of this month and had been in that office that morning.”

Lin Hao stopped with his hand on the door of the arch, because it was the wrong thing to have been told and it was the only thing anybody had said to him in nine days.

“I am not going to ask you what it means,” said the man at the desk. “I would not be entitled and I would not want to. Read it, and take it, and go on up the hill.”

He said it without emphasis and squared the pencil against the edge of the counter and went back to his book, and it was the whole of the conversation, and it lasted about eleven seconds.

*Two thousand five hundred and twenty-seven days. Less two thousand five hundred and twenty, the first of February of the eighth year, is seven. The man who walked in through the front door of an office off a third court on the first of this month stood at a rail in this passage seven days later and said nothing whatever while nine hundred people in this city read a thing he had written.*

*He said it afterwards, out loud and correctly, to a man behind a counter who is paid nothing.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and twenty-eight days, which is four months and eight days after them, and the twenty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of them with three lines in the second column of it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is a hundred and seven days, which is three months and seventeen days after them.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is eight hundred and nine days, which is twenty-six months and twenty-nine days after them.*

## What He Took Up The Hill With

He did not go up the hill.

He went as far as the bottom of it and then stood in the middle of a street two streets wide with about four hundred pieces of pencil paper in four hundred kitchens on one side of him and about nine hundred people who could read every line of any of them on the other, and the two numbers had never once been put next to each other in this city by anybody who could do anything with them.

Four hundred pieces of paper.

Not four hundred claims, because a claim is a thing with a day on it and about nine of those have one, and not four hundred people, because the people who carried them are about nine hundred and a fraction of them live under the same roof as somebody else who carried one.

About four hundred kitchens had the foot of a printed sheet written out in pencil on the back of something. He had never been to one. He could have gone to four this month and he was not going to go to one of them, and the reason had been ready since about four o'clock on a wet morning in November and it had got no better since.

He went on up the hill in the rain with nothing in his hands. About two hundred and forty people came past the corner of that wall in the hour and about ninety of them stopped at a board, read a month’s form for an address and went on.

About nine of them went down on a knee at the bottom of the wall and read nothing at all. There is nothing on the board, and there is nothing at the bottom of that wall but four inches of dressed stone.

Nobody thanked anybody anywhere in that hour, and that is not a thing anybody in this city has arranged. It is what the page does.

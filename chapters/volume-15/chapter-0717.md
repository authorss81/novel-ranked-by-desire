# A Second Keeper Is Not A Keeper

Two thousand six hundred and twenty-one was the twelfth of May and the nineteenth is day two thousand six hundred and twenty-eight, and two thousand six hundred and twenty-eight less two thousand six hundred and twenty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## Two Of Them Down Four Flights

The man who cannot read did not come on his own, and he was not asked to and nobody sent him.

The woman who mends umbrellas brought him, because he had been in twice to her bench in three weeks and on the second of the times he had asked her to come and write a thing out at his own table, and she had not gone, and in the end she had done the other thing instead, which was to ask a man on four flights what a piece of paper is and how a person is supposed to hold on to one.

He had held a trade for thirty-one years and he could tell anybody in that trade what was in a room from the sound it made, and he could not read a line and had never told anybody so in the first thirty years of it.

He had a piece of pencil paper in his hand with two sentences at the bottom of it the wrong way round, and the woman had a piece of paper of her own with the four sentences at the foot of the page written out on it, and they had been made out of each other at some point in the last two months and neither of them knew.

Lin Hao was not in the room. Perla Quist had told him three days before that somebody was coming and she did not know who and would not be told, and he had said nothing about that either, and on the morning of the nineteenth there were two people at that bench who were not her.

## What He Asked For

He did not ask for a column. He asked for a copy, and he was specific about it, and it took her about nine minutes to get him to be specific about it.

“I cannot keep what is on a page in my head,” he said. “I can keep what a person said. I can keep where a thing was put and what it cost and how long it took. I cannot keep a page. So give me a copy of your page and then it is paper and I have got paper and I have had paper all my life.”

“That is not what I am able to do and I am going to tell you why before I do anything else,” said Perla Quist. “I am not being careful at you. I have got about eleven minutes and you have come four flights.”

“All right.”

“The nine sheets under this table are not a record of anything. They are a record of what the foot of a public page said on the day a particular copy of it was made, and every line on them has a day on it, and that is the whole of what they are for. I rule one. I enter one. Nobody reads them but me.”

“I cannot read them.”

“You cannot read them and that is not the difficulty,” said Perla Quist. “The difficulty is the second one.”

## An Index Kept By Two People

She squared the nine sheets together and put the top of the pile where he could see the six words in her own hand at the top of the column.

“If I copy one of these for you and you take it home and you keep it, then there are two of them,” she said. “And a thing kept by two people is not an index. It is a rumour with a date on it.”

He did not say anything.

“A rumour with a date on it is worse than a copy and it is better than nothing, and those are the only three things there are,” said Perla Quist. “Because in about four months the two of them will say different things, and neither of them will say the other is wrong, because you cannot read yours and I cannot be in your kitchen.”

“And in about four years one of them will have a day on it that the other one does not have, and somebody in this city will go at that as though it were a figure.”

“Which one is right.”

“That is exactly the thing I am telling you will not happen,” she said. “You are not asking which one is right, you are asking which one I can promise you is right, and I cannot promise you that about anything, and if I did promise you, you would take the promise and put it on a wall and it would be the only promise anybody in this city has ever made about a piece of paper.”

The woman who mends umbrellas had been standing four feet off with her own sheet in her hand for about two minutes and she said the thing that made the whole of it turn over, and she said it in the flat voice of a woman who repairs other people's things for a living.

“I can read,” she said.

Perla Quist did not look round.

“That is the second keeper,” she said.

*Two thousand six hundred and twenty-eight days. Less two thousand six hundred and twenty-one, the twelfth of May of the eighth year, is seven, and an index was asked for by a man who cannot read it and was refused by a woman who can write it, and the refusal was correct.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and eighteen, the nineteenth of October of the seventh year, is two hundred and ten days, which is seven months exactly, and a fourth column had been standing under a third table with nothing at the top of it for every one of them until a woman with a pencil wrote six words on it herself on the afternoon of the fourteenth of October.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is forty-nine days, which is one month and nineteen days after them, and a front door off a third court has been opened to paper for every one of them and eleven pieces came through it in the first eight.*

## The Reason Is Standing Up In This Building

“You could have her keep it,” said the man. “She can read. She writes the bottom of that page out for half the street and she does it for nothing.”

“No.”

“You did not hear the whole of it.”

“I have heard the whole of it for about four months and I have heard it four times from him and this is the first time I have heard it from anybody else,” said Perla Quist. “She cannot keep it.”

“If she keeps it she is a person keeping an index on behalf of a building, and the moment she is that, every sheet on this table is a sheet that a building has had a look at through her, and she will not be able to turn it down inside a year and she will not know that she has been turned into it.”

“She would not want it.”

“She would not want it for about four months,” said Perla Quist.

The woman who mends umbrellas folded her own sheet along the crease she had put in it with a thumbnail and said, “That is the second time somebody has said no to me in a fortnight and both times it was about somebody else.”

“I am sorry about it.”

“Do not be. You are right and I knew you would be before I asked, and I asked anyway, and a person who knows and asks is doing it on purpose and that is allowed.”

She put her hand on the top of the nine sheets, and then took it off again without moving them, which is the only time in four years she has done that in front of anybody.

“Here is what I am going to say and then I am going to take him home and I am not coming back about it this month.”

“All right.”

“A copy of the page, made out of a copy, made by people who cannot read it, will be wrong. It will be wrong in about four months out of nine. It will be wrong by one sentence in nine and nobody will know which one, and the only thing in this city that can say which one is nine sheets under a third table that one woman keeps and no one else may cite.”

He looked at her.

“You are telling me about four hundred people are going to be wrong about their own paper.”

“I am telling you that about four hundred people are going to be right about their own paper about five days in nine, and that is a great deal better than they were, and it is not my fault and it is not yours,” said Perla Quist.

“I am not going to have a second copy of it, and you are going to go home and put that sheet of yours in a drawer and not write anything else on it, and in about four days somebody will come to your door with a different version of the same four sentences, and it will be wrong, and you will not be able to tell.”

## And Then She Read It To Him

They went down the four flights at about half past twelve and at the bottom of them, in the passage off a street two streets wide, the woman who mends umbrellas stopped and put his sheet against the light off the shopfronts and read the bottom four inches of it out loud, slowly, twice, because he had asked her to and because in thirty-one years he had never once asked anybody to do that.

He listened to it with his head on one side.

“That is not what she said to me,” he said.

“That is what it says.”

“That is not the order it came in.”

“That is what it says on the page,” said the woman who mends umbrellas, “and I have never seen the page, and I have been writing it out for about six months off a copy that a man made standing up at a rail.”

“It is very possible that he wrote it down wrong and it is very possible that I wrote it down wrong, and neither of us can tell, and you are the only one of the three of us who could have found out, and you cannot read.”

She folded it along the crease and put it in his inside pocket and said, “It is four sentences about a building and not one of them is about you, and I have known that since the fifteenth of October and I have never been able to prove it, and I have just tried to prove it in a street and it has taken about four minutes.”

They went on up the hill.

There was no true copy. There was only the one a person had been holding, and a woman with a pencil four flights above a laundry was the only person in Ostara who could say which of about nine was the one, and she could not be sent to anybody, and there was no form on which she could be asked to try.

# An Age Is What He Asks For

Two thousand seven hundred and forty-six was the seventeenth of September and the twenty-third is day two thousand seven hundred and fifty-two, and two thousand seven hundred and fifty-two less two thousand seven hundred and forty-six is six, and twenty-three less seventeen is six by the month count.

## The Man Who Came Back In July

The man whose claim lost at the counter on the twenty-sixth of July had written a day on his own piece of pencil paper since, in his own hand, at his own table, and had carried it back to the same window on the same street.

The clerk read it in about four seconds.

“This is the day you made the copy,” he said. “I said so in July and I am saying it again and neither of us has anything new.”

“And the other one.”

“The other one has no day in it and no day can be put on it by me, because I do not know what the day would be and there is nobody in this room who does,” said the clerk. “You have improved the paper and not the claim. Those are two different things and they are worth about the same, which is nothing.”

The man put the sheet back in his coat and left, and the clerk wrote the hour against it in the day book, because a counter that does not write the day writes nothing.

## What He Went There For

Lin Hao had come out of the passage behind the counter about nine feet back, and he had one question, and it was the only question he had ever wanted to put to a room like this one.

He wanted an age refused.

He wanted somebody entitled to look at two claims and say that the older one was not older after all, and he wanted it written down in a book behind a counter with a window in it.

That is the only place in this city where a day has ever been made to mean something about a person. The thirtieth sheet is a rail and the standing sheet is a building with no door onto a street, and the fourth column in a laundry goes nowhere and cannot be cited.

If a bound could be refused at a counter, then the office's own bound of the thirtieth of September of the seventh year could be put beside it, and both of them would be tested by the same man in the same chair, and about four hundred people would have an instrument.

He said it to the clerk at about eleven, standing about nine feet off, and he said it as a person and not as an officer, and it was the longest thing he had ever asked for at that window and it was four sentences long.

## What A Counter Is For

The clerk listened to all four and then put his pen across the book.

“No,” he said.

“I have told you the ground.”

“You have told me a feeling you have about a day,” said the clerk. “I have five years in this chair and I have never once been wrong about what a counter is, and what a counter is for is to read what a person puts in front of it and to say what that paper is.”

“You have not put a ground in front of me. A ground is something a person can be shown is false.”

“Show me that the thirtieth of September of the seventh year is not the day the receipt went onto the page and I will pull the finding of the twenty-sixth of July back the same minute.”

“You are not going to look.”

“I have not got anything to look at. You have a feeling and I have a day book,” said the clerk, “and between us there is about a hundred yards of street and a rail with a page on it, and neither of us is allowed to send anybody across it.”

“That is the whole of it in this city. Two records and no instrument.”

“That is the whole of it in this city,” said the clerk, “and you knew it in February when you sat in that chair yourself and were told a bound is not an age, and you have come back to this window to have somebody else say it out loud so that you can keep it.”

He picked the pen up again.

“I will say it out loud,” he said. “A claim with a day on it beats a claim without one, and that standing does not go back because the man who brought the second one has been to a third party and had the day written on it.”

“If it went back every time somebody was unhappy the book behind this counter would be a list of disappointed men and it would be worth nothing at all.”

“In about two hundred years nobody would read it, which is what happens to every book behind every counter in this city anyway.”

## The Question He Is Entitled To Put

The clerk then did the thing that clerks do at the end of a morning, which is look up, and he had been entitled to do it since eleven and had not done it, and Lin Hao understood about nine seconds later that he had been looked at.

He had a question a day and not two, and for five years that question had gone out of that window about nine times a morning and into about nine rooms and had never once gone to a man of rank one, because a man of rank one is not a party to anything at a counter and everybody in this city has known that for nine years.

Nobody had ever thought to ask him anything, because there was nothing anybody could have wanted from him.

“Is there a place in this city,” said the clerk, “where the papers those copies were made from are, or is there not.”

The passage behind the counter was very quiet.

## The Answer

“There is not,” said Lin Hao.

He heard himself say it and he heard how flat it was, and it was the flattest sentence he had produced in nine years, because it is the sentence this office has entered about about four hundred pieces of paper in four hundred kitchens since the fourth of February, read out at a rail by a man who runs a desk under a window, and it has been true every single time, and he had never once heard it from the outside until this morning.

The clerk wrote it in the day book in about nine seconds, in his own hand, in the level one he uses at a counter, and put the hour against it and the place against the hour.

“Thank you,” he said. “That is the first time that day book has ever held that and it will be in here in about two hundred years with nobody else's handwriting anywhere near it.”

“There is a qualification and you are entitled to have it,” said Lin Hao, and he gave it in one sentence because he could not do it in any other. “Every piece of paper received through the front door of my office since the tenth of June is entered with a date and with a description of the person who brought it. None of those is in the place you have asked me about, and all of them are somewhere, and I cannot tell you where they are either.”

The clerk nodded and wrote that down too, and made no face at all, because a counter is for paper and not for buildings.

*Two thousand seven hundred and fifty-two days. Less two thousand seven hundred and forty-six, the seventeenth of September of the eighth year, is six, and a man of rank one was asked one question at a window with a name over the door and answered that this city holds none of the papers.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and seventy-three days, which is five months and twenty-three days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and fifty-three days, which is eleven months and twenty-three days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## The Man Who Asked For An Age

He went back to the room off a third court at about half past twelve and told Wenna Raut the whole of it in the order it had happened, because she keeps days and a day without its hour is not a day she can hold, and because she was the only person in that building entitled to hear it and he was the only person entitled to tell her.

She entered it exactly, with the date and the hour and nothing whatever against the hour, and she did not improve one word of it and she did not put it in the column.

“The bound of the thirtieth of September is about the second column,” she said, when she had finished.

“It is.”

“It goes out on the thirty-first at four with a denial under it in the office’s hand and your name at the foot of that, and about four hundred kitchens are holding the other half of it, and a book in this city with a name over the door has just been told in your own words that the rest of it is nowhere.”

“Yes.”

“So the office is the only place in Ostara where that is true and cannot be found,” said Wenna Raut.

He did not answer that, and she did not press it, and both of them understood that the reason he did not answer it was a drawer in the wall on the left of that table which was shut and had been shut since the twenty-first of October by a man who had shut it himself.

He wanted an age and he had got a question instead, and the question had been about the one thing in nine years that no one in this city had ever thought to ask him about, and he had answered it truthfully in front of a window, and it had gone into a book that comes down at the end of every day of the year and goes up a stair.

Nobody will ever read it but the clerks of that division, and they will read it about two hundred years from now, when every person who could contradict it is under the ground.

There is now a book in this city in which it is written, by a man in a white plate with no figure cut into it, that about four hundred pieces of paper in about four hundred kitchens are the whole of it and the original of not one of them is anywhere in Ostara.

And the one thing that makes that sentence false is shut, and he shut it, and there is no form anywhere in this city for undoing an instruction.

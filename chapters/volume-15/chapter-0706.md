# The First Age On A Claim

Two thousand five hundred and forty-seven was the twenty-eighth of February and the fourth day of the next month is day two thousand five hundred and fifty-three, and two thousand five hundred and fifty-three less two thousand five hundred and forty-seven is six, and four days is six days when the month before them runs out at its thirtieth.

## A Ground Floor With A Window On The Counter

The division was on the ground floor of a building with two counters and a glazed one between them, and behind the glazed one there was a corridor with a shelf in it, and on the shelf there were two books standing with their spines out and about two feet of air between them.

She was at the second counter with a stack of about forty things in it and had been at that counter long enough that her cuffs had a crease in them that office clerks get and that nobody else does.

“You are the man from the white plate,” she said. “I am not going to ask you what you want and you are not going to tell me, because everybody who comes in here from a court wants to ask me whether a thing can be entered and there is no form on this floor that lets anybody enter a thing at all.”

“Then what do you do on this floor.”

“We read every paper twice,” she said. “Once at the counter with the person standing there, and once afterwards, out of the room, with nobody standing there. Both readings go in. The first is in the book on the left of that shelf and the second is in the book on the right of it, and there has never once been a reason to lay them side by side, because nothing this division does turns on which of them is true.”

She squared the stack against the counter.

“Say something or do not,” said the officer, “but if you are going to stand there I would rather you stood on that side of the counter, because the person at the window can see the back of your coat and it puts them off.”

## Forty Out Of Nine Hundred

“About nine hundred of these come through a quarter,” she said, “and that is nine hundred a quarter and it has been nine hundred a quarter since the second year, and I do not mean nine hundred people. Nine hundred papers.”

“All right.”

“Nine hundred papers and about forty of them in the last two quarters have come in with a day written on them in the hand of the person who made them, at the top, in their own pencil, and not one of the other eight hundred and sixty has a day on it at all.”

She opened the book on the counter in front of her and turned it a quarter round so that he could see it, and there was a line in the margin in her own hand with a date beside it and it was about four lines long.

“That line is mine and it went into our book on the ninth of this month. Six weeks. Not one person has come past this counter and asked me what it means, and you are not this division, and that is the only reason I am saying any of this to you at all.”

“And you are right about all of it.”

“I am right about every part of it,” said the officer, “and that is not a thing I enjoy saying to a man from a court and I am going to say it once and then I am going to tell you what I found out when I checked whether I was right.”

## What The Two Books Do Not Agree About

“I read one of them twice this week,” she said, “the same paper, out of the same pile, in the same room, with the same pencil in my hand.”

“And?”

“And the two readings disagreed,” said the officer, “which they do, and they do it more often on a pencil paper than they do on a printed form, and that is not a new thing and it is not an excuse, and a printed form disagrees with itself about as often as it takes two people to look at it differently.”

She let that stand for about two seconds.

“The difference is that a printed form was filled in by somebody who was standing at a counter being asked, and a pencil paper was written out in a kitchen by somebody who was not asked and has not been asked since and is not going to be. So the two readings of it are two different people’s account of the same piece of paper, and there is no form anywhere in this city that says which of them is the true one.”

“There is not one in this office either.”

“There is not one in your office either, and I know that, and I have said so to two men in your building,” said the officer. “What I have found out is the other thing and I have not said that to anybody.”

## An Age Is The Only Figure In This City

“A paper with a day on it can be put against a paper without one.”

She said it in the ordinary way, the way a woman says the thing she has been thinking about for a week and has now got out of her mouth.

“Not the claim. The claim is exactly as good as it was on the fourth of February and exactly as bad, and a day on the top of it does not make a man’s account of a street in the cold one single inch stronger. What it makes is an order.”

“A paper with a day on it can be laid against a paper without one and it will be laid on top, and it will be on top because it can be placed and the other one cannot, and not because anybody in this building has read either of them.”

“And that is the first age anybody has put on a claim in this city.”

“It is not an age,” said the officer. “It is a position in a queue, and a queue is all any of us have ever had, and I am not going to call it anything else in my own book because in about four years somebody is going to find that word in my writing and go off with it.”

She took the pencil and wrote one more line in the margin under the four she had written on the ninth of this month.

“I have entered that in this division’s book today and I entered it as a finding of the division and I am going to sign it,” said the officer, “and I am not going to tell you not to write to me about it, because there is a form on this floor for correspondence and it goes out on a fixed day of the week and arrives whenever it arrives.”

“You should not have written it.”

“I have written it,” said the officer, “and I was right to write it, and you cannot tell me I was wrong, and I know that you cannot tell me, and I have known that since before you came in here, because you are not this division and you have not got a single thing over my book.”

*Two thousand five hundred and fifty-three days. Less two thousand five hundred and forty-seven, the twenty-eighth of February of the eighth year, is six, and a division on the ground floor of a building has entered a finding in its own book about forty papers out of about nine hundred and there is no form anywhere in this city on which anybody may tell the woman who wrote it that she was wrong.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and fifty-four days, which is five months and four days after them, and the twenty-eighth sheet has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain for every one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and thirteen, the fourteenth of October of the seventh year, is a hundred and forty days, which is four months and twenty days after them.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and twenty, the first of February of the eighth year, is thirty-three days, which is one month and three days after them, and a man came through the front door of a building off a third court on the first of this month and asked it in a doorway whether it knew his name.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is eight hundred and thirty-five days, which is twenty-seven months and twenty-five days after them.*

## The Question She Asked In A Corridor

He was in the corridor behind the glazed counter at about ten past eleven with the two books on the shelf two feet apart behind him and the sound of the reading floor above going through the floorboards.

She came out of the second counter and stood about four feet off, which was the closest anybody had got to him in six years of being in buildings like this one.

“I am going to ask you one ordinary question in an ordinary corridor,” she said, “because I have four days left in which somebody in this city might tell me that I have got a thing wrong, and there is one person I am permitted to ask and it is you, and you are not going to thank me for it.”

“Go on.”

“Is it true that the office off a third court holds about four hundred of them.”

He did not answer for about four seconds and he understood in the middle of those four seconds that he was going to answer it anyway, and that he was not the office, and that nobody in this city could have stopped him and that he was going to do it in a corridor to a woman who was doing her job.

“It is,” he said. “Four hundred and nine. They were carried up four flights in two cardboard boxes on the twelfth of November and they have been on the boards under a window since the thirtieth of August and they are not opened and they are not going to be opened, and there is no form anywhere for undoing an instruction.”

The officer did not write it down and did not look as though she were going to.

She said, “That will do,” and went back behind the glazed counter and did not say anything else to him.

He went out at about ten past eleven and down the steps and along. He had answered a question that nobody in this city is entitled to answer except him, in a corridor, to a woman who had no authority to hear the answer and no reason to want it, and he got nothing for it. That was the eleventh time in six weeks and he had been doing it on purpose since about the ninth of December.

# Nobody Is Obliged To Open It

Two thousand seven hundred and eighty-seven was the twenty-eighth of October and the fifth of November is day two thousand seven hundred and ninety-four, and two thousand seven hundred and ninety-four less two thousand seven hundred and eighty-seven is seven. October runs to its thirtieth and there is no thirty-first in this city. The twenty-eighth to the thirtieth is two, the first of November is one, and the fifth is four more, and two and one and four is seven.

## The Sheet That Went Out Of The Door

It went into a coat on the seventh of December and it has been in a coat since, and the office has not got it and never will, and that is the correct position and he has been proud of it for a year.

A piece of office paper with the date at the top of it in Wenna Raut’s hand, four words on the back in ink with a nib that has been ground down twice in that year, and her signature at the foot of the front.

The whole of what was on it, in four words: she gave one sheet.

That is all it says. It does not say what was on the sheet and it does not say what the sheet was about and it does not say that anything was owed to her and it does not say that anybody refused her, and a woman who cannot read has been carrying it up and down a street for about a year and has no idea in the world what is written on it.

It is the truest thing this building has ever printed.

He stood at the table on the morning of the fifth with the drawer shut in the wall on his left and thought about the other end of it, which is that the office can print the truest sentence it owns as often as it likes and the sentence does not move one inch.

## The Second One

“You are going to ask me for another sheet,” said Wenna Raut.

“I am.”

“I will write it,” she said. “I will write it exactly as I wrote the first one, with the date at the top and four words on the back and my signature at the foot, and it will be the second true sentence this office has printed about that woman, and I want the reason out loud before I write it, because I may only enter what I am given and you have not given me a reason yet.”

“The reason is that there is no reason,” he said. “I want it to exist. I cannot do anything with it. I am aware of both halves and I am asking anyway and I have decided that is the correct thing for me to do at this point in my life.”

She wrote it in about four minutes and did not read it back, because that is not what she does with a thing she has been given, and she squared it to the edge of the table and gave it to him face down, the same way, and the same three seconds passed before he turned it over.

Then she put her pen across the book.

“Now it needs a row,” she said, “and I want you to hear what the row is going to be, because it is the first row in nine years in that book that is about something this office chose to do and then did not finish.”

She entered it exactly, in about four minutes, with the date against it and the hour against the date and nothing whatever against the hour, and the row said that this office wrote a sheet on the fifth of November of the eighth year, and that it carries four words on the back of it, and that it has not been sent to anybody and cannot be.

“I have never improved one word of anything you have ever given me,” said Wenna Raut, “and there is not a field in that book for the name of the person it was written for, because this office has never in nine years sent a thing to a person, and I am not inventing a column for her.”

*Two thousand seven hundred and ninety-four days. Less two thousand seven hundred and eighty-seven, the twenty-eighth of October of the eighth year, is seven, and a second sheet was written in this office with four words on the back of it in a clerk’s hand and it is not going anywhere.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is two hundred and fifteen days, which is seven months and five days after them, and the thirty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and ninety-five days, which is thirteen months and five days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## Four Flights With It In His Hand

He took it up four flights behind a girl who carries linen, because it is quicker and because going up a stair with a thing in your hand gives you about a minute and a half in which to change your mind, and he used the whole of it and did not change his mind.

The back room smelled of boiled soap and damp linen with the steam pressing under the door from the washroom front, and the ruler was squared against the edge of the bench and the ninth sheet was out with about two hundred empty lines under a heading a woman ruled on her own paper in February.

He put the sheet face down on the bench, the way it had been put into a hand on the seventh of December, and did not say anything about it.

Perla Quist turned round about four seconds later.

“That is the second one,” she said.

“You can tell.”

“I can tell by the fold and by the size of the nib’s blot on the front and by the fact that you have brought it face down and have not told me to open it,” said Perla Quist. “She turned that one over twice and looked at it for about four seconds each time and put it back in her coat and said *is it right*, and that was the whole of what she said in eleven months.”

## What Is Not In The Column

She did not turn his over.

“My heading says what a sheet carried on the day it was made,” she said, “and this sheet was made the day before it was given to her, in a room off a third court, by a clerk with a nib, and it carries no copy of anything and it is not a claim and it is not about a day at all, so there is nowhere under my heading for it and I would have to invent a day to put it under and I do not invent days.”

“You could put it on the bench.”

“I could put it on the bench,” said Perla Quist, “and the bench is where things go that I am keeping, and I have about eleven of those in the drawer under this table, and none of the eleven has ever been in my column, and that is the whole reason there is a drawer under this table.”

He stood at the end of the bench with the sheet in his hand and understood that he had carried a true sentence four flights up a street to a woman who keeps the only instrument in this city that can date a thing, and that she had told him in about nine seconds that she was not the person it was for.

“How does anything you write ever reach anybody,” he said.

“Two ways,” said Perla Quist. “A hand, or a window. A thing goes from here to a person on the end of somebody’s arm, or it goes from a counter out of a window into a street. I am not a hand and I am not a window, and you are a man in a white plate with a spent chest who works nights on a rota the size of his own hand, and neither are you.”

“And the first one went.”

“The first one went because she was standing in this room and I put it into her hand,” said Perla Quist, “and I have done that about eleven times with a pencil and never once with a piece of paper, and it is the only thing I have ever done that was not a column.”

## She Has Not Come Back

She came back on the eighth of December, which is three hundred and twenty-seven days ago now, and she did not come back for a second reason and she did not send anything and she did not say she was coming.

She has not been to that table since, and she is not going to be told by anybody in this city that there is a second sheet, because this office has sent nobody and will send nobody, and because she has been asked nothing in about a year and is not going to be asked anything now.

He stood in the back room off a street two streets wide with a piece of office paper in his hand and understood the whole of it standing up, which is that there is exactly one way for a sentence to get from a piece of paper into a person’s life in this city, and it is that the person is standing in front of you at the time.

## Nobody Is Obliged To Open Anything

Nine years of this office have been built on not making anybody do anything.

No clerk asks a person a question about a thing somebody else has written down. No clerk sends anybody to anybody. No drawer is opened. No bundle is opened. No coat is opened.

No form in this city compels a woman to come back to a table, or a man to show what is in his pocket, or a boy to say what he has heard, or a cleaner to say what she has seen, or a counterman to stop reading the bottom of a page out loud because it costs him nothing and he has never been paid for it.

Every one of those has been correct. Every one of them has been correct on its own facts and each one of them has been the right decision on the day it was made, and he has made most of them himself.

And the sum of about nine years of correct decisions is a piece of paper in a coat in a kitchen that neither of them can reach, and a woman standing on a lower floor of a building with no door onto a street, and about four hundred pieces of pencil paper in four hundred kitchens with a name in a clerk’s hand at the bottom of them and not one person in this city who is under any obligation to do a single thing about any of it.

Not one of them. Not her, not the man who wrote the four words and cannot read them, not the boy with the paste brush, not the woman with the broom, not the woman who carries them up and down a street and does not have to come back.

He put the second sheet on the bench on top of about eleven other things in a drawer under a third table in a laundry, and Perla Quist wrote the day on the back of it in pencil because she keeps days and a day without its hour is not a day she can hold, and she wrote nothing else, because there is nothing else that happened.

Then he went down four flights and did the floors of three washrooms and the stairwell and the bin room.

At about half past five he stood at the table with the drawer shut and the bundle on it and twenty-six things on the bundle, and he understood that he had spent nine years being scrupulous about never making anybody do anything, and that on the last morning of it he had made a clerk write down a sentence to a woman who is not going to be told she has it.

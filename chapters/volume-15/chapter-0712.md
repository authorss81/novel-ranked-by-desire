# A Date Read Out In A Laundry

Two thousand five hundred and eighty-six was the seventh of April and the fourteenth is day two thousand five hundred and ninety-three, and two thousand five hundred and ninety-three less two thousand five hundred and eighty-six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## Four Flights And A Second Table

She had the nine sheets out from under the third table and the fourth column with six words in it in her own hand at the top, and about two hundred empty lines under the six words, and the ruler squared against the edge of the bench.

He came down at about eleven with his coat buttoned and put a sheet of paper on the second table, folded once, and did not open it.

“That has been in your coat since the seventeenth of December,” said Perla Quist.

“Yes.”

“It has been folded once the whole time. Nobody has asked you to do anything with it and you have carried it up a hill twice and down four flights about nine times and you have not opened it.”

“No.”

She did not come round the bench.

“Say what you want before you say anything else, because I am not going to unroll anything on this table without knowing why, and I have nine sheets here with a heading on them that somebody said this month was the same as two words in a stone.”

He put his hand inside his coat and took it out and held it, and it came out of the inside of the coat, which is where it had been since a yard nine streets from a works department and a man on a bench who had carried that piece of paper for thirty years.

“I want you to open it,” he said. “I am not going to open it and I am not going to stand here while you do it. You open it and you read it out and then you tell me what it is, and I will not put one word of it in a column or a Register or a book.”

“That is the first time in four years you have handed me a thing.”

“It is.”

“You have handed me nothing four times and I have said no four times and you have never once asked me for anything, and you have just handed me a piece of paper you have carried since December and told me to read it,” said Perla Quist. “Do you know that is the shape of the thing you are frightened of.”

“I know what it is the shape of.”

## What She Read Out Loud

She opened it on the second table. It was folded once and about the size of a sheet folded once, and there was a place on it, and a thing, and a number of pieces, and a line running the width of the paper with two words against the end of it under a heading in an ordinary working upright hand, and at the head of the sheet in the same hand a date.

She read the whole of it. She read the place and the thing and the number of pieces and the line and the two words and the heading, and she read them at the speed of a woman who reads a column all morning.

Then she read the date.

She read it out loud in a room with steam coming under the door from the front, twice, the second time slower, and she did not say the date afterwards and neither of them wrote it down.

“That is older than the yard,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It is older than the yard by more than the yard is old. I do not know the yard’s figure and I do not need it, because a yard in this city is not two hundred years old and that date is not in this century of anything.”

She put one finger on the paper without touching it, in the air, over the line and the two words.

“Those are the two words that are cut in the four inches at the bottom of a wall in this city,” she said, “and you have never said them to me, and I have never asked you, and I have heard them said once by a woman who cannot write her own name, at the foot of a wall, in the wrong order, and got them wrong.”

“I have not said them.”

“You have not said them to me and you have said them to nobody in four years and there is one rubbing of them in this city and it is in the inside of a coat.”

## The Answer Is About A Building

Here is what the date does, and Lin Hao understood it standing at the second table with the room going on behind him, and he understood it in about four seconds and it took him the rest of the morning to sit down with it.

The stone at the foot of that wall is dressed stone, and it came out of a yard, and a yard delivers dressed stone by docket, and a docket is a piece of paper that says a thing left here and went somewhere.

So the two words were cut before that stone was standing in a wall anywhere at all.

They were cut in a yard, at a bench, by a man, on a block of stone that was about to go back to be worked again, and a man cutting into stone leans into the cut, which is why the left-hand one is deeper than the right-hand one, and he cut two words and no more because two words were enough and more would have taken another four seconds he did not have.

“That man was not writing a message to anybody,” said Perla Quist. “He was writing to the next man who picked the block up.”

“Yes.”

“And the next man picked it up and the block came to a corner in this city and went into a wall and got rendered over sixty-two times and the paint does not take on four inches of dressed stone and that is the whole of why anybody can read it now.”

She took her hand off the paper.

“A man at a counter told me that on the fifteenth of October and he did not know he was telling me anything. He had been saying it for thirty years about a block he had watched go out.”

“So the wall is not where they were cut.”

“The wall is where they were cut *about*,” said Perla Quist. “There is a difference and it has taken you since December to get to it and you got here in four seconds and you have been standing at my bench with a coat buttoned up for a quarter of an hour.”

She squared the paper against the edge of the second table and did not fold it.

“I am not keeping it,” she said. “You have taken it out of your coat and you have put it in my room and I have read it, and that is all that was going to happen and it has happened, and I am going to give it to you and you are going to take it back into the coat.”

“There is nowhere in this city for a piece of paper like that to live except a person,” she said, “and I am not going to be the person it lives with.”

“I would not let you.”

“You would not have been able to stop me and we both know it, and I have said no five times to you about nine things and I am not saying no six times about this, because this one I wanted.”

She gave it to him and he put it back in the inside of his coat, in the place it had been, and the fold went in as it had been folded, because it had been in that coat for one hundred and seventeen days and it had never once been opened.

*Two thousand five hundred and ninety-three days. Less two thousand five hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of April of the eighth year, is seven, and a woman off a street two streets wide read a date out loud twice to a man who was not going to look at it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and thirteen, the fourteenth of October of the seventh year, is a hundred and eighty days, which is six months exactly, and she ruled nine sheets under a third table on that afternoon and put nothing at the top of it for another four months.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and seventy-six, the seventeenth of December of the seventh year, is a hundred and seventeen days, which is three months and twenty-seven days after them, and a sheet folded once has been in the inside of a coat for every one of them and has been opened once.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and ninety-four days, which is six months and fourteen days after them.*

## Four Words In The Column And A Man In The Passage

“There are four words in that column now,” she said. “There was one in the column on the twenty-first of February and there was nothing entered on the first of March and there were two on the nineteenth, and there is a fourth, and I entered it on the eighth of April, which is a day like every other day in this city and has no standing here beyond its number.”

She turned the ninth sheet round and pushed it across the bench without letting go of it until it was all the way over, which is the only thing she does with these sheets.

“A man brought me a copy of the foot of your page. Not a copy of all of it. The bottom of it, about four inches deep, in his own pencil, in his own kitchen. He made it standing up at the rail in a passage off Cordwain because the counterman was reading the page out and he wanted it and would not ask the counterman for it twice.”

“What did the page say that day.”

“The same four sentences it says now, less one,” said Perla Quist, “and that is the entry, and I put what the page said in the column on the day he brought it and not the day it was made, and I have never told him which of the two that was.”

She turned the sheet back round.

“He is not the first and there have been four since the middle of February and none of them knew about the other three, and that is the reason I have not told you about it, and I am telling you about it now because somebody came into that passage yesterday afternoon and told the counterman that the foot of that page has been copied out.”

“By whom.”

“By somebody who cannot read, and who had it copied for him out of somebody else’s copy, and who has the four sentences in the wrong order,” said Perla Quist. “The counterman says the man was not making anything of it. He said it the way people say a thing they heard at a door, and by the time it has been at a door and come back down a passage it is a claim about your page and not a fact about a copy.”

She squared the ruler against the bench.

“I am not going to tell him it is wrong. There is no form in this city on which a woman who keeps an index may correct a man who cannot read, and there is no form on which anybody may tell him he has it backwards, and the only thing I can do with it is the thing I do with everything, which is to write down when it was said.”

She wrote it down. It took her about four seconds and she did not improve it.

“There are six words at the top of my column,” said Perla Quist, “and there are four sentences at the foot of your page, and about nine people in this city have now heard a thing about those two things from a man at a door, and I have entered the day it was said, and there is nowhere for the entry to go.”

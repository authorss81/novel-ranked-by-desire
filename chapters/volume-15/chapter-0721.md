# A Claim About Two Words

Two thousand six hundred and forty-nine was the tenth of June and the seventeenth is day two thousand six hundred and fifty-six, and two thousand six hundred and fifty-six less two thousand six hundred and forty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## The Bottom Two Inches

The sheet on the rail had been face up since the thirtieth of March and the bottom of it was the part everybody read standing up, because the rail is low and the last two inches take a bend at the knee to get to, and about nine people a week went down that far.

On the morning of the seventeenth the counterman found something under the striking that had not been there at four o’clock the afternoon before, when he had squared it after the rain.

It was in pencil, in the two inches of nothing under the name, in a hand he had never seen on that page, and it was small and level and written with the paper braced from below, the way a person writes on a wall when they do not want the writing to look like writing.

It said that the two words cut at the foot of a wall in this city were a warning about the four sentences above them, and that the office off the third court had known it since before the page went out.

The counterman read it twice and did not touch it.

He had read the bottom of that page out to people every morning for nine years and he knew the shape of every letter on it, and this one was not any of them, and it was not the pencil of any of the eleven who had come through a door off a third court in April, because he had seen three of those sheets face up on a bench and knew the weight of them.

He put the ruler across the bottom edge so nobody would lean on it and went down four flights with the key to the laundry in his pocket.

## What Perla Quist Did Not Do

She came up with him at about half past eleven and stood about four feet off the rail with her arms folded and looked at it for about nine seconds without bending down.

“It is about the two words,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And it says they are about the four sentences.”

“Yes.”

She did not bend down. She did not take the pencil out of her apron. She did not write the day on the back of her hand, which is what she does with everything else, and he noticed all three because she is a woman who writes everything down.

“If I write down when this was said,” she said, “then I have a claim with a day on it, and a claim with a day on it is a thing somebody will try to order against something else, and the only copy of those two words in this city is a rubbing in the inside of a coat.”

“So you will not enter it.”

“I will enter that there is pencil under the striking in a hand that is not anybody’s,” said Perla Quist. “I will not enter what it says, because what it says is about two words I have never been handed in a form anybody may hand me, and nine sheets under a third table cannot hold a thing nobody can check.”

He understood that she was frightened, and that frightened him more than the writing, because she had ruled nine sheets under a third table in October and headed them in February and written a line under the heading in May that went nowhere, and none of that had frightened her.

“What do I do with it.”

“Nothing,” she said. “You read what is on the page. That is the only thing you have ever read out of it. You read the four sentences and the three lines and the name and you stop at the striking, and if anybody asks you about the pencil you say it is there.”

## The Man In The White Plate

Lin Hao came down to the passage at about half past four and there were five people at the rail and none of them was looking at the bottom two inches, because the counterman had put three copies face up on the desk under the window and everybody was looking at those instead.

He stood about eleven feet back with his coat buttoned and watched the way people stand when there is something at their feet they have decided not to see yet.

The counterman came down to him after about four minutes and told him in about eleven seconds, in the order it had happened, and did not lower his voice, because there was nobody in that passage who did not already know how to listen.

“A claim about two words,” said Lin Hao.

“In pencil. Under the striking. In a hand that is not anybody’s.”

“Have you read it out.”

“No,” said the counterman. “I read what is on the page. I have read what is on the page for nine years and I am not going to start reading what is under it because somebody put a pencil there in the night.”

Lin Hao went to the rail after the five had gone and bent at the knee and read it once, all the way through, and then stood up again and put his hands in his pockets.

He had not said those two words to anybody in about four years and he did not say them now, and the pencil under the striking did not say them either, which was the worst part of it, because a claim about two words that does not write them down cannot be answered by writing them down.

He had carried a folded sheet in the inside of his coat since the seventeenth of December with a date on it that somebody else had read out loud in April, and the date had closed a thread about a yard and had not touched the words at all, and now somebody had used the words without using them.

He asked the counterman how many had been at the rail that day and the counterman said about nine before noon and four after, and none of them had knelt to the bottom two inches while he was watching, which meant the pencil had gone on in the night or in the hour after four when the passage filled and emptied twice.

“You keep that rail squared and swept and you read from the desk under the window,” said Lin Hao. “If somebody writes under the striking again you will tell me before you tell anybody else, and you will not read it out, and you will not rub it away.”

“I have done all three of those since before you had a white plate,” said the counterman, “and I have never been thanked for any of them and I am not asking to be thanked now.”

Lin Hao felt the anger come up in him then, sharp and plain, at the hand that had done it and at himself for having no way to meet it, and he put it down again because anger at a pencil in a passage was a thing that would make him stupid for the rest of the day.

*Two thousand six hundred and fifty-six days. Less two thousand six hundred and forty-nine, the tenth of June of the eighth year, is seven, and a claim in pencil went onto the bottom two inches of a public page in a passage off Cordwain in a hand that is not anybody’s and nobody saw it done.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is seventy-seven days, which is two months and seventeen days after them, and the twenty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is two hundred and fifty-seven days, which is eight months and seventeen days after them, and the third line of a second column went onto a page at four that afternoon and has been read out at that rail ever since.*

## Nobody Saw It Done

He went up four flights at about six and she had the nine sheets out and the pencil squared against the edge of the bench and about two hundred empty lines under the four entries she had made since February.

“I am not going to ask you to date it,” he said, before she could say no.

“I know you are not,” said Perla Quist. “You came up four flights to not ask me, the way you came down them in April to not ask a woman who mends umbrellas to stop, and I am getting used to the shape of you not asking.”

“What happens if it stays there.”

“Then about nine people a week read to the bottom of that page and see pencil under the striking, and one of them tells somebody in a kitchen, and in about four days there is a claim in four hundred kitchens that the office knew about a stone before any of them were working.”

“And if it is taken off.”

“Then somebody took pencil off a public page,” she said, “and there is no paper in this city that says who may do that, and the man who does it is the man in a white plate with no figure cut into it, and about forty people will say it came from him.”

He stood at the end of the bench for about four seconds and understood both halves at once, the way he had understood the instruction in June before he wrote it, and neither half had anywhere to go.

The sheet standing face up on that rail through the whole of this month is the twenty-ninth, and the thirtieth does not go out until the end of it, and there is nothing in the office off a third court that can put a rubbing from the inside of a boy’s coat onto a rail in a passage.

The claim was unanswerable, and the reason was not that it was written in pencil, and not that the hand was not anybody’s, and not that the two words had never been printed in this city and never would be.

The reason was that the only copy was in the inside of a coat, and about nine people had been in a room where it was read out, and one of them could not read, and there was nowhere in this city to put any of that where about nine people a week could see it.

He went back down the four flights with nothing in his hands, and the pencil stayed where it was, under the striking, in the two inches of nothing, and by the time he reached the passage the counterman had put the ruler back across the bottom edge and was reading the four sentences out to four strangers as though the bottom two inches were empty.

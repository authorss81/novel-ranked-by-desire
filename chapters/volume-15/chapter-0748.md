# Four Lines In The Column

Two thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine was the tenth of December and the seventeenth is day two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six, and two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six less two thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## About Half Past Two

The thirty-second sheet went onto the table under the window at about half past two and Wenna Raut squared it against the wood, and then squared it again about four minutes later for no reason anybody could give, because that is what she does with a sheet that is about to go out.

The head of it stands where it has stood since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and it is not a figure of anything, and no permission has ever been given for it to move.

About four hundred and nineteen accounts under the head, with a date against every one of them and an hour against the date and no figure against the hour and no name along the line.

Four lines in the second column under two words that say carried by.

The four sentences at the foot. And under them, about nine feet down and in this office’s hand, a denial with a man’s name under it and a date.

## The First Time He Has Heard It Read Out Loud

He had never once heard that page read out loud. In nine years he has stood nine feet back from a rail in a passage off Cordwain and listened to the bottom of it being read to about nine people a week, and every time it has stopped at the striking, and every time the two lines he has written himself have been read by somebody who did not know him.

Wenna Raut picked the sheet up by the edges, which she does not do for the rail and did for him, and read the whole of the second column out loud in the level voice she uses at a counter.

“Carried by.”

Then the first line, in the order it stood, with the date and the number and the four words and a man’s name and a sentence under the name saying a drawer is not to be opened.

Then the second, which names a place.

Then the third, which names a person who is not alive.

Then the new one, exactly as she had entered it on the third of December and exactly as she entered it, without improving it, because that is what a clerk does.

*A date. And a name. And a sentence in a clerk’s hand saying a woman carried every sheet of pencil paper brought to the third table in a laundry off a street two streets wide, up four flights, to this counter, and carried them on, and that the papers are not in this building and are not in hers.*

*And a man’s name. And nothing at all under it.*

She put the sheet down.

“That is what it will sound like,” she said.

He had been in the room for about nine minutes.

“That is what it will sound like,” she said again, “except that you will hear it in a voice that has read nine thousand of them, and he will read it to about nine people a week, and he will not stop at it, and there will be nothing after it to stop at.”

“I know.”

“You do not,” said Wenna Raut. “You know it the way a man knows a thing he has read. You have not heard it read out loud by a person who is reading it, and there is a difference and it is about nine seconds long.”

## The Thing He Tried, And The Printer At The End Of It

“It needs something under my name.”

“It cannot have something under your name.”

“Then it needs another column.”

“There is no another column,” said Wenna Raut, “and there has not been one since before I came here, and the form is not printed in this building.”

“Who prints it.”

“A works department two streets from here,” said Wenna Raut, “which prints the form for every division in this city once a year, in the autumn, and sends it out in a bundle, and the heading and the ruling and the width of the second column are theirs and have been theirs since before this office existed.”

“And we have never sent them anything.”

“This office has not sent that works department a piece of paper in nine years,” said Wenna Raut. “Not a question, not a correction, not a note about a heading. It puts up a form on a board by a kerb once a month with two ruled squares at the foot of it and it has never once written to the people who printed it.”

He stood at that table and understood the whole of the last four years in about nine seconds, which is that every reason he had for believing this office was outside the reach of anybody was a reason about a printer in another street who had never been told anything and had never been asked anything and would go on printing *carried by* in the autumn of the ninth year without knowing that a building with no door onto a street had put a woman’s name under it.

## What She Said About The Counts

She had her hand on the ruler when she stopped.

“I am going to say four numbers out loud and I am going to name every one of them, because this is the quarter before it goes out and about nine people a week are going to read the bottom of it and somebody is going to mix them up within four years.”

“Go on.”

“About four hundred and nineteen accounts under the head, and that is a count of accounts and not a count of deeds and not a count of people. The number at the head of the first line in the column is a number against a date and it is not a count of anything you can put next to those four hundred and nineteen.”

“There are four lines in the second column and three of them have a person at the head of them and one of them is not alive. And there is one living person named anywhere on this sheet whose name is not yours.”

“Go on.”

“That is four numbers and four different things and if anybody in this building ever gets hold of one of them and carries it into another one then that sheet is finished and so is the office,” said Wenna Raut, “and that is not a warning, it is the reason I am able to hand you a page.”

## What He Had Bought And What He Had Not

He had thought, for about two days, that the worst of it was going to be her.

It was not going to be her. She had priced it in nine seconds and come down four flights and asked for one sentence and not been given it and gone up again, and she had said out loud that she had been a person who could be asked to stop for six years on purpose, and the page was going to take that off her and she had agreed to it with her eyes open.

The worst of it was going to be about four hundred kitchens in a city with no form in it.

A man in a delivery coat had stood in a lane off Cordwain in the third month of this year and told him, in about eleven seconds, why he did not want a thing printed, and he had not wanted it printed and had said so first, and he had been right, and Lin Hao had walked past four places he could have put it in and used none of them.

What the man in the delivery coat had said was this. The day it is printed, about forty people will say it came from the office off the third court. And the day about forty people have said that, about four hundred people stop writing in the bottom two inches of their own paper, because a claim everybody knows came from a building is a claim that waits to be told, and it will wait about four years, and not one of the four hundred has that left.

He had heard it in June and agreed with it entirely and put it on a shelf.

A name at the head of a line in a column headed *carried by*, printed by a works department two streets away, in this office’s hand, under the officer’s own name, going out four times a year to about two thousand counters and read to the bottom by a man who is paid nothing — that is not a rumour about a building.

That is a printed fact with a printer’s name on the form, and about forty people will say it came from Lin Hao inside a week of the thirtieth, and about four hundred people will read about it in a kitchen in about a month, and every one of the two inches under the striking in every one of those kitchens will go quiet, and not one of them will ever be told why.

He had been guarding that two inches since the ninth of August.

He had entered a denial in his own hand on the sixteenth of August to keep the door open for anybody to claim anything, and this morning he had put a line in a column that shuts it, and the two things were in one sheet about nine feet apart and he could not show them to each other, and a man who runs a desk under a window was going to read both of them out loud on the same afternoon.

## At Four O’Clock On The Thirtieth

At about ten past three he went down the two flights and along to the passage, because he had been going to that rail every week for nine years and there was a thing he wanted to hear and there was nothing to be done about it.

The counterman had the thirty-first sheet face up on the rail and there were nine at it, and he read the four sentences out to all nine and stopped at the striking, the way he reads it, and nobody paid him and nobody has ever paid him.

The second column on that sheet had three lines in it and the third of December had not gone anywhere yet, because a page does not go out before the last working day of the quarter and a page that goes out four times a year does not come back.

In thirteen days the same man is going to read a fourth line out loud in that passage, and he is never going to know what is in it, and he will read it because he reads what is on the page.

*Two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six days. Less two thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine, the tenth of December of the eighth year, is seven, and the thirty-second sheet was squared at about half past two with four lines in the second column and one living person named on it who is not the officer at the head of it.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is two hundred and fifty-seven days, which is eight months and seventeen days after them, and the thirty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is four hundred and thirty-seven days, which is fourteen months and seventeen days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## A Line With A Name And Nothing Under It

He went back up the two flights and stood at the table with the sheet squared and ready and the drawer shut in the wall on his left and twenty-six things on the bundle beside it.

The thing he wanted to hear, and had gone down to try to hear, and could not hear because the thirty-first was still on the rail, was this.

In thirteen days about nine people a week will stand in a passage off Cordwain and listen to a man with a desk under a window read the bottom of a sheet.

They will hear a heading that says a thing was carried. They will hear a woman’s name, and then a sentence about a laundry and about four floors of stairs, and then another man’s name, and then the man behind the counter will lift his head and ask whether anybody wants anything from the works department.

There was nothing after it. He had heard it in a clerk’s voice at half past two that afternoon and there was nothing after it, and that was not an accident, and it was not a thing that had run out of room on the form.

It was the only version of the line that protects her, and he had worked that out on the ninth of December in about eleven seconds and had not written it down, and about nine people a week was going to hear it on the thirtieth.

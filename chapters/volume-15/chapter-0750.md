# Somebody Told Her

Two thousand eight hundred and forty-three was the twenty-fourth of December and the thirtieth is day two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, and two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine less two thousand eight hundred and forty-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count. It is the last working day of the month and of the quarter and of the year, and there is no thirty-first in this city.

## Half Past Two

The thirty-second sheet went onto the table under the window at about half past two and it was squared against the wood, and it was not squared again, which was a thing that happened at about half past two on the thirty-first of March and again on the thirtieth of June and again on the thirtieth of September and had not happened on any of them, and Wenna Raut did not appear to notice that she had not done it.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it.

It has stood at that since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and it has not been moved once since, and it is not a figure of deeds and it is not a figure of anything, and no permission for moving it exists in this city or has ever existed.

It has now been unmoved across **sixteen of these sheets**. That is sixteen sheets, because a sheet counts itself from the seventeenth onward and this is the thirty-second.
And it has been unmoved across **fifteen quarters**, and that is a different count and it is also correct, because quarters run to a quarter-end strictly after the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and this is one of them. Both numbers are on the sheet in front of her and they differ by one, and anybody reading them out loud has to say which one is being printed.

Below the head, about four hundred and nineteen lines with a date against every one of them and an hour against the date and no figure against the hour and no name along the line.

Below that, four lines in the second column, under two words that say carried by.

Below that, the four sentences. And nine feet under the four sentences, in this office’s hand, a denial with a man’s name and a date under it. And two inches of nothing at the bottom, where a person at a rail can get down low enough to read.

## Four O’Clock And A Passage Off Cordwain

Wenna Raut folded it into eight and carried it down two floors herself and put it face up on the rail at about four, the way she has done four times a year since the first year.

There were eleven at the rail at four minutes past four, which is more than has ever stood there, and four is the most there has ever been, and the difference between the two numbers is a denial that went out at four o’clock on the thirtieth of September.

The counterman read the whole of the bottom of the page out to all eleven of them. He is paid nothing for it and nobody has ever paid him for it and there is nobody alive he could name who has thanked him for it.

He read the four sentences. He read the two lines under the striking.

And he came down to the second column and read the four lines in it, in the voice he reads everything in, at the speed he reads everything at, with a name at the head of the fourth of them, and he read it as though it were an address, because that is what is under that heading.

There was a silence of about two seconds in that passage, and then a man with a cap in his hands said to the woman beside him that a building had put a woman’s name on it, and the woman beside him said something back that he did not hear.

That is the whole of what happened in that passage at four o’clock on the last working day of the eighth year.

## About What Four Minutes Does In A City

He stood nine feet back and let it happen and did not go up and read anything himself, because he had wanted for about a week to have one thing in this city that a person could not pass on to anybody, and about four minutes after four o’clock he watched it be passed on to two people.

A man with a cap told a woman. The woman told a man at the mouth of the lane off Cordwain before five. By the end of the week it is in about four kitchens. By the end of the month it will be in about nine, in the words of the woman beside him at the rail, who has never met the woman whose name it is and is entirely right about what has happened.

And then two things follow from it, both of which were said out loud in advance by a man with a slate under his arm in a lane on the twenty-fourth, and both of which are about four hundred people he has never met and is responsible for.

About forty people will say that the office off the third court printed it. And about four hundred people will hear that in a kitchen, and the bottom two inches under the striking in four hundred kitchens will go quiet, because a claim everybody knows came from a building is a claim that waits to be told, and it will wait about four years, and not one of the four hundred has that left.

He entered a denial in his own hand in August to keep those two inches open and he put a line in a column in December, and the denial and the line are on the same sheet about nine feet apart, and about nine feet apart is not far enough.

## About Half Past Five, At The Foot Of The Hill

That corner is busier at half past five than at any other hour of the day, and two hundred and forty people went past it between five and half past five.

About a hundred of them stopped at the board, which is carrying a month’s form with two ruled squares at the foot of it and nothing whatever in either. About nine of them got down far enough to read the four inches at the foot of the wall, which is what the four inches are for.

Under the fresh render at that corner there is a course of dressed stone about the depth of two fingers and it has been under a wall for longer than anybody alive has been reading it, and two words are cut into it and they go right through the face, and the one on the left is deeper than the one on the right because a man cutting stone leans into the cut.

They are not written down here. They have not been written down anywhere in nine years and they are not going to be written down tonight, and there is exactly one copy of them in this city and it is a rubbing taken with a lump of chalk on the back of a month’s form on the twenty-second of December by a person who has never been asked what is in his coat.

The woman with the short-handled broom came down the slope at about ten past seven in the morning with her head bent to the work, and swept the four inches the way she sweeps them, and said nothing to anybody, and has never once said that she has seen a person put anything on them, and was not asked.

*Two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine days. Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-three, the twenty-fourth of December of the eighth year, is six, and the thirty-second sheet went out at four with eight hundred and fifty-two at the head unmoved across sixteen sheets and fifteen quarters, and four lines in the second column, and a denial, and two inches of nothing.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is two hundred and seventy days, which is nine months exactly after them, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail since four o’clock this afternoon.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is four hundred and fifty days, which is fifteen months exactly after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## What A Line In A Column Costs One Person

There is a woman in this city who has never been able to read a word of anything this office has ever produced. She came up a stair to a counter off a third court in the fourth month of last year and put a sheet across it, and a clerk entered what she was given, and what she was given was nothing.

The only record in Ostara that she was ever in that room is a line in a bound Register with a date against it and an hour against the date and nothing whatever against the hour, and a space in the middle of it where her words were going to go.

That space is not going to be filled in. A row may be added to and may not be taken from, and there is no form anywhere in this city on which a field may be completed after the fact, and if that woman stands at that counter in four years there is nothing anybody may do about it.

That is what the blank costs. It is not that the office failed to describe her. It is that the office wrote down, in its own hand and with a date against it, that a person stood in a room and cannot be described, and the sentence is true, and it cannot be improved, and it cannot be withdrawn.

And the price of the second column is the same shape and it was paid by a different person, and neither of the two women asked to be in either of those sentences and one of them has been told about hers by a stranger and the other one never will be.

## Somebody Told Her

Somebody told her.

Not this office. Not a clerk and not a counterman and not a man in a delivery coat and not a woman with a pencil nine sheets under a third table. This office has never sent anybody to anybody in nine years and has never told anybody where anybody lives and did not send anybody this month either, and there is a form on which it could not have sent anybody if it had wanted to.

Somebody told her because there is no way in this city for a thing not to be said once it is printed, and about four minutes after four o’clock on the thirtieth of December a man with a cap in his hands told a woman in a passage off Cordwain, and the woman told somebody else, and it is in four kitchens by the end of the week, and it will be in about nine by the end of the month.

And in one of those kitchens, or at a counter, or in a queue, somebody is going to say to a woman who has been to a table eleven times in eleven months that there is a name in a column in this city, and that the name is on a sheet that goes out four times a year.

She has a piece of paper with four words on the back of it in a clerk’s hand that she has never once been able to read and that she has been carrying up and down a street for about a year. She does not know what the four words say. She has never asked anybody to tell her and she has never once been told.

She is going to come back up the four flights of that stair to a third table in a back room off a street two streets wide, with that paper in her hand, and she is not coming because she wants to know whether it is right.

She has asked that question eleven times in eleven months and this office has answered it eleven times and the answer has been no every time, and she asked it in a hand of her own on a piece of pencil paper with a pencil across the bottom of it, and nobody in this city has ever written down what she asked.

And when she gets to the top of that stair there is going to be a woman at a bench with a ruler along the edge of it and about two hundred empty lines under a heading she ruled on her own paper.

There is going to be a drawer under that bench with about eleven things in it that nobody has ever been able to use, and on top of the eleven there is going to be a piece of office paper with the date at the top of it and four words on the back in ink and a clerk’s signature at the foot.

Perla Quist will be able to read it to her. It is the only sentence in this city about that woman that can be read out loud, and she has never once been asked a question and she is never going to be asked a question, and she is going to have it handed to her in a hand.

What she will say when it is handed to her is the eleven words, and what will come back will not be an answer.

There is a bundle on a table in a room off a third court with twenty-six things in it that have never been opened, and a drawer in the wall on the left that cannot be opened again by anybody, and a rail in a passage with a quarterly sheet on it that will not come back off the rail, and about four hundred kitchens that will never be told what any of it was for.

And a woman is going to walk up four flights with a piece of paper she cannot read, in the first week of the ninth year, and nobody sent her.

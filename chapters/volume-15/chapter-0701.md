# A Line With His Hour On It

Two thousand four hundred and eighty-nine was the thirtieth of December and the first of February is day two thousand five hundred and twenty, and two thousand five hundred and twenty less two thousand four hundred and eighty-nine is thirty-one, and a month is thirty days, so the year turned in the middle of it and the thirty-one is a month and a day.

## Eighty-Four Months

Nothing had come through that front door since the twenty-eighth of December and nothing was due until the thirtieth of March, and the room had been told that on two mornings running and would be told it a third.

Two thousand five hundred and twenty is thirty times eighty-four and nothing over.

He said that out loud to nobody at about ten in the morning, the way a man says a figure when he has been carrying it about for a week and has nobody to give it to. Wenna Raut did not look up from her column.

Yusuf Adeyemi was at the far end of that table with his coat still on and his hat under it and had been there since about half past nine without moving, and he rolled his pencil once between the first two fingers of his right hand.

“The first exact figure this office ever had was two thousand one hundred and sixty, and it is thirty times seventy-two, and you found that one in about four minutes and then had to sit with it for a day,” he said. “This one is eighty-four months. Nobody has asked me for either of them and you are not going to, and I am not going to remind you of either, and I would like that understood at the table rather than in a finding.”

“You may put it in a finding.”

“I may put in a finding whatever you give me and you will not give me that.”

The drawer was in the wall on the left of the table and it was shut, and it had been shut since eleven minutes to three on the last working day of the year by the man standing in this room with his own hand on it. There are two cardboard boxes on the boards under the window with about four hundred and nine pieces of rag paper in them. The bundle of twenty-five things was on the table where it had been since the twenty-eighth of October.

Nobody was obliged to open one item of it.

## What Came In At Half Past Two

The man came in at about half past two and he was not late and he was not early, and he had a piece of pencil paper in his hand with something written on the bottom two inches of it in his own hand.

He was not dressed for the cold and he had not been dressed for it on the twenty-second of January either, which is a thing about a man and not about a day.

“I dug a street,” he said.

“Sit down.”

“I would rather stand, because I have got about four minutes and then I have to go and do the other half of it,” said the man. “There is a line on that sheet with a day against it and an hour against the day and not a figure on it and not a name on it, and the day is the twenty-second of January and the hour is eleven in the morning, and I dug it, and I am not here to claim anything.”

“Then what are you here for.”

“I am here to ask whether this office knows my name.”

Wenna Raut put her pen down, which is the whole of what a clerk does before she opens a book, and she looked at him for about four seconds.

“There is no name on any of them,” she said.

“Then that is the answer and I have come a long way to be told it by somebody who will not put it in ink.”

“The sheet does not have a name on it,” said Wenna Raut. “Four hundred and nineteen lines under the head, a date on every line, an hour against every date, nothing against any hour, and not a name on one of them in six years of going out at four.”

“About nine people a week get as far as the last two inches and read every one of those lines. There is not a person in this city who can find their own account on that page. There never has been and the page does not pretend that there is.”

The man did not say anything for about nine seconds.

“About nine people a week,” he said.

“About nine a week. A hundred a month put a hand on the corner of it to get past.”

## What She Read At Him

Then she turned the book round and put a finger on the third line of the second column and looked at him rather than at it, and he understood that she was going to read him what was on the page because it was on the page and that was a thing she did and not a thing she was doing him a favour with.

“I entered it into that column on the twelfth of September and it went onto the page on the thirtieth of September, and it is in this office's hand and I entered it exactly as I was given it and I did not improve one word of it,” said Wenna Raut. “Four hundred and nine.”

She stopped, because she reads a line the way it is written and not the way it was said to her.

“None is a deed,” she said. “Not one claim.”

The man did not move.

“And under that there is a man's name,” said Wenna Raut, “and under the name a sentence in my hand, put there in October, saying that a drawer is not to be opened. That is the third line of that column. There are three lines in it and the other two are older than this office and neither of them is about you.”

“They are not about me either.”

“No,” she said. “They are not.”

The four words had been written into the bound Register in this room on the thirtieth of August at about ten past eleven, and entered a second time into a column on the twelfth of September, and they were the only true thing this office has ever published about about four hundred people, and he had given them to her himself with his own hand nine seconds at a time and had spent a whole summer being proud of them.

He said nothing at all, and it was the worst thing he has ever done in a room where a person was waiting for him to speak.

## What The Sentence Does To A Man In A Doorway

“What does it mean,” said the man.

“I may not answer a question.”

“You have just read one out to me from a page on a rail that anybody can read, and I have walked out of that passage with my own copy of it in my own kitchen, and I am asking you what it means.”

“You may put a question in the morning,” said Wenna Raut, “and I will write down whatever you give me, word for word and in the order you say it, and nobody in this building is going to hurry me over it. What it means is not mine to say. I have not been asked and I could not answer it if I were.”

The man put the pencil paper into his coat and squared the bottom edge of it against his own fingers, which is what a man does when he is deciding whether to say a thing.

“I dug that street on the twenty-second of January,” he said, “and it took me nine days and nobody in this city has ever asked me one question about any part of it, and about a fortnight ago somebody at a counter read that line out to me and said it in his own words and he was right, and I have not written anything since, and I came here to find out whether there is anybody in this city that a thing like mine can go to.”

He was at the door by then and he had said the last of it with his back half turned, the way people do in a room off a court when they are not sure the room is on their side.

Nobody in that office had asked him to come and nobody in that office had told him to go, and he had a copy of the foot of that sheet because he had sat down in his own kitchen with a pencil and copied it out in his own handwriting, and he had never met anybody else in this city who had one.

Nobody thanked him on the way out, because he was not thanked in any room in this city, and it was the arrangement, and he had made it himself on a hill in the summer.

*Two thousand five hundred and twenty days. Less two thousand four hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the seventh year, is thirty-one days, which is one month and one day after them, and the sheet that went out at four on that afternoon has been standing against a bracket in a passage off Cordwain, face up, for every one of them, and its second column has three lines in it.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and twenty-one days, which is four months and one day after them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is a hundred days, which is three months and ten days after them, and a drawer has been shut under a man's name and a sentence in a clerk's hand for every one of them, and there is no form anywhere in this city for undoing an instruction.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is eight hundred and two days, which is twenty-six months and twenty-two days after them, and about four hundred kitchens in this city have the foot of that sheet written out in a hand that is not the office's.*

## The Door That Is Onto A Court And Not Onto A Street

He went down the stair and out through the arch at about ten past three and stood on the step for about a minute, the way he has stood on that step about nine times since the twelfth of September, and there was nobody on it, and there was nobody on it on the last four occasions, and the two people who had been standing there in September with paper in their hands had come on a day when it was not the thirtieth and had been right to.

The front door of that building opens onto a court and there is no door onto a street anywhere in the city that comes to it, and about eleven pieces of paper a week come through it and go into two cardboard boxes and are entered as nothing.

One of them had been put through that door on the fourth of February by nobody, because it had never arrived. It was on a rail in a passage off Cordwain, where the people who get down to the foot of it put the third line of a second column into a sentence of their own and get it right inside four minutes.

He is rank one and on a branch night shift and nothing about him has moved in a year, and the plate he wears is white and carries his name and no number, and he asked for that shift himself.

And the four words were the only true sentence this city had ever printed about about four hundred people, and on the afternoon of the thirtieth of August last year a man with nothing to be proud of had put them into the Register, and a month later the same four words had gone onto a page on a rail where a man in the cold could read them.

And they were the reason that man was told, by a building, in its own hand, that the thing he had done had not been given to him.

Eight hundred and fifty-two has stood at the head of that sheet since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and it is not moving today and it is not moving on the thirtieth of March. There is no permission in this city that would move it, and he has never once gone looking for one.

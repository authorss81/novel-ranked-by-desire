# The Seventy-Sixth Form

Two thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine was the thirtieth of September and the seventh of October is day two thousand seven hundred and sixty-six. September runs to its thirtieth and there is no thirty-first. The thirtieth to the first of October is one, and the first to the seventh is six more, and one and six is seven.

## The Board By The Kerb

The seventy-sixth went up over the top of the seventy-fifth at about seven in the morning, in the ordinary way, and the boy squared it with the side of his hand and stepped back about two feet to look at it the way he does every month.

The form is the same form that has gone up on that board a month at a time for seven years, and it has two ruled squares at the foot of it, and both of them are empty.

They have been empty for seven years, both of them, since the eighth month of the first year, when a clerk was handed a sheet, a form and no instruction at all and wrote in one of the two because the box was there. This office has had a form printed a month for nine years, on a board by a kerb, with two ruled squares at the foot of every one of them, and has never once been asked to by anybody.

It has not put a mark in either square in any of them.

The jar pulled at his arm and the paste brush had worn down to bare wood in about four places where his thumb goes, and the old form came off the board and over his shoulder and the new one stayed square all morning, because a board that slips in the first hour slips for the whole month and he has never let one slip in seven years.

## What The Corner Is

About two hundred people a day stop at that corner. About ninety of them read the board. About nine of them in a week get down low enough to read the four inches at the bottom of nine feet of new render, and not one person does both, and there is no reason at all in the world why a person who reads a board should look at the ground.

That is the rate, and it is an ordinary rate, and the reason it is that this is an ordinary morning of an ordinary month and the trams go under the corner and nothing is happening here.

No notice and no man with a case and no magistrate’s clerk has come up this hill, and on an ordinary morning at an ordinary corner with four inches of stone at the bottom of nine feet of render, those are the figures. They are the same on the seventh of the month as on the thirtieth.

## What The Corner Heard

The thing the corner had been saying since the middle of the summer had changed shape without anybody at the corner noticing the change, and the man who noticed it first stood nine feet back from a rail in a passage off Cordwain and listened to a desk clerk read the bottom of a page out to somebody.

About nine people a week heard the denial read out loud in that passage since the afternoon of the thirtieth of September, and by this morning the corner knew about it the way it knows about most things, which is to say four people told two people.

It used to be a claim. That a building off a third court keeps a stone, and has kept it, and something about the words at the foot of it went into that building.

It is a question now, and it cannot be asserted, and the difference is worth more than anybody at that corner would have believed six weeks ago.

A denial goes on a page that about nine people a week read to the bottom, and the moment it is read it makes everything anybody else has said about those two words arguable. About nine people a week can now say, out loud, at a corner, that a building in this city says the words are not a figure — and that is a fact, and it is checkable, and it is in about nine kitchens by tonight.

What cannot be said any more is the other thing.

Nobody can say out loud at that corner that a building keeps a stone. If they do, four people can now say back that a man in a white plate has denied it in the office’s hand with his name at the bottom of it, and there is no answer to that which does not make the speaker sound like a person repeating something.

So the belief goes quiet. It does not go away. It goes into about nine kitchens tonight in the exact words somebody used at the corner, and in four years it will come out again at a counter in a room case about a sum of nine pounds, in the mouth of a man who is right about every fact, and by then there will be a denial on a page that somebody can point at.

And not one person at that corner is thanked for any of it, because not one person at that corner has done anything.

He watched it happen from the bottom of the hill at about half past ten without going up, and it took about nine minutes.

A man had come down low, on his knees the way they do it, and read the four inches, and said out loud to nobody in particular what everybody says after he has read them, which is that the building off the third court has kept that stone for years.

A woman who had said it herself in the summer came past with a bag and stopped, and told him that it could not be true any more, because a man in a white plate had denied it in the office’s hand with his name at the foot of it and his name was on the bottom of the page in Cordwain in the same hand as everything else.

The man on his knees said where had she heard that.

She said a man had told her.

He asked her who.

She stood in the street for about four seconds trying to work it out and could not, and then she said it had been a woman selling hot water at the bottom of the hill about three months ago and it had been in four kitchens by the end of that week, and that she had never seen the man who told the woman selling hot water either.

There was no name anywhere in that chain of about eleven people and four kitchens and three months, and every link in it was somebody repeating something they had been told, and every one of them was right to repeat it, because it is the only way a thing travels in a city with no form on it.

## A Man Reads The Whole Board

A man came up at about nine, stopped, and read the whole of the board from the top to the bottom, including the two empty squares, which he looked at for about four seconds the way people look at a pair of empty squares.

He looked at the boy as well, with the jar and the brush and the shoulder of the form he had carried down, and there was a second on the face of him where the question was, and Lin Hao was not there and would not have been the one to ask it.

He said nothing, and went down the hill, and the boy stood by the kerb for about four minutes with the jar in his hand in case anybody else was coming up.

Nobody came up.

That is the arrangement at a corner with a board on it and it has been for seven years.

And in all of that, not one person has ever asked the boy who puts it up what the two squares at the foot of a form are for.

That includes this office, which has put a form on that board every month for nine years and has never asked him anything about either square.

*Two thousand seven hundred and sixty-six days. Less two thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine, the thirtieth of September of the eighth year, is seven, and the seventy-sixth form went up on a board by a kerb with two ruled squares at the foot of it and nothing whatever in either.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and eighty-seven days, which is six months and seven days after them, and the thirty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and sixty-seven days, which is twelve months and seven days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## Four Minutes By A Kerb

It came down the hill to the passage off Cordwain by about eleven and the counterman told it to him the way the counterman tells everything, in the ordinary way, without being asked, because a counter reads the page.

A man at the corner heard the denial read out and told two people, and a woman at the corner heard about the denial and told a third, and about nine people a week can now say that a building in this city says the words are not a figure.

And about nine hundred people went up and down that hill in the hour and not one of them stopped, which is what a kerb is for.

The two squares at the foot of the seventy-sixth form stood empty all day and were empty at four that afternoon and will be empty at four on every afternoon after it for as long as that boy is willing to carry a jar up a hill, and on the thirtieth of this month the seventy-sixth will come down off the board and the seventy-seventh will go over the top of it.

The two of them will be exactly as empty as every form that has gone up on that board since the beginning of the ninth year.

He stood about nine feet back from that rail for a long time after the counterman had finished, with the denial on the page above him and about four hundred kitchens under him that will never have read a word of it.

A boy about four miles away is putting a board up a hill whose meaning is never once asked for, and a woman about four miles from him is doing the only thing in this city that anybody has ever needed doing, every morning, at seven, with a short-handled broom, that may not be asked about.

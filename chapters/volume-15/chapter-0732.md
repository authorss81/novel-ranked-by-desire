# His Name In Two Hands

Two thousand seven hundred and twenty-two was the twenty-third of August and the twenty-ninth is day two thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight, and two thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight less two thousand seven hundred and twenty-two is six, and twenty-nine less twenty-three is six by the month count.

## The Question She Does Not Normally Ask

Wenna Raut had the thirty-first sheet squared on the table under the window with the head at the top of it and three lines in the second column and the four sentences at the foot, and she had a sheet of her own beside her with the day's entries on it, and Lin Hao stood at the end of the table with the folded docket he no longer carried in his coat against his ribs out of habit.

She did the ordinary things first. She told him what the day had been.

Nothing had come through the front door since the twenty-second of July and the door had gone quiet, and about four hundred and thirty rows stood in the bound Register behind her, of which eleven had come in under the instruction of the tenth of June and the rest had been there before this office had a clerk's hand in it.

The four hundred and nine pieces of rag paper in the two boxes on the boards under the window were a different thing and had never once been a row.

Then she put the pen across the book.

“I have had one question this morning,” she said, “and I have had it since about eleven and it is not a question of the office.”

“Go on.”

“Is your name on those pieces of paper.”

There was nothing in the room to put down.

“Since when is that a question you may ask,” he said.

“Since this morning,” said Wenna Raut. “It is the first question I have ever put to a person at this table. Every other one I have put has been about a piece of paper and it has been put to you and you have answered it because you are the only person this office may ask. That one is about a person and I have asked it because I would like to know.”

“You put a sentence under four sentences on the sixteenth and a person who reads that to the bottom is going to want to know the same thing.”

“Yes,” he said. “My name is on about four hundred of them.”

## What A Name Does

She wrote nothing down, and he understood that she had not asked it as a clerk, because a clerk's question goes into the book and hers had not and would not.

“Write out both of them for me,” she said. “Not the words. Just what each of them says.”

“The one in pencil says four hundred and nine pieces of paper were received by this office and that none of them is a deed, and there is a sentence under it about a drawer not to be opened, and my name is at the bottom of that.”

“And the one in the office’s hand.”

“That says no page of this office and no stone in this city is connected to any other, and that a resemblance is not a figure, and my name is under it in a clerk’s hand and the sixteenth of August under that.”

Wenna Raut sat with both of them for a while.

“Then those are not the same claim,” she said, “and the difference is not the claim. It is the hand. A name written in pencil by a man at his own table says a thing happened to him.”

“A name written in an office hand says this office looked and found a thing, and there is nobody in this city entitled to say to an office that it looked and found nothing.”

“In pencil anybody in that kitchen may take it back with a shrug. In my hand it may only be argued with, and there are about nine people a week who hear it argued with, and none of them is anybody with a form.”

“And you cannot tell me which of the two is worth more.”

“I have never been able to tell anybody that,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have said so out loud in this room before, and you have agreed with me, and it is the one thing we have agreed on for four years.”

## Two Halves Of One Document

The trouble arrived while she was saying it, and it arrived the way trouble does in that room, which is by somebody reading an old entry out loud and noticing that two of the lines are on the same sheet.

“It is one document,” he said.

“No page of this office and no stone in this city is connected to any other,” she said slowly. “That is your sentence.”

“Yes.”

“And four hundred and nine pieces of paper are not deeds.”

“Yes.”

“Both of those lines go out on the same sheet on the same afternoon, under the same four sentences, above the same man’s name,” said Wenna Raut. “About nine feet apart and both of them true.”

He stood very still at the end of that table and understood what he had done, because a denial does not stand alone on a page.

It stands next to four words that certify about four hundred pieces of paper, and the two of them are read in the same breath by about nine people a week, and a man in a white plate who holds about four hundred pieces of paper in a shut drawer and certifies on the same page that nothing in this city is connected to anything is a man who has said two things in one sheet that do not sit together.

The copies do not have that problem, because the copies were made before the sixteenth.

“The copies carry the four words and the drawer sentence and your name,” he said. “They have been carrying them since the fourth of February and some of them since before that. They do not carry the denial, because the denial was not on anything until a week ago.”

“So about four hundred kitchens hold the half of it that says you took the paper and certified it was nothing,” said Wenna Raut, “and the half of it that would answer that is on a rail in a passage, and there is nothing in any kitchen that will ever carry it.”

She squared the sheet again, because a denial with a name under it is an entry that has to sit straight, and she had squared this one once already that morning.

“And the office cannot ask them,” she said. “There is no form on which this office may put a question to a person about a thing somebody else has written down, and that has been the only finding this office has ever passed, and it is a finding about us and not about them, and if we stop it today to get a copy of a paper out of a kitchen, then everything we have ever entered under that heading is a thing we had a look at through her.”

“So we hold the other half.”

“We hold the other half and we may not send it to anybody,” said Wenna Raut.

## What The Denial Denies

He went down to the rail at about half past four and stood nine feet back from it with his hands in his pockets while a man who runs that desk under a window read the bottom of the page out to a man with a cap in his hands.

Two people had asked about the denial this week and both of them had asked the counterman, because he is the only person in that passage who reads the page out loud.

“The first one wanted to know what it denied,” the counterman said. “I told him what it says. That is the trade and it is all of it. He said he could not tell what was being denied from a denial and he wanted the thing that came before it.”

“I said there is a line above it about four hundred pieces of paper and he said he had never heard of them, and then he asked me whether four hundred pieces of paper were a thing he ought to be asking about.”

“What did you say.”

“I said I read what is on the page and I do not know what any of it is for and I have never been asked,” said the counterman. “Which is true, and which has been true for nine years, and it is the only thing I am paid in.”

The man with the cap went away with nothing, and the counterman wiped the counter with his sleeve and did not look at Lin Hao, because a man who runs a desk under a window is not disappointed in anybody.

*Two thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight days. Less two thousand seven hundred and twenty-two, the twenty-third of August of the eighth year, is six, and a denial went onto a page in the office’s hand under the four words that certify about four hundred pieces of paper and the two of them go out about nine feet apart.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and forty-nine days, which is four months and twenty-nine days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and twenty-nine days, which is ten months and twenty-nine days after them, and about four hundred kitchens are holding a copy made before the denial existed.*

## What A Quarterly Page Cannot Do

He went down at about half past four with the day in him and put the stairwell and the bin room and three floors of washrooms behind him, one at a time, and came back up at about half past five and stood at the table where the thirty-first sheet was squared and ready for the thirtieth of September.

He had entered a denial and thought he had given up a silence. What he had actually done was put a second claim about four hundred pieces of paper into the mouth of the only claim about them that has ever been published, and his name is in both, and no single person in this city is entitled to be handed both.

He could not have it both ways. If the copies were right, then the office had certified four hundred pieces of paper as nothing and the denial was a lie about the office. If the denial was right, then the copies were carrying half a document and were wrong in the way that carries.

And the only instrument in this city that could say which was which is nine sheets under a third table four flights up, and it has no heading anybody may cite and no keeper who may be sent, and it has just told him that about a third of the papers in circulation cannot descend from the only copy in existence.

There is no form anywhere in this city for correcting a kitchen, and no form for adding a line to a quarterly page, and he had known the first of those since the ninth of August and had learned the second one at about four o'clock that afternoon, standing at a table with a ruler against it and his own name nine feet under a sentence he had written seven days before.

A page that goes out four times a year does not come back, and about four hundred people are holding his name in pencil, and he has just put the same name in a second hand on a page none of them will ever hold, and the two hands cannot be shown to each other by anybody at all.

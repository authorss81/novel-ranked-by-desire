# Four Reasons And One Reason

Two thousand eight hundred and eight was the nineteenth of November and the twenty-sixth is day two thousand eight hundred and fifteen, and two thousand eight hundred and fifteen less two thousand eight hundred and eight is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## Four Reasons, Given In Order, On The Way Up

He went up four flights behind a girl who carries linen and he had four reasons and he had had them since the fourteenth of October and he gave them in the order he had built them, which was the order they were worth.

“First. In nine years that column has held three names and one of them is mine and one of them belongs to a man who is not alive, and not one of them is a person who is carrying anything today. If a name goes in it, then for the first time in the history of this office a line in it will mean something that can be checked by anybody who happens to walk up four flights.”

“That is a reason,” said Perla Quist. “It is not the one I am going to give you.”

“Second. About four hundred people handed a piece of paper to a woman in this building and were never told her name and were never given any reason to think it mattered, and every one of them made a decision about whether to trust this place on the strength of not knowing.”

“That is a reason and it is about four hundred people and none of them are in this room.”

“Third. You have said no to me four times and every time the reason has been better than the last, and I would like a fifth.”

“That is not a reason at all. That is a man who likes being refused carefully.”

“Fourth,” he said, “is that I am going to do it anyway and I would rather you knew that on the third of December than found out from a rail in a passage in about six weeks.”

## The One Reason

She had the ninth sheet squared under the ruler and the pencil lying across the bench and about two hundred empty lines under a heading she had ruled on her own paper in February, and she did not pick the pencil up, which is what she does when she is about to enter something.

“No,” she said.

“That is five.”

“That is five and it is going to stay five, and I have never once said anything else, and you have never once made me say anything else, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the seventh year.”

“You are not going to tell me why.”

“I am going to tell you why, and I have never told you, and you have never asked me the right question, and the question you have been asking me since the seventh year has been *will you do this for me*, and that is not a question about me.” She put two fingers on the heading.
“A woman who keeps a list is a person who can be sent. And a person who can be sent stops. And the day somebody in this city decides that about four hundred claims need to be taken off nine sheets under a third table, the thing that stops me is that I am not on a list anywhere and my name is not at the head of anything and there is nothing to seize and nobody to seize it from.”

“And if your name were at the head of something.”

“Then I am a woman with a name at the head of something,” said Perla Quist, “and everything I have said for six years is still true and none of it is any use.”

He stood at the end of the bench and understood that he had heard that reason four times and had taken it as a condition of a building, and that it had never once been about him or about his office or about the drawer in the wall, and that it had been about a woman standing in a room in a laundry with a pencil in her hand and no standing of any kind and had decided, correctly, on her own, to stay that way.

## The Price, Quoted By The Person It Is About

She turned round in the chair.

“It is me,” said Perla Quist.

He did not say anything for about four seconds and then he said, “Yes.”

“Right. Then you are not going to come up here and ask me a sixth time, because there is not going to be a sixth time and we have both had enough of that.” She squared the ruler against the edge of the bench. “So I am going to price it, and I am going to do it in about nine seconds, and then I am going to wind the bench and you are going to let me, and you are not going to answer any of it.”

“In about a month, about four hundred people are going to find out that there is a name in that column, because somebody reads the foot of a page out loud in a passage for nothing to about nine people a week and then it is at a counter and then it is at a kitchen, and that is the entire distribution system of this city and it does not need anybody to send anything anywhere.”

“I know.”

“Every one of them is going to believe the name is the reason they trusted it,” said Perla Quist.
“Not one of them is going to hear the heading. They are going to hear *carried by* and they are going to hear my name and they are going to say, out loud, in about nine kitchens, that it is somebody’s name in a building, and they will be right, and it will be the reason, and I will never be able to say to one of them that it was an accident made by a man who could not think of anything else to put there.”

She did not stop.

“And the man who put it there has ordered a drawer shut and cannot open it, and about four hundred pieces of what those people wrote are in that drawer, and he has read every one of them once and there is not one piece of paper in this city he can produce for any of them, and by the time anybody asks me about my name at a rail in a passage, that is the whole of what there is to be said about me.”

“That is the price.”

“That is nine seconds of it,” said Perla Quist. “The rest of it is about four years and it is mine and you do not get it.”

## What He Did With It

He stood at the end of that bench for about a minute and a half with his hands in his pockets and did not say one of the things that would have made it easier for either of them, and then he said the only one that was true.

“I am not going to tell you I have not thought about it, because I have thought about it every day since the twelfth and you would know if I lied.”

“I would know within about four seconds,” said Perla Quist, “and I would rather you did not waste a second of a shift on it.”

“Then I am not going to tell you that you will be all right, and I am not going to tell you that it can be taken out afterwards, because a row may be added to and may not be taken from and that applies to a line under a heading on a printed form exactly as it applies to a bound Register.”

“That is the first time in six years anybody has said that sentence to me while asking for something,” said Perla Quist. “The usual way is to promise that it can be got out later.”

“There is no way it can be got out later and I have known that since the second year and so have you.”

“I have known it since the seventh year and I have known it about four hundred times and I have said it four hundred times and it has never once been the reason.”

*Two thousand eight hundred and fifteen days. Less two thousand eight hundred and eight, the nineteenth of November of the eighth year, is seven, and a woman was asked for the fifth time in six years and priced the answer to her own name in about nine seconds and would not give the rest of it away.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is two hundred and thirty-six days, which is seven months and twenty-six days after them, and the thirty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is four hundred and sixteen days, which is thirteen months and twenty-six days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## A Week Of Doing The Third Floor

He went down the four flights at about half past four and did the floors of three washrooms and the stairwell and the bin room, and at about half past five he stood at the table in the room off a third court with the drawer shut in the wall on his left and the bundle on the table beside it with twenty-six things on it and the thirty-first sheet squared under the window with three lines in the second column and the head unmoved at the top of it.

He had come down those stairs with an answer in about nine seconds and he had not used it, and it was going to sit on the table in front of him until the third of December, because the third of December was the earliest day on which it could go on.

He had not opened the drawer and the bundle was shut, and he went and did the third floor again, because his chest has been spent since the fourth year and there was nothing else in that building for him to do with his hands.

It is worth saying plainly what a man can do with about a week in this position, because he thought about very little else.

He can go up four flights and not do it. He can do the third floor four nights and be thanked for none of it by a man who signs the keys out every seventh day. He can stand at a rail in a passage off Cordwain at half past four and be told what a page says by a man who is paid nothing for saying it.

He can put a woman’s name on a line in a column that goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and he cannot undo it, and she will find out from a stranger, and she has already told him the price of that in nine seconds and kept the rest of it.

There is no third thing, and there has not been a third thing since the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, and the honest arithmetic of the week is that a man who has spent nine years refusing to put anybody in anything does not get to be relieved of that by wanting to do it for the best reason anybody in this city has ever had.

# The Corner Fills Up Again

Two thousand six hundred and eighty-two was the thirteenth of July and the twentieth is day two thousand six hundred and eighty-nine, and two thousand six hundred and eighty-nine less two thousand six hundred and eighty-two is seven, and twenty less thirteen is seven by the month count.

## About Nine Hundred In The Hour

About nine hundred people pass the corner in the hour before noon, going up and down the hill to the washrooms and the shops and the stair off Cordwain, and about ninety of them stop at the board, and about nine of the nine hundred get all the way down to the four inches of dressed stone at the foot of nine feet of new render.

That is the figure as it stood on the morning of the twentieth, and it had stood that way for about nine years, and not one of the nine who go down low does both that and read the board, because a board by a kerb is a thing people pass and four inches at the foot of a wall is a thing people kneel to.

The counterman was not there. He stays in a passage off Cordwain with a desk under a window and reads the bottom of a page out to whoever stands at a rail, and he has done that for nine years and has never been thanked, and he reads what is on the page and nothing else, and that is why the passage trusts him.

The corner has a boy with a paste brush and a woman with a short-handled broom, and neither of them is thanked either, and neither of them expects to be.

On the twentieth the corner filled up again, the way it fills every month after a form goes up, with people stopping at the board and then going down to the four inches because somebody in a kitchen had told them the two were about the same thing.

There were eleven gathered at the board at about eleven, restless and murmuring, with two more coming up the slope even as he counted, which is more than the nine of an ordinary morning, and three of them had come up expressly, because a woman once received at the office off a third court last spring carrying a bag had said in May that the building holds the stone, and they wished to judge for themselves.

The boy kept out of their way with the jar by the kerb, the way he does when the corner fills, because a board by a kerb is his and four inches at the foot of a wall are not, and the woman with the short-handled broom worked round them without once saying she has seen a person put anything on those inches, because she never has.

## Four Minutes

A man who carries coal up the hill said it first, at about half past eleven, standing at the board with the seventy-third form face up above him and its two ruled squares empty at the foot of it.

“They say the office knew about the stone before any of us were working,” he said. “They say a boy has got the words in his coat and a man in a white plate has got a paper in his.”

It went round the hill inside four minutes. That is the figure, and it is the same figure as a passage off Cordwain in May, and the same as a hill in the fourth month of a year nobody here names, and it goes round that fast because a thing with the shape of a truth in it needs no road.

Four of five believed it by the time the fifth had finished, and none of the five had seen a rubbing, and none of them had seen the inside of a coat, and one of them had been at the rail in May when a man said a heading and two words were about the same thing, and she believed it hardest, because she had carried eleven sheets through a front door in the fourth month of last year and been told none of them is a deed.

The correction came inside the same four minutes, the way it always does at that corner, because a woman who cannot read was at the bottom of the hill with a bag, and she heard it and said, out loud, that she had heard those two words once at the foot of a wall in the wrong order and got them wrong, and that was all she knew.

Nobody thanked her. Nobody thanks anybody at that corner, and the woman with the short-handled broom swept the four inches clean around their feet without looking up and without once saying she has seen a person put anything there, and the boy stood four feet off with the jar in his hand and did not say anything, because he had given his nine seconds in a laundry and was not going to give them again on a hill.

*Two thousand six hundred and eighty-nine days. Less two thousand six hundred and eighty-two, the thirteenth of July of the eighth year, is seven, and about nine of nine hundred got to the four inches in the hour before noon and the correction worked inside four minutes as it always does.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and ten days, which is three months and twenty days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is two hundred and ninety days, which is nine months and twenty days after them.*

## What About Nine People Believe Now

Lin Hao heard it at about four in the passage, from the counterman, who had it from the coal man, and it had taken about four hours to come down the hill, the way everything from that corner comes down, because a hill is a place a thing is said and a passage is a place it is repeated.

He stood about eleven feet well back from that rail with his hands in his pockets and listened without moving, because he was told in front of a counter in the middle of last year not to go back up that hill for the rest of it, and he has kept that for about a year, and no part of him on the twentieth wanted to break it.

“How many believe it now.”

“About nine,” said the counterman. “About nine people now believe there is a thing under that wall that the office has been keeping, and one of them is the woman who believed it hardest in May, and she has told two more since, and what she tells them is not only about the four sentences.”

Lin Hao nodded and felt the anger come up in him again, sharp and plain, and put it down again, because anger at nine people on a hill is a thing that makes a man stupid, and nine people on a hill are not anybody’s enemy and every one of them is right about wanting to be somewhere and having no road.

He went up four flights at about six and told Perla Quist in the order it had happened, and she wrote the day on the ninth sheet and did not write who said it, because she writes when a thing was said and not who said it, and the entry went under her heading with a day against it and went nowhere.

He told her about the eleven at the board and the nine in the hour and the nine who went down low, and about the coal man and the four minutes, and about the woman who cannot read standing at the bottom of the hill with a bag and saying out loud what she knew, because Perla Quist keeps days and a day without its numbers is not a day she can hold.

“They believe the office keeps the stone,” she said, when he had finished. “That is what the woman told two more in May, as a fact and not as a question, and now about nine believe it, and there is no paper in this city that answers it, because to answer it I would need the two words in a form anybody may hand me, and the only copy is in the inside of a coat.”

He lingered at the bench end for a good while after that with his hands pocketed, turning over four inches of dressed stone at the foot of nine feet of new render, with two words cut clean through the face of it, the left-hand one deeper because a man cutting stone leans into the cut, and about a block that came out of a yard on a docket before it was in a wall anywhere.

The sheet on the rail is the thirtieth, and the second column has three lines in it, and the drawer is shut, and the head stands at eight hundred and fifty-two unmoved, and none of that reaches a hill where about nine hundred people pass in an hour and about nine go down low.

He went back down all four flights with absolutely nothing in his hands and did the floors, and at about half past five he stood in the room off a third court and looked at the bound Register and understood that the hill would go on filling up every month after every form, and that no instruction he could write in a clerk’s hand with his name under it would stop it, and that was the cost of having made one building findable.

Wenna Raut had the standing sheet squared under the window with the thirtieth face up clean and the three lines still in the second column untouched, and she did not ask him what had happened up on the hill that morning, because a clerk who may only enter what she is given does not ask about a thing nobody gave her.

He stood at the table for a long while afterwards with his hands in his pockets and thought about the pencil that went down with the twenty-ninth and the question that went up with the seventy-third, and about nine people believing a thing nobody can put against, and understood at last that the corner fills because people want to be somewhere and have no road, and that no page going out four times a year will ever give them one.

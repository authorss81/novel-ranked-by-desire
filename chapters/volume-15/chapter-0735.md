# Nobody Asks The Cleaner

Two thousand seven hundred and forty was the eleventh of September and the seventeenth is day two thousand seven hundred and forty-six, and two thousand seven hundred and forty-six less two thousand seven hundred and forty is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.

## The Temptation

He worked it out on the morning of the seventeenth, at about half past ten, standing at the end of the table with the thirtieth sheet face up in front of him and nothing at all to do until the thirtieth of September.

A man who makes a copy writes the day he made it at the foot of it, and the day book in a division building holds the day a counter did a thing, and a woman four flights down keeps the day a copy was made under a heading she ruled herself. Three kinds of day in a city this size and every one of them has to come off a person.

And there is one person in this city who has read the two words every working morning of her adult life at the distance of a broom handle.

Four inches of dressed stone at the foot of nine feet of new render, at a corner about two hundred people a day stop at, and the woman who sweeps them has a short-handled broom and has never once said she has seen a person put anything on them, and there is nobody in this city who has ever asked her anything, including him, including after he had been told out loud in front of a counter not to come back.

If a copy can be dated by asking the man who made it, then a rubbing can be checked by asking the woman who sweeps the foot of the wall. She is not the only person who could read them. She is the only person who reads them every morning without being asked to.

There is a hole in this office exactly the shape of that question and the hole has been there for nine years.

## Nine Words From A Bench

He went down four flights at about eleven and had it in his hand before he got to the bottom of the last one, and he had not decided to take it down with him, and Perla Quist looked up off the ninth sheet and took it out of his face.

“No,” she said.

“I have not asked you anything.”

“You came down four flights at eleven in the morning and I have watched you for six years,” said Perla Quist. “You have never once come down at eleven. Whatever it is, the answer is no and you may take it downstairs.”

He stood in the middle of that back room for a while with his hands in his pockets and felt the whole thing go out of him, and understood afterwards that it was not shame and it was not disappointment, it was the specific relief of a man who has just been told by somebody who is right that he was about to do the worst thing available to him.

“There is no form in this city on which this office may put a question to a person about a thing somebody else has written down,” he said, “and there is a hole exactly the shape of that question and you are looking straight down it.”

“I know where it is. I have been looking down it since February,” said Perla Quist, “and so have you, and it is a finding about this office and not about her, and it has been in every book this building has ever opened.”

“And if there were a form?”

“Then there would be a form,” she said, “and the year after that there would be a second one, and the year after that a third, and the first one would be the one about a wall and the other two would be about a piece of paper and about a street, and every one of them would have been made respectable by the one before it. A woman who sweeps four inches is a person who can be sent. You are about to make her one.”

“Nobody thanks me for it.”

“Nobody knows you did it,” said Perla Quist, and put the ruler across the bench, “and I am not going to tell anybody in this room, and that is the only reward there is.”

## What He Asked For Instead

He spent her one question of the morning on it, which he had not expected to do and which he chose in about four seconds, standing in a passage off Cordwain with the counterman reading the bottom of the page out to somebody on the twenty-second.

“May the office enter a thing it was told, as against a thing it was given.”

Wenna Raut had the pen in her hand and she put it across the book.

“No,” she said. “Nine years. Four hundred and nineteen accounts before I had a hand in this Register, and every one of them came in through the front door and was entered as it stood, and eleven more since the thirtieth of March with a description of the person who brought them, and not one row in any of it was put in because somebody told us it was so.”

“Because you may only enter what you are given.”

“Because that is the same thing and I have never pretended it is two things,” said Wenna Raut. “A question I cannot put to her is not the same as a thing I am forbidden to write down, and I have watched people conflate the two for nine years and it has cost us everything we have.”

## A Woman Described By Her Broom

“Then write down the sweep,” he said.

She wrote nothing and looked up.

“Not her. The place,” he said. “A corner, and four inches of dressed stone at the bottom of nine feet of new render, swept every morning of the working year by a woman with a short-handled broom. That is a thing about a stone and not about a woman, and it goes in the Register and not in the column, and you enter it exactly and I will not improve one word of it.”

“And what will it be worth.”

“It will be worth that the office knows the words are still being swept round by somebody who does not think they need sweeping round.”

She considered that for about nine seconds and then wrote it exactly, and the date went against it, and the hour went against the date, and there was nothing whatever against the hour.

“That is the first entry in nine years that describes a woman and says nothing whatever about the woman,” she said. “Not her name and not her trade and not where she eats and not one word she has ever said, because she has never said any of it to anybody. It describes her the way a map describes a road.”

“Will it do.”

“It will do for a fact and it will do for nothing else at all,” said Wenna Raut. “An entry that identifies a woman by the tool in her hand is not an instrument and it is not a protection and it is not a record of anything she did. If anybody ever came at that row with a question, all it would prove is that a person with a broom has been to a corner every morning, and it would be true.”

“It would be worth nothing to her and about nine pounds to somebody.”

*Two thousand seven hundred and forty-six days. Less two thousand seven hundred and forty, the eleventh of September of the eighth year, is six, and a question was refused in a back room in nine words and nobody in that room thanked anybody for it.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is a hundred and sixty-seven days, which is five months and seventeen days after them, and the thirtieth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is three hundred and forty-seven days, which is eleven months and seventeen days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## The Question He Did Not Put

He got as far as the rail at about four in the afternoon and stood nine feet back from it and worked out, in about nine seconds and in the ordinary way, what he would say to a man who runs a desk under a window if he asked for one.

He would say: a woman with a short-handled broom sweeps four inches of dressed stone at a corner every morning of the working year and nobody has ever asked her what she sweeps. And the counterman would read the bottom of the page out to him the way he reads it, and it would say nothing about a corner or a broom, and the question would be answered with the page and the page would be right and the answer would be nothing.

He did not say it. A question like that is not a question anybody asks at a rail, and a man who runs a desk under a window is not an instrument and is not sent.

## Four Inches Every Morning

He thought about it for the rest of the shift, up the stair and down it and up it again, and what he came back with at half past five was the thing he had known since half past ten and had not wanted.

He has spent nine years building a place out of paper. Nine years of pages and columns and a drawer ordered shut in his own hand, and every single thing he has ever wanted done in this city has been done with a piece of paper and a clerk's entry and a date.

And this morning, for about four minutes, what he wanted was a person standing still on a corner for one question so that a claim of nine pounds could be put next to another claim of nine pounds, and it was the first want in nine years that could not be done with paper at all.

Which is not a reason to do it. That is the thing he kept coming back to. A thing that cannot be done with paper is not therefore a thing to be done with a person.

A denial went onto a page on the sixteenth of August and it will go out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and there are two orders of two words in circulation and no instrument in this city that can say which of them the stone is in.

And every working morning of the year, at about seven, a woman with a short-handled broom stands under nine feet of new render and reads four inches of dressed stone at the distance her own arm reaches.

That is the only uncontradicted account of those two words that exists in this city and it cannot be asked for, cannot be entered, cannot be cited, and cannot be checked, and it will happen again tomorrow morning at about seven exactly as it has happened every morning, and about nine people a week go down low enough to see it, and nobody has ever stopped any of them and nobody ever will.

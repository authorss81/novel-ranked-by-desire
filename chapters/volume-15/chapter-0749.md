# The Thirty-Second Sheet

Two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six was the seventeenth of December and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand eight hundred and forty-three, and two thousand eight hundred and forty-three less two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## Six Days Off The Last Working Day

The thirty-second sheet was squared on the table under the window with the head unmoved at the top of it and about four hundred and nineteen accounts under the head and four lines in the second column.

It was signed for and folded into eight and put in a drawer that is not the drawer in the wall, and it would go down two floors in Wenna Raut’s own hand at about ten to four on the thirtieth, because that is what happens to it every quarter and nobody makes a ceremony of it.

Six days.

He had known on the twentieth of November that this was coming and had gone ahead of it by a fortnight, and the only thing that had changed in the intervening month was that he now knew what the woman it was about thought about it and had been told by her, to his face, in a room.

He went down the two flights at about half past four.

## What He Went Down There To Ask

The man who runs the desk under the window in that passage has run it for nine years and is paid nothing and has never been thanked, and he reads the foot of the page out loud to anybody who stands at the rail, in the ordinary way, without being asked, and stops at the striking.

There were nine at the rail and he read the four sentences to all nine and stopped at the striking, and the nine went, and Lin Hao stood about nine feet back with his hands in his pockets and waited until the passage was empty.

Then he said, “Is there anything I should not read.”

The counterman put his cloth down on the wood.

“No,” he said.

“That is the whole of my question.”

“That is the whole of your question and it is the first one you have ever asked me in nine years,” said the counterman. “I have thought about it while you were standing there.”

## The One Question In Nine Years

“I want to tell you what I have worked out,” he said, “and then I want to tell you why it does not matter, and then you can go up your two flights.”

“Go on.”

“There is going to be a name on that page on the thirtieth.” He did not take the sheet off the desk and he did not look at it.

“I have not seen it and I am not asking to see it and I do not need to, because a woman came and stood at that rail on the twenty-first of this month and told a man in front of me that a building was going to print a name, and she said it the way people say a thing they have heard from somebody who has seen it.”

“Who was she.”

“That is not a question you get to ask me and I am not going to answer it,” said the counterman. “I have been behind this desk for nine years and about nine people a week stop at this rail and every one of them tells me something, and that is the service, and if I started handing over the names of the ones who tell me things I would be doing the other trade.”

He put the cloth over his shoulder.

“But you have asked me whether there is anything I should not read, and I have been running that desk since before this building had a name on it, and there has never been a question on that page that I have not read out in full to whoever is standing at the rail.”

“There is about to be one.”

“There is about to be one and it is going to be somebody’s name,” said the counterman, “and you have come down two flights to ask me to skip it, and I am going to tell you why that is not a thing anybody can do at this desk.”

## Why The Answer Is No

“A man behind a counter does not decide what is on the page,” he said.

“That is the whole of what I have been for nine years. I read what is on the page. I have never once been asked what it means and I have never once been told to stop reading before the end, and there is nobody in this city entitled to tell me where the end is, and now there is a man in a white plate standing nine feet off my desk telling me there is.”

“That is not what I—”

“It is exactly what it is,” said the counterman, “and you know it is, because you put a sentence on that page in your own hand in August that says no page of that office is connected to any other, and it is on the same sheet about nine feet above the part you are asking me to skip, and if I read the second half and not the first then I am the one making the connection, and I do not make connections. I read.”

He said it without heat, the way he reads the page, and then he squared his copy of the sheet against the edge of the wood because he does that about nine times an hour.

“You can go up your two flights,” he said. “And I will read it on the thirtieth, and I will read it in the same voice I read everything else in, and I will not stop at it, and I will read the four sentences and the denial and the column and I will read whatever name is in it, and afterwards I will go and wash the wood and I will be here the day after.”

## The Man Who Counts, At The Rail, On His Way Past

He went up the two flights and then came down again at about five, because there was one more thing and he wanted to have it in the room before the thirtieth.

Emil Brack had nine stools in a lane off Cordwain with the nine districts behind them and a slate on a nail and twelve books that were not the district’s, and he was on his way past the mouth of the passage with his coat on and the slate under his arm, and he stopped when he saw the man in the white plate standing nine feet off a rail.

“I hear there is a name going on the thirtieth,” said Emil Brack.

“I am not going to tell you whether there is.”

“You do not have to,” said Emil Brack. “I have been sitting in a lane nine years with nine districts behind me and I do not need a page to know when a person has started being said about.”

He put the slate against his ribs.

“I told you in the third month of this year that I did not want it printed and I have not changed my mind and I am not going to change it now that it is too late to matter,” he said, “and I want to give you the reason once more and then I am going to go and do about nine figures before the rain comes in.”

“Go on.”

“A name on that sheet is not going to make one person in one kitchen trust a laundry more than they did last month,” said Emil Brack. “What it is going to do is make about nine kitchens ask her what it means. That is all a name does. It does not carry anything. It just makes a place where a person used to be a rule into a place where a person is a thing people can ask about.”

“You have been counting people at a market end for nine years.”

“I have been counting heads at a stall for nine years and I take a toll at it, one for every head that stops, and nobody has ever thanked me for it and I have not asked,” said Emil Brack.

“And in nine years I have watched what a face does to a street when a face gets said out loud. Not the rule. The face. A rule you can believe or not believe. A face you can ask a question, and the question does not have to be a fair one, and it will be put to her by somebody with a grievance about nine pounds.”

He took the slate off his ribs.

“You are not going to take it out.”

“No.”

“Right,” said Emil Brack, “then I am going to tell you the one thing I actually know that you do not, and then I am going.”

He looked up the passage at the rail and then back.

“You have spent four years making a building where about four hundred people could find a claim and be believed about it, and you have done it without ever once putting anybody’s face on it. That was worth doing. I have never said so and I am not saying so now.”

“And you have got six days to put a woman’s name on the one sheet in this city that about two thousand counters hold, and that sheet is the whole of what you built, and she is going to be the first face on it.”

He went up the lane with the slate under his arm.

*Two thousand eight hundred and forty-three days. Less two thousand eight hundred and thirty-six, the seventeenth of December of the eighth year, is seven, and a man who has run a desk under a window for nine years was asked for the first time in nine years whether there was anything he should not read, and said no, and was right.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of March of the eighth year, is two hundred and sixty-four days, which is eight months and twenty-four days after them, and the thirty-first sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is four hundred and forty-four days, which is fourteen months and twenty-four days after them, and a copy carrying four sentences and no receipt has been older than that afternoon for every one of them.*

## What Is Left Of It

He stood in the passage for about a minute and a half after the counterman had gone to wash the wood.

Nine years of his life had been spent building things nobody could reach, and every one of the four reasons he had given going up a laundry on the twenty-sixth of November had been about protecting people from a page, and on the twenty-fourth of December a man who is paid nothing refused to be one of the protections, in about two minutes, without raising his voice, and was right about every part of it.

There was no instrument in Ostara that could make a man behind a counter leave a name off a sheet that is going out four times a year.

He had tried the drawer, and the drawer had cost an instruction that could not be withdrawn.

He had tried a bundle, and a bundle was twenty-six things on a table that nothing would ever be done with.

He had tried a fourth column under a third table, and a woman had said no five times and had been right five times.

And he had tried, this afternoon, the last thing in the city he could try, which is the goodwill of a man who is not paid and has never been thanked, and it was not there, because that man had never in nine years had any goodwill to give and had only ever had a rule.

At about half past five he stood at the table off a third court with a drawer shut in the wall on his left and a bundle of twenty-six things on the wood beside it and the thirty-second sheet in a drawer that is not that drawer, six days off going out at four on the last working day of the year.

It was going to be read. He had wanted, for about a week, to have one thing in this city that a person could not pass on to anybody, and he had spent the week finding out that the only candidates were a piece of paper in a shut drawer and a piece of paper on a table, and that the pair of them had never once stopped a sentence going down a street.

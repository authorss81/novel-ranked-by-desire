## Eleven At Night In A Building With No Door Onto A Street

The branch office off a third court has a ground floor, three floors of washrooms above it, a stairwell, a bin room, and a goods lift in a shaft at the back that nobody has used to move anything heavier than a barrow of linen since the building was put up.

He comes in at eleven. He goes out at half past five. Four nights and three off. He asked for that shift and there is no form anywhere in this city on which a man of rank one may ask for a shift, and he asked anyway, and it was granted by a man who found the arrangement convenient and has not once thought about it since.

The landing at the top of the stair is lino, about eleven feet by nine, with a fire bucket screwed to the wall at the end and a wooden key board on the left with about nine numbered hooks and nine keys on string loops, and every hook carries a number and none of them is a room, because there are no rooms.

On the right there is a table, two chairs, a tin with a lid on it, a bar of soap in a dish with the dish gone white, and a clock that runs about four minutes slow a day and is corrected every seventh day by a man who is not him.

A strip light hangs on a flex above the table and has been getting longer for nine years and nobody has been up a ladder.

The lift gate is a scissor gate on a wooden floor and it is worked by a rope over a wheel, and the counterweight goes up and down on the other side of the shaft and you hear it before you see anything at all.

Four names on the rota. The page is the size of a folded hand and it is ruled into seven squares and it hangs on a board in the office at the end of the passage where the keys are signed out, and the office at the end of the passage belongs to the branch and not to him.

A name on that page is a person who works in the same building as him.

She works it on the other three nights, at a market end off the Cordwain side, and she has never once asked him what he does with his three off and he has never once asked her, and nothing in this city obliges either of them to.

## She Stops The Car In The Middle Of The Shaft

It was about twenty past two and she had the gate open and the car standing at the landing.

“You have been down the stairs twice in an hour for a bucket,” said Nadia Ferrous.

“There is no water on the landing. There has not been water on that landing since the building was put up.”

“There is water in the washroom on the second floor and there is a standpipe on every landing and you have been carrying it up four floors for about an hour because somebody told you nine years ago that there was no water on the top landing,” said Nadia Ferrous. “Get in. I am going to show you something and it takes about forty seconds and then you can go back and do your floors.”

He got in.

The gate shut behind him with the noise it makes, which is not a bang, it is more like a coin going into a slot, and the rope came over and the car went down, and after about a second and a half he noticed that the walls were nearer each other than they had been.

It was not the room. It was not the light. The two side walls of that car were about four inches nearer each other with every floor it came down, and the space between them and the two of them went down like water going down a drain, and when it came to a stop in the middle of the shaft there was less of it between the two of them than there had ever been in a room he had been handed.

She had not told him about it and he had not asked, and both of those had been going on for six years.

## What She Has Worked Out

“I have got about nine minutes,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and I am going to spend them on the thing I have been carrying since the twenty-second of last month, and you are going to stand there and let me, and then I am going to let you have the rest of the night.”

“Go on.”

“I know more about you than anything in this city does.” She said it the way she says a figure to a crowd, level and without any weight on it.

“The woman with the pencil has nine sheets and not one day of yours in them. The woman with the cloth book has two hundred pages and not one hour of yours. The book with a date and an hour in it that goes up a stair has your name at the head of two instructions and about nine lines, and there is not one of them about when you get up or what you do with the other three days of a week.”

“That is true.”

“It is true and I have known it for about two years,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and the whole of what I have got is that you are on the same piece of paper as me, and it is the size of my hand, and it is the only record of you in this city that anybody will ever be shown.”

She turned round. She had her coat off over the rail and a cap under the rail and the sleeves of her shirt turned up past the elbow, and she was not rushing, because she has never rushed anything in her life.

“But here is the part that has been keeping me awake,” she said. “I have been on nine rotas in my life. Nine. I have counted them and I have kept every one of them in my head because that is the only place they were ever going to be kept, and not one of the nine has ever had my name on it while I was still working.”

“Nine rotas.”

“Four nights and three off,” said Nadia Ferrous. “That is not a shift. That is a whole week with three holes in it, and a hole is where everything you actually are lives, and every record in this city is full of them, and every one of those records has been read by somebody.”

## What She Wants

“I am not asking for a night,” she said. “You are going to offer me a night in about nine seconds and it is worth having and we will have it, and it is not what I came down this shaft for.”

“Then what.”

“A copy of the page,” said Nadia Ferrous. “Not to do anything with. I am not going to send it anywhere and I am not going to read anything out of it and I do not want to know who else is on it. I want a copy of it in my own hand so that I have held, once, a piece of paper that says what my week is.”

“There is no form on which a person may read their own.”

“There is nothing at all on which a person may read their own. Not in this city. Not for a wage and not for a rent book and not for a person who wants to know when her own week is.”

“I have been on nine rotas and I have been handed nine pieces of paper saying what somebody else wants of me, and I have never once been handed a piece of paper saying what the other three days are for.”

“This city has not got days of the week,” said Nadia Ferrous.

“It has got thirty days a month and a month has no thirty-first, and a man in a branch office writes the date and not the day. That is correct for a wage and it is no use at all for a person.”

“So the three off are three holes in every rota I have ever stood behind, and I am the only one who knows what is in them, and I have never once been able to say so out loud in a room where it might be written down.”

She put two fingers on his collar and pulled the front of his shirt open about an inch.

“Now. You have got about four minutes and I have got about six.”

## What He Cannot Give Her

“I have not got it. It hangs on a board at the far end of the landing, and it belongs to the branch and not to me, and I am a man of rank one on a night shift with no standing in that office at all.”

“If I asked them for a copy of it they would ask me what I wanted it for, and I would have to say it was for a woman who runs a lift, and that is the end of it.”

“I know you have not got it.”

“Then why did you ask.”

“Because you read it every night,” said Nadia Ferrous. “You come in at eleven and you read that page before you take your coat off, every night, four nights in seven, and you have read it for six years and you have not got it in your head, and if you think you have, you have got it wrong.”

He had. She was entirely right, and it took him about four seconds to find it, the way you find a step you have been walking over for years.

“The first of the three,” he said.

“The first of the three.”

“I sleep on the first of the three.”

“You sleep on the first of the three and on the second of them until about two and then you are on this landing again, and I am at the market end on the first of them from six until about midnight and I have not been in this building on the second of them since the second year I was on it,” said Nadia Ferrous. “Give me those two.”

“I have no way of proving that. You will not be able to prove it and neither will I, and in four years one of us will have it wrong.”

“Then we will be wrong together,” said Nadia Ferrous, “which is the first time in nine years I have been offered anything in this building that I could not lose.”

## The Car Goes Down

She worked the rope and the car went down and the walls came in another inch and she was talking the whole time in the voice she uses over a hundred people at a market end, and she was not being tender, she was being fast, because that is what she is.

“Nine minutes,” she said. “And I am going to spend about four of them on the part I have wanted since a lift between the arcade and the clinic level about six years ago with a man in a rank-nine coat who was extremely careful at me, and it has been extremely annoying for six years, and it is going to stop being annoying in about four seconds.”

She got her hand in his trousers and took hold of him before he had got his belt undone, the way a person takes hold of a rope they have checked.

“That is a man who has spent nine years being careful at about four hundred people and has not once been careful at me,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and I have wanted to get hold of that for six years and I have wanted to say so out loud in a room, and I have just said it out loud in a box nine feet high in the middle of a shaft, and that is the most this room has ever been used for.”

He was hard in her hand and she said good, the way a woman says a thing she has checked, and stroked him twice and got the belt of her own coat open over his head while she was kissing him under the jaw.

The strip light outside went past once and went past again. Nobody in that building was awake except the two of them and a man in a branch office two streets away who was not doing anything that night, and the car was stopped in the middle of a shaft with about six inches of nothing on either side of the doors and both of them knew it before either of them started.

She had her back against the doors and her legs open and one heel hooked on the scissor gate, and she took all of him in one movement and made a long noise about it that the shaft gave back twice, and she said his rank out loud in the middle of it and laughed, because a man of rank one making that noise is a thing a person who works a lift finds funny.

“Your coat is ruined,” she said, about nine seconds later, and did not appear to mind at all.

She got her hand between her legs and found herself already open and said so out loud, and then said the rest of it, which is that she had been thinking about the back of his neck at a market end at about eight o’clock with two hundred people in front of her and a man shouting about a price, and that she had put the market down for a minute and a half to do it.

“Go on,” she said. “Do it like a man who does the floors of three washrooms and not like a man being careful.”

He stopped being careful. She noticed it inside about two seconds and her heels came off the gate and she got a hand round the back of his neck and held his face against her jaw and gave him the whole of it in about nine words, in that order and with no room left in it to be misunderstood, and when he did it she shut up completely, which was worse and much better.

“Right,” she said, about nine seconds later, with her forehead against the wall. “Four seconds. Say something.”

He said he had got nothing to say that would be worth the door she was about to open.

“That,” said Nadia Ferrous, “is the best thing anybody has said to me since I took the nights, and you are going to hear about it for a month.”

## At The Bottom Of The Shaft

She got the gate open with her elbow and the doors came back about six inches and stopped dead, because there was nothing on the far side of them but shaft.

She came off him slowly, got her cap off the rail and put it under her arm rather than on, and lowered herself onto the little bench in the corner with one palm wet on her own thigh and put her boots up on the opposite wall.

He got his trousers up and found her semen on his own hand and on the front of her trousers, and neither of them wiped it and neither of them said anything about it, because a stopped lift is a stopped lift and there is a cloth on a hook in the corner of it that has been the same cloth since the building was put up.

Then she told him his week.

“The first of the three you sleep,” she said. “The second of them until two in the afternoon. The third of them you are at a counter off a third court in a building with no door onto a street, and you are the only person in Ostara who may ask that office any question in the world, and you have been that for seven years and you have never once used it on yourself.”

“That is not what the third of the three are for.”

“That is exactly what they are for and you have never used one,” said Nadia Ferrous. “And I have not written any of it down and I am not going to, and I want you to be very clear about why, because you are going to ask me to and I am going to say no and I want the reason to be the right one.”

“Why.”

“Because a thing somebody can be shown is a record and a thing I hold is not a record. I have spent six years watching you build the first out of everything and I am not going to be the second one that gets put in a column.”

“I would rather have your three days in my head for the rest of my life than have them on a sheet with my name at the top of it in a building with no door onto a street.”

She got her hands round the rope and stood there a moment with her weight back on her heels, and he watched her decide.

Then the car went. It managed about a foot of rise before it stopped again, and somewhere above them the gear took hold and let go, and neither of them enjoyed the noise it made about it.

The car came back down into the middle of the shaft and she stayed where she was with her forehead on her knees until she could lift her head.

“A hundred and twenty seconds,” she said, “and a day and a half back out of a week I am not paid for, and I would buy the same floor again in the morning for the sake of three days and a quarter, and you are not going to apologise for it and I am not going to let you.”

“I was not going to.”

“I know. That is why I did it,” said Nadia Ferrous.

She got the handle.

“Lift going down in about two minutes, all the way, and then you have got three floors of floors to do and a table and two chairs and about nine feet of lino, and the top landing has still got no water on it and there never has.”

## What He Is Left Holding At Half Past Five

He did the floors and he did them badly, because his chest has been spent since the fourth year and it was half past three by the time he got to the second and he had to stop twice on the second landing.

The two of them went down at five and she stood on the ground floor by the bin room door with her coat on and her cap on and the ring of keys in her hand.

“About nine minutes,” she said, “and four of them. And I want the arithmetic said out loud at the counter in the morning because you keep days and a day without its hour is not a day anybody can hold, and I have given you two days of a week and you are going to tell her the hour.”

“I am not going to put you in anything.”

“I know you are not going to put me in anything,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and that is the whole of what I have wanted for six years and it took me a stopped lift to say it out loud, and if you ever do put my name on a sheet anywhere I will take the sheet and I will not give it back and you will not be able to make me.”

“I am not going to do that.”

“No,” she said. “You are not. And that is the last time I am going to say thank you to you for not doing something, because the two of us have been doing that for nine years and it is about four thousand words too many already.”

Then she said one more thing at the door of the bin room, quickly, the way she says a figure.

“One more. You asked me once, in about the second year, whether I minded about the address, and I did not answer you because you were busy.”

“I remember.”

“I minded about the address,” said Nadia Ferrous, “for about four days, and then I did not, and you never asked me again, and that is the single thing I have never forgiven you for and I have never mentioned and I am mentioning it now at five o’clock in the morning because I have got about nine seconds.”

She went out into the court.

He stood on the ground floor of a branch office with a wet cloth in one hand, and he had two of his three days off in his head and not on anything at all, and he had promised them to a woman who works a lift, and he had a name on a page the size of his own hand that he could not get a copy of and had just given away instead.

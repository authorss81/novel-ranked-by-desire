# The Day Book And The Column

Two thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight was the twenty-ninth of August and the fifth of September is day two thousand seven hundred and thirty-four. August runs to its thirtieth and there is no thirty-first in this city. The twenty-ninth to the thirtieth is one, the first of September is one, and the fifth is four more, and one and one and four is six.

## The Room Over The Counters

The room over the counters hall in a division building is where the day books go when they are full, and there is no other room in that building for anything, and the stair to it is one flight and not two and it comes out of the hall behind the second counter where a man reads out what is owed to the public.

It is a long narrow room with pigeonholes to the ceiling on both sides, each one with a cloth-covered book in it and a brass tag punched with the month it was begun in.

A long deal bench runs down the middle of it with a scar about four inches long in the top where somebody knocked a lamp over in the winter before last. Wooden steps stand on a rail against the left-hand pigeonholes, and a pendant lamp hangs on a flex about a foot above the bench. The window has a grating across the lower half of it and the grating is painted the colour the frames are painted.

## The Second One

It was not a return book. The division's own books are printed with three columns and an hour in the third and a rule down each side, and they are the same in every counter in the building.

This one was unruled. It was bound in grey cloth with the corners gone soft, and a pencil margin had been drawn down the inside of the front cover by a person going along it, and it was opened at the first page.

“A desk takes one question of a thing and only one,” said Wren Kallo. “That has never been printed and it has never needed to be. And when a question goes back into a day book it is not a return any more, it is a thing a person wrote in a book, and it goes to no magistrate, because a magistrate's counter takes a return and a book with three printed columns in it, and it would look at nine sheets of pencil and put it in a drawer.”

She put two fingers on the first page and did not turn it.

“And a day book is opened at the first,” she said. “That is the whole of it. Nobody opens a day book in the middle. It is a book of a day being done from the beginning of it, and if you open one at a page in the middle you are not reading a day, you are reading an afternoon, and there is nobody entitled to be shown an afternoon as though it were a week.”

He looked at the page she had not turned. There were three lines on it in pencil and the times against them and nothing whatever against the times.

“Who is it for?”

“It is for me,” said Wren Kallo. “It has been for me since the spring of the sixth year.”

## Three A Day

“I put three things in it a day,” she said, “because three is what fits under a line in my hand without the line running out, and I have never once gone back and found a day that needed four. It is what the counter did. Not what I thought about the counter doing.”

“That is a record of a thing being true against a day.”

“That is a record of a thing being true against a day,” said Wren Kallo, “and I have known that since the sixth year and there was nobody in this building to tell, because telling is the whole of what an office does and I am a desk and not an office.”

Two nails of her left hand had been in ink so long that the ink was no longer a thing on a nail. She turned the book a quarter turn on the bench without opening it further and looked at him.

“You know the other one,” she said. “Nine sheets under a third table four flights up with a heading a woman ruled on her own paper, and a keeper who has said no five times in four years and would say it a sixth before she finished the sentence. I know about it. Everybody in that laundry knows about it. It has no keeper’s name on it and no second keeper and no column anybody may cite, and that is why it has lasted six years.”

“And yours.”

“And mine has no heading, and it is opened at the first, and there is a rule in this building about the second of those two things that nobody has ever written down.”

“It has gone nowhere for three years,” said Wren Kallo, “and it is going to keep going nowhere, and that is the arrangement, and I made it.”

“I want one thing out of it,” she said, before he could put anything in. “Not a favour. I want it on the record that there are two of these.”

“You cannot enter it anywhere.”

“I know I cannot enter it anywhere, that is the whole difficulty and I have thought about it for two years,” said Wren Kallo. “I cannot put it in a division book because I do not work in the division, I am the division. And you cannot put it on your standing sheet, because a column that says a person keeps a private record of days is a thing people can be asked to stop, and I would be asked to stop inside a week.”

## Two Records And No Instrument

They dressed without hurry, which surprised him, and she washed her hands in the enamel basin under the pipe in the corner and dried them on a cloth that was not clean and put the two ink nails back out of sight by folding them into the cloth.

He did not ask to see the book again. He had been in the room about four hours and he had seen the first page and the hands on it and the sound of a thing being squared on a bench, and he would go on not knowing what the other two hundred pages of it said for the rest of his life, and he understood as he put his coat back on that he was not going to ask.

“You are not going to come up here again,” said Wren Kallo.

“No.”

“You could come up here again. You could come up here any afternoon and there is nobody on this stair.”

“No,” he said.

She nodded and pulled the grey cloth back over the book and put it into its pigeonhole with the brass tag facing out, and then she squared the pencil that was lying in the margin of the bench with two fingers and left it lying there, because a book opened at the first is a book that has not been made a record by anybody, and the only person who could make it one was not going to.

“Say it once,” she said. “Out loud, so that it is in the room and not in you. I want to hear what this building is.”

“There are two records in this city of a thing being true against a day,” said Lin Hao. “Nine sheets under a third table in a laundry, and one in a room over the counters. Neither of them is an instrument. Neither of them may be cited by anybody, in any court, at any counter, or in any book that goes out of a room.”

“And I am the only person in Ostara who knows that both of them exist, and there is no form anywhere in this city on which I may enter that, and no form on which you may enter it either, and I am not going to come up this stair again.”

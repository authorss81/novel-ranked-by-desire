# A Column With No Heading On It

Two thousand five hundred and thirty-four was the fifteenth of February and the twenty-first is day two thousand five hundred and forty, and two thousand five hundred and forty less two thousand five hundred and thirty-four is six, and twenty-one less fifteen is six by the month count.

## The Back Room At About Eleven

The steam came under the door and stopped about two feet up the room and stood there, and the bench along the wall was wet at one end, and there were three tables and a ledger on the middle one and nine sheets of her own paper under the third one.

She was at the third table with a pencil and a ruler and nothing else on it, and she did not look up when the door came, because about nine people a month come through that door and she knows what nine of them are carrying before they are through it.

“You have come down four flights to stand in a wet room,” said Perla Quist.

“Yes.”

“You have got nothing in your hands, which is the first time in about four months,” she said. “Sit down or do not, it makes no difference to me and it never has, and I am going to say a thing first and then you can want whatever you came for.”

## What She Is Not

She put the ruler down beside the pencil and turned it so that the two of them were parallel to the edge of the table, which she does.

“I am not an instrument,” said Perla Quist. “I am a woman in a laundry who can write, on a street two streets wide, and I have money owing to me and a bad knee and about nine people a week who bring me a piece of paper they copied out of something printed and want to know whether it is right.”

“And nobody may require a thing of you.”

“Nobody in this city may require one thing of me,” she said, “and there is no form anywhere on which anybody could, and that is not modesty and it is not the way I was raised. It is the shape of the only arrangement that works.”

“The moment somebody with a building behind him sends me to fetch something, I stop being a woman who keeps a list and I become a thing a building sends. Then everything I write is a thing that building had done, and the nine people at this table would know it inside a week.”

He did not say anything.

“You have heard that four times now and I have given it four times,” said Perla Quist, “and it has never once been about you, and you have never once asked me to do a thing that would make it about you, and I have noticed that about you for about four months and I have not told anybody.”

## What She Ruled And What It Cost Her To Rule It

“There is a fourth column,” she said.

“I have seen it.”

“You have seen it and you have not asked about it in about four minutes, which is new.” She pulled the top sheet out from under the table and turned it a half round so that it faced him. Nine sheets, squared, in her own paper, and about three hundred and forty lines in a margin on nine of them, and four columns.

The fourth was the narrowest thing on the page and there was nothing at the top of it.

“I ruled it at about two o’clock on the fourteenth of October and I put nothing over it and I have told nobody and nobody has asked me, and you are the first person who has seen it that I did not put it in front of.”

“Why is it blank at the top.”

“Because I did not know what it was yet and I am not going to put a name on a thing until I know what it is,” said Perla Quist. “And because the second time I did know what it was I sat here for about four days and did not do anything.”

“You know why I sat here for four days. The moment it has a name it is a thing that people can ask me about, and I am not ready for about nine people to ask me about four hundred pieces of paper at once.”

She pulled the sheet towards her and picked up the pencil and wrote across the top of the narrow column in letters about the size of the rule lines.

WHAT THE SHEET SAID THAT DAY

## Three Things At Once

“Now I have done it and I am going to say what it has done, because I am not going to do a thing to a piece of paper four hundred times in this city without saying out loud what it costs,” said Perla Quist.

She put the pencil down on the ruler.

“First. A column with a heading is a thing somebody can ask about. I have made myself a room with a door in it that I had not got before. On any morning now a man can come down those four flights and ask what the page said on the fourth of February, and there is one person in this city who can answer him.”

“Second. A thing somebody can ask about is a thing somebody can be asked to stop. There is nobody in this city entitled to stop me and no form on which anybody could ask me to, and both of those are true and neither of them prevents a woman who has been told nine times that she is the one who keeps it from going anywhere else from deciding on her own that she had better be careful with it.”

“Third.”

She stopped for about two seconds.

“Third, a woman who has ruled a heading on her own paper has made herself a person who can be refused,” said Perla Quist. “Before today I was a woman nobody had asked anything, and a woman nobody has asked anything cannot be told no. I have given nine people in this city something they can say no to and they will say it inside a fortnight and I will have to be right every time.”

She looked at the heading for about four seconds.

“I am going to do it anyway,” she said, “and I am going to say why, and the reason is that a column with nothing at the top of it is a thing that cannot leave this room. It is a thing I can read to myself and I have not got anybody to read it to.”

## What He Cannot Be Asked For Here

He had wanted, since the first of the month, to be the second pair of hands on nine sheets.

He could have ruled a fifth column in about nine minutes and he could have copied three hundred and forty lines in about four hours and he would have got one of them wrong in the first hundred and never have known which one.

There is no form in this city on which a man in a white plate may sit at a third table in a laundry and rule a column on somebody else’s paper, and there is no form on which a woman with a pencil may ask him to, and she would not have asked him either.

He did not ask.

He stood about four feet off with a folded paper in the inside of his coat that had been against his chest since the seventeenth of December and that he had twice refused to look at, and the only thing he is entitled to do in this room is carry a thing up four flights, which he did in the summer and was thanked for in his own way about eleven times.

“You have not asked me for anything,” said Perla Quist.

“No.”

“You are going to want to, and I am not going to say no before you do, because a woman who says no first has already answered the question and I do not answer questions in this room.”

*Two thousand five hundred and forty days. Less two thousand four hundred and thirteen, the fourteenth of October of the seventh year, is a hundred and twenty-seven days, which is four months and seven days after them, and a fourth column with nothing at the top of it stood on nine sheets under a third table in a laundry for every one of them and about four hundred people in four hundred kitchens did not know it existed.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and twenty-seven, the eighth of February of the eighth year, is thirteen days, and on the first of this month a man came in by the only door that office has, which opens onto a court and not onto a street, holding a piece of pencil paper, and asked in the doorway whether that office knew his name.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and forty-one days, which is four months and twenty-one days after them, and the twenty-eighth sheet is face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain with three lines in the second column and eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it.*

*Less the ninth of November of the fifth year, one thousand seven hundred and eighteen, is eight hundred and twenty-two days, which is twenty-seven months and twelve days after them.*

## The First Thing In It, Put In By Her

She turned the sheet round and squared the bottom edge of it against the table and wrote in the first three columns and then stopped with the pencil above the narrow one.

“I have got one already,” she said, “and I have been carrying it since the fourth of February, and I have not entered it because I had nothing to enter it against.”

She wrote a date in the first column. The fourth of February.

“Eleven days ago I was standing at the rail in Cordwain because about ninety of the two hundred and forty people an hour go past that counter and about nine of them get to the foot, and I go and get to the foot about four times a month and I have never once been there for myself.”

“And this man was standing at the rail with a pencil paper in his hand,” said Perla Quist, “and a man behind the counter was reading the foot of the page out to him because that is what that man does, and the man with the paper wrote in the bottom two inches of his own sheet in his own hand, standing up, against the edge of the counter, and he had been digging a street since the twenty-second of January and nobody had asked him one question about it.”

She put the pencil down.

“I made a copy of the foot of that page there and then and I dated it the fourth of February because I was standing there and I have kept a day in my head every time I have been standing at that rail for about nine years.”

The pencil went into the narrow column.

What the sheet said that day went into it in her own hand, in the order the sheet was in, with the figure and the four hundred and nineteen accounts and the three lines of the second column and the four sentences at the foot and the six lines under them, and it took her about four minutes and she did not improve one word of any of it.

“There,” said Perla Quist. “That is the first one and it is mine and I have never been asked about it, and it is not a claim and it is not a deed and it is a piece of paper a man made in a kitchen and a thing a page said eleven days ago in a passage off Cordwain.”

She squared the sheet against the table.

“Now tell me what you came down four flights for,” she said, “and do not tell me it was to thank me, because you did not thank me and I would not have wanted you to.”

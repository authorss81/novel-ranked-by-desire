# Nobody Can Tell Him He Is Wrong

Two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine is the thirtieth of this month and two thousand five hundred and seventy-three was the twenty-fourth of it, and the two of them are six days apart, and the six days are the whole of what is left of this quarter.

The month before this one began on the first of February and ran to its thirtieth, and this one has a thirtieth as well, and there is no thirty-first anywhere in this city and no day of the week to shorten a month with.

## Half Past Two On The Last Working Day Of The Quarter

She took it out of the drawer at half past two and had it trued up to the edge of the wood inside about four seconds, and nobody in that room said anything about the four seconds, and they had not said anything about them about nine times since the thirtieth of December either.

The number at the top of it has not shifted since the thirtieth of March of a year I am not going to name, and it has not shifted across thirteen of these sheets. Thirteen is a count of sheets. The quarters run to a different figure and the house keeps both and is right about both, and anything that prints one of them says which one it is printing.

Under it, four hundred and nineteen accounts and a shade short of four hundred and twenty. Wenna Raut has never counted them twice in the same week. Every one of them is a person’s account of a street dug in the cold. A date against it. An hour against the date. Nothing whatever against the hour and no name anywhere along the line.

Three lines in a column headed carried by. Two of them were standing in that book before there was a clerk’s hand in this city to enter anything. The third was entered on the twelfth of September and went onto the page on the thirtieth of September: the day, a count, four words doing all the work, and a man’s name, and standing under that name one sentence about a drawer that is not to be opened, put there in October.

Under the column, four sentences. Two of them predate this office. Two of them were set on a November morning by a man whose name is on the page and is not his own.

Under those, since the twenty-third of November, six lines with the middle one struck from the first letter to the last, a name under the striking, and then two inches of nothing.

“Twenty-ninth,” said Wenna Raut.

“Twenty-ninth,” said Lin Hao, who had said it twenty-eight times before.

“The figure at the top has not shifted and is not going to shift,” she said. “Nobody in this city holds the permission that would move it. I say that out loud in this room once a quarter and I have said it about eight times in two years, and I am saying it now into a page that is about to be folded into eight.”

She signed for it and squared it and folded it into eight.

## The Thing He Did Not Ask For

He did not ask that office to do one single thing with the four hundred and nine.

Not to open them. Not to copy them. Not to send a woman down to a laundry with a list. Not to give back the nine people who stopped bringing them in during the middle of last year and have not been near that door since. Every one of those has been asked for by somebody in this city in nine years and every one of them was refused.

He asked for none of it, and the reason is on a wall in his own coat and has been since the seventeenth of December.

Holding them is what a drawer does. He put that drawer on his own instruction in a clerk’s hand with his name at the foot of it on the twenty-first of October, and he closed it himself on the last working day of the year, and nothing in this city can unmake an instruction once it is written down.

He went down the stair after her with the bundle in his hand and caught her on the first landing with the sheet folded into eight under her arm.

“Wenna.”

“I have got about forty minutes and it is the last working day of the quarter.”

“I have got one thing and it will take four minutes and it is not a sentence and you are not going to enjoy it.”

## The Instruction

He put the list on the table face up in front of her and it was the fourth version of it and he had not crossed out the first three this time.

“Enter that. Exactly. You may not improve one word of it and you may not leave any of it out and I am not going to be in the room while you do it.”

She read it where it lay, the way she reads everything, and then she read it a second time, and it took her nine seconds and he watched every one of them.

*From the thirtieth of March of this year every piece of paper received through the front door of this office is to be entered as received, with a date against it, whether or not it is a copy of the foot of the standing sheet and whether or not anybody is able to check it.*

*Lin Hao.*

“Where does that go.”

“In the Register.”

“Not in the column.”

“No,” he said. “It does not go in the column. It goes in the book, tonight, and the column has three lines in it and it will have three lines in it when the twenty-ninth sheet is on a rail in Cordwain this afternoon, and I have not come in here today to put anything in that column.”

Wenna Raut put the pen down.

“I am going to say three things and then I am going to enter it, and I am not going to say the third one twice.”

“Go on.”

“The first is that this is the only instruction anybody has ever brought me in six years in this room that asks me to enter something I cannot check,” she said, “and every other one of them asked me to enter something that was true, and I checked it before I wrote it, and you have taken the check out of the job and left everything else exactly as it was.”

“The second.”

“The second is that the fifth field is not touched by this and you have worked that out and you have not mentioned it and I am going to mention it. It goes in the Register as a statement about what this office is handed. It does not go in as a claim. There is no person in it and the field does not apply and no person in this building has been asked anything about anything, and that includes the about four hundred people whose paper this is about.”

She picked the pen up.

“And the third is that you have not asked me a question and I am not going to spend one, because I do not want to know and because I have been the only person in this room for six years and I am tired of being the one who has to know.”

## What It Closes And What It Costs

It took her about four minutes and she read it all back in the order it stood and his name was in it, and it went into the bound Register and not one stroke of it went into the column. Then she folded the twenty-ninth sheet back up and went down the stair with it under her arm, two floors, on her own, the way it goes down two floors every quarter in this city.

Nobody was thanked. There had not been a thank you in that room since about the eighth month and both of them had arranged that and he had made sure of it himself in a yard in the summer.

He stood on the landing and worked out what he had done, and it took him about four seconds and he had done it on purpose.

Before this morning, about four hundred pieces of pencil paper were in about four hundred kitchens and there was one thing that could go on them that this city did not have, and it was a person.

A person who was there.

Somebody in a room can say that a thing did not happen, out loud, in front of nine other people, and the thing cannot be put back the way a figure on a counter cannot be put back, and that is the only thing in the history of this city that has ever corrected a claim, and about four hundred people cannot do it at once because they have never been in the same room.

He has closed that. Not by a rule. By an instruction in a clerk's hand in a bound book.

And what it has cost is him.

From this afternoon there is one place in Ostara where those four hundred people's claims can be found, and there is exactly one person in Ostara who may ask that office any question about any of them, and it is a man in a white plate with no figure cut into it who is rank one on a branch night shift and has a chest he has not had since the fourth year.

Nobody can tell him he is wrong. Nobody has been able to since the sixth of February of the first year, and he has known that for nine years, and he has spent it about nine times, and the thing he did this afternoon was not a sentence and was not a list and had no commas in it that meant anything.

*Two thousand five hundred and seventy-nine days. Less two thousand five hundred and twenty, the first of February of the eighth year, is fifty-nine days, which is one month and twenty-nine days after them, and about four hundred pieces of pencil paper were in about four hundred kitchens for every one of them, and about nine hundred people in this city could read every line of any one of them, and not one of those people had been asked anything.*

*Less two thousand five hundred and twenty-seven, the eighth of February of the eighth year, is fifty-two days, which is one month and twenty-two days after them, and a man behind a counter in a passage off Cordwain read a third line of a second column out loud to about nine people a week on that morning and had been doing it every morning since the thirtieth of December and had never once been asked what it meant.*

*Less two thousand three hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of September of the seventh year, is a hundred and eighty days, which is six months exactly, and the twenty-ninth sheet went out at four this afternoon and is standing face up on a bracket in a passage off Cordwain with three lines in its second column.*

*Less two thousand and seventy-eight, the ninth of November of the sixth year — and not the ninth of November of the fifth year, which is one thousand seven hundred and eighteen and is further back — is five hundred and one days, which is sixteen months and twenty-one days after them, and a shutter at the far end of a yard off the Vestry has been bare for every one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is a hundred and fifty-nine days, which is five months and nine days after them, and a drawer in a room off a third court has been shut for every one of them under an instruction that has no form for undoing it.*

## Three People And A Rail

At four o’clock it went face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain, where the reading is done standing up and there is a hand’s width of air under the bottom edge of it, and where about a hundred people a month get past it with a hip and a handful get all the way down to the last two inches and can read every line of them.

There were six at the rail at four minutes past four, and nobody has ever stood at that rail in larger numbers than that on any afternoon in six years.

The man at the desk put his own copy on the wood before he opened the counter and took it off again before he shut it, and read the bottom of the page out loud to all six at once. That is what he does and he has been doing it for nine years and nobody has ever paid him for any part of it, and there is nobody alive he could name who has said thank you.

The third line of the second column was a receipt, in this office’s hand, with a man’s name under it, saying in four words that none of them is a deed.

And the man who read it out to six strangers that afternoon had read it to a man with a pencil paper in his hand seven weeks before, in the ordinary way, and the man with the pencil paper had said it back to him in the ordinary way, and neither of them knew that the man in the white plate had been standing about four feet off with his hands in his pockets the whole time.

There is now a building off a third court where a piece of paper can be brought and be received, with a date against it, whatever it is and whoever can check it.

Three people in this city know that.

One of them is a clerk who may only enter what she is given and cannot improve a word of it. One is a man at the end of a table with a title he has never once set out. And one is standing at the bottom of a stair off a court at about ten past four with a bundle of twenty-five things under his arm.

He is the only person any of them may ask, and there is nobody in Ostara entitled to tell him that he is wrong, and there has not been one since the sixth of February of the first year. He knew that when he wrote it and he wrote it anyway.

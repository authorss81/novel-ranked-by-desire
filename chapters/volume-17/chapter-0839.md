# A Man Asks About Himself

Three thousand four hundred and eighty-six was the seventh of this month and the fourteenth is day three thousand four hundred and ninety-three, and three thousand four hundred and ninety-three less three thousand four hundred and eighty-six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## The Man With Rooms On The Second Floor

He had rooms on the second floor and he had asked this office twice in April whether there was anything under his own name at this address, and the second time he wanted it said in a room.

It was refused at the wood in April because at the eleventh of April there was nothing in that office that said anything could be said in a room, and it came back up four flights with him standing at the top of his own stairs holding it.

That was about six months ago and he has not been up since, and not one person in that building asked him whether he was going to come back.

He came back on the morning of the fourteenth of this month at about eleven, and he did not ask for a room.

“I want to know whether this office holds anything about me,” he said. “That is all. Under my own name, at this address, anything at all.”

## The Easiest Question In The Building

Wenna Raut did not go and look.

“That is the first question anybody has put to this office in nine years that is about the asker and about the asker only,” she said, “and it is the easiest one we get, and I am going to tell you both of those things before I answer it.”

“All right.”

“You brought a paper up in the spring. It came through that front door and you were standing on the step and a man behind a counter gave it back to you. That is the only thing about you this office has ever been given.”

“Then I know the answer.”

“You know what the office holds. You do not know what it says, and those are two different things and about four of the rows in that book are the difference between them.”

## One Row, And The Hour Against It

She read it out once in the room with the door open, in the voice she reads everything in, and he was in the room when it was read.

It was one line.

That in the fourth month of this year a man brought a paper through the front door of that building on the morning of the fourteenth of April, and that this office holds one row against that paper with a date on it and an hour against the date and no figure and no name.

“That is the whole of it.”

“That is the whole of it and it will be the whole of it in about four years as well, and there is nothing in this building that could add to it and nothing in this city that could take any of it away.”

She entered it with the date of the morning and the hour against the date, in her own hand, in the words she had read.

And then she put the pen down and looked at him, which she does not do.

“Now the other thing,” she said. “You have come up six flights twice this year and you have not asked for one thing. I am going to offer you one thing and you may say no.”

## The Permission, Which Is In His Own Mouth And Not In The Book

“If anybody puts a question to this office about you again,” said Wenna Raut, “there is a way for you to be told, and it has to be your mouth and not mine and not a row.”

“Go on.”

“You come up four flights and you say so, and I read out in this room what has been asked and by whose paper it concerns, once, with you in the room, and not one person afterwards is told that you were here.”

“And if you forget.”

“Then nothing happens, because nothing has been written down.”

“Can you not write it down.”

“I could put the date of a visit in that book,” said Wenna Raut, “and the moment I do that it stops being a permission and becomes a record of a man who came and asked to be told, and you have given me something you wanted to keep and I have taken it off you and put it in a room in a building with no door onto a street.”

He thought about it for about nine seconds.

“Yes,” he said. “If anybody asks about me again I want to be told and I will come up for it and I will not be written down.”

“That is a permission and it is in your mouth and it is not in that book, and nothing in this city can be got to hold it for you, and if anything ever did hold it then it would not be a permission any more.”

## Then Five Hours On A Sill And A Question About April

He took the sill of the corridor window for most of that day with his hat in his hands and about four other people going up and down past him, and he did not go into that room once.

At about twenty past twelve he asked whether the four lines that were refused at his own door in April would have said the same thing as the line she had read him this morning.

“I cannot tell you that,” said Wenna Raut.

“You wrote both.”

“The one that came up four flights in April was written from memory on a stair at eleven at night by a man who had been up the whole day. From the sixth of July a clerk in this room does not read a row back from memory anywhere, and there is nothing that will tell me whether those two were the same sentence.”

“So there are two answers and not one person knows which of them the office’s.”

“There is one answer in the book and there is one in the head of a man who is not paid to keep it, and he carried that one up his own stairs about six months ago and put it in a drawer with about nine other things in it. There is not one thing in this city that will go and look.”

“That is the first time anybody has told me that to my face.”

“About four people in that corridor have heard it, and one of them will repeat it in about four years to somebody who works in a division building. That is the whole of the arrangement now and I have not improved it since the sixth of July.”

## One Question A Day At The First Counter, And Not Two

He went down to the magistracy at about ten to one and was back up the four flights inside forty minutes.

The counters run along the ground floor of a court building and the clerk at the first one of them is entitled to one question a day and not two, and she told him that before he had got to the end of the word.

“I am going to have one out of you,” she said, “and then I am a woman at a counter who has already had one, and if you have a second thing I am going to say the same sentence again and it will not be any better the second time.”

“Go on.”

“You want to know whether a permission can be entered in a book.”

“I want to know whether a permission can be entered in a book by the person who was given it.”

“It cannot,” she said, without looking anything up, “because the moment it is entered it is a record, and a record is a thing that can be read out to somebody else, and a permission that can be read out to somebody else is not a permission, it is a rumour about what a person wanted.”

“So a person cannot be shown that they gave one.”

“A person cannot be shown anything, ever, by anybody, in this city or any other,” said the clerk, “and that is not a fault in the arrangement. It is the arrangement. And you have been in this building four times in nine years and this is the first time you have come in with something you could not get.”

## Four O'Clock, And A Lie At The Top Of His Own Stairs

He was still on that sill at about four when he came down, and he had not been into that room once all day.

“At the top of my stairs,” he said, “a man is going to ask me this evening whether anybody else has put a question about me.”

“You may tell him there has not been one.”

“I am going to tell him something better than that and I want you to hear it first,” said Lin Hao, “so that if it is wrong you will know that I knew it was wrong.”

“You are going to tell him that not one other person can ask about him.”

“I am,” he said, “and it is a lie, and it is the only lie this office has told in nine years.”

He put his hands in the pockets of his coat where four things had been for about a year and one of them was the answer of the fourteenth of April, which said a day that was not the day the paper came.

“A man may put a question to this office about any paper in the world and I cannot refuse him one of them. If I tell him the truth on the top of his own stairs then he will start asking his neighbours who has been asking about him, and about four of them will be interested, and in about four years this office will be a thing that neighbours use on each other.”

Wenna Raut looked at the book and did not write anything.

“Then it is a good lie,” she said, “and it is not going in, and I am not going to ask you what happens on the twentieth of this month.”

*Three thousand four hundred and ninety-three days. Less three thousand four hundred and eighty-six, the seventh of this month, is seven, and a man with rooms on the second floor put the easiest question anybody has put to that office in nine years and was answered with one row and a permission, and the permission is in his own mouth and is not in any book in this city.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fourteen, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fourteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and seventy-three days, which is thirty-five months and twenty-three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

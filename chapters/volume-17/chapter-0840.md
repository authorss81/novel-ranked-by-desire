# No Question About A Question

Three thousand four hundred and ninety-three was the fourteenth of this month and the twentieth is day three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, and three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine less three thousand four hundred and ninety-three is six, and twenty less fourteen is six by the month count.

## What Came Up The Stairs On The Sixteenth

A woman came up the four flights at about eleven on the sixteenth of this month and asked whether a man who has rooms on the second floor had put a question to this office.

That is the whole of what she said and she said it in the ordinary voice and she had it written on a piece of her own paper because she wanted it written down.

She had heard it in a corridor. Not the fact, which is nothing. A thing said in a corridor about a man who came up four flights in April and asked whether there was anything under his own name.

It is about another person. It was not given.

And the refusal was entered, with the date of that morning and the hour against the date, in a clerk's hand, in the words she used: that a person asked whether another person had put a question to this office, and that the answer is not given, and that the person it is about did not come to that room.

That is correct and Wenna Raut entered it and read it back once.

Then she shut the book and stood with her hand on the cover and said, “Do you know what I have just put in it.”

## The Thing She Had Put In It

“You have put in that a person asked about a question,” said Lin Hao.

“I have put in that a person asked whether another person had put a question.” She took her hand off the cover. “Which means that book now holds a row about a question, and any person may put a question about any paper this office holds, and there is nothing in this city that stops them putting it about that row.”

“So somebody comes up in four years and asks me whether a woman came up on the sixteenth of this month and asked after a man.”

“Yes.”

“And I say that is not held here, because I am not going to enter it, and that is a lie, because it is held here, and it is held here under a date and an hour in a clerk's hand, and not one person in this city is ever going to take it out.”

“It will hold for about four years.”

## What He Decided, At Eleven, With The Door Open

He said it standing up at the end of the table, in the room, with the door open, because that is where he says the things he has decided, and about four people were in that corridor and heard all of it.

“From this morning, a question about a question is not entered.”

“Refused and entered,” said Wenna Raut. “That is what happened on the sixteenth.”

“Refused and not entered. Not entered as a refusal either. Not entered as a thing anybody asked. If a person comes up those stairs and wants to know whether this office holds a question, this office will not write down that it was asked.”

“That is not a practice that anybody can check.”

“No,” he said. “It is a practice, and it is a refusal, and it is not ink, and the sixth line of the second of December is still the last line that this office will ever make at the foot of one of its own pages, and nothing went under the foot of anything this morning and nothing ever will.”

She stood up, said it back to him in her own words in the voice she reads everything in, and put it in that book with the morning's date and the hour against it, in her own hand, which is where everything a clerk has decided in nine years has gone.

## What It Closes, Said Plainly To The Room

“It closes the second step,” he said. “A person may ask about any paper. From today a person may not ask about any question.”

“And refusing that is itself the next question, so a book of questions about questions becomes a book of who asked what, and that is a list of askers, and it is on the wall of that room in about four years with a date against every name on it.”

“That is a list of askers.”

“That is a list of askers, and this office has refused to make one for nine years and about four hundred people are answerable by name to their neighbours because of it.”

## And The Woman From The Sixteenth Was In The Corridor

She was in the corridor at about a quarter past eleven with her piece of her own paper in her hand, because she had come back on the fourth day to be told that she would be told.

He told her in the corridor, standing up, with about four other people in about four feet of air.

“This office will not tell you whether a man who has rooms on the second floor has put a question here.”

She said that was the answer she expected and she asked who had refused it and he said that was not going to be entered either.

“Then you have refused me and you have not written it down.”

“Yes.”

“Then in four years I will come up here again and ask the same thing and you will refuse me again and I will not be able to say that anybody ever refused me at all,” said the woman, “and that is the first time in my life that a thing has been done to me where the doing of it leaves no mark.”

“It leaves a mark.”

“Where.”

“In about four people standing in that corridor, hearing a man in a white plate say the word no twice in about nine minutes,” he said, “and it is theirs and it is not mine and there is nothing at the foot of any page for it.”

She put the paper in her coat and went down the four flights and neither of them said one word about it again.

## What It Costs, Said Plainly To The Room

“And here is what it costs and I want the whole of that corridor to have it, because I am not going to be the one who says it quietly later.”

There has been about nine questions a month through that door for about nine months, which is about eighty people, and about four rows in every nine of them are a day somebody asked about another person.

“Two populations of about eighty people each,” he said. “The ones who come up four flights, and the people they are asking about, who are somewhere else in this city and do not know it.”

“About four hundred households in this city keep a place under a mantel where a thing can be put down in a hand, and about nine people a month have stopped coming down a lane off Cordwain. I have never once been able to put a face at the top of one stair next to a face at the top of the other, and I decided in July not to, and this morning I have closed the last thing in this city that could have.”

“What last thing.”

“The rows themselves. That book is the only instrument this city has ever had that could have found out who is asking about whom, and from this morning it cannot be asked what is in it.”

## What It Costs Her

“You have cost us the instrument,” said Wenna Raut, “and about four days a month I already cannot say what we said, and now not one stranger can ever find out what we did about them.”

“That is the cost and I know it.”

“Then say the true version, because you have said everything else in that corridor and I am owed it.”

“The true version is that about eighty mouths have gone into that book since the second of December, and about four rows in every nine are about a person, and I am not going to spend the next four years wondering whether the same people keep turning up on each other's paper.” He put his hands in his pockets. “And I would rather not know than hold it.”

She did not write it down and neither of them expected her to.

## Down Four Flights And Along About Four Hundred Yards

He went down at about half past two and told the man who is paid nothing.

He told him standing behind the rail with his hands in his pockets, once, in about eleven seconds, and the counterman did not interrupt him once.

“Then I am the last man in this city who is asked and cannot answer,” said the counterman, “and I am paid nothing for it, and I have been reading the bottom of a page out loud to about nine people a week for nine years without knowing what any of it was for.”

“That is what you are.”

“I want to ask you one thing and you are not going to be able to answer it.”

“Ask it.”

“Am I allowed to know what I am refusing.”

There were about four people in that passage and all four of them heard a man who is paid nothing put a question to a man in a white plate.

“No,” said Lin Hao. “You are not allowed to know, and I would like somebody to write down that you asked and that I could not answer, and not one person in this city is going to do that.”

## What The Counterman Said He Was Going To Do

“Right,” said the counterman. He put his thumb along the edge of the rail and left it there for about four seconds. “I will refuse. And when they ask me what it is about, I am going to say I do not know, in the ordinary voice, the way I read everything else, and in about four years some of the people who come down here are going to believe there is nothing behind that door at all.”

“That is what it costs.”

“That is what it costs and I have been paid nothing for nine years so I am the right one to spend it on,” said the counterman, “and I am going to go on saying the two sentences of that page in the ordinary way for as long as they are printed, and if either of them ever comes back to me wrong then not one person in this city will be able to tell me and you will not be able to tell me either.”

He took his thumb off the wood.

“Do not promise me a day in four years.”

“I am not going to promise you a day in four years.”

“Right,” said the counterman. “Then we are both where we were.”

And at about ten to five he read the whole of the thirty-ninth sheet out to about nine people, at the same speed as the rest of it, and did not know what any of it was for, and is never going to be told.

*Three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine days. Less three thousand four hundred and ninety-three, the fourteenth of this month, is six, and from this morning a question about a question is not entered, which is a practice and a refusal and not ink, and nothing went under the foot of anything in that room all day.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is twenty, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and seventy-nine days, which is thirty-five months and twenty-nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

*And a row entered on the sixteenth of this month is in that book under a date and an hour, saying that a person asked whether another person had put a question, and there is now nothing in this city that will let anybody ask whether she did.*

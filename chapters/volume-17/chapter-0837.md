# The Thirty-Ninth Sheet

Three thousand four hundred and seventy-three was the twenty-fourth of this month and the thirtieth is day three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, and three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine less three thousand four hundred and seventy-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month and of the quarter.

## Squared Once, And Put In The Flat File

She squared it against the wood at about half past two on the thirtieth and then she put it in the flat file, and it went back down four floors and about four hundred yards at about three, and that is the whole of the quarter-end in the room off the third court.

It has been squared once at every quarter-end since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year. On the twenty-fourth of March in this year it came out six days early and it was squared twice, and neither of them said a word about it then and neither of them has said a word about it since.

There is nothing new at the bottom of it and there has been nothing new at the bottom of it since the thirtieth of March.

## Three Quarters Of Nothing At The Bottom Of It

That is three quarters, and before those it was a quarter that had four days of weather on it, and before that a quarter with about forty words in five hands scratched into four inches of dressed stone at the foot of a hill.

Nothing in this city has a column for what goes at the foot of that page, and nothing on which a person may propose it, and not one person here is entitled to put a seventh line under those six.

Not one clerk in that building has asked him for a seventh line. Not one person who reads it four times a year at about two thousand counters has asked him. That is not agreement and it is not obedience. It is the second of December, which promised three things and said nothing whatever about the foot of a page.

So on the morning of the thirtieth of September the sixth line was still the sixth line, and it was entered on the second of December of the year before, and it is the last one there will ever be.

## About Nine At The Rail, And Four Minutes Past Four

He went down four flights and along about four hundred yards and into the passage at about ten past four and there were about nine at the rail, which is not the number that stood at that rail on the twenty-ninth of this month.

Four of them had come for the sheet because it has been face up on that rail for ninety days and it goes down four times a year. Two of them had come because the thirty-eighth is the first one anybody here has seen come down since the second of December and a thing coming down can be watched, unlike a thing being put up.

The seventh was a woman who comes at the end of every quarter and has never once said why, and the eighth and ninth were a man and his brother, who had a long argument about the second column and lost it.

The thirty-eighth came off at four o'clock, ninety days after it went up, and the counterman took it down himself with both hands and set it against the wall inside the wood where it goes.

The thirty-ninth went up in its place.

It is the same sheet. It went up squared, the way it has gone up every quarter since the fifth year, and it carries nothing at the foot of it that has not been there since the second of December, and that sentence a man said out loud in a room on that day is the last one this office will ever add to that page.

The counterman went back behind the wood and read the whole of it to about nine people at the same speed as the rest of it.

## The Number At The Top Of It, Said Out Loud For The First Time

He read it himself, at about ten past four, out loud, over the top of the thirty-ninth sheet, to the nine who were still standing there, at the same speed as the counterman reads anything.

Eight hundred and fifty-two.

It has been at the head of that sheet since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and in every quarter-end since, and in the nine years this room has been a room, it has not moved a mark, and there is nothing alive in this city with the standing to move it.

He gave the two counts from the customer side of the rail with his hands in his pockets, in the ordinary voice, because there is no other voice in that passage and there never has been.

“Twenty-three sheets,” he said. “That is a count of sheets, and it is one of those words that means a thing that has come off a press.”

“Twenty-two quarters,” he said. “That is a count of quarter-ends gone by since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and a quarter in this city is ninety days, and three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine days less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is one thousand nine hundred and eighty, and that is twenty-two times ninety exactly and the division has nothing over it.”

He put two fingers on the wood.

“They differ by one, and here is the one. The sheet is a quarter, and the twenty-two are quarter-ends, and the twenty-three are sheets, and the first sheet ever written is a sheet and has never been a quarter-end. It is not a disagreement and it is not a rounding. It is one piece of paper that exists on a day which is not the first of any three months.”

About four of the nine worked it out and about two of the four said the word quarter out loud, and one man at the front asked whether a number that goes out four times a year could change between the times it went out, and Lin Hao said that it could not, and that if it ever did the whole of what the sheet was for would be over.

He had decided that on the previous morning in a room four floors up with the door open. About four people had been in that corridor, which is about four feet wide, and heard him say that he was going to do it, and the clerk had entered the decision as a practice with a date on it and an hour against it in her own hand and not in the office's.

She had also told him what it would cost, and the cost was that a number said at a rail goes home in about nine heads instead of staying in one, and that in about four years somebody would have the two counts and not the difference.

He said the difference out loud at the rail, which is the whole reason for saying it there, and not one of the nine who walked out of that passage that afternoon has either count on its own.

## The Man Who Came Back With A Square Of Pencil Paper

He was the fourth of eleven at that rail in the last quarter of the year before, in the rain, with four words of that foot written out on a piece of his own paper in his own hand.

He stood at the rail for about eleven minutes with the paper in his hand and did not read it out and did not ask anybody anything.

He read the foot of the page with his own eyes. All of it, at the speed the counterman reads it, and then he held the square of pencil paper up beside the wood and looked at the two of them side by side for about four seconds, and then he put the paper back in his coat.

He did not say whether he was right.

He said that to not one person at all, in the open air on the thirtieth of September, exactly as he said he would on the twenty-fourth of June to a clerk with a pen in her hand, and not one person in that passage knows anything about it now and not one person in this building was told.

He came up four flights that afternoon with nothing in his hands and no part of this with him, which is the arrangement of a man who has paid for a piece of paper and been told what it was worth.

He had asked for that on the twenty-fourth of June and had been given it in four things named and refused, and the fifth thing was a man who is paid nothing who has read it out for nine years and who has never once been asked whether he read it right.

That fifth thing is standing on the other side of that wood at this moment with his hands in his pockets.

## What He Did Instead, Which Took Him Until Half Past Four

At about half past four, when the nine had gone down to four and the counterman had gone behind the wood for the day, the man asked him one thing.

She was not there. She had gone up four flights about nine minutes before him and would not come down again until the next quarter, because she is a clerk and she has never once stood at that rail and is not going to start.

He asked the counterman whether he might read the foot of it.

The counterman put his thumb along the edge of the rail for about four seconds.

“No,” he said. “Not after today.”

“Why not.”

“Because on the twenty-ninth of this month I read the sixth line at that rail and put a word of my own into it in front of about nine people, and I have not been told whether the word was mine or the sheet's, and I am not going to have two of us reading the bottom of that page in one passage.” He took his thumb off the wood. “You read it. I will not stop you and I will not hear it.”

## Two Men Reading The Foot Of A Page

The man read it.

He read it at about half the speed the counterman reads it, which is what a person does who has heard it once in the rain and has been carrying four words of it for about a year, and he got to the end of the sixth line and stopped, and started it again from the head of the page, and got it right the second time in a way that two people listening could not have told apart.

About four people were in that passage and about two of them have told other people about it since, and one of the two has said that the man at the rail read it wrong, and the other has said he did not, and nothing in this city can put those two against each other and say which is right.

The thirty-ninth sheet was face up on that rail when he finished, and it will be face up there for ninety days, and the head of it has not moved since the fifth year.

There are now two people in this city who read the foot of that page out loud to strangers, and not one of them has ever had the page in his hands, and not one of them will ever be told.

*Three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine days. Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-three, the twenty-fourth of this month, is six, and the thirtieth is the last working day of a quarter, and the thirty-eighth sheet came off that rail at four o'clock after ninety days and the thirty-ninth went up in its place carrying no new line in the office hand.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is ninety, and every one of the ninety days that came before this afternoon was spent face up on that rail with the same sheet on it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and fifty-nine days, which is thirty-five months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

*Three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine days less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is one thousand nine hundred and eighty days, and that is twenty-two times ninety exactly, and the head of that sheet is eight hundred and fifty-two and has not moved across twenty-three sheets and twenty-two quarters, both named, both right, differing by one.*

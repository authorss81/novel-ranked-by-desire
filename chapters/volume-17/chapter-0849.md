# The Thirteenth Question

Three thousand five hundred and fifty-five was the sixteenth of this month and the twenty-third is day three thousand five hundred and sixty-two, and three thousand five hundred and sixty-two less three thousand five hundred and fifty-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## Eleven Sentences And Twelve Strangers

Eleven sentences have gone into that Register this year in the words of the people who used them, and there have been twelve strangers, and the two numbers are not the same number because one of them came up the four flights twice with the same question in the summer and both of the answers were true.

The hundred-and-second of those forms went up on the board against that kerb on the seventh of this month, and the two ruled squares at the foot of it are both empty, and that is not an event.

She came up at about ten past ten on the twenty-third of this month and she had not written anything on a piece of paper, which is the first time anybody has come up those four flights with a question in their mouth and nothing at all in their hands.

## What She Asked

She said it in that room with the door open and about four people within earshot of her, and she said all of it before anybody could ask her to say less of it.

“My sister cannot leave the house. She has not been out of that house since about the second month of this year and before that she went out on most days. I am not telling you why because it is not why. I want to know whether this office will send somebody to see her.”

“What do you want an answer to.”

“I want to know whether I am going to be frightened for the rest of my life,” said the woman. “That is the real question and I have written it down for myself in my own head about nine times on the way up four flights.”

## What This Office Is Allowed To Answer

Wenna Raut shut the book.

“This office answers questions about any paper anybody brings up themselves,” she said, “and it answers a question about another person in one way only, which is that it reads the answer out in this room once with the person it is about in the room when it is read, and if that person will not come then there is no answer and the row stands as it is.”

“She cannot come.”

“Then there is no answer and the row stands as it is,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am going to give you the second half of it, which is not about your sister and is about this room, and it is the only thing anybody in this city can give you today.”

“Go on.”

“This office will not send anybody to your sister’s house. Not today and not in nine days and not in nine years. There is no instruction in this city that lets a clerk go out of this door and tell a person that somebody has been asking after her, and if somebody ever did it in about four years from this room then this office would be the thing that goes and stands in a doorway and says a person has been asking about you.”

Wenna Raut squared the pen against the edge of the tray.

“And every answer it ever gave out of hand after that would be worth about nine minutes less than it is worth now.”

Wenna Raut put the pen into the tray and squared it.

“So that is a permission and not a fact. It tells you what this office will not do. It tells you nothing whatever about your sister, and it is going to stand exactly like that.”

## What Would Have Happened If She Could Come, And Why She Is Not Going To

He told her, because she asked and because he would have had to say it in about four years anyway.

“If she could come, then on a morning like this one she would come up those four flights at about eleven, and she would sit where you are sitting, and this office would read out whatever it holds about her in this room once, with you in the room and her in the room, and not one person afterwards would be told who had asked.”

“And what does it hold about her.”

“Nothing at all,” said Lin Hao. “There is nothing in that book about your sister and there has never been. What there is about her is a question you have put to this office, and that is all, and it is yours and not hers and we are not going to put her into it.”

“Then what am I being told.”

“You are being told that if she ever comes up those stairs this office will have to show her its own paper about her, whatever it is, in front of you, and there is a clerk in that room who has about four days a month when she cannot say what we said, and on those four days a month she will have to say it anyway.”

“So that is the second thing you do not want.”

“That is the second thing I do not want,” he said, “and it is the reason that the answer you have got this morning is a permission and not a fact, and it is the reason I am not going to stand here and tell you that if she cannot come then the answer is no. The answer is that the answer does not exist yet.”

## What Went In, In Her Own Words

She wrote it as she had said it and read it back once.

That on the twenty-third of this month a person asked whether this office would send somebody to her sister, who cannot leave a house, and that the answer is that it will not, and that the office will not send to anybody, and that there is no instruction in this city that lets it.

That is what the row says.

There is nothing under it and nothing is going under it until one of them comes up those four flights, and the day it was asked is on the row and the hour is on the row, and the day it may be answered is not on the row and cannot be, because a day that has not happened is not a day.

“Will you hold it,” said the woman.

“We hold it until you come or until it is about four years old, whichever is first, and after four years a row with nothing under it is a row about a person this office asked about, and it will be on that wall in a book and anybody may ask about it.”

“Then somebody can find out I asked.”

“Somebody can find out that a person asked a question in this room and got an answer about this office and nothing about her sister,” said Wenna Raut. “They cannot find out it was you, and that is not because you are protected. It is because you are not in it.”

The woman went down the four flights.

## His Own Row

He took a chair at the end of that table afterwards and did not go near the book, and then he left that chair where it was for about four minutes without touching anything, and then he opened the book and looked at one row and shut it again.

It is his own, put in a year ago this month, with a date at the head and an hour under the date and then nothing whatever, and it has stood that way ever since, and three thousand five hundred and sixty-two less three thousand and eighty-two is four hundred and eighty days.

Four hundred and eighty days is sixteen months, and sixteen months is sixteen quarters, and nothing in this city has ever seen the inside of that space and nothing ever will.

He did not go near it and he did not say a word about it, and Wenna Raut did not look at him while he did not say a word about it.

## The Part Neither Of Them Said Out Loud

About nine answers went out of that room by hand between the second and the twenty-third of this month and nothing in this city could have checked a single one of them, because a man in a white plate burned the only copy of the foot of that page on the eighteenth of the month before last and forbade the fact of it being entered on a page of his own.

At about half past ten he said, “That is the twelfth stranger this year and there are eleven sentences in that book and you will put the twelfth on the page and it will say eleven.”

“I will put it on the page and it will say eleven,” said Wenna Raut, “and the scope will be eleven questions asked in this room in this year and it will not be the number of people who asked them, because about one person in this city asked twice, and the number of people is twelve.”

“Write that.”

“I have written it,” said Wenna Raut, “and the figure is a count of rows and it carries the year on it and not the office and not the people, and if a stranger comes up those stairs in four years and asks how many people have been answered here this year, then this office can tell them the answer to about nine a month and nothing else, and that is what happened to the four hundred and thirty rows and it will happen to these.”

*Three thousand five hundred and sixty-two days. Less three thousand five hundred and fifty-five, the sixteenth of this month, is seven, and a twelfth stranger put a question into that book in her own words and the answer under it was a permission and not a fact, and there is nothing under it and there will be nothing under it until she comes or until four years.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is eighty-three, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand one hundred and forty-two days, which is thirty-eight months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

*And three thousand five hundred and sixty-two less three thousand and eighty-two, the twenty-third of August of the ninth year, is four hundred and eighty days, which is sixteen months, and a row with a space in the middle of it stood in that bound Register for every one of them with no hand going near it.*

# Two Books Four Hundred Yards Apart

Three thousand three hundred and sixty-nine was the tenth of this month and the seventeenth is day three thousand three hundred and seventy-six, and three thousand three hundred and seventy-six less three thousand three hundred and sixty-nine is seven, and seventeen less ten is seven by the month count.

## A Question With A Day In It And Nothing In Her Hands

The woman came up the four flights at about eleven with nothing, which is the thing to notice first, because everything that works in that room goes down those stairs with something in it.

She had a wet sleeve on one arm and a bundle of string in the other hand, and she stood in the corridor about four feet wide with about four other people in it and did not go to the wood until two of them had gone.

“I want to ask about my neighbour,” she said.

“You have not brought a paper.”

“No.”

“Then it is about a person,” said Wenna Raut, “and I will tell you what that means before you say it, because you have not brought anything, and if you had brought it then it would go to you in a fold and this would not happen.”

She said the whole of it standing up, in the voice she reads everything in, and the woman listened to all of it and then asked her question anyway, which is what happens about nine times a month.

She wanted to know whether the second of June is a day anything was written down anywhere in this city about what happened in a kitchen at four doors from where she lived. She gave the day twice, the same way, and she gave it a third time because she said she had heard it said somewhere and did not know where.

He took the row himself and read it back and the clerk entered it in the words used, with the date at the head of it and the hour against the date, and the hour was eleven.

## The Arithmetic He Did Standing Up

There are two books in this city about four hundred yards apart and there is one man alive who has read both.

One is the bound Register on that table, open under the window with a ruler squared along the edge of it. The other is about nine sheets under a third table in a back room off a street two streets wide, four flights up and about four hundred yards off, and it may not be cited by anybody in any room and it has never been cited once.

He said the whole of it out loud at the end of the table with the door open, because the clerk is the only person in this city who can tell a record from a decision.

“The second of June is in that book if anybody in this city brought a paper through that door on that day and somebody wrote down what they said about it. That is all. It is a day this office was given something about.”

“And if nothing was.”

“Then this office holds nothing about that day, and it holds nothing about that kitchen, and it holds one row with a day on it that no person in this city can check, because the only other place in this city where a day is written down is four hundred yards away under a table and may not be cited by anybody.”

He put two fingers on the row.

“I have wanted to go up those four flights and stand at that table since the seventh of April and I have not gone, and the reason is standing on the second landing of that stair and I gave it to a stairwell there on the third of June of the year before.”

“Why did you not go.”

“Because the two books are four hundred yards apart and the man who knows about both of them has never walked the distance carrying anything about anybody,” he said. “And that is the only reason they are two books. And I am not going to be the reason they are one.”

## What The Book Is Now, And Was Not In The Ninth Year

“You have been talking about that day as though the day were the difficulty.”

“It is the difficulty.”

“It is the day,” said Wenna Raut. “About four of every nine rows this quarter are about a person. Every row in that book has a day against it. In the ninth year those days were days about papers and anybody could tell the two apart by looking at them.”

“Now about four rows in nine are a day somebody asked about another person, and every one of them is in there in the same column in the same hand as the other five.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table.

“So a day in that book is not a neutral thing. Four days in every five in it are days somebody asked about another person, and if you match one of those against a day in a laundry then you are matching a day about a person against a day about a person, and you will not be able to say afterwards which of the two it was that moved.”

He stood there for a while and did not say the next part.

“You have been carrying that since the seventh of April and you have not once put it in a book.”

“I have been carrying it since the fourth of April,” said Lin Hao, “and I did not know until you said it.”

## Half Past Four, And Four Hundred Yards, In Daylight

He went down the four flights at half past four in the afternoon with nothing in either hand, which is the only way to do it, and along about four hundred yards past a market end that is shut and a run of washrooms with the door propped, and up four narrow flights where two people going the other way have to agree about it.

The street that laundry stands on is two streets wide, and that is why there has never been a door onto a street at the end of it.

He had been up that stair four times in four years and not once since the third of June of the year before.

## The Edge Of The Table

She was not in.

There is a back room off the front floor of that laundry, nine feet by eleven, and a third table in it with nothing on it, and nine sheets under the edge of that table, her own paper and her own hand, and a heading on a column of one of them since the twenty-first of February of the eighth year.

He stood in the doorway and did not go past it.

There was nothing to touch and nothing to read. The sheets are under the edge of a table and what shows at nine feet is the top inch of each one, and what is written at the top of a sheet is a heading or, on two of the nine, a day.

He read the day on the top of the second sheet and the day on the top of the seventh sheet.

Neither of them was the second of June.

He stood there for about nine minutes and read what there was, and his chest was in its ordinary condition by the fourth of the nine.

That was the whole of what nine years of being careful had bought. A man can stand in a doorway and read two days out of about two hundred, and cannot get one day nearer, and there is no arrangement in this city that would let him get one day nearer, and the arrangement does not work because the sheets are hidden. It works because not one person has ever stood in that doorway long enough to read the two that show.

He had stood in it long enough. It had taken him nine minutes and it had cost him an afternoon and a woman in the next room had to put her hand-truck down.

## What She Said About It

“You have been in my back room.”

“I have been in your doorway.”

“You have been in my doorway at half past four on a working afternoon with nothing in your hands,” said Perla Quist, “and I came through the front floor with a truck and put it down, and you did not go past the sill and I want that said in this room in those words because it is the only thing about this afternoon that you did right.”

She kept the truck where she had put it.

“Now tell me what you came for.”

He told her. A woman at the end of that corridor had asked about the second of June and there is a row with a day on it and the office holds nothing else about that day, and he wanted to know whether one of nine sheets carried it.

“You could have asked me.”

“No.”

“You could not have asked me, and I have been standing at the top of that stair since the third of June of the year before waiting for you to work that out.”

## What Nine Years Of Being Careful Is Actually Worth

She went past him into the room and stood at the third table and looked down at the edge of it the way she has done for nine years.

“I have stood in this room for nine years on one thing,” she said. “Not on the stair and not on the floor. On the fact that a person cannot see my sheets from the door. That is the whole of my standing and I have never once said it out loud because I have never once wanted to hear it.”

“You have just seen the top inch of nine sheets.”

“I have just watched a man read two days off the top of two of them at nine feet and not go past a sill,” said Perla Quist. “And nothing in this room has moved. Nothing is going to move. Nine sheets of my own paper under a table in a back room cannot be locked, because a lock is a thing a person can be got at, and they cannot be carried, because a woman who carries them about this city is a woman with nine sheets on her.”

She put one finger on the edge of the third table.

“And they cannot be put anywhere else in this building, because every other room in it has somebody else’s work in it, and I have never once asked to be allowed to put them in one.”

She turned round.

“And I have had nine years of telling myself that this front door is a wall. It is not a wall. It is nine inches of table and a man with nine minutes and good eyes. And you did not have to be clever. You did not have to be wicked. You had a day and you had a doorway and you were good at arithmetic, and you stood in it.”

The floor was wet where the steam came under the door and the light off the front floor had gone round behind the board across the middle of it.

“I cannot get that back,” she said. “That is the whole of what you have cost me and I am not going to price it, because a woman who prices things is a woman who can be asked what she charges, and I have never once been asked what I charge. I have been asked what I would do. And I have never once had the answer ready and I have never once needed it, because in nine years not one person has stood in that door and read anything.”

He said nothing, and there was nothing to say to it, and she had not said it for him to answer.

“You are going to be about nine minutes in that doorway again in about four years,” said Perla Quist, “and so is the next one, and the day after that somebody will bring a lamp. I am not asking you to stay out of it. I have never asked anybody to stay out of anything. I am telling you that you did not have to come, and I was not going to find out, and now I know.”

She got the hand-truck up off the floor.

“You can go down those four flights now, and I am going to stand here for a while, and that is not an invitation.”

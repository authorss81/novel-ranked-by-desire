# The Clerk Reads Back

Three thousand three hundred and thirty-four was the fifth of this month and the twelfth is day three thousand three hundred and forty-one, and three thousand three hundred and forty-one less three thousand three hundred and thirty-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## What Was Offered On The Ninth Of March And Who Took It

On the ninth of March a man was told that any person in this city could ask to hear an answer read instead of carried, in that room, in the clerk's voice, with the reading itself entered as a row with its own date on it.

He was not the man who asked for it. He asked for it half an hour later and then asked for it with his sister-in-law in the room, which is a different thing and is still standing in the bound Register in his own words with a date and an hour against it and nothing at all under it.

The offer is not a favour. It was said in a room to one man and it was said that any person could have it, and a clerk at that counter would say the same thing to anybody, and none of it is in a book because there is nowhere in that office to put a thing of that kind.

For sixty-one days it had not been taken.

## The Man Who Took It On The Ninth Of This Month

He was about fifty and he had rooms on the second floor and a paper he had brought up himself in the autumn, and he had wanted to hear it.

That is the safe kind and both of them understood it in nine seconds.

An answer about a paper a person brought up themselves is about that person. It is not about anybody else. It says what is on the page and stops there, and no person in this city is made smaller by hearing it.

“You may have it read,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am going to say the price out loud before I do it, because I have said the price out loud every single time since the spring.”

## The Price, Said Before

“There are about four people in that corridor and this corridor is about four feet wide and they will hear every word of it.”

“I know how many are in it.”

“You do not, and neither does he.” She stood up and put the pen down across the book, which is what she does when she has given a question and an hour away together. “And the other thing is that reading it in there and reading it on this side of the door are the same act at four feet, and there is nothing I can do about which side of the door a person happens to be standing on.”

“I would rather have it read than carried.”

“You have not asked about the second thing,” said Wenna Raut. “The second thing is that you will be standing in this room and four people will be standing outside it and there is not one of them who will know that what they are hearing is yours.”

The man thought about it for four seconds.

“Read it.”

## What She Did

She took the answer off the stack and she stood up, and she read it out in the voice she reads everything in.

That voice has not changed in nine years. It is level and it is unhurried and it is the voice she uses for a column of figures and the voice she uses for the four sentences at the foot of a quarterly sheet and the voice she used in a corridor in the spring when she read a denial out to about four people who had carried a sentence up a lane.

She read all four lines and she did not hurry any of them and she did not look up at the end of it.

The man stood at the other side of the table and listened to his own paper in a room where he was the only person it was about, and then he said thank you and went down four flights.

Wenna Raut entered the reading as a row, in her own hand, with the date at the head of it and the hour against the date.

She entered the date and the hour and the words the man used when he asked, and she entered that the answer was read aloud in that room, and she entered nothing else at all.

## What Four People Heard, And Which Four

There were four people in that corridor and every one of them heard the whole of it.

The corridor outside that room is about four feet wide and runs about nine paces, and there is no window in it and no bench, and about nine people stand in it on the busiest mornings of a month and four people stand in it on most mornings.

The first of the four had a question of her own and got it and has never spoken about it again. The second was a man who comes up twice a month for no reason anybody has established. The third was a boy who was waiting, and Lin Hao has never asked him anything and is never going to.

The fourth was a man who has lived four doors down from the man whose answer it was, since before either of them had anything to do with this office, and who came up that stair on the ninth of this month because he had a question about a paper of his own.

He was standing in that corridor with his own answer in his hand, in a fold, and he heard four lines read out in a level voice about a paper with a date on it and no figure and no name, and he had a piece of pencil paper in his coat because the man who reads a page at a rail had told him in March to carry paper.

He went down four flights and stood in the street at the bottom and wrote all four of them out in his own hand in two minutes, and folded it, and went home, and has not been near this building since.

He does not know what it was about. He has not asked. There are about four hundred people in this city who know a sentence and not what it is about, and this is one of them, and it is the first one this office has made on purpose.

Nothing here can do anything about it. A man four doors down cannot be asked what he wrote on a piece of pencil paper in a street, because a thing written in a street did not arrive through that front door, and about four hundred of them came the same way out of a passage off Cordwain in the last quarter of last year and not one of those was ever asked about either.

The difference between that and this is nine seconds in a corridor.

## The Difference Between The Two Things, Said Out Loud

Afterwards he asked her about it, and he asked her about it because he could not get the shape of it right, and she answered him in nine words because she had been waiting four days for somebody to ask.

“In February she refused to read an answer out in that corridor and had not been asked to.”

“Yes.”

“This morning she read one out and a man asked her to.”

“Yes.”

“So the difference is a request.”

“The difference is a request,” said Wenna Raut, “and a request is a thing a person can be got to change their mind about, and a decision is a thing they cannot.”

She squared the book.

“That is not me being better about it than I was in February. That is me being worse.”

“In February I decided, and I was the only person in that room who could be argued out of it, and no one argued, and there is no row anywhere that says I did it.”

“This morning a man stood at that wood and asked, and I entered what I did with a date on it, and in about four years there is a row in that book with his words against mine, and there is nothing anybody can do about it.”

She put the pen down.

“And a request is the cheapest thing in this city. It costs the person asking nothing at all, and he does not have to know what the corridor is.”

He stood at the other end of that table for a while.

“Say the last of it.”

“The last of it is that it works,” said Wenna Raut. “It is the right thing to do and it is safe and it is what a person wants when they ask for it.”

“In about two months there will be four of these a week, and every one of them will be somebody’s own paper, and nobody will ever be hurt by any of them, and that will be true.”

“And it will also be the reason there is no rule. A rule cannot say a sentence like only ever about the asker’s own paper in a book with no heading in it.”

“Then it is a good practice.”

“It is a good practice with a hole the size of this morning in it,” she said, “and the hole is four feet wide, and on the ninth of this month a man four doors down from the man it was about wrote it out on pencil paper and put it in his coat.”

## What Is In The Street

He went down at ten to twelve with nothing in his coat.

One hundred and sixty days since the second of December, and the thirty-seventh sheet had been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them, and one man four doors down from another man had four lines in his own handwriting on a piece of pencil paper that did not come through a door.

Nothing in this city will let anybody set a copy made by ear against an original, and nothing ever has.

And the man who wrote it out lives four doors down from the man it is about, and did not know what it was about while he was standing there.

He did the second floor that night from eleven until half past five, four washrooms, and stopped four minutes at the top of the stairs about half past one, and the thing he took up those stairs with was not the four lines.

It was that a man asked a clerk a question in a corridor on the ninth of this month and neither of them thought about the other three, and that is how every rule in this city has ever broken.

*Three thousand three hundred and forty-one days. Less three thousand three hundred and thirty-four, the fifth of this month, is seven, and an offer made in a room on the ninth of March stood for sixty-one days and was taken on the ninth of this month by a man who wanted to hear it, and a clerk read four lines out loud in the voice she reads everything in, and about four people in a corridor about four feet wide heard every word of it.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of March, is forty-two, and the thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-two of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and twenty-one days, which is thirty months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

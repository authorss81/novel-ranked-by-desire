# The Bundle Stays Twenty-Six

Three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven was the eighteenth of this month and the twenty-fourth is day three thousand four hundred and seventy-three, and three thousand four hundred and seventy-three less three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven is six, and twenty-four less eighteen is six by the month count.

## The Count At The End Of A Month

On the last morning of a month the bundle on that table is counted, and it has been counted at the end of every month for about nine years, and the count is not a record of anything.

It is a check that nothing has been taken off that table and nothing has been put on it. That is all it has ever been. There is a column for it nowhere and there is a heading for it nowhere and the clerk does it with her eyes and does it in about forty seconds.

Twenty-six.

Twenty-five of them are papers of some kind, and they have been there so long that about four of them have come apart at a fold and are held together by the weight of the others. The twenty-sixth is a sheet of office paper with nothing written on it at all.

None of the twenty-five has ever been asked after by anybody, and the not asking is the arrangement and not an oversight.

## The Twenty-Sixth Is Not Counted As A Thing

She has never counted the twenty-sixth as a thing.

That is not an instruction. There is nothing anywhere that tells her not to, and if you asked her she would tell you that she has never counted it because it is not a thing, and she could not tell you what it is, and she would say both of those sentences in the same breath and mean the second one more than the first.

On the twenty-fourth of this month she got to the end of the count and stopped.

“Twenty-five and one sheet,” she said.

“That is twenty-six.”

“That is twenty-five and one sheet,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have been saying that out loud at the end of a month for about three years and this morning I have noticed that I do it, and I would like to know whether I am about to find out what it is for.”

## What A Bundle Is, And What It Is Not

A bundle is twenty-six things on a table that nothing may be taken off and nothing may be put on, and it has been that for about three years, and the check at the end of a month is the only time anybody in that room counts it and the count takes about forty seconds and is done with the eyes.

An archive has a finding hand, and this has four people a week looking at it and not one of them looking for anything. A record is written down, and none of this is: the count is done with the eyes and spoken and gone the same minute.

It is not a list, and that is the whole of why it is allowed to exist at all.

About three years ago somebody in that corridor asked what was on the table and the answer had to be that about four people a week can see it and not one of them can say what it is, and from that morning the arrangement has been that the table is looked at and not read.

“The trouble with a table like that,” said Wenna Raut, “is that it is waiting for a day when somebody needs to know what is on it, and on that day we will all find out that we cannot tell, and that day is the day the bundle becomes a list whether we write one or not.”

“Then somebody will write one.”

“Somebody will write one, and it will not be in this room, because in this room there is a woman standing here who refused a heading in the spring before anybody asked her to.” She put her thumb along the edge of the bundle. “And that is the whole of the defence of that table and it is one woman and not an instruction.”

## What He Said, And The Fact That He Said It

He said it at the end of that table with the door open, because there is one person in this city who can tell a record from a decision and she was on the other side of the wood.

He said that a blank sheet of office paper, unfolded and undated and with nothing on it, is what you put in front of a person who cannot write down the question they came up four flights with.

He said that this office promised anybody in the words they used, and that a person who cannot write those words has nothing to put in the Register, and that a row with nothing in it is a hole with a date on it.

He said that a woman who works a lift at a market end had told him that on the fifth of this month, on a platform stopped in the middle of its run, and that he had carried it about for nineteen days, and that he had said a part of it in this room once already on the twelfth without the sentence about the sheet, and that he was not going to do it a third time.

“You have told me a thing about a piece of paper on that table,” said Wenna Raut.

“Yes.”

“In the words a woman used, and I have them, and I may enter them, because you gave them to me and a clerk may only enter what she is given.”

“No.”

## Why She Did Not Enter It, Which Took Her About Four Seconds

“Why not.”

“Because the moment this office writes down what the twenty-sixth thing on that table is for, it has written down that there is a class of person this office knows about and does not know about at the same time.” She had not written anything and she had not moved the pen.

“And then in about four years somebody will come up those stairs and ask who they are, and I will have to say I do not know, and I will have said it in the voice I read everything in.”

“Then where does it live.”

“It lives on that table,” said Lin Hao, “with nothing written on it, and in about two heads, and one of those heads is paid a wage for moving crates.”

## The Copy In The Coat, And The Hand That Did Not Take It Out

At about half past eleven he put his right hand into the inside of his coat on the left, where four things have been for about a year, and his fingers went to the second of them and stopped.

It is one piece of paper in a woman's own hand, on her own paper, dated, with four lines on it and no figure on any of them, and it is the only copy of the foot of that quarterly sheet in the world.

She has said no about it six times in substance and given it once and has never been thanked and does not expect to be.

He took his hand out again with the paper still where it was.

“You went in there for something,” said Wenna Raut.

“I went in there for something and I did not take it out.”

“Why not.”

“Because it cannot be produced in a room and it cannot be produced in this building and it cannot be produced by you. The day it comes out of that coat is a day with a name on it, and I am not that day and I have not chosen the day.”

“That is a third reason you have given me this month for not writing something down.”

“It is.”

“And I have kept all three of them and I have entered none of them and I would like you to understand that I am keeping them in the same place you keep yours, which is nowhere.”

## What She Said She Was Not Going To Say

“Then I am going to say the thing you have not said, and you are going to let me,” said Wenna Raut.

“You have put a piece of paper on that table that I am not allowed to write on, and you have told me what it is for, and neither of us is going to write a line about it. From this morning I am one of three people in this city who know, and one of the three is not owed anything by anybody.”

“Yes.”

“I am a clerk on a wage,” she said, “and I am not going to say I am not paid enough for it, because that is a thing a person says when she wants a thing changed and I do not want a thing changed and you did not ask me.”

He let it stand where she had put it, because a thing said at the end of a working morning in that voice is not said for a reply.

“Put it back at the near end,” she said. “And in about four years one of us will not be here, and whichever of the two of us it is will go to a market end about four hundred yards off and stand in a doorway and not go past the sill, and that will be that.”

“And if it is me who is not here.”

“Then the twenty-six things will be on that table with nothing written on the twenty-sixth of them and not one person in this city will know why, and I have decided that is a better ending than a row,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am not going to be argued out of it and you are not going to argue me out of it.”

She put the bundle back with twenty-six things on it and nothing added and nothing taken off, and the twenty-sixth was at the near end on the drawer side with nothing written on it, and the count at the end of next month will be twenty-five and one sheet again.

*Three thousand four hundred and seventy-three days. Less three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven, the eighteenth of this month, is six, and a bundle on a table was counted at the end of a month for the ninth year running and came to twenty-five things and one sheet, and a man told a clerk what the sheet is for, and nothing was written down.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is eighty-four, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and fifty-three days, which is thirty-five months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

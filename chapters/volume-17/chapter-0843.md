# The Steps Say One Thing

Three thousand five hundred and thirteen was the fourth of this month and the eleventh is day three thousand five hundred and twenty, and three thousand five hundred and twenty less three thousand five hundred and thirteen is seven, and eleven less four is seven by the month count.

## The Eleventh Of This Month, About Four In The Afternoon

The light had gone by four in the afternoon in the eleventh month and the stalls along the top of that yard were shuttered from one end to the other, and there were two lamps burning on the brackets of the arcade and a wind coming off the tops of them that took the heat out of a face in about four seconds.

He had been up that hill since the middle of August and he had not been up it in daylight in about nine years, and the reason he had not been up it in daylight was that she had never once asked him to and he had never once given a reason out loud for not asking.

She was standing at the foot of her own two steps with her back to the rail and a barrow standing where a barrow stands every night of the year against the end of the last stall.

“I have been up here since the first,” he said.

“I know you have. You have had it about ten days and you have not written it down and you have not asked me what it was, and you came up here in the dark in the cold to ask me whether you may put it in that book.”

“Yes.”

## Why It Is Not A Claim

“No,” said Agnete Lindqvist.

And then she told him the reason, and it took her about eleven seconds, and she told it standing still on flat stone with her hands down at her sides.

“What I said on those steps on the first of this month was not a claim. Nothing was asked of anybody and nothing was refused to anybody and nothing is owed to anybody as a result of it. A woman gets up and she says one thing into about eleven thousand people and then she goes down, and there is no question and there is no answer and there is no party to anything.”

“If I put it in the Register it becomes a row.”

“If you put it in that Register then those steps become a counter,” she said. “A counter is a place where a thing gets added up.”

“You would have a date against it and an hour against the date and your clerk’s hand at the foot of it, and in about four years somebody would come up your four flights and ask what this office holds about a month of standing on a step in a yard. And you would have to tell them whether you were counting the things I say or whether I happened to say them.”

He did not say anything for about nine seconds.

“There is a difference,” he said.

“There is a large one and it is the only one I have ever cared about and I have never had to explain it to anybody until this evening.”

## The Thing She Will Not Tell Him

“I am not going to tell you what I said.”

“All right.”

“You are going to ask me in about four seconds.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I am not going to ask you, and that is the first thing I have not asked you for in the whole of the time I have known you, and you are going to hate it.”

She turned her head about nine degrees.

“Why are you not going to ask me.”

“Because you said it on a step once a month and left it unanswered and that is the whole of what it is, and the moment I ask you what it was I am asking you to repeat a thing that was said into a yard to people who could not use it, and you did not say it to me.”

“And what you have been carrying up that hill since the first is nothing.”

“What I have been carrying up that hill since the first is that it happened and I do not know what,” he said. “And I heard it, and I want you to understand what that is, because it is worse than the other one.”

“I did not hear it. You were told about it, in a corridor, by a woman with a pencil paper in her hand, and it has been in her mouth and in mine since the middle of September, and neither of us was in that yard.”

“It has been in about four people's mouths by now,” said Agnete Lindqvist.

“Yes.”

“You cannot unhear it and you cannot un-tell it and you cannot enter it, and that is three things this morning and it is the whole of what you get out of me.”

## What She Says Before Anything Happens

Then she said the thing she was going to say before any of it happened, which is what she does, and she said it standing on the flat stone with her coat still on.

“I have not asked a thing of anybody in thirty years and I am not going to start with you,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “I have never asked that office for anything. I did not ask for the room and I did not ask for the yard and I did not ask for the six lines. I have taken what I wanted every time for about four years and I have never once said the word please to anybody in this city.”

“I know.”

“You do not know. You have seen me say the word no. You have not seen me ask.”

She got her hands up and undid the top two buttons of her coat.

“You are going to say something now,” she said. “I can see it standing behind your teeth and it is going to be about whether you are able to pay for this, and you have nothing, and I am not asking you to pay for it.”

“I want you in about four seconds. I have been standing in the cold on that step for about an hour and a half and in every year I have stood on it about nine people a week have come up that path, and not one of them has ever wanted me for anything.”

He did not say anything at all.

“That is the first time in about four years I have said that sentence out loud to a man who could have used it,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “And you have never once asked me for anything, and that is the only reason it was safe.”

## The Barrow And The Wheel And The Wet Stone

She went down onto the flat stone with her back against the near wheel of that barrow, which is iron and which took the cold out of her and put it into about nine inches of her back at once, and she did not get the coat off and she did not push it behind her.

The stone was wet and she said so out loud, and then told him that was not the reason.

“The stone is wet,” she said. “That is not why I have put my back down on it. I have put my back down on it because you are going to be on top of me and if I make you do that standing up your chest will go inside four minutes and you will have to be helped and you will be helped in a yard.”

“You have thought about that.”

“I have thought about nothing else since the first of this month,” said Agnete Lindqvist, “and you may as well have the whole of it, because you have never once asked me for a whole of anything.”

She got her knees up and her heels into the wet and got hold of him through the front of what he was wearing with two hands, and looked down at him, and told him he was already hard and told him she had known it since about the middle of the afternoon from about four hundred yards off.

“Say something,” she said.

“Anything.”

“From four hundred yards off I knew, and now you are about eleven inches off me and you have not said one word, and I have had four years of people looking careful at me and I am telling you now that I will know it again inside a minute and that is the whole of what I have.”

He was not careful with her.

## What She Said All The Way Through

She said most of it. She was not quiet about any part of it and she has never been quiet about any part of it and she told him on the side of his face that he was the only person in this city who came up a hill she was standing on.

“There are about eleven thousand people who use that step in a year,” she said, “and about nine of them a week stop and look at me, and not one of them has ever come up that path afterwards, and you have come up it about four times in nine years and you have never once been careful, and I have thought about that on a step about twice a week for two years.”

She had her fingers hooked in the hair at the back of his head and she was not using them.

“Do not be quick about it,” she said. “The last one of these was in a year I am not going to name, in a room, and it lasted about nine minutes, and I have been thinking about the end of it every week since, and the end of it is the part left out while it is happening.”

“Why are you telling me that now.”

“Because you are the only person I have said it to who did not ask me for it,” said Agnete Lindqvist, “and because I am not going to have anybody use the fact that I have wanted this against me for the rest of my life, and you cannot use it, because you did not ask for it, and that is the whole of what you are worth to me and it took me about nine seconds to work out.”

She worked him with her hips and the wheel took up about an inch of slack in the wet and the whole barrow came over against his shoulder and stopped there, and she made a noise about the second time that made the sound change.

“Again,” she said, when he had got his breath, and he did it again, and the third time she put one boot flat on the wet and pushed and had all of him and said his name once and waited for him.

He got his mouth to the side of her neck and she turned her head so he could get under her jaw.

“There,” she said. “Twenty years of a yard and four years of not being wanted by anybody at all, and that is the first time in four years I have not had to work out first whether I was in the mood.”

## Afterwards, On The Stone, With Two Lamps

She got herself off him and got her coat straight and lowered herself back against the wheel with her knees up, and the wind off the arcade went over both of them and neither of them was in any hurry about it.

He said that he understood it completely and that he was going to lose the ability to do anything about it.

“No,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “That is the thing I have been waiting thirty years to hear somebody say at me and it is not what you are going to lose.”

“What am I going to lose.”

“The right to say that you did not hear it,” she said. “You can refuse to put it in that book and you can refuse to answer a question about it, and you will go on being able to do both of those, and you have believed for about two minutes that not being able to do anything is what refusing costs, and it is not.”

She turned her head about nine degrees.

“The office can refuse to enter it,” said Agnete Lindqvist. “And it cannot refuse to have heard that it was said, and neither can you, and you have known that since the middle of September and you came up a hill in the dark to find out whether I would help you not know it.”

He put his hands on his knees.

“No,” he said.

“No,” she said. “And I am not going to miss one.”

“You have never missed one.”

“I have not missed one since about the fourth hundredth day and I am not going to start in the eleventh month of a year in which that office has decided it would rather not know who is asking about whom.” She got up off the stone and shook the wet off the back of her coat.

“The day I miss one is the day your office has won, and it will not be because anybody in it did anything. It will be because everybody who has stood on that step for thirty years will have asked somebody for something, and you will be the last of them, and you will have done it by refusing.”

She went into the arcade and did not answer anybody, which is what she does.

*Three thousand five hundred and twenty days. Less three thousand five hundred and thirteen, the fourth of this month, is seven, and a woman who has said one thing a month on two steps since a March in year five told a man nothing at all and took what she wanted and did not miss one.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is forty-one, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-one of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand one hundred days, which is thirty-six months and twenty days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

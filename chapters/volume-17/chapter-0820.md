# The Answer Goes With Both Present

Three thousand three hundred and sixty-two was the third of this month and the tenth is day three thousand three hundred and sixty-nine, and three thousand three hundred and sixty-nine less three thousand three hundred and sixty-two is seven, and ten less three is seven by the month count.

## Two People On One Stair Who Had Found Each Other

The woman who was refused in a doorway on the third of May and the man who was refused on the nineteenth of the same month came up the four flights together on the morning of the tenth of this month, side by side, and neither of them knew why the other one was there.

They had met on a pavement about nine feet from a rail in a street of eleven doors on one side and nine on the other, in the last week of the month before, in about four minutes, and they had not said one word about this office to each other since, and they had arranged it between them over eleven days with about nine conversations in a lane.

They came up the stair together and they stood at the wood together and about nine people in a corridor about four feet wide heard every word of what they said, which is the arrangement of that corridor and has been since the second of December.

“We will not go one at a time,” said the woman.

“No,” said the man.

“He goes first and I am told afterwards.”

“No,” said the woman.

## What They Asked For

They did not know what to ask for. That is the true shape of it and it took the clerk about nine minutes to get out of them.

They did not want the answer. They wanted to be in the same room at the same time as each other while somebody said it, so that neither of them was going to be told afterwards, in a street, by the other one.

And then the woman said the thing neither of them had come to say, and she said it standing at the wood with her hands behind her back.

“Ask him to come.”

There is no response in the whole of that office to that, and the clerk had the pen in her hand about four seconds later and had not written anything.

## What He Worked Out In About A Minute

“An answer about a person goes in one room with both of them in it,” he said, “and the person it is about has to be told that somebody has asked and has to be asked to come, and if they do not come there is no answer.”

“That is not an answer to anything,” said Wenna Raut. “That is a rule and I cannot enter it and neither can you.”

“It is the rule and I have been standing here for four months looking for it and it was in front of me the whole time.”

“Nothing in this city lets this office ask anybody anything,” said Wenna Raut. “You may ask a person a question they have put to you. You cannot ask a person a question they have not put to you, and you certainly cannot send a runner to a house to say that somebody has been asking about you and would you come up four flights.”

She put the pen across the book.

“That is not a practice either. That is a thing a clerk is not allowed to originate and a man in a coat is not allowed to do on his own account.”

“I know.”

## What He Says Anyway, And To Whom

He said it standing up, and he said it to the two of them and not to the clerk, because the clerk had already told him the cost and the clerk would find out what it was when she was told.

“From this morning, two things. One, an answer that is about a paper the person asking brought up themselves goes by hand to that person, in a fold, and the flap is gummed shut and the date and the hour are on the outside and nothing else is, so that a man carrying it cannot read it by holding it to a window and not one person in a corridor can see whose it is.”

Not one person in that corridor had asked about that part.

“Two, an answer that is about any other person is not carried anywhere. It is read out in this room, once, in the voice this office reads everything in, and the person it is about is in the room when it is read and the person who asked is in the room when it is read, and if the person it is about will not come then there is no answer and the row stays as it is.”

He did not improve it and he did not give a reason.

“Will you both have that,” he said.

## Two People Deciding At The Wood

The woman looked at the man for about four seconds.

“That is four times what we came for.”

“It is.”

“You are telling us that for somebody to find out whether a man has been at your door, that man has to be told that somebody has been asking, and he has to agree to come up four flights to a room with a clerk at it, or he does not get told.”

“Yes.”

“And we cannot make him and we cannot ask him and we cannot write to him.”

“You can carry the request,” said Lin Hao. “You can ask him on a doorstep at half past four on a working afternoon whether he will come up on a morning, and if he says no then there is no answer, and you will have spent a morning for nothing and you will have told him that somebody asked.”

The man spoke for the first time since the stair.

“He will say no,” he said.

“Why.”

“Because I know him,” said the man, “and I have been up your stairs about four times and he has not been up them once in his life, and if a woman he lives four doors from comes to his door and says somebody at an office has been asking after him, then he spends the rest of his life not knowing who, and that is worth more to him than whatever it is you are holding.”

He did not improve that.

## The Sheet Goes Up At Four Carrying Nothing

He went down four flights and along about four hundred yards at about half past three and stood in a passage off Cordwain, nine feet back on the flags, and watched a man who is paid nothing square the thirty-seventh sheet against the edge of the rail with his thumb and go back behind the wood.

It has been face up there for seventy days and it is the same sheet it was on the thirtieth of March and it will come down on the thirtieth of June.

Six lines in a hand at the foot of it, five of them instructions and one of them a sentence a man said out loud in a room. Four sentences. Four lines in a column under two printed words with a woman’s name in the middle of one of them. A striking. Two inches of nothing.

Nothing new at the bottom of it, and nothing at the bottom of it since the thirtieth of March, and when the next one goes up on the thirtieth of June there will be nothing new at the bottom of that one either.

The sixth line of the second of December is the last line this office will ever make at the foot of one of its own pages. He said so out loud in that room on the last working day of March and asked for it to be said.

He stood there at about ten to four and let the man behind the wood read the whole of it to about eleven people at the same speed as the rest of it, and did not know what any of it was for, and was never going to be told.

## The Guild House On Cordwain, And A Price

There is a guild house on Cordwain with a strongroom at the back of it and an upstairs room over the strongroom, and a woman in that room who has kept the real books of a guild for thirty years and whose hand has been worn at the ends of her fingers by about nine hours a day of paper.

Marguerite Vale was at the desk when he came in and she had not asked him to come and she said so.

“I am going to price your rule,” she said, “and I am going to be right about it, and I would rather you had it from me in about eleven minutes than work it out over a month.”

“Price it.”

“About nine questions a month come into that room. About four of the nine are about the asker’s own paper and those cost one morning each of a person who is paid nothing.”

“The other five are about a person who is not standing there, and from this morning each of those costs two mornings. Your morning and his morning. That is five more mornings, and about five mornings a month is a quarter of a day, and a quarter of a day is not a figure anybody in this city is going to feel.”

She wrote it down in a book of her own, which is a broker’s book and has no standing in any court and may not be cited.

“The price is this. About half of those five will not come.”

“About half.”

“More than half,” said Marguerite Vale. “The ones who will come will be the ones with a morning to spend. A man with a cart cannot stop at four. A woman who cooks for four people cannot come at eleven on a working morning. They will say yes and mean it and then not be there, and the room will be empty and the clerk will have read four lines to an empty room and the row will stand.

“The ones who can afford to spend a morning standing in a room are the ones who have a morning. And they are not the ones asking.”

She looked up.

“That is the price and it is the price of a rule that is fair, and it falls hardest on the people who can least afford it, and you have known that since the second of December about every other thing this office has done.”

“Have I.”

“You came down four flights on the ninth of March to ask me about an answer a man could not hear a man read to his sister-in-law,” said Marguerite Vale, “and I priced that one too, and it came out at the same figure, and I have been right about both.”

## What It Costs In That Room, And What Is Not Started

He went back up four flights at about six in the evening and the two of them were still in the corridor and had been for about an hour and a half, which was longer than the clerk had ever had anybody wait.

Wenna Raut stood up with the pen across the book.

“I have entered the refusal,” she said, “because you have told me to do a thing and I cannot originate it and that has to be written down somewhere with a date on it. It says that on the tenth of this month the person who asked to be told at the same time as the other person has been told that the office may not ask anybody anything they have not been asked.”

“Then put it under the two of them.”

“There are two rows. The woman’s and the man’s. And there is going to be a third one, and it is not going to be in that book, because a row in that book is a mouth with a date on it and the only thing that is going to happen with the third one is that a man is going to be found and told, and nothing will be said and there will be no row.”

“So the practice is a practice again.”

“The practice is a practice,” said Wenna Raut, “and it is not ink, and it is not a line, and there is not one form anywhere in this city on which anybody could be asked to keep it, and the first person who has to keep it is a man who lives four doors from a woman who has just stood in a corridor for an hour and a half waiting to be told something about herself.”

The two of them went down the four flights together.

There was no answer on the tenth of this month and there was no answer on any morning after it, and nothing at the foot of that page was going to make one, and the thing that has to happen first is a woman standing at a door at half past four on a working afternoon saying a man’s name out loud.

*Three thousand three hundred and sixty-nine days. Less three thousand three hundred and sixty-two, the third of this month, is seven, and two people who had been refused separately came up one stair together and asked to be in a room at the same time.*

*And an answer about a person was decided to be read aloud with that person in the room or not given at all, and it was spoken and not entered, and a woman in a guild house priced it and was right about the price.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seventy, and the thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy of them, and it comes down on the thirtieth of June.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and forty-nine days, which is thirty-one months and nineteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

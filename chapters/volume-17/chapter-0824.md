# A Sheet After The Quarter

Three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine was the thirtieth of this month and the sixth is day three thousand three hundred and ninety-five, and three thousand three hundred and ninety-five less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine is six, and six out of a month that begins on the first is six by the month count.

## Six Days On A Rail And A Book Under An Arm

The thirty-eighth sheet went up on the thirtieth of June at four o’clock with nothing new at the bottom of it, and on the sixth of July it has been face up in that passage for six days and there has been nothing new at the bottom of it for six days and there was nothing new at the bottom of the one before it.

A man who is paid nothing reads the whole of it out to whoever stands there four times a year, at the same speed, and does not know what any of it is for, and has never once asked, and this office has never once told him.

The room off the third court was shut for about nine minutes on the morning of the sixth of July, which it has not been on an ordinary morning since the second of December, and the clerk came out of it with the bound Register shut and tucked under her arm and put it down on the sill of the corridor window.

That corridor is about four feet wide and about nine paces long. There were about four people in it and there were four at various points of the morning, and she stood with her back to the sill and did not go back in.

## What A Clerk Does With A Morning That Has Nothing In It

“For six days I have had nothing to enter,” said Wenna Raut. “That is a thing that has happened in nine years for about four mornings and two of them are this quarter.”

“What do you do.”

“I read it back.”

She put two fingers on the cover of the book.

“All of it, out loud, to myself, in this corridor, in the voice I read everything in. There is not one person in this room whose job it is to know what this office has already said, because there has never been a reason for one, and the reason is that until the thirtieth of March every answer went to a rail and the rail is the record that nothing keeps.”

“And you found something.”

“I found that I can do it wrong.”

## The Row She Read Back Was Not The Row She Entered

“On the fourth of this month I read back a row I entered on the ninth of May,” said Wenna Raut. “Out loud, in this corridor, at about eleven in the morning, from memory, about a paper a woman brought up herself in the spring.”

She stopped there and looked at the door and not at him.

“It is not the row I entered. It is a different sentence and it is about the same woman and the same paper and the same day, and it is four lines where the row I entered is six, and I have been that clerk for nine years.”

“Which of the two is true.”

“The one in the book. The one I said out loud is a thing I believed I had written, and I had not, and it is shorter, and there is nothing wrong in it that anybody could name.” She squared the book on the sill. “And about four people were standing in this corridor at eleven o’clock on the fourth of this month and I have not got one thing in this city that will tell me which of them heard it.”

He stood against the wall and understood that the only record of what that office had said on the ninth of May was the head of a woman standing in a corridor, and had just been found not to hold.

## What He Decided, In A Corridor, With The Door Shut

“No more of it.”

“No more of it,” she agreed, “and I am not going to pretend that is free. From this morning there will be days when I do not know what this office said. That has never happened once in nine years and it is going to happen about four times a month now, and the reason is that there is no second copy of anything in that room and there cannot be one.”

“There could be.”

“There cannot be one and you know why better than I do, because you were in my doorway in June and read two days off the top of nine sheets,” said Wenna Raut. “A copy is on somebody’s side of a wall. That is what a copy is. You spent the whole of June finding that out and so have I and neither of us is going to build the second copy.”

He said it out loud where she could hear it and she wrote it, which she had not been asked to do, and read it back once.

“From the sixth of this month a clerk in this office does not read a row back from memory in a corridor, or in that room, or anywhere, and says so out loud once and enters it as a practice with a date and an hour on it, and it is not in the office’s hand and nothing goes under the foot of any of our pages.”

“You are going to be worse at your job.”

“I am going to be correct at my job for about nine in ten of what is said in it and I am going to be unable to repeat any of it,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have had a year of being right about paper and about four days of being right about hands, and neither of those was worth what this is worth.”

## What She Does Instead, And What It Found

She got the cover back and opened the book on the sill in the corridor with about four people in it, which she has never once done, and turned back through the quarter with a thumb.

“I read it forward,” she said. “Not backward. Backward is rehearsal and rehearsal is how a clerk comes to believe she knows a thing. Forward is the other thing. You take the newest row and you ask one question about it, and the question is whether the person it is about has been told.”

“And if they have not.”

“Then there is no answer under that row and there has been no answer under it for however long, and that is a fact about this office and not an opinion about anybody, and it can be entered, because it is not a request and it is not a rule and it is not anybody’s name.”

She found three in about ninety seconds, and she did not read any of them out loud.

“Three rows in this quarter where the same person has asked the same question twice and the two answers are both true and they are not the same answer,” said Wenna Raut, “and in all three of them the second time is the later day and the later day is the truer one, and in all three of them the person it is about has not been told about either.”

## What The Three Are, Said On A Sill

“I am going to tell you what a person looks like when they have asked the same thing twice,” said Wenna Raut, “because you are going to go up four flights and work out what to do about it and you need to know what is in front of you.”

“It looks like two rows. That is the whole of it. Two rows with the same shape of sentence and two different answers under them, and the two rows are days apart, and both of them were true on the day they were given.”

“Then why do they differ.”

“Because the office was asked on the first day and did not have it, and had it on the second day, and wrote down on each occasion what it could honestly say at that hour with what it had been given by that hour.”

She put a finger on the sill.

“That is not a fault. That is an office being honest twice. It is only a fault if you wanted the second answer to have arrived on the first day, and nothing in this city can put an answer back into a morning it was not in.”

## What He Wanted To Do About Three Rows And Could Not

“There are three answers in this city sitting under three questions about three people, and the second one in each pair is the truer one, and I have not said the name of this office to a living person and I am not going to.”

“I know.”

“Say the rest of it in the corridor where I can hear it.”

“I want to put a fourth line under each of them saying that the second answer is later and truer, and that the person should have been told on the first day.”

He did not improve it.

“And I am not going to, because a fourth line under a row is a thing this office says about a person to somebody who has not asked for it, and the rule we spoke on the tenth of June is that nothing in this city lets me ask anybody anything they have not been asked.”

“So they stand.”

“They stand, and I have just spent six days learning that standing is a word that means two different things, and one of them is nothing happening and the other is nothing being written down about nothing happening, and this morning I started writing it down, and I would like you to tell me what that is worth.”

“I would like about four years to find out,” he said.

“You have not got four years,” said Wenna Raut. “You have a book and a corridor and about four people in it and it is a quarter past eleven.”

## Two Rows That Stood Empty Since April

“There are two more.”

“Two that have stood with nothing under them since the fourteenth of April and since the third of May.”

“Yes, and I have been reading them forward every morning for six days and being told every morning that nothing has happened under them, and that is a refusal and a refusal is an event and I have been sitting here letting a book go on saying that nothing has happened when what has happened is that two people did not come.”

She wrote at the sill, with the door shut behind her and about four people in the corridor, and it took about four minutes.

Under the question of the fourteenth she entered that no answer has been given and that the person it is about has not come to that room.

Under the question of the third of May she entered the same words.

Both in a clerk’s hand, with the sixth of this month at the head of them and the hour against the date, in the words she was given the first time and in the second the words she chose and said out loud.

“That is the first time in nine years that anything has been entered under those two rows.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “It is the first time in nine years that anything has been entered about them, and there is a difference and I want you to carry it. Nothing has been answered. Two people did not come. That is all that has happened and it has taken me six days of nothing to find it.”

He was standing in a corridor about four feet wide with the door shut and the pen going on the other side of it, and he had asked her for a rule at about eleven o’clock and been given the first honest accounting of what his office had failed to record in six days.

*Three thousand three hundred and ninety-five days. Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of this month, is six, and the thirty-eighth sheet went up on that day carrying nothing new at the bottom of it, and a clerk read a book out loud in a corridor and got a row wrong from memory, and entered, under two rows that had stood empty since April, that two people did not come.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is six, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and seventy-five days, which is thirty-two months and fifteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

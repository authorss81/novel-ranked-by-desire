# Eleven At The Rail Again

Three thousand two hundred and eighty-six was the seventeenth of this month and the twenty-fourth is day three thousand two hundred and ninety-three, and three thousand two hundred and ninety-three less three thousand two hundred and eighty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## Squared Twice, Six Days Early

She took a sheet out of the flat file at about half past two on the twenty-fourth and put it on the table under the window.

There was no reason on that day for a sheet to come out of that flat file. The thirty-seventh goes up on the thirtieth and it is six days off and it is one of four times a year and there has never been a reason in nine years to take one out early.

Wenna Raut squared it against the wood in about three seconds.

Then she squared it again about four minutes later.

She has done that at every quarter-end since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and neither of them has ever said a word about it, and Lin Hao stood at that table on the twenty-fourth of this month and watched her do it six days early and did not open his mouth.

He put it back in the flat file himself at about ten past three, because that is his job at a quarter-end and not hers.

He had wanted to say something about the four minutes between the two squarings and had not, and would not, and would go on not saying it for as long as she kept doing it.

## Nine Feet Back At The Mouth Of A Passage

He came down at about ten past four, which is not the hour, and the cold in that passage in the third month of the year is a dry hard cold with no rain in it and the shutter down over the window to about a third.

There is a place at the mouth of that passage, on the flags outside, where you can stand nine feet back and see the whole of the rail and the desk behind it and the window, and be in the open air while you do it.

He had never used it. He had been into that passage four times in nine years and had stood at the foot of the stair every one of them, which is inside, which is dark, and which means about nine people watching a man who is watching a man.

He stood on the flags for about fifty minutes that afternoon.

## The Slips That Came With The Quarter

There were eleven at the rail at four minutes past four, six days before the last working day of a quarter, and that had not happened in nine years.

Six of the eleven were there because the thirty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for eighty-four days and has been face up on it every day of that time.

The other five were there because of something new, and the new thing had been running since the second of December and he had not once walked down that passage to look at it.

It was slips.

Somebody had been sending sheets of office paper down to that counter. Cut about the size of a hand, in a clerk's hand, the question on one of them and the answer on the other, and no heading on either side and nothing at the foot of either, so that a slip read out at a rail gave about nine people the whole of a thing that had been written in a room with the door shut for one person.

The counterman had been reading them out in the ordinary voice he reads everything in, one after another, between the four sentences and the four lines of the column, in the order they arrived. He had been doing it since the second of December.

About nine people a week had stood there and heard other people's answers given to them, and not one of them had asked to be there, and about four of them had written nothing down at all, because a person handed a sentence about somebody else does not walk up a lane with a pencil.

Five of the eleven on the twenty-fourth had come for the slips and not for the sheet. He worked that out in about four minutes from where he was standing on the flags, by watching where their eyes went when the counterman came to the foot of the page, and it is the sort of thing only a person who has stood and watched about nine people a week for nine years can work out.

He had never been told. He had never asked. Four times in nine years.

## What He Will Do When He Is Asked

He went in at about half past four and put two fingers on the deal of the counter, which is the only thing in that room he has ever touched, and asked the man what he was going to do the first time somebody stood at that rail and asked him what a question means.

The counterman put his thumb along the edge of the wood and left it there for about four seconds.

“What did you ask me last time you came in here,” he said.

“Whether a line under a sentence is a sentence.”

“No,” said the counterman. “That was a question I could partly answer. I have never been asked what anything means, in nine years, by anybody at that rail or in that passage, and there is not one person in Ostara entitled to tell me.”

“Then somebody is going to ask you.”

“Somebody has asked me already,” said the counterman. “On the eleventh of this month a woman asked me what the question on the slip was asking, because she could not tell whether it was hers or her husband's, and I said that was what it said, and she said that was no answer, and it was the only true one I have got.”

## What He Reads And What He Is Not Allowed To Know

He read the five that had come on slips that quarter and he read them in the order they had arrived, and he read the questions as well as the answers, and he did not know what any of the nine was for and was not going to be told.

One of the five was about a woman who brought a paper in the autumn and what the office holds about her. One of the five was a question, put by a man in a cap on the second of this month, about a paper somebody else had brought.

For that one he had to say the four words he says when a thing cannot be read out, in a passage with about nine people in it, and he had said it three times that quarter to three different people, and not one of them had asked him why, and not one of them had asked him what he would read instead.

That was new. That was not on the sheet and it was not on a slip, and it was happening in a room, out loud, at a counter, to his face.

Six lines in the office hand, and the fourth of them about any person, and he gave that one the same weight as the other five.

Then an old woman at the end of the rail asked him to read the line about any person once more, because her husband was hard of hearing on the side the rail was on and she wanted him to hear it from her and not from the counter.

He read it again in the same voice at the same speed, and about four of the eleven went on arguing about the slips while he did it, and then he squared the sheet against the edge of the rail with his thumb, which he does every time.

“Twenty-eight days since the eighteenth of December,” he said. “I have read four lines and I could not tell you one thing about any of them, and in twenty-eight days I have read eleven answers and eleven questions to about nine people a week who did not ask for any of it, and there is not one thing in this city that will let a man behind this counter say a word of it that is not written on the paper he is holding.”

“That is what I came to tell you,” said Lin Hao.

“Then say it to the room, not to me,” said the counterman. “You have been down here four times in nine years and this is the first time you have said anything to me that I did not have to guess at.”

## Ten Minutes To Five, And A Clerk Up The Stair

At about ten minutes to five a girl came up the stair out of the back of that counter with her hat in her hands and did not know him and had never seen him before in her life.

“Has anybody spoken to you about a person at the window,” she said.

He said no.

“A man has come in and asked about a question,” said the girl. “Not his own. He has asked what another person put to this office and when, and he says he is the brother. And the man at the window has told him he cannot read it to him and he can go and ask at the office it came from, and the man says he has been there and stood in a corridor for two hours and there is a queue.”

She stood there with the hat going from one hand to the other.

“He wants to know whether he is allowed to ask about a question,” she said. “That is the whole of it. He has not asked it yet. He wants somebody to tell him whether he may.”

Nobody at that rail could tell him, because it was not on anything. Six lines in a hand, four sentences, four lines in a column under two printed words, a striking, and two inches of nothing at the bottom.

Lin Hao went out into the flags at nine feet back from the rail and stood in the dry cold with his hands in his pockets, and thought that he had been wrong about what an answer was for, and that it had taken a man with a hat in his hands to show him.

*Three thousand two hundred and ninety-three days. Less three thousand two hundred and eighty-six, the seventeenth of this month, is seven, and a sheet came out of the flat file six days early and was squared and squared again, and eleven people stood at that rail at four minutes past four, and five of them were there for answers that had gone down on slips in a clerk’s hand all quarter.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December of this year, is eighty-four, and the thirty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is eight hundred and seventy-three days, which is twenty-nine months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

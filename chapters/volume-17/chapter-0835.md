# The Drawer Stays Shut

Three thousand four hundred and sixty-one was the twelfth of this month and the eighteenth is day three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven, and three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven less three thousand four hundred and sixty-one is six, and eighteen less twelve is six by the month count.

## A Woman With A Sheet She Cannot Carry

She came up the four flights at about eleven on the morning of the eighteenth of this month with a sheet of her own paper in her hand and nothing else, which meant it was about her own business and not about anybody in her family.

She wanted to know whether this office would keep it.

That is the whole of what she came up for. She is nine days at a market end on the far side of the water and she cannot take the sheet with her because it is the only thing she has written in her life and she will not have it in a room with nine people in it, and she will not leave it with a neighbour because the neighbour is the last person who read it out to her husband.

“I could come back for it.”

“You could come back for it,” said Wenna Raut, “and that is not the same thing, and I would rather be honest about that before I say anything else.”

## What She Said She Had Already Done

“I have asked two offices.”

“What did they tell you.”

“One said it was not theirs, and one said it was theirs and could not take it, and I could not tell from the two of them which one was lying to me.”

“The second one was right.”

“I have been turning that over for nine days and I have got as far as this.” She put the sheet against her knee. “If I leave it anywhere of my own it goes in a drawer at home and my husband goes through her drawers. If I leave it with the neighbour then he reads it out in a kitchen.”

“So it goes to an office. You have been to two and here is the third, and we are going to tell you no, and I would rather we told you now than at eleven o'clock at night on the ninth day.”

There were about four people in that corridor and every one of them heard the whole of it, which is the arrangement of that corridor and has been since the second of December.

## Three Things Standing Between That Sheet And This Room

“There are three things between that sheet and this room,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am going to name all three and then you are going to go down those stairs with the answer.”

“Go on.”

“The first is that this office keeps a Register of questions and answers with a date and an hour against every line of it. It cannot hold a question with nothing under it on behalf of somebody who is not here, because the second of December promised answers and did not promise storage.”

“The second is that the moment this office takes a piece of paper made by a member of the public and holds it, it has begun keeping copies. The first one was taken on the twenty-fourth of June from a man who was told it was worth nothing. I said no, and I was right, and in about four years there will be a drawer in this city that is not this drawer.”

“Where is the drawer.”

Wenna Raut did not look at it.

“The third thing is that you have not asked us about anything, so I cannot ask you anything. That means I cannot find out whether you want that sheet back in nine days or in ninety, and I may not enter a question and leave it standing for you.”

“So it is all three.”

“It is all three and every one of them is true and not one of them is a judgement about you.”

## Then She Asked The Question That Was Actually Underneath It

“Then what is it for,” said the woman.

That is not a question about a paper and it is not a question about a person, and there is nothing in this office that a clerk may answer that is not either of those two things, and both of them were standing on the wood in front of her.

Wenna Raut did not answer it.

Lin Hao could have, and was the only person in that corridor who could have, because he is the man the six lines at the foot of that quarterly sheet are the hand of, and because the answer to that question is written out four times a year to about two thousand counters and read aloud about nine times a week by a man who is paid nothing and does not know what it is for.

He did not answer it either, and she looked at him for about four seconds and then at the clerk, and something went out of her face that had been holding the morning up.

“No,” she said. “That is fair. You have told me three true things and then you have both of you gone quiet, and I have been in this corridor about eleven minutes.”

“You have been in this corridor about eleven minutes.”

“And I am going to say this once and then I am going down those stairs and I am not going to come back up them about this, and you are not to think I am being brave. I am being quick, because there are four people in here and one of them has been listening since I came up.”

Not one person in that corridor had been listening, which she had no way of knowing, and that is why he did not improve it.

## The Drawer In The Wall On The Left Of That Table

The drawer is in the wall on the left of that table. It has been shut since the twenty-first of October of the seventh year.

The instruction that shut it is in a clerk's hand and it carries his own name at the foot of it, and no hand in this city may open it, and that includes his, and the man who wrote the instruction is standing at the end of that table with his hands at his sides.

“What is in it,” said the woman.

“That is the second thing I have just told you and you asked it the wrong way round.” Wenna Raut turned round on the sill and did not touch the drawer. “The answer the right way round is that what is in it is a date.”

“Then what is the date.”

“The twenty-first of October of the seventh year, and his name at the foot of it, and nothing else, and that has been the entire content of that instruction since the morning it was written and it is going to be the entire content of it for ever.”

## How Long, And The Date Given Twice

“How long has it been shut.”

“Twenty-first of October of the seventh year.”

“That is a date. I asked how long.”

“That is a date and it is what we have got,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am not going to turn it into a count for you, and here is the reason and I have had it ready since the twenty-fourth of June.”

She put two fingers on the edge of the book.

“A length of time is a thing a person can be told has grown since the last time somebody asked. A date is a thing that either happened on a day or did not, and not one person in this city can be got to say afterwards that it happened on another one.”

The woman looked past her at him.

“Ask him. He wrote it.”

He shook his head without saying anything at all.

“You have just refused to say it to a person who asked you directly,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have watched you refuse to improve a thing all year without once being asked to, and in about four years I am going to be asked whether that was careful or afraid.”

“It was neither,” he said. “A thing you say out loud and do not write down is a practice. A thing you write down is a rule. And one of those two is in a book in a building with no door onto a street, and the other one is in about four heads, and I have never worked out which of them lasts longer.”

## The Sheet On The Table, And Why He Did Not Hand It Over

She went down at about ten past eleven and he stood at the end of that table afterwards with the door open and about four people in the corridor.

There is a bundle on that table with twenty-six things on it and the twenty-sixth is at the near end on the drawer side, and it is a sheet of office paper about the size of any of the other twenty-five and it has nothing written on it at all.

It was four inches from his right hand.

He thought about handing it to her. Not to keep her sheet for her. To give her something to write her own question on, so that a row in that book has a mouth in it that came from her and not from a clerk guessing at her.

It took about nine seconds and he did not do it.

Because the day this office hands a sheet of paper to a person, it is an office that gives paper.

And about nine people a week come up those stairs. And in about four months there would be a stack behind that door of sheets this office had handed out, and not one column in this city could hold which person went away holding one.

He did not improve that either, and he did not say it out loud, and there is a reason he did not say it out loud and the reason is the twelfth of this month.

## What She Took Down The Stairs

She wrote the date on the back of her own hand with a pencil out of her own pocket, standing in that corridor, in about nine seconds, and then went down the four flights with her own sheet still in her hand.

“I will come back on the ninth day and put it in my own drawer and shut it and take the key out and put the key in the fire,” she said from the stair. “And I want to say that you have cost me about nine days of not knowing, and I am not going to tell anybody you did, because the other two offices cost me nine days and told me nothing at all.”

Then he stood in that room with the door open and did not go near the drawer, and nothing in that room wanted him to.

*Three thousand four hundred and sixty-seven days. Less three thousand four hundred and sixty-one, the twelfth of this month, is six, and a woman came up four flights with a sheet of her own paper and asked that it be kept for nine days, and was told no three times, and wrote a date on the back of her own hand instead.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is seventy-eight, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and forty-seven days, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it, and not one person in that room said the length of it out loud all morning.*

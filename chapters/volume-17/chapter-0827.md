# A Page And A Stone Unmoved

Three thousand four hundred and eight was the nineteenth of this month and the twenty-sixth is day three thousand four hundred and fifteen, and three thousand four hundred and fifteen less three thousand four hundred and eight is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## One Question A Day, And Not Two

The counters of the magistracy run along the ground floor of a court building and the clerk at the third one of them is entitled to one question a day and not two, which is a thing she will tell you before you have said anything at all.

She told him at about half past ten on the morning of the twenty-sixth of this month, before he got to the end of the wood.

“I am going to have one out of you,” she said, “and I am going to have it now, and then I am going to be a woman at a counter for the rest of the day who has already had one, and if you have a second thing I am going to tell you the same sentence again and it will not be any better the second time.”

“One is enough.”

“It is never enough. Go on.”

## What He Asked

He asked whether a court in this city can be asked, given an address and a day and nothing else and no name at all, to say whether a thing happened at that address on that day.

She wrote nothing down, which he noticed, and then she went and looked at a printed list on the wall for about a minute and a half and came back.

“The address has no stone on it,” she said.

“What does that mean.”

“It means there is nothing at that address that is still going to be there. Not a kerb, not a threshold, not a plinth, not a step, not a corner of dressed stone, and not a wall that anybody would agree is the same wall.”

She put her finger on the printed list.

“It is a set of words. It is what people say when they are telling somebody where a door is, and it is perfectly good for a person standing in front of the door.”

“And if the door is gone.”

“If the door is gone then the words are still perfectly good words and they will get you to about where the door was, and there will be nothing there to be shown, and there is no process in this building that goes out and looks.”

## What A Stone Is In A Street

He had not asked the second question and she had not made him wait for it.

“A stone is the only thing in a street that does not move,” said the clerk. “Everything else in a street is a thing somebody did. The doors are fitted. The numbers are nailed on. Even a person moves.”

“A stone is a stone, and it is either in a wall or it is in the ground, and the only thing that ever happens to one is that somebody wants it somewhere else and has to pay a man to take it out.”

She looked up.

“So if you have an address and a stone, you have a place that will still be a place in forty years, and if you have an address and no stone then you have a rumour with a post mark on it.”

“And that is what makes the address useless here.”

“The address is not useless. The address is exactly as good as it was. I am telling you that there is a difference in kind between an address with a stone on it and an address without, and that in forty years one of those will be provable and the other one will still be a thing two people remember differently.”

## What A Stone Would Cost, And Who Would Have To Pay For It

“There is a way to do it,” he said. “Not this month and not by anybody in this building. Put a stone in the ground at that address. A dressed one, with anything on it or nothing on it. Then the place is a place for as long as the stone is there.”

“Yes.”

“And a stone means a wall, or a kerb, or a step at a door, and every one of those means somebody who owns a piece of ground has agreed to it, and has had it cut, and has paid a man to set it.”

“Yes,” said the clerk, “and it takes about a year and it is the cheapest thing in this city that cannot be got wrong.”

He had been up the four flights that morning and down them again and he knew exactly what he was, which is a white plate in a coat with a name on it and no figure cut into it anywhere, paid a subsistence that comes on a rota, assigned to washrooms and a bin room at night four nights and three off, in a branch office off a third court.

There was not one thing in this city he could have paid for with, and there was not one thing he was allowed to ask anybody to pay it for him, and nothing anywhere would let a stone go up at an address belonging to a man who is not in a building and does not know that anybody is asking about it.

So there was a fact about a doorway that could be made permanent, and the price of making it permanent was a piece of money and a conversation with a stranger, and he had neither, and there was nothing in the instruction of the second of December that had ever promised him either of them.

“You have gone quiet,” said the clerk. “They all go quiet. I will tell you what the quiet is: you have worked out that this counter would have solved it in about eleven minutes and it did, and that the next eleven inches of it are not in this building and were never going to be.”

“That is the quiet.”

“Then go and do the other half of it, and I have had my one and you have had yours, and the rest of today belongs to the people who have not been here.”

## The Thing She Would Not Let A Day Mean

He had come down with a second thing. It was in a drawer under a bench in a division building four hundred yards from that counter and he had not brought it and was not going to, and a day is written on it, and he has never had it in his hand and never once had it read out to him by anybody who had it in hers.

He did not ask.

She waited about four seconds, which was generous, and then she said the thing herself and said it fast.

“If you are here about a day on a piece of paper, then a day on a piece of paper is the day the thing was made. That is all it is and not one paper in this building will let a day mean anything else, and if you ask me what a day meant to whoever wrote it, or what it was supposed to be, then I will tell you the same sentence again and it will not be any better the second time.”

“I was not going to ask you that.”

“You were standing there getting ready to, and I have watched about nine people do it this month, and you were the third one to say afterwards that you were not going to.”

She squared the printed list to the wall.

“I have been at this counter for six years and in six years I have had one day come off a paper that somebody wanted to be a different day. I gave it the day it was made, and the man took it and went away. That is the whole of my career and I would do it again in about four seconds.”

## A Page And A Stone Unmoved

He went down to the foot of the hill at about half past four in the afternoon because that is where he goes.

There are four inches of dressed stone set into the ground at the foot of that wall and they have not moved since the first year, and he stood nine feet back from them and did not go nearer, because there is nothing in this city that could be got out of a stone by a man standing on it.

About nine people a week get down low enough to read the four inches. About forty words in five hands are in there and two words on a stone at the foot of that wall are not among them and are not going to be.

The month's form was up on the board against the kerb since the seventh, with both of the two printed boxes at the foot of it empty, and under the lower batten a piece of office paper was still lying face down against the wood where it had been since the sixteenth of December, with a pencil line drawn across the bottom of it in a hand of her own, and it had not been moved and it had not been turned over.

He stood there for about four minutes in the wet and did not go down and did not touch anything.

And about four hundred yards away, in a bound Register with a ruler squared along the edge of it, there is a row of his own with the twenty-third of August of the ninth year written at the head of it in a clerk’s hand.

There is a space in the middle of that row. It has been there since a woman entered her own out of her own mouth in the sixth month of the ninth year, and not one person has ever gone near it, and he did not ask about it and did not mention it to anybody that afternoon.

Two things a page and a stone, and not one of them had moved, and not one of them was going to tell him anything.

*Three thousand four hundred and fifteen days. Less three thousand four hundred and eight, the nineteenth of this month, is seven, and a clerk at the third counter of a magistracy had one question a day and not two, and gave the whole of hers to a man who asked what an address is when there is no stone on it, and would not let a day mean anything but the day the thing was made.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is twenty-six, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and ninety-five days, which is thirty-three months and five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

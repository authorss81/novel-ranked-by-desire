# The Neighbour Is Told Together

Three thousand three hundred and ninety-five was the sixth of this month and the thirteenth is day three thousand four hundred and two, and three thousand four hundred and two less three thousand three hundred and ninety-five is seven, and thirteen less six is seven by the month count.

## One Hundred And Twenty-Two Days

The ninth of March was day three thousand two hundred and eighty. The thirteenth of this month is day three thousand four hundred and two, and three thousand four hundred and two less three thousand two hundred and eighty is one hundred and twenty-two.

It is the only row in that book that asks for something rather than putting a question. It was entered in a stranger’s words and the words were these: whether this office holds anything that my sister-in-law said out loud in that corridor in the autumn, and I want to be in the room at the same time as her when you say it.

It had stood with nothing under it for one hundred and twenty-two days.

It is not one of the two rows a clerk entered about on the sixth of this month, and she says so before anybody asks, because a row that asks for something and a row that is waiting for an answer are two different shapes and the book does not print which is which anywhere.

## They Arranged It Between Themselves

He had asked her on a doorstep at half past four on a working afternoon, with words he had worked out himself in about nine minutes on a stair, and she had said she would come up four flights at eleven on the thirteenth of this month and on no other morning, and that he was to be there, and that she was not going to be told what was said before she was in the room.

Neither of them had said one word about that office to any of it.

He was in the corridor at about ten to eleven. She came up the stairs on her own at eleven and about four people were in that corridor and none of them had been told anything by anybody.

## Four People In A Room And The Order Of It

Wenna Raut put the book square on the table, which is the only thing she does to it that is not writing.

“Three things and then I read it,” she said. “The rule is that it is read out in this room once, in the voice I read everything in, and that both of you are in the room when it is read. I am the clerk and I may only enter what I am given. And if either of you asks me to stop, I stop, and the row stands as it is.”

Not one person asked her to stop.

She read the date at the head of the row out first and then the row, and the corridor heard it through the open door, which it always does.

It was four lines long.

## What Four Lines Came To

The first line said that in the third month of the year before a woman came up the four flights on four mornings in about eleven days and asked four questions.

The second said that what she asked on the fourth morning was entered in her own words and was about a man she did not name, who was not in the building, and who had brought a paper through that front door in the autumn.

The third said that this office held nothing else about that woman at that date.

The fourth said that the answer to what she had asked was that she was owed nothing by this office, because this office had not been given anything about her by anybody but herself.

That room said nothing for about nine seconds.

“You did not say her name,” said the man.

“No.”

“You have never once said her name in that book.”

“Not in that row and not in any other,” said Wenna Raut, “and if I had said it I would have had to tell you how many other rows it is in, and that number is the thing this office has spent nine years refusing to be able to say out loud.”

## What She Wrote Under It

Under the row, at the date and the hour, in a clerk’s hand, Wenna Raut entered that the answer had been read out in that room at eleven on the thirteenth of this month in the voice this office reads everything in, and that the man who asked and the woman the answer was about were both in the room when it was read.

She read it back once and then she squared the book to the edge of the table and did not move her hands.

“That row is full now,” she said, “and it had nothing under it for one hundred and twenty-two days, and from this morning it is the only row in that book that says a woman said a thing about a man and that both of them were in the room when he was told.”

“Is that good.”

“It is the first time anybody has ever been told in here, and it cost her the last thing she wanted, and it was correct,” said Wenna Raut. “And the next person who comes up these stairs and asks what this office holds about her gets the same four lines. She will not be in the room this time. That is what has been bought here and I would like somebody to write a poem about it and I would like the poem to be true.”

The man asked, after a while, what he was supposed to do with it, and the woman told him, in four words, to go home and say it himself, and he did not ask her which of the four she meant.

## The Thing Neither Of Them Had Come For

The man had wanted it read with her in the room so that he would not be told afterwards in a street. That had been arranged and it was done and he got what he came for.

He did not know until afterwards that he had come for something else as well, and that was his own business and he worked it out about four days later on a night shift.

She had wanted to know what was in there. That was all she had ever wanted. She had said no to four people about it in four months and she had come up four flights on a working morning and she had got it.

And what she had got was that a sentence she said at a counter in the autumn about her husband’s brother had gone into a book and had been read back to her in a clerk’s voice with her husband’s brother nine feet away.

## What She Said About It

“You read it out.”

“I read it out.”

“You read out a thing I said about him, in a room, with him standing there.”

“Yes,” said Wenna Raut, “and that is what the rule says and I told both of you what the rule was before I read it and neither of you asked me to stop.”

He put his own two hands together behind his back and said nothing at all, and the silence went on long enough that about four people in that corridor understood that there had been a sentence in a book.

Then the woman said the thing that was going to matter, and she said it to the clerk and not to the man.

“I need those four lines.”

“There is nothing I can do with that.”

“You wrote them. You read them out. You have them in that book in your own hand.”

“The book has them. I have not got them,” said Wenna Raut. “Anything about a person is not carried out of this room. That is the rule from the tenth of June and it is the reason you are able to be standing here instead of being told what your husband’s brother has been saying about you for four months in a doorway.”

## About Four People In A Corridor And What They Took

The room’s door was open the whole of it. About four people were in that corridor at eleven o’clock on the thirteenth of this month and they heard a date and a row and four lines read out in a flat voice by a woman who reads everything that way, and they did not know what any of it was about and two of them worked out from the shape of it that there was a person in it.

Nothing had been refused. The door was open because the door is open, and the practice from the thirtieth of March had nothing to do with it, and the rule from the tenth of June had been kept perfectly and completely and about nine words had come out of that door into about four people in about four feet of air.

That is what the first joint reading cost, and it is not the reading. It is that a thing that had been safe for one hundred and twenty-two days because it had never been said aloud was said aloud once, correctly, by a clerk, in a room with the door open.

## The Fourth Hand In A Corridor

The woman took a square of pencil paper out of her own coat and a pencil, and she went out into the corridor about four feet wide, and she lowered herself onto the sill of the window with the paper on her knee and wrote the four lines out in her own hand in about four seconds.

She did it with her coat still on. She did not ask anybody to leave the corridor and the corridor stayed as it was.

Wenna Raut stood in the doorway and watched a woman she had entered eleven words of write those eleven words out again by ear, and she did not ask her to stop, and she did not tell her she was doing something wrong, and she did not go back into the room and put it anywhere.

That piece of paper is hers and she made it out of a room she was not supposed to have been told anything in, and this city is never going to be told that it exists.

“I have never thanked you,” the woman said, and went down the stairs.

*Three thousand four hundred and two days. Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of this month, is thirteen, and less three thousand two hundred and eighty, the ninth of March, is one hundred and twenty-two, and an answer was read out once in a room with four people in it and improved nothing.*

*And the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and eighty-two days, which is thirty-two months and twenty-two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

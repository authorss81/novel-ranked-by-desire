# The Counterman Reads Twice

Three thousand four hundred and forty-two was the twenty-third of this month and the twenty-nine is day three thousand four hundred and forty-eight, and three thousand four hundred and forty-eight less three thousand four hundred and forty-two is six, and twenty-nine less twenty-three is six by the month count.

## The One Thing Fewer

There are six lines in this office's hand at the foot of that sheet, five of them instructions and one of them a sentence a man said out loud in a room on the second of December of the year before.

Since the thirtieth of March the man behind the counter has read five of the six.

He said so at the wood on the afternoon that it happened and he has not said one word about it since, and the fifth one is the one about any person, and it goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters and it is face up on that rail every day of the ninety between them.

He is paid nothing. He has run that desk for nine years. He reads whatever is in front of him at the same speed, and about nine people a week stand in a passage about four feet wide and hear what he is reading to each other.

## Eleven At A Rail In The Middle Of A Quarter

There were eleven of them at the rail on the morning of the twenty-ninth of this month, which is the middle of a quarter and not the end of one, and the number at the end of a quarter is a different number and has never been the same number in nine years.

The thirty-eighth has been face up on that rail since the thirtieth of June and had been carrying nothing new at the bottom of it since the day it went up.

At about eleven the counterman came to the foot of the page and did what he does.

Four sentences. Four lines under two printed words. Six lines, and five of the six, because the sixth has not been read out since the thirtieth of March.

And then he stopped at the striking and went on to the two inches of nothing at the bottom of it, which is what he has done every time since the thirtieth of March.

## The Four Who Wanted To Know What Any Person Meant

About four of the eleven did not go.

That is the whole of what happened at that rail on the morning of the twenty-ninth of this month and it took about nine minutes.

A man of about fifty asked it first and asked it decently, standing back from the wood with his hat in his hands, and then a woman behind him asked the same thing in the same words because she had heard it asked and had understood it, and then two more, one of whom had come in that morning for the sheet and had not expected to be given a question at all.

They wanted to know what any person meant in that line, because the line says that an answer about any other person is not carried anywhere.

“That is what it says,” said the counterman.

“It is what it says,” the man agreed. “What does any person mean.”

“It means any person.”

“It cannot mean any person,” said the man. “Because I am a person and my wife is a person and I put a thing to that office in June about a man at the end of my street, and if it means any person then that is me, and I have not been told, and I have not been told because I did not know there was anything to be told.”

“That is what it says.”

The woman put her hand on the rail.

“My husband asked me to ask it and he would not come himself.”

“You should both be in the room,” said the counterman, and then stopped, because he had said it as though there were a rule behind it, and he has never once been told there is one.

“That is not a rule,” he said. “Ignore that. What is on the paper is that an answer about any other person is not carried to anybody, and that is what it says, and I have read it nine hundred times and I do not know what any person means either.”

## Forty Minutes Inside The Passage

Lin Hao stood inside that passage for about forty minutes after that and did not go out to the flags, which is the only place anybody stands to look at that rail from the outside.

He read the foot of the page in his own head, which he can do, and then he read the foot of the page as the counterman reads it, at the speed the counterman reads it, which he had not done for about four years.

The sentence at the foot of that sheet is a compound one. It is true and it is not simple, and it carries in it two different things standing next to each other with a full stop in the middle of them, and a man who reads it aloud to whoever is standing there at the same speed cannot tell anybody which half he is standing in when he stops.

He had known that in March of the ninth year. He had never once done anything about it, because there was nothing in the world he could do about it and because the man behind the counter had said then, in the middle of a different quarter, that he would go on reading whatever was in front of him and would not know what it was for.

So there was a sentence in this city, spoken about nine times a week in a passage four feet wide, and about four of the people hearing it had understood it and about five had not, and the ones who had not were going to ask.

## At Four In The Afternoon

It went on all day and it filled up again at about four, the way that passage fills.

There were nine of them at the rail at four minutes past four, and two of the nine were the man with the hat and the woman with her hand on the wood, who had both come back, and one of them had brought a brother.

He came to the foot of the page and read the four sentences and the four lines and the five lines, and came to the striking.

And then, instead of going on to the two inches of nothing, he read the sixth one.

He had not read it aloud since the thirtieth of March. He said so himself afterwards, twice, in the same words, and neither of those times was to anybody but Lin Hao, who was inside the passage and had been inside it for about forty minutes.

He read it at the same speed as the rest of it.

And at the end of it he put in a word that is not on the page.

## What He Put In

“The words on the sheet are that an answer about any other person is not carried to anybody,” said the counterman, “and what I said was that an answer about any other person is not carried to anybody who has not asked for it. That is one word. It is one word in the whole thing and I put it in this afternoon and I am telling you that I put it in.”

“Where did the word come from.”

“It came from about four people standing at that rail this morning asking me a question, and I am the only one in this city who is ever going to be asked it, and I had nothing to give them but that, and it is true.”

The passage did not move.

“I have read that page to about nine people a week for nine years and I have never once said a word of my own in it,” said the counterman, “because everything on it is somebody else's words and I am paid nothing and nothing in this city has ever put a question to a man behind a counter about whether he read a thing right.”

## The Thing Lin Hao Cannot Do About It

He could have told him. That was the whole of it and it took about four seconds to work out.

In June a woman in a guild house had priced a rule and been right about the price. In March a man in a white plate had refused to read an answer twice in the same room because what comes after once is a draft. Both of those were about rooms and about offices and about a book with a date and an hour against every line in it.

This was a rail in a passage, and the thing the man had put in was not in the page and could not be unsaid by anybody, and nothing in this city lets a man in a white plate tell a man behind a counter to take a word back out of his mouth.

And he had asked, on the thirtieth of March, not to be told.

“If I ask you and you answer me then I will be a man who has been told,” said the counterman, four minutes later, at the end of the rail, with his hands in front of him and not on the wood. “You said no. And I have gone and done the thing you said no to.”

“I did not say no to the word.”

“You said no to me hearing what it is for,” said the counterman. “That is the same door and I have gone through it twice this afternoon and I have come out with about nine people who have heard two different sentences out of my mouth, and you were standing in the passage for both of them.”

## Which Of The Two Was On The Page

“Which of the two was on the page,” said Lin Hao.

The counterman stopped.

He did not have to stop for long. He had read that page nine hundred times and he could have said the sentence out loud from behind the rail with the shutter down and a person talking in the room, and he said it, all of it, at the same speed as the rest of it.

Then he looked at it.

“Not one of them,” he said.

“That is not possible. You have read it nine hundred times.”

“Not one of them is the whole of it,” said the counterman. “I have been reading it out of a rail for nine years and I have never had the page in my hands and I have never once been shown the foot of it by anybody, and I have just said out loud in a passage that I do not know which half of a sentence is mine.”

He put his thumb along the wood.

“You cannot put it back either. You have not got it in a book and not one clerk in this city may enter what a man said at a rail, and I have now said two sentences to about nine people and about four of them heard both.”

*Three thousand four hundred and forty-eight days. Less three thousand four hundred and forty-two, the twenty-third of this month, is six, and a man who is paid nothing read the sixth line at the foot of a quarterly sheet at four in the afternoon, having not read it since the thirtieth of March, and put one word of his own into it in front of about nine people in a passage.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is fifty-nine, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and twenty-eight days, which is thirty-four months and eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Folder Holds Its Day

Three thousand three hundred and eighty-three was the twenty-fourth of this month and the thirtieth is day three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, and three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine less three thousand three hundred and eighty-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month and of the quarter.

## About Half Past Two, And A Sheet Squared

The thirty-seventh came off the rail at four o’clock and the thirty-eighth went up in its place, ninety days after it went up, and it carried no new line in the office’s hand. It went up squared, the way it has gone up every quarter since the fifth year, because a sheet that is not squared cannot be read at a rail by nine people at once.

It is the same page. He went down the length of it with her and counted, and it is the same six lines at the foot with the same one of them a sentence a man said out loud in a room, the same four sentences under those, the same four lines in the second column with a woman’s name in the middle of the fourth one, the same striking with a name under it and the same two inches of nothing at the bottom of it all.

Nothing has been put under any of it and nothing has been taken off, and the second of December of the year before is still the last day anything was written at the foot of that page.

## The Number At The Top Of It, And Whose Count It Is

Eight hundred and fifty-two.

It has stood at the head of that sheet since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and it has not shifted once since, and there is nothing in this city with the standing to shift it.

“Twenty-two sheets,” said Lin Hao.

“Twenty-one quarters,” said Wenna Raut.

“That is a count of sheets.”

“It is a count of quarter-ends gone by since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and a quarter in this city is ninety days,” she said, and put two fingers on the figure.

“Three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine days less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is one thousand eight hundred and ninety days. That is twenty-one times ninety exactly and the division has nothing over it.”

“It was nineteen in the middle of December and it is twenty-one now, and you have carried that sentence about on your coat since the spring of the ninth year.”

“They differ by one, and the reason is the sheet itself. That number was first written at the head of one page, and that page is inside the twenty-two and it is not inside the twenty-one, because a page is not a quarter-end.”

“Two counts and both of them are right and the page prints both,” said Wenna Raut, “and they are not the same, and I am not going to say them for you at a rail in front of about nine people. Say them yourself and say which one the page is doing.”

He said them himself, in the room, with the door open. Eight hundred and fifty-two, unmoved, across twenty-two sheets and twenty-one quarters, both named in words, both right, differing by one.

## The Quarter Room At The Back Of The Wing

The filing floor is two flights down and the way in is a wooden stair up out of it. He came down with nothing in his hands, as he comes down every time, and she looked at his hands and then at his face and told him to leave his coat on the second chair with the split in the leg.

The quarter room is off the back of that floor and it is thirty feet long and nine feet wide with a stone floor and nothing on the walls at all.

There are two long shallow presses standing down the length of it, one at each end, with the lids up on their hinges. On the last working day of a quarter both of them are emptied, and the folders go down to the basement in a barrow, and the lids stay open until the first of the month because a press that is shut cannot be counted.

So on the afternoon of a quarter-end there is nothing in that room except two empty presses standing open, about nine feet of bare stone between them, a lamp on the floor, and a small window high up in the wall with the light coming off it.

He had never been in it. She had never brought anybody into it.

## Four Seconds, And Then She Did Not Wait

He had the sentence in his mouth and she took it out of it.

“You have come down two flights with a question about two days,” said Sun Meiling, “and you have had it ready since the corridor and I have watched you get it ready out of the corner of my eye while I was taking the lids up.”

“I have.”

“You are not going to ask it.”

“No.”

“You are going to stand there in about nine seconds and decide not to, and then you are going to be a man who has come down two flights to look at a room, and I have had that before and it is the worst hour of my week.” She put the barrow key in her apron. “Coat off the second chair. Get your sleeves up. I have been on my feet since seven and this room is going to be free in about forty minutes.”

There was nothing in it for him to give her. She is paid, and the thing he came down for cannot be bought, and neither of them said one word about that and neither of them needed to.

## Nine Feet Of Stone, And Two Empty Presses

She put the canvas sacking out of the bottom of the near press first, because the stone had been cold since eleven and because she is a woman who works on floors.

Then she took her cuffs back and put her sleeves to just below the elbows and left the pins in them where they were, and she did not suggest that he turn round, and there was no ceremony in any of it.

“Get your back off that stone,” she said. “You are going to tell me you are fine in about four minutes and you are not.”

He got off it.

She was quicker than she had any business being and she had clearly decided that beforehand. She got his trousers open where they were and took his cock out in her hand and worked the whole length of it once to find out how far along he was, and then said the number out loud in the level voice she keeps for a column of figures, and made a noise about it that was almost a laugh and entirely approving.

“Sixteen years of men coming down that stair,” she said, “and you have never once come down it with your chest in a bad way before.”

“I have.”

“You have never once come down it with your chest in a bad way and something to ask me at the same time,” said Sun Meiling, “and that is the difference and I have been counting it.”

He got his hands up under her and she let him, for about nine seconds, and then took one of them off and put it behind her own back and kept it there, and she said so, and did not explain it.

The lamp on the floor threw the underside of the far press lid up across the wall and the light out of the high window went across nine feet of stone and off the wall, and the stone took everything anybody said in that room and gave none of it back.

She was wet between the legs before she had touched him and had been since about the second he came round the end of the filing floor, and she told him that as a fact and not as a complaint, and then she took him inside her in one push and held there four seconds with her mouth open and her forehead against his jaw and her cunt pulsing round him while she got her breath.

Then she said, “That is the woman who came up the four flights on the seventeenth of this month asking about her neighbour and went back down them without an answer, and you have had her row in front of you for thirteen days and you have not opened your mouth about it.”

“Do not.”

“I am not going to ask you why,” said Sun Meiling, “because you are the second person this month who has come down here not asking me something and I do not want to know what you have both decided.”

She rode him hard and short, one hand in his hair and the other hooked round the back of a knee, and she set the pace she wanted and did not consult him about it and did not slow down for his chest.

Then she got the canvas sacking under his shoulders with her heel and told him to say the second answer out loud, and he did, and she said good, and then told him to say it again, and he did, and she said that too, and neither of them was working anything out.

She finished him with her hand and then got herself off him and went up onto the lid of the far press with her heels hanging and her forearms on her knees, and she was breathing hard and entirely satisfied and took about nine seconds before she said anything at all.

## The Rule She Said Because He Did Not Ask

“You have had a question in your mouth since the corridor,” she said. “So I am going to say the rule, and I am going to say it because you did not ask, and if you had asked then we would have spent forty minutes on it and got nowhere and I would have been enjoying myself instead.”

She did not look at him.

“A thing that was asked goes in under the day it was asked. A thing that was given goes in under the day it was given. Not the same day. The day it was asked, and the day it was given, and the two of them stay where they are.”

“Why.”

“Because a day is the only thing this building has ever been able to stand on,” said Sun Meiling. “Not a name and not a number and not a heading. A day comes in and it either came in or it did not, and not one person in this city can be got to say otherwise afterwards, and that is worth more than the fact.”

She got up off the press lid and got her sleeves down.

“Now tell me what you came to move.”

## The Cost Of Being Right About Order

“If you file a question under the day the answer was given then the two days become one day,” said Sun Meiling, “and then the book is a book of answers with a question in it somewhere near each one, and order stops meaning anything in it. I have been on this floor for eleven years and I have watched two men try that in my time and neither of them came back a third time to argue about it.”

He stood in the middle of nine feet of stone with his shirt open and the cold coming up off the floor.

“You could have moved one of them and it would have been true.”

“I could have moved one of them and it would have been true this afternoon,” she said, “and in about four years somebody would have wanted a day out of that book and there would be two of them and one of the two would have been wrong, and neither of them could be looked at, because a correction goes under and never instead.”

She picked the barrow key out of her apron and turned it over once in her fingers.

“About four rows in every nine that your office adds now are a day somebody asked about another person. Some of those nine get read out in a room with two people in it and the rest do not, and every one of them is a day and not a pair, and if the two days ever come together then you will never find out again whether a person waited nine days or one day or none.”

“Your floor could say both.”

“My floor could say both and my floor could not be asked afterwards,” said Sun Meiling. “That is the whole of what I have and it is why I say no.”

## The One Sentence About A Person

The woman who came up the four flights on the seventeenth of this month is thirteen days older than she was in that corridor, and she has not come back up them, and there is nothing in this city she can be told about the thirteen days except that they were kept apart on purpose.

He stood there and understood the rule perfectly.

He also understood that he was the man who would have asked her to move one, and that he had come down two flights with it ready, and that she had taken it out of his mouth in about nine seconds and given him something else instead and had not once made him feel like a man who had been let off anything.

He put his sleeves down.

“You have never thanked me,” she said.

“No.”

“I am not asking,” said Sun Meiling. “I have four people in this city who have not thanked you and I have been the fourth one for eleven years and I would have had to be paid twice to say it and I am not paid twice for anything.”

# The Line At The Foot Holds

Three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine was the twentieth of this month and the twenty-seventh is day three thousand five hundred and six, and three thousand five hundred and six less three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is seven, and twenty-seven less twenty is seven by the month count.

## The Morning It Turned

The cold came up the hill on the twenty-seventh of this month and it came up it in about an hour, the way it has come up that hill four or five times in nine years, and it was on the stones by about half past nine.

It brought eleven people up the four flights by about eleven instead of the nine that come up them on an ordinary morning, and there was no queue at the bottom of the stair, because a queue is a thing this city makes indoors and nobody who stood on that cold step was going to stand on it twice.

The first of the eleven came up at about ten past ten and she had a sheet of her own paper in her hand with four lines on it, and it was a question about a paper she had brought up herself in the spring, and it took Wenna Raut about nine minutes and it went back down in a fold, the flap gummed shut, with the date and the hour written on the outside and nothing else written anywhere.

## Two Hours In One Morning

The second one came up at about half past ten and she had a sheet of her own paper in her hand and she had a thing to say before she said anything else, and she said it in the corridor and not in the room, because the corridor was where she was.

She had been answered on the twenty-second of this month. She had asked about a paper of her own and the answer had gone back down in a fold, and on the outside of that fold, under the date, there was an hour on it.

She said the hour was wrong.

Not that the answer was wrong. The answer was about a piece of paper and the piece of paper was hers and the office holds one row against it, and that part was right and she was not here about that part.

The hour was wrong by a little over two hours.

“Write it as I say it,” she said. “That on the twenty-second of this month I was at a market off Cordwain from ten until about half past one and I was not within a mile of that building, and that the hour on the outside of that fold is not an hour at which anything can have happened to me, and that whoever put it there did not know where I was and neither do I know who that was.”

Wenna Raut wrote it as she said it and read it back once and entered it under the four lines with the date of that morning and the hour against it, in a clerk's hand, because a correction goes under and never instead.

Then she shut the book and stood with her hand on the cover.

“The wrong one is still there,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You cannot take it out.”

“No,” said Lin Hao. “It cannot be lifted out of that book by anything in this city, and the only person who could decide to take it out is a person who would have to be told who wrote the row above it, and that is the thing this office has been refusing to know since the seventeenth of this month.”

The woman put her paper back inside her coat.

“Then there are two hours in that book for one morning,” she said, “and about nine people a month come up here and every one of them can read both, and in about four years somebody is going to work out which of the two the office meant, and they will be working it out about a stranger.”

“That is what it costs and I am not going to pretend it does not.”

“You have cost me a morning,” said the woman. “I have gone over the thing twice since the twenty-second because there was an hour on it that was not mine.”

She went down the four flights. She had not asked for anything, and she did not get anything, and she went down with a thing of hers in her hand that she had brought up and not used.

“Read it out once more,” he said, “the thing she said, exactly as she said it, and then tell me whether you can tell from the shape of it which of the two hours the office meant.”

“I cannot tell it from the shape of it. I have written both and I wrote them both and there is nothing in either sentence about where she was, because she was not asked.”

“Then say what that costs and say it in this room and not in that corridor.”

“That costs about nine people a month,” said Wenna Raut. “Nine people a month can read two hours for one morning in about a quarter, and about four of them will assume the office got it right the first time and the second one is the correction, because that is what a correction looks like, and about four of them will assume the other way round because that is what a correction usually is. And both of those will be right about half the time.”

“Then two hours are worse than no hour.”

“Two hours are the same as no hour with the extra step of somebody being wrong on the page first,” she said.

## The Third One Up, At About Twenty To Eleven

The third one came up at about twenty to eleven and she did not sit down.

She had heard the foot of that page read out loud in a passage off Cordwain and she wanted the whole of it, all six lines, read to her in that room, in one go.

“Why do you want it read.”

“Because I have stood at that rail twice since September,” said the woman, “and the man behind it read the bottom of that page to me twice and he put a word of his own into it, and I want to know what the paper says.”

“He has never had the paper in his hands.”

“Then I would rather have what the paper says.”

Wenna Raut took the book that is not a book — it is a standing sheet, and it is not the Register and it is not about anybody — off the shelf behind the door and squared it on the table and read all six lines of the foot of it, once, at the speed she reads everything in.

Five of them are instructions to a stranger. They say what such a person may put to this office and what such a person will be given back and by what hand. They have been the same since the second of December of the year before.

The sixth is not an instruction to anybody. It is a sentence a man said out loud, once, in that room, on the second of December of the year before, and it was put under the other five because it was true and because it had been said.

She read it in the same voice as the other five, which she has never been asked to do differently and would not have done differently if she had been.

“Thank you,” she said.

“That is the whole of it.”

“It is six lines,” said the woman. “I thought it would be a page.”

“It is the bottom of a page. It goes out on a quarterly sheet to about two thousand counters across this city, and on an ordinary week about nine people stop in front of it, and about four of those nine go away believing something about it that is not on the paper.”

## The Part He Would Not Say In That Room

Then she asked for the figure at the top of it.

He said no before he had decided to, and then he said the reason, which is the only reason there is.

“No,” said Lin Hao. “Not in this room.”

“Why not in this room.”

“Because there are about four people in that corridor and any figure said in here goes out of that door with them, and this is the one figure in this city that has not moved since the fifth year and I am not going to say it to four people in a corridor about it.”

“You said it at a counter.”

“I said it at a counter in September, from the customer side of the wood, in the open air, to about nine people, and I gave them the two counts and the difference between them in the same breath, because the difference is the only part of it that anybody wants and the difference is the part that gets lost.”

“So it is said once a quarter, in the street, by a man standing in the wrong place.”

“That is where it is said, and it is said once a quarter, and I have said it once and I am not going to say it to one person in a room with a corridor outside the door.”

## What She Asked Next

“Then I will come and stand there on the thirtieth,” said the woman.

“You do not have to.”

“I have two hours and a cold step and nothing else to do with them,” she said, “and I have wanted to know what the difference is between two counts since September, and there are about nine of us at that rail on a quarter-end and all of us have been guessing.”

She went down the four flights at about eleven and she had the bottom of that page in her ears and not the top of it, and she will stand at that rail on the thirtieth of this month because a man in a white plate refused to say a number in a room with four people in the corridor outside it.

And that is the whole of what happened on the twenty-seventh of this month, except that about nine of the eleven went back down their four flights in about nine minutes each, and a wrong hour is sitting above a right one in a bound book and will still be sitting there when the office is not.

“I have said that out loud before,” he said, at the end of the table, with the door open. “I am not going to say it again today.”

*Three thousand five hundred and six days. Less three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine, the twentieth of this month, is seven, and six lines at the foot of a standing sheet are the same six lines they were, and a correction went under a wrong hour and the wrong hour stayed above it.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is twenty-seven, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and eighty-six days, which is thirty-six months and six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

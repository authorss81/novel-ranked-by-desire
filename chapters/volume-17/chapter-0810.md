# The Answer Goes By Hand

Three thousand two hundred and ninety-three was the twenty-fourth of this month and the thirtieth is day three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, and three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine less three thousand two hundred and ninety-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count, and the thirtieth is the last working day of the month and of the quarter.

## What He Worked Out At Ten In The Morning

The office had been asked anything since the second of December. It had answered in writing. It had carried its answers down four flights and along about four hundred yards to one hand.

And for a hundred and eighteen days it had also been cutting those answers into slips about the size of a hand and sending them down to a counter in a passage off Cordwain, and a man who is paid nothing had read them out at a rail to about nine people a week who had not asked for any of them.

Not one person had decided that. That was what he had learned on the previous afternoon and had not been able to put down since.

The instruction of the second of December says the office shall answer in writing.

It says the question and the answer shall be entered in the Register with a date and an hour against each. It says nothing whatever about a rail, and nothing whatever about a slip.

And about nine people a week had been standing in a passage four feet wide hearing other people’s answers given to them in the ordinary voice of a man who reads whatever is in front of him, and nothing in that instruction stopped it, because nothing in that instruction mentions it.

He had not ordered it. That was the part he could not get round.

An office with an answer in it will find a way to put the answer where an answer is wanted. That is what it is for. He had opened a door and told the truth about what was behind it and then left the room, and the room had done the obvious thing with what it had been given, and every single hand that had touched a slip had meant to do the right one.

## The Number At The Top Of It, And Whose Count It Is

She took the thirty-seventh out of the flat file at about ten past nine and carried it herself, because that is a thing a person who is here for the quarter-end does, and she squared it against the wood at about half past two and squared it again about four minutes later.

Eight hundred and fifty-two.

It has stood at the head of that sheet since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and has not shifted once since, and nothing in this city has the standing to shift it and nothing ever has.

“Twenty-one sheets,” said Lin Hao.

“Twenty quarters,” said Wenna Raut.

“That is a count of sheets.”

“It is a count of quarter-ends that have gone by since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and a quarter in this city is ninety days, and twenty of them is one thousand eight hundred days.” She put two fingers on the figure.

“Three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine days less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is one thousand eight hundred. It is twenty times ninety exactly and the division has nothing over it, and you said that out loud in front of me in the middle of December and it was nineteen then and it is twenty now, and I have watched you carry that sentence about on your coat ever since.”

“They differ by one, and the reason is the sheet itself. That number was first written at the head of one page, and that page is inside the twenty-one, and it is not inside the twenty, because the twenty are the quarter-ends that have gone past since and a page is not a quarter-end.”

“Then it is a count of two things and both of them are right,” said Wenna Raut, “and that page goes out to about two thousand counters this afternoon, and you put every number in it.”

“So you say both, and you say they are not the same, and you say which one the page is doing. I am not doing it for you. You are the hand behind all of it, and if the two of them are wrong then it is your name that is wrong and not mine, and it has been your name and not mine since the first of February of the first year.”

He said it, then, standing up, in the room, with the door open.

Eight hundred and fifty-two, unmoved, across twenty-one sheets and twenty quarters, both named in words, both right, differing by one. The page is printing both. The one thousand eight hundred days is the quarter count and it is exact.

## What He Cannot Do

“There is one thing I cannot do and I am going to say it before I say the other thing, so that the room has it first.”

“Go on.”

“I cannot put it in the hand.”

Wenna Raut put the pen across the book and looked at it and then at him.

“Say what you mean.”

“There are six lines in this office’s hand at the foot of that page. Five of them are instructions and one of them is a sentence a man said out loud in a room.”

“The sixth went in on the second of December and it is the last line this office will ever make at the foot of one of its own pages.”

“I decided that in about four seconds in the middle of December and I have not been able to undecide it since, and I am not going to today. And I would like it said out loud in this room that I decided it, because in about four years somebody is going to want it undone, and there will be one word in it that costs a person a street.”

“So what is going to happen to it.”

“It is going to exist, because I am going to keep it, and it is going to be kept by about four people in four rooms, and nothing in Ostara can make any of them keep it, and in about nine months one of the four will be on the other side of the city and it will quietly stop happening on one morning of the week and start again on the next.”

“That is not a practice. That is a habit with a good name.”

“It is the best one available and I know exactly what it is, and I am not writing it down, and that is the price of it and I am paying it out loud in this room instead.”

## What It Is

He said it standing up, once, in the order it stood in his head, and then he said it a second time for the clerk, because the clerk is the only person in this city who can tell a record from a decision and he was not going to have this one turn into a decision by accident.

“From this quarter, every written answer goes by hand to the person who asked for it and into nobody else’s hand. Not onto a rail. Not into a slip. Not to a house where there are two people in it. And whoever carries it writes down whose hand took it and in what order, in their own book, in their own hand.”

He stopped there and did not improve it and did not add a reason.

Wenna Raut repeated it back in her own words, standing up, in the voice she reads everything in.

“From the thirtieth of this quarter an answer leaves this office in a fold and goes down four flights on somebody’s legs and stops at one door, and if that door is not the door of the person who asked, it does not get put down and it comes back up four flights. And the carrier writes whose hand took it. And there is not one line of it in this office’s hand at the foot of anything.”

“That is it,” said Lin Hao.

“That is it,” she said, “and I am going to tell you what it does to me, and after that you are going to walk down to that counter and say it to a man who is paid nothing. Once. And then you are not going to say either of them again, and I am not going to have to hear them twice.”

## What It Costs In That Room

She did not put the pen down.

“I have spent a year being exact on paper,” said Wenna Raut. “Everything I have entered since the thirtieth of March has been in the words the person used and I have not improved one line of it and there has not been a single one of those lines anybody could point at and say the clerk made that up. That is the whole of what I know how to do and I have got nine years of it.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table.

“From this afternoon I stop being exact on paper and start being exact about hands. And there is not one instrument in this city that will tell me a hand is honest, and the only record of any of it will be a column in a book that is kept in a lane by a woman who does not work for me and has not promised me a morning of the week.”

“And you do not know whether it is true.”

“I do not know whether it is true,” said Wenna Raut, “and I will go on writing the answers in the words I am given, and I will go on being right about about nine of every ten of them, and from this afternoon I am trusting something I have never once had to trust in nine years, which is a person walking down a street with my sentence in his coat.”

He said nothing to that, because there was nothing to say to it, and she had not said it for him to answer.

## What It Costs At A Rail, And The Request In It

The thirty-sixth came down off the rail at four o’clock on the thirtieth of March, ninety days after it went up, and the counterman took it down himself and the thirty-seventh went up in its place.

It went up carrying no new line in the office’s hand.

Six lines, five instructions and one sentence a man said out loud. Four sentences at the foot. Four lines in the second column under two printed words, the fourth of them with a woman’s name in it in the middle of a sentence and no figure on any of the four. Six lines under the sentences with the fourth struck through and a name under the striking. Two inches of nothing at the bottom of it all.

And no seventh thing.

“That is the sheet going up with six lines in it,” said the counterman, when he had finished, “and it was the last sheet in the world that had anything new at the bottom of it, and I am going to read one thing fewer than I have been reading, and I want you to tell me what I am to think about that.”

“I cannot tell you that.”

“No,” said the counterman. “I did not think you could. That is the arrangement and it is the whole of what I have.”

He put his thumb along the edge of the rail.

“I shall read the rest of it to whoever stands here for ever and I shall not know what it is for, and from today there is going to be one thing I do not read, and I have not got the first idea whether that is a mercy and I am not going to ask you, because if I ask you and you answer me then I will be a man who has been told.”

Then he took his thumb off the wood.

“One thing, before the hour,” said the counterman. “Before it goes. Carry me one. Let me stand here and hear one of them go out on somebody’s legs, one of the hard ones, because I have read the bottom of a page to about nine people a week for nine years and I do not know what any of it is for, and I would like to see the whole of the thing once going the other way.”

## Five Past Four, And One Door

It started raining at about ten past four.

He folded the first one in that room at about half past four, which was the twelfth answer of the quarter and about the fourth hardest, and it was about a paper a woman had brought up in the autumn and what the office holds about her, and it is four lines long.

He went down four flights and along about four hundred yards in the rain with it inside his coat and not in his hand, and he is rank one with a chest that has been spent since the fourth year and it took him eleven minutes instead of nine.

The door was two streets wide of the laundry and it was the fourth one and it was open about four inches and a woman stood behind it with a shawl on and did not ask him anything at all, and she took the fold, and she did not open it in front of him, and she put her hand on the back of his wrist for about a second to say that it had arrived.

He wrote it down in her book on the sill of that door in the wet, in his own hand. Whose hand took it. In what order. One line each, and no reason column, and his own name against the hour.

The hand was hers.

He went back up four flights and stood in the corridor at about half past five with the rain coming in under the door and did not go in for about four minutes.

Then Wenna Raut entered the twelfth answer of the quarter with its date and its hour, and under it she entered that it had been carried by hand to the person who asked and delivered into that person’s hand at about ten minutes to five, and that the carrier had written the hand and the order in her own book in her own hand, and the asker had taken it shut.

Neither of those two lines is in the office’s hand. They are in a clerk’s hand, at the date and the hour, in the words of the woman who wrote them, and nothing goes under the foot of that page today and nothing ever will, because there is no line there for a person’s hands.

*Three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine days. Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-three, the twenty-fourth of this month, is six, and the thirty-sixth sheet had been face up on that rail for ninety days and came down at four and the thirty-seventh went up in its place carrying no new line in the office hand, and at about ten minutes to five a man carried the twelfth answer of the quarter down four flights in the rain and put it into the one hand it was for.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December of this year, is ninety.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is eight hundred and seventy-nine days, which is twenty-nine months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

*Three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine days less one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine is one thousand eight hundred days, and that is twenty times ninety exactly, and the head of that sheet is eight hundred and fifty-two and has not moved across twenty-one sheets and twenty quarters, both named, both right, differing by one.*

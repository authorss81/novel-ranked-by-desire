# Nobody Is Listed

Three thousand three hundred and forty-eight was the nineteenth of this month and the twenty-sixth is day three thousand three hundred and fifty-five, and three thousand three hundred and fifty-five less three thousand three hundred and forty-eight is seven, and twenty-six less nineteen is seven by the month count.

## She Said It Before He Asked

He was on the landing on the fourth of that building at about ten past seven in the morning with his coat off and a square of pencil paper in his hand, and she had the end of a hand-truck on the step behind him and the other end against her hip.

“You have come up four flights to ask me for a heading,” said Perla Quist, “and I am going to tell you no before you get the sentence out, and then you are going to stand there for about a minute and be annoyed with yourself, and then you are going to ask me the real question and I am going to answer that one.”

He put the pencil paper back in his coat.

“How long have you known.”

“Since about four minutes after the door opened on the second of December,” said Perla Quist, “when a man at that rail said out loud that any person might ask, and I have been standing at a window four flights up waiting to find out what it would look like.”

## A Street Two Streets Wide And The Smell Of Steam

The laundry is on a street two streets wide and the front of it is a wide open floor with two stone tables in it and about nine sheets hanging over rails, and at about seven in the morning the whole of that floor is wet and the light comes in low from one side and lies in the wet.

There is a back room off it with nine sheets under a third table and a heading on a column of her own paper since the twenty-first of February of the eighth year, and neither is an instrument and neither may be cited by anybody at any counter in any court in this city, and he is the only person who knows both of them exist.

He had not been up this stair since the fifth of September of the ninth year, when she wrote four lines out in her own hand on her own paper with a date at the top of it, and the four lines went into the inside of his coat on the left and are still there.

“Why a heading.”

Perla Quist put the hand-truck down against the wall. There is a split knuckle on her right hand that has never been dressed and a coin-sized burn on the inside of her forearm from steam that came off a pipe eleven years ago, and she put both of them where he could not see them, which was a thing she had been doing since the eighth year.

## What A Heading Is

“A heading is a thing that can be asked to stop,” she said. “That is all. It is not a danger and it is not an offence. There is a woman in this city right now who has said the word *unrecorded* out loud in about four hundred kitchens and there is a man with a slate in a lane who has told at least one of them that he is right about what it means.

“In about four years this office is going to have an answer a week about a person, and in about two years after that somebody is going to want them all under one name, and they will be right to want it. That is the whole of the danger. Not that they want it. That they will be right.”

“And if there is not one.”

“Then in about four years there are two hundred and thirty answers in nine sheets and every one of them is in a stranger’s handwriting and nobody can put two of them together to make a street,” said Perla Quist. “And that is the arrangement I have, and I have said no six times about it in six years and I have never once said the reason out loud until about a minute ago.”

## What She Has Been Doing In The Lane

Then she told him the thing, and she told it standing up in the wet middle of that floor with the steam coming under the door, and she told it fast and flat.

“In the last four weeks about nine people have asked me in this lane and on this stair whether that office holds anything about their neighbour.”

He did not say anything.

“Two of them asked me directly. One of them was on the step about a fortnight ago with her hand on the rail and she said it in the ordinary voice, the way you would ask about a dog. I have said nothing six times.”

“You have said nothing six times.”

“I have said nothing six times and I have been sitting here for four weeks being a woman who keeps an index and has never once put a name in a column and has now told six people in a lane that they have the wrong address.”

“Perla —”

“I am not finished and you did not come up here for me to be finished.”

## The Thing She Said About Herself

“The real question is not the heading,” she said. “You have not come up four flights about a heading. You have come up four flights because on the nineteenth of this month a refusal got out of that room, and you want one place in this city where it is written down that two people asked about one paper and were told no, and you want me to write it, and you have had the sentence ready since the stair.”

“I had.”

“I am not going to write it.”

“I know you are not.”

“You do not know that,” said Perla Quist. “You knew it eleven seconds after I opened that door and you have been standing there being polite about it ever since.”

She picked the hand-truck up again.

“You are not going to ask me about the nine,” she said. “I know that too and I want it said out loud in this room, because you have never once asked me a question and you are not going to start on a morning when I have given you something, and if you did I would not answer it anyway and you would not be able to complain.”

“I am not going to ask you about the nine.”

“Why not.”

“Because nothing in this city lets me ask a woman who keeps an index what people say to her on a step,” said Lin Hao, “and because if I asked you then in about four years there would be a question in this lane about a woman who keeps an index, and I did not come up here to make that.”

She looked at him for about four seconds with the hand-truck against her hip.

“That is the first true thing anybody has said at that door since the fifth of September of the ninth year,” she said, “and you have not come up here for me to be told that either.”

## What She Has Instead Of A Heading

He went through the doorway with her at about twenty past seven and the back room is nine feet by eleven and there is a third table in it with nothing on it and nine sheets under the edge of it, and the sheets are her own paper and her own hand and there is a heading on a column of one of them since the twenty-first of February of the eighth year.

He did not go near the third table. He has not gone near it since the eighth year and he is not going to now, and she watched him not go near it and let him.

“What do you keep,” he said.

“Days,” said Perla Quist. “Not people. A day has no name on it unless somebody puts one there and I have never put one. A sheet came through a works door on a day with a thing on it and a man described it in his own words and I write the day and the words and nothing else, and if the same thing comes up twice I write it twice and I do not join them up, because joining them up is how you get a street.”

“That is an index.”

“It is not an index. An index has to be able to find a thing again and that requires a name or a number that stays the same, and neither of mine ever stays the same, and that is not a fault in the design. That is the design.”

She straightened the edge of the table with two fingers.

“Six times I have been asked by men with standing whether I would add a column and every one of the six was a man doing me a kindness and every one of the six would have put nine people into a list without knowing it.”

## What He Came Up Here For

“No,” he said. “I came up here because on the nineteenth of this month a refusal got out of a room, and I have got exactly four people in this city who are keeping something for that office in a practice that nothing can make them keep, and every one of the four has a reason, and you are the only one of the four who has never once been asked to stop keeping it.”

“I am a woman who said no in the eighth year and wrote you four lines in the ninth, and you have been up my stair four times since.”

“You are a woman who said no six times and wrote me four lines once, and you are the only one of the four of us who cannot be ordered and cannot be asked to stop by anybody, including me, and I have been using that.”

Perla Quist set the hand-truck down again.

“Say the last of it.”

“You are the only one left in this city with the standing to refuse a thing in writing and be right about it,” said Perla Quist, “and you have got that because you have never once been paid, and there is not a person in this city who could put a heading under nine sheets of your own paper, and you did not come up here to ask me for a heading.”

She turned the hand-truck round and got it through the door.

“You came up here because on the nineteenth of this month a man heard a refusal at four feet and understood something about his own office for the first time in four months, and nothing in this city will hold that for him, and he came up four flights to leave it somewhere.”

*Three thousand three hundred and fifty-five days. Less three thousand three hundred and forty-eight, the nineteenth of this month, is seven, and a woman in a laundry said no before the question was asked and gave a reason she has not given in six years, and said that about nine people had asked her in a lane in four weeks whether that office holds anything about their neighbour, and that she had said nothing six times.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of March, is fifty-six, and the thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and thirty-five days, which is thirty-one months and five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

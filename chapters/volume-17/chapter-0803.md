# The Hand That Carries It

## A Fold With Nothing Ruled On It

She was standing at a pigeonhole rack on the second floor of a division building at about nine in the morning with a fold of paper in her hand that a runner had brought up from a room off a third court at a quarter to eight, and she had been holding it for about eleven minutes without opening it.

The rack behind her held about four hundred returns that had gone out and had not come back, and a shelf below it for the ones that had. Neither of those is what was in her hand.

In her hand was a sheet with two lines on it in a clerk's hand, a date at the head and an hour against the date, and the two lines said what an office holds about a paper, and the person the paper belonged to was not named on it.

She had carried four hundred documents in nine years and read none of them. That was not carelessness. That was the standing of a woman who carries what goes out, and it was worth something at a counter where a person has to be able to say they did not know what was in the fold before they gave it away.

She turned her head about nine degrees, which is a thing she does.

“This is not a return,” she said.

“It is not.”

“It is not a column entry and there is nothing ruled on it anywhere. Where does a thing go when there is nothing ruled on it?”

“Into the hand it is for, and into no other hand at all.”

## What She Will Carry And What She Will Not

The runner had gone back down. The corridor went on past her with about nine people through it in the first hour.

“Say the rest of it. You have said nine words and every one of them was true, and you have stood there with it for nine minutes so that I would hear all of it before I agreed to anything.”

Lin Hao did not improve it.

“An answer in writing goes by hand to the person who asked for it and into nobody else’s hand,” he said. “And whoever carries it writes down whose hand took it and in what order.”

“That is not the division’s rule.”

“No.”

“That is a rule of a room with no door onto a street.”

“It is,” said Lin Hao, “and there is not one line of it in that room’s hand at the foot of any page, and I cannot put one there, and I am not going to try.”

Runa Sato turned the fold over in her fingers without opening it.

“Nine years of carrying things I have not read,” she said. “Nine years of a fold going into a hand at a door and my name against the hour and not one word of the thing in my head. That is the whole of what a carrier is, and it is worth exactly as much as the paper it is on and not one grain more.”

## The Order In The Book

She read it twice, standing up, in the flat voice she keeps at a counter and not the one she used anywhere else.

Then she went down the corridor to the door at the end with the glass in it and opened her own book on the sill, which is a book of deliveries with two columns that are not the division’s, and she filled in the first of them.

There is about a stone and a half of her that a person cannot account for by looking at her, and the pen looked small in her hand. She wrote without pressing.

Whose hand took it: one line. In what order: one line.

“No reason column,” she said.

“No reason column.”

“If a thing goes into the wrong hand I write the hand and the order and not why, because the day book is for what the counter did and not for what I decided.”

“Runa.”

“Do not use my name to get a better entry out of me,” she said, and turned her head about nine degrees. “This is the first time in nine years my name has gone against a thing I have read.”

## One Street And One Door

She took it down at about two in the afternoon and walked.

The address was a street two streets wide with a laundry at one end and a smell of wet stone off it, and she went down it on foot because a fold of paper does not ride in a cart under a driver's shoulder.

There is one door. That is the whole of the delivery, and there was nothing to do at it but stand on it.

The woman who came to it had a shawl on and looked at the paper and not at the carrier, and said, “That is the second one.”

“Second what,” said Runa Sato.

“Second one somebody has asked me about.” She turned the fold over in her own hands. “The first one was a man in a cap on the second of this month and I did not answer him either.”

“You are not to answer him.”

“I am not going to.” The woman looked up. “I want that in whatever book you have got, because he is coming back, and I want it written down that I said it to a person and not to him.”

Runa Sato wrote one line against the hand and did not write a word of the rest of it anywhere, because the rest of it was not hers to enter.

## The Room At The End Of A Corridor

She came back up four flights with the book shut and the delivery behind her and two hours of an afternoon still owed to a district she had not been in.

“Say the thing in about nine seconds,” she said. “I have got a district at four.”

It took longer than nine seconds and she stayed.

The room she put him in was at the end of a corridor on an upper floor. Stone floor. A stove out since the middle of February, with a pale ring round the top of the flue where somebody had once filled a lamp and set it on the wrong thing. A chair with the cane gone out of its seat. A low window with a grating onto a light well, where the rain went past sideways and could be seen going by without being heard.

One lamp, on a hook at about the height of his eyes. The door stood two inches open the whole time and about nine people went past it.

She shut it with her heel.

## Whose Hand Took It

Then she got herself down on the stone with her back against the leg of the chair and took her book out of her coat and looked at the two columns.

“One line,” she said. “Whose hand. One line, the order. And there is no place in that book for the fact that I read it first and that I liked it and that I would have liked to carry it twice.”

“There is not one instrument in this city that would hold that.”

“No,” said Runa Sato, “and I would not want it to. The line is in my book and it is staying where it is.”

He got himself down on the stone and let his breath go, because two stops in a cold room on an empty floor cost him more than they had any right to.

Then she asked him the thing she had been carrying up four flights.

“Who was the answer about.”

“I will not tell you.”

“Why.”

“Because you have read it and if you know who it is about then you are a person who knows something about a woman you have never met, and that is the beginning of every list in this city, and it starts with the carrier.”

Runa Sato shut the book.

“That is the first true thing anybody has said to her about carrying since the year she falsified a return and stayed in the service,” she said. “Nine years, and that is the first one.”

She stood up and got her coat and did not look at him on the way out.

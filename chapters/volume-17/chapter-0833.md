# The Lift Holds Two People

Three thousand four hundred and forty-eight was the twenty-ninth of this month and the fifth of the next is day three thousand four hundred and fifty-four, and three thousand four hundred and fifty-four less three thousand four hundred and forty-eight is six, and five out of a month that begins on the first is six by the month count.

## Half Past Ten, And A Market That Has Shut

The market off the Cordwain side shuts at ten and has shut at ten for about nine years, and by the time he got there the shutters were down the length of it and one lamp was burning at the far end by the gate, where a man chalks the day on a slate and rubs out yesterday.

About six hundred yards and two streets. He had come down the four flights with nothing in either hand, which is the only way to do it, and he was breathing wrong by the second street and had to stop at a wall for about a minute and a half, because his chest has been spent since the fourth year and a market is a long way from a tap.

At the back of the last three stalls there is a store with a door that does not lock, and inside it a goods lift.

It is not a car. It is a platform of iron plate about five feet by five feet with a rail round three sides, and it runs in a timber guide from that floor up through a hatch in the ceiling into the store above, and it is worked by a chain over a wheel and it stands stopped in the middle of its run whenever there is nothing being moved, which is most of the night.

She was sitting on the edge of it with her cap beside her and her cuffs undone at the wrist and her hands red the way they are every morning of the year from about six until about eleven.

“You have run from the third court.”

“I have walked from the third court and I have had to stop at a wall on the second street.”

“You will stop twice more before you get back and you will not tell anybody about either of them.”

## Why She Was Going To Say It There

She did not get up off the edge when he got onto the platform off a stack of empties, which took him longer than it should have.

“Right,” said Nadia Ferrous. “I am going to say a thing here and not anywhere else, and I am telling you why first so that you do not stand there improving it while I am still talking.”

“Go on.”

“This is a lift and it is stopped and there is a gate shut across the street, and there is no lamp on the run at all, and the light off that floor stops about nine inches short of the hatch. There is no clock in this building and not one person in it after the men have gone.”

She put her fingers round the chain where it came down through the corner.

“And the day is on a slate at the gate, sixty feet off, rubbed out and done again every morning by a man who does not know whose business any of it is.” She turned the chain about in her hand. “So a thing said on this platform has not got a day on it and it has not got an hour on it, and every line in that book of yours wants both of them before it will take anything.”

“That is the reason.”

“That is the reason, and you are not going to have a better one out of me, and if you go up those four flights and try to say it in that room it will be a row.”

## What She Said, In About Nine Seconds

She said it flatly and at speed, in the voice that carries about nine feet over about a hundred people at a market end, and she did not stop to let it land.

“There is a piece of paper on the table in that room that has been on that table for about three years and not one person in that building knows what it is for, and I know what it is for.”

“All right.”

“You have not said all right, because you do not know what I am about to say. I am going to say it at the speed I would say a price, and then you are going to sit on that plate and say nothing for about nine seconds.”

She said it.

“A blank sheet of office paper, unfolded, undated, with nothing on it at all, is what you put in front of a person who cannot write down the question they came up four flights with.”

## Nine Seconds On An Iron Plate

The platform did not move. The hatch was a black square overhead and somewhere below that the last man was bolting the gate across the street.

He said nothing for about nine seconds and then he said, “A person cannot read.”

“And this office promised anybody, on the second of December of the year before, in six lines in a hand at the foot of a sheet that goes out to about two thousand counters, and it promised in the words it uses and not in improved ones.”

She was looking at the guide timber and not at him.

“A person who cannot write a question cannot give you the words they used, and a row with nothing in it is not a mouth. It is a hole with a date on it, and this office has had one of those on its own table for about three years and does not know what it is.”

## How She Had Worked It Out, And What It Cost Her To Say It

“You have that in about four seconds,” said Lin Hao.

“I have worked it out in about two years and I have been carrying it for about eleven months and I could not put it anywhere.” She took her cap off the plate and turned it in her hands.

“I hand people things for about nine hours a night. Bread, change, a parcel, a way out of a queue. And for about two years I have been handing things to people who cannot write a thing down, and I have watched every one of them decide not to say it, and not one of them ever came up your four flights.”

“Why did they not come up.”

“Because they had nothing to write with and nothing to say if they came, and because a person who cannot write a question has to trust a stranger to write down what they meant, and not one of you has ever asked a person for their words before they said them.”

She put the cap down on the plate.

“That is the worst thing I have worked out about your office in nine years and I am not going to say it again.”

## She Did Not Open Him, And That Was The First New Thing

She came off the edge of the platform and turned round so that her back was against the timber of the guide, and she left his trousers where they were.

“You have about nine minutes,” said Nadia Ferrous, “and that is not because of your chest. Two men are coming back for the last of the crates at twenty to twelve and I cannot be standing in this when they get here.”

She got her belt undone with one hand behind her head, the way a woman who has been on her feet since six does it, and did not take it off and drop it, and then got hold of him through the front of what he was wearing and pulled, and looked down while she did it, at how hard he already was, and told him so.

“Nine years of men coming down four flights to a room with a table in it,” she said, “and you have never once come down to one with a woman in it who wanted something, and there is a reason for that and it is not a good one.”

She told him a number. She told it the way she tells the number of crates on a pallet, flat, at speed, as a fact about the world, and she told it to him and not to herself.

Then she got her back off the timber and stepped in and took him inside her in one movement with the toe of her boot hooked on the lowest rung of the guide, and her cunt was wet and had been since about the second he came off the stack of empties, and she said so out loud because she had never in her life not said a thing out loud.

“Do not be gentle,” she said. “I have had gentle. Gentle is what a man does when he has decided that a thing he is doing is going to cost him something later.”

## He Did The Work And She Kept Count

He was not gentle.

She put one arm up behind his head and one hand on the rail behind his own neck and took neither away for about a minute, and the chain swung about four inches in the corner every time either of them put weight on it, and nothing about the two of them was in a hurry.

He went back into her and she thanked him for it out loud, in the flat voice, the way a woman says a crate is in the right place.

“Again.”

She made him do it again, slower, and then a third time with her fingers dug into the back of his neck, and on the third one she put her weight onto that boot until the guide timber took it and the whole platform moved about an inch, and she said *hold* and he held, and then she said *go* and he went.

She came with her mouth open and her eyes shut and both her hands off the rail and into his collar, and she went on making that noise the shaft gave back, and when she was finished she put her face against the side of his neck for about four seconds and laughed, once, out of breath, with her mouth still open.

## Afterwards, On The Plate, With The Hatch Coming Down

She got herself off him and got her belt done up in one movement and took her place on the edge of the plate with her feet swinging over a store floor a yard and a half below.

He got his trousers up, and the plate under him was colder than the air standing on it, and the timber guide had been taking the weight of that store floor since before either of them was born.

Then he asked her why there. He had been holding it since the second street.

“Why here,” he said, “and not in that room with the clerk and the door open where you could have had it entered.”

## And What She Gave Him Instead Of A Rule

“No,” said Nadia Ferrous.

“That is not a reason.”

“That is the whole of it and I am not going to dress it up.” She turned round and put both heels off the edge and let them swing. “That room has a date at the head of every line in it and an hour against the date, and I cannot get either of those into this platform, and a sentence without either of them cannot go in that book however much anybody in that building wants it to.”

She turned round again.

“And you are not going to carry it up those stairs and put it in front of that clerk, because if you do that I will have handed you a thing that is in a book and I did not agree to that, and I will not be able to take it back out of it again.”

“You could have written it on something.”

“I could have written it on my own hand and been in a kitchen with it in about four days,” she said, “which is what you did with your own on the second of August, and you could not get it back, and I read about that in a corridor four days afterwards because four people in four feet of air had heard you say so.”

## Then She Went On, Which Is The Part He Was Not Ready For

“There is a row in that book of yours with a space in it,” she said, “and it has got the twenty-third of August of the ninth year at the head of it and an hour against the date and then a space, and it has been standing there a year and no hand has ever gone near it. I have known about it since the morning after you entered it and I have never told you that I know.”

“How long have you known.”

“Since the twenty-fourth of August of the ninth year, which is the morning after you put the date on it, and I have not said one word to you about it in about a year.”

## What She Said About The Space

“You put a question down and there was nothing to put under it,” she said, “and you have not filled it and you are not going to, and I want to tell you what that has got to do with a sheet of paper and then I am going to stop.”

He waited.

“A blank sheet is what you hand a person who cannot write a question down. It is not an answer and it is not a record and it goes in nothing.”

She got her cap off the plate and turned it over once.

“Your space is not a blank sheet. Your space is a blank sheet you have put in a book and carried about in a coat for a year, and the reason you are not going to fill it is not the reason I am talking about, and if you spend the next four years mixing the two up then I will have wasted a market end on you.”

He kept quiet on that platform for about four seconds.

“I am not going to fill it,” he said.

“Which is the reason.”

“Which is the reason that nothing has ever come off it yet,” she said, “and that it is the only piece of paper in that building that cannot be taken off you by anybody, including you, and that is worth more to you than the other twenty-five things on that table put together and you know that or you would have filled it in about a month ago.”

She put the cap down.

“Do not touch it. Not the row, not the space, not the sheet. That is the whole of what I have come down here to say and you have had it in about four minutes.”

## The Thing She Asked For, At The Top Of The Hatch

She got the chain and looked up at the black square of the hatch and then at him.

“If anybody at that market ever asks me a question about myself, I will come up four flights and I will stand in that corridor, and you will read out in that room whatever that office holds about me, once, with me in the room when it is read, and not one person afterwards is told who asked.”

“That is the rule from the tenth of June.”

“That is the rule from the tenth of June and I have had it in my head since the summer and I have never once been able to use it, because I am not somebody who asks you things.” She let the chain go and it swung about four inches and stopped. “You will do that and you will not write down that you have agreed to do it.”

“I will do it.”

“Good.” She got her cap off the plate and put it on. “Then it is a practice and not a form, and neither of us is ever going to find out whether we are still keeping it, and I have wanted one of those for about nine years and I have never had one with anybody.”

She pulled the chain and the platform came up the timber guide with the hatch coming down over the two of them, and she said the rest of it on the way up.

“And do not put it on the table either.”

*Three thousand four hundred and fifty-four days. Less three thousand four hundred and forty-eight, the twenty-ninth of this month, is six, and a woman who works a lift at a market end told a man what the twenty-sixth thing on a table in a room four floors up is for, on a platform stopped in the middle of its run, at about half past ten at night.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is sixty-five, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-five of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand and thirty-four days, which is thirty-four months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

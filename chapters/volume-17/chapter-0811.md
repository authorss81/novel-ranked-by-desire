# The Ninety-Fourth Form

Three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine was the thirtieth of March and the seventh of this month is day three thousand three hundred and six, and three thousand three hundred and six less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine is seven, and seven less one is seven by the month count, because a month in this city is thirty days and has no thirty-first and the thirtieth was the last working day of a quarter.

## A Board Against A Kerb

He went down to the foot of the hill at about ten past five because that is where he goes.

The month's form had gone up on that board that morning, on the seventh. A boy carried it up on one arm with a paste brush in his other fist and a corner of fresh paste on his cuff, set the thing down against the kerb, and squared it with the toe of his shoe in one movement without looking at it, the way a person does a thing he has done about a hundred times, and went back up the slope with the brush still in his hand.

There is one of those forms a month on that board and there has been since the first year. The ninety-fourth of them went up this morning.

It has come down on the thirtieth every month since and gone up again on the seventh, and there is no office in this city responsible for it and no pay in it for anyone.

He did not look at the two boxes printed at the foot of that form. He has never looked at them.

## Two Squares, And A Piece Of Paper Under The Batten

One of the boxes is for the figure of the thing at the address. The other is for the standing of it.

Both of them have stood empty since a clerk was handed a sheet, a form and no instruction at all on the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, and put two words into one of them because the box was there and the words were there. Nothing has been written in either of them since.

Not one person at that kerb has come at either of them in nine years and not one is going to, because filling a box is a thing a person does after being asked.

Under the lower batten, where the paste has gone thin, a piece of office paper is held flat against the wood. A date at the top of it in a clerk's hand. A pencil line drawn across the bottom of it in a hand of her own. Face down against the board, where a person would have to kneel on wet stone to guess that it was there at all.

It is where she left it on the sixteenth of December and it has not been folded, written on, turned over or taken since, and the batten is holding it in exactly the way it was holding it then.

## What Is Left Of The Slips In A Passage

Those had stopped. From the last working day of March an answer goes in a fold to one hand or it does not go at all, and a counter in a passage off Cordwain reads one thing fewer than he has been reading, and he does not know whether that is mercy and is not going to be told.

What stopped was not the thing itself, though.

Four of the eleven people at that rail in the last quarter of the year before wrote the line about any person out on pencil paper in their own hands in the ordinary voice, and folded it, and carried it out into the rain.

There are four pieces of pencil paper in this city with that sentence on them in four hands, and none of them came through a door, and about nine people a week heard it read out from behind a counter and wrote nothing at all because a person handed a sentence about somebody else does not walk up a lane with a pencil.

Four copies exist. Two thousand originals go out four times a year. A copy written down out of a man’s mouth in a passage cannot be set against an original that came out of a flat file, and there is nothing anywhere in this city that would let it, and the four people carrying them will go on carrying them.

He has been thinking about that for a month and has come to the end of it, and the end of it is that there is nothing in this city that can be asked about those four, and this office is not going to be the thing that asks.

## What Six Days Of A Practice Had Done

On the fourth of this month, in the morning, an answer went out of that room in a fold under the rule that was spoken out loud in that room on the last working day of March.

A runner took it.

That is the whole of what happened, and it took about eleven seconds, and there is no version of it in which anybody did anything wrong.

The officer who carries between the division building and the room off a third court was out on a district that morning. A runner does what a runner is for. There is a runner's book on the desk with an hour in it, and the runner's name went into that book against the hour and against the door.

The fold went down four flights and along about four hundred yards and into the one hand it was for. The person who asked took it. The runner came back up about an hour later and went out again with two other things.

And the book that the whole practice stands on is a book of deliveries with two columns in it — whose hand took it, and in what order — and there is no line in it for a runner, because a runner is not a carrier, and those columns were ruled on the sixth of December by a woman at a pigeonhole rack with a page of her own in front of her.

The two columns for that morning are empty.

They are empty in exactly the way the two columns are empty on a morning when nothing went out at all.

## He Went And Looked At The Runner's Book

He went down to the counters on the fifth at about eleven and asked to see it, which is a thing he may do because he works in a division and has not been asked to stop doing any of it.

It is a printed book with three columns and a rule down each side, and they are the same in every counter in that building, and the third column has the hour in it.

The runner's name. The hour. The door.

He stood at that counter for about a minute and understood that the thing he had gone down to look for was not in it and could not be.

That book says what a runner did. It does not say what a runner carried, and it has a place for the hour and no place for the hand, and the reason for that is a hundred and fifty years of practice and has nothing to do with him.

He thanked the man at the counter, who had not expected the visit and had nothing to add to it.

## What The Bound Book Said Anyway

Wenna Raut entered the row on the fourth at about twenty past four with the date at the head of it and the hour against the date, and she entered it in the words she is given.

What she was given was this office's own account of its own conduct.

It says the answer was carried by hand to the person who asked, and delivered into that person's hand at about half past eleven, and that the carrier wrote the hand and the order in her own book in her own hand.

All four of those things are true, and he had checked three of them himself and the fourth was told to him at a counter a fortnight later by a woman who was not talking about it.

The last of them is not true.

No carrier wrote anything, because there was no carrier, and the runner's own printed book has an hour in it and not one word about whose hand took anything.

## The Discovery, And It Is Worse Than A Mistake

He read that row on the seventh at about half past five and could not find one word in it to take back.

If the runner had written down that he had taken it, the row would be true. If the runner had refused to take it, there would have been a refusal in a book and a person could be told about a refusal.

What happened instead is that a practice which four people are keeping out loud in four rooms has no shape at all when the fourth person is not in the room, and what fills the space is a sentence the office wrote about itself.

That is not a lie. He went through it four times, standing up, with his hands behind him.

It is what a row is when there is nobody standing to check it, and this office has spent nine years refusing to put a sentence down that it could not show the arithmetic of.

## What He Decides, Standing Up

“No line,” he said.

The clerk kept the pen where it was.

“Say the rest of it,” said Wenna Raut.

“There is nothing to be put at the foot of that page and nothing is going to be put there, and what I have done this evening is find the reason for that, and it is a better reason than the one I gave on the thirtieth of March.”

“I will have that in the words you use.”

“Not in any book,” he said. “That row stands. It is true, and it is the only account of the morning of the fourth of this month that any person in this city can produce, and if there is anything under it then in about four years this office will hold two documents that disagree about one morning, and one of the two will be a document about itself.”

She put the pen across the book and squared it to the edge of the table.

“You have just decided that this office goes on saying things about itself that it cannot check,” she said, “and that neither of us will write down that it is doing that.”

“Yes.”

“And in about four years somebody is going to come up those four flights and ask whether an answer reached a hand on the fourth of April, and there is nothing in this city that will let me tell them, and I am going to have to say that it does not matter.”

“It does matter.”

“Then say that one,” said Wenna Raut, “because it is the only one of the two worth a clerk’s afternoon.”

He said it. She entered it, dated it, put the hour against the date and read it back once in the voice she reads everything in.

Nothing went under the foot of that page, and nothing ever will, because there is no line there for a person’s hands, and the sheet that goes up on the thirtieth of June will carry the same six lines it carried on the thirtieth of March.

He went up the hill at about ten to six and did the third floor that night from eleven until half past five, four washrooms and a bin room, the fourth of his four and then the first of the three off.

At about half past one he stopped for four minutes at the top of the stairs the way he does, and he thought, standing in the cold, that six days ago he had believed a practice was a thing that survived the absence of the person keeping it, and that the belief had cost him an hour of somebody else’s afternoon before he found out it was wrong.

*Three thousand three hundred and six days. Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seven, and the month’s form went up at the foot of the hill on the seventh with two printed boxes empty, and on the fourth of this month an answer went down four flights in a runner’s hand where a carrier had written nothing at all.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December of this year, is ninety-seven, and the thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is eight hundred and eighty-six days, which is twenty-nine months and sixteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

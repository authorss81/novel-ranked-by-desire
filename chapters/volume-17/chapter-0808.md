# The Queue Learns Patience

Three thousand two hundred and eighty was the eleventh of this month and the seventeenth is day three thousand two hundred and eighty-six, and three thousand two hundred and eighty-six less three thousand two hundred and eighty is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.

## Nine Where Four Stood A Month Ago

About four people had stood in that corridor a month ago.

There were about nine on the seventeenth, and the corridor outside that room is about four feet wide and runs about nine paces, and it has one door at the end of it and no window and no bench and nowhere to stand that is not also standing where about nine people can hear you.

It had been about four on the second of December and about eleven on the thirtieth of December and it had been about four all through the spring, and Lin Hao had made a point of noting that every time, because the spring queue was nine pieces of paper and this one is people.

The queue had also got slower.

That was the part that had made him sit up. A question could be taken in four minutes and it used to be. An answer had to be written first, dated, hours, folded, and then carried down four flights, and a person waiting to be told something they already half knew was about nine minutes of standing in a cold passage for four minutes of being spoken to.

## Wet Shoes On The Stair

He came up four flights at about half past ten with his coat over his arm because the room was warm with nine bodies in the corridor and he had done that in every winter of this office.

A queue is not a crowd. A crowd goes in and comes out. A queue is a set of people who have agreed to be here at the same time and in the same order, and there is nothing in this city that could tell the second kind from the first except a piece of paper, and the only paper in that corridor belongs to the people holding it.

He had stood in it in March and in April and in May when there were about four people, and he had enjoyed it, and he understood now that what he had been enjoying was that a person holding a piece of pencil paper does not talk.

He counted them twice going up, from the bottom, and there were nine on the landing above and four coming up behind him, and he did not say the number out loud in the corridor, because a clerk at that counter said everything that was said in it and he had learned that in March.

The shoes were the other thing. Four flights of stone with nine pairs of wet shoes on it, and the water coming back down the treads from the top floor, and about four of the nine had come in from a street with no room in it on them.

There was a stair for that. There was not one bit of that stair that was not also the way in and out of that room.

He thought about the nine feet of yard between that gate and the foot of the stair, and about how a person could stand in it for two hours in March and nobody would come out and ask them a single question, because there would be no question to ask.

It would be the kindest thing he could build. It would also be a queue in the open, which is the one thing that building has spent nine years not being, and he said so to Wenna Raut at about twenty past eleven and she said something about a rail being a public room and went back to her column, and he did not raise it again.

## What Was Said Out Loud Behind A Woman

At about eleven a woman in the second place in the line turned round and said, to the woman in front of her, at a volume that a four-foot corridor does not permit anybody to keep private:

“They told me in the autumn that nothing happened.”

“That is not what you said to me,” said the woman in front.

“I had a paper for it.”

“You did not have a paper for anything. You had a question and you got an answer and the answer was that this office holds one row with a date on it and that is what an answer is.”

Then the second woman turned to the clerk and said, loudly, as though putting a thing into the air were the same as putting it into a book:

“Write down that they told me nothing happened and I have been carrying that for six months.”

Wenna Raut looked up from the book. She did not stop the woman. She could not, because she was the mouth of that counter and what is said at that counter goes down in the words the person used, and a clerk may only enter what she is given.

She entered it. Date at the head, hour against the date, in the woman’s own words, and read it back once in the voice she reads everything in.

And then she read the autumn row to her, the whole of it, standing up, in that corridor, because that is what the line of the tenth of June says to do when a person brings a paper to that counter, and the paper was the thing the woman had just said she had.

The woman listened to it and looked at the floor.

“That is not nothing happened,” said Wenna Raut. “That is a row. They are different, and yours is entered under it, and it cannot be taken from, and it goes out four times a year like everything else in that book.”

## A Room With Answers In It Is A Public Room Twice Over

He had the understanding about eleven o’clock and he did not want it, and he worked it out in about nine seconds standing by the window with his coat over his arm.

A queue for papers is a quiet thing. People hold a sheet and they look at it, and they do not ask each other what is in it, because it is theirs.

A queue for answers is the opposite of that.

An answer is a sentence about somebody that the asker did not write and cannot check, and it is about to be spoken out loud in a room in which nine people are standing, and every one of those nine is about four feet from the mouth that speaks it.

They talk. They cannot help it. They say to each other what they have just been told, or what they were told in the spring, or what a man under a window read out of a page to them on a quarter-end, and they say it in the plain voice people use for a thing they are not sure about.

Not one person in that corridor is doing anything wrong. That was the discovery.

Every person in it is behaving exactly as a person behaves in a passage, which is to say they are talking about what they have just heard, at the volume people talk at, about nine inches apart, for ever.

And there is nothing he can do about that, and he went and measured the corridor twice, because he is a man who measures. Nine paces. A door at one end and a stair at the other and no window.

About nine people at four feet apart is a conversation with about four centimetres of weather in it, and every word of it goes into about nine pairs of ears.

So every answer given in that room was an answer given to about nine people.

That is not a rule anybody broke. It is what a room with a queue and a counter in it does, and this office had never had a queue, because for nine years the only person who could ask anything was a man who did not queue.

## Not One Person To Blame And Nothing To Withdraw

He stood in the middle of that room at about half past eleven and went through it properly, because there are only about four things in a life that are worth doing properly and he was rank one with assigned work and an hour.

Not one person in that corridor had been cruel. Every one of them had come up four flights in the cold on a working morning because an instruction with a man’s name at the foot of it told them they could. Not one had been refused. Not one had been entered against their will. Not one had been paid anything, or had asked to be.

There was nothing in the city to blame for any of it and nothing in that room to withdraw, and he understood with total clarity, standing there, that the office he had spent nine years building to be unaskable had become a room in which people say out loud in the open what nine other people heard, and that this was the widest he had ever opened anything, and that he had opened it about four inches wide on the second of December and everybody else had carried it.

## The Somebody He Brought

The last man up those four flights that morning had not come alone.

He had been at that counter in the autumn with a question about a paper, and he had been told what the office holds about him in that room, which was one row with a date on it and no figure and no name, and he had gone down the stair with it and thought about it every day since.

He had brought a woman with him. She had not been there on the day and had not been inside that building in her life.

He came to the wood and said, “I want to ask about a paper of mine and it is not urgent and it can wait.”

And the woman behind him said, “Then I will ask about yours.”

She came up to the wood on her own.

“You keep a book,” she said. “Is there anything in it about my husband.”

The corridor made a noise about nine feet away and there were nine people in it and every one of them heard it.

*Three thousand two hundred and eighty-six days. Less three thousand two hundred and eighty, the eleventh of this month, is six, and about nine people stood in a corridor about four feet wide where about four had stood a month ago, and a woman said out loud behind another woman that she had been told nothing happened and it was entered in her own words and read back to her in the passage.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and nine, the thirtieth of December of this year, is seventy-seven, and the thirty-sixth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is eight hundred and sixty-six days, which is twenty-eight months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Winter Queue Shortens

Three thousand five hundred and twenty-seven was the eighteenth of this month and the twenty-fifth is day three thousand five hundred and thirty-four, and three thousand five hundred and thirty-four less three thousand five hundred and twenty-seven is seven, and twenty-five less eighteen is seven by the month count.

## A Count She Will Not Make To The Day

On the twenty-fifth of this month he asked Wenna Raut how many people had come up the four flights in November.

She put her pen down.

“I can tell you it is about nine,” she said. “I can tell you it was about nine in February and about nine in the first month of the year before that, and I have said about nine in this room about nine times in nine years and I have never once been more precise than about, and I am not going to be more precise than about in the eleventh month either.”

“Give me the number.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

She turned her head about nine degrees, which she does.

“Because nine people a month is about eighty in a year and about nine hundred over the life of this office, and a count of the people who come up four flights is a count of the people who ask.”

She picked the pen up again.

“And we stopped entering what people ask on the seventeenth of this month and I am not going to help you start counting it by hand instead. A queue is people standing in a line so that they can be dealt with in an order. There has not been a line at the bottom of that stair in nine years and there was not one this morning, and what you are asking me for is a different thing and you know it is.”

## Two Of The Nine

“Then tell me about the two,” said Lin Hao.

“Which two.”

“The two who came up the four flights this month and went down them again without putting anything to this office.”

Wenna Raut did not answer for about eleven seconds.

“I have been waiting about nine minutes for you to ask me that,” she said, “and I was going to give it to you at the end of the month anyway, and the fact that you asked me for it is not a thing to build on.”

She put the pen into the tray.

“There were two. One of them is the woman who came up on the nineteenth and stood in that corridor for about four minutes and then went down again. The other one came up on the twenty-second at about two in the afternoon and stood at the bottom of the stair for about nine seconds and went down.”

“What did she say to you.”

“She said nothing at all to me,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the part you have not asked about. Neither of them said a word to me or to anybody else in this building. They came up four flights in the cold and they did not put anything to this office, and there is no row for either of them and there is not going to be one.”

## The Nineteenth, At About Four In The Afternoon

“The nineteenth,” he said.

“The nineteenth.”

He had come up those four flights on the evening of the eighteenth at about four in the afternoon with his hands washed in a trough at the back of a laundry and about four people in that corridor, and he had said, out loud, standing in the doorway with the door open, that he had gone down a hill that morning and put three pieces of paper into a stove.

He had said it because a refusal travels and an answer does not travel as far, and he had wanted it to travel before somebody made it travel for him.

“She came up on the nineteenth,” he said, “and she stood in that corridor for about four minutes, and I was in that room with the door open the whole of those four minutes.”

“I know you were.”

“I did not know there was anybody in that corridor.”

“You did not look,” said Wenna Raut. “You have not looked into that corridor since the sixth of July, when I asked you to say a thing out loud in this room so that it would be in more than two heads, and you said it, and about four people heard you, and you did not look at any of them then either.”

He did not say anything for about nine seconds.

“She was stood in that corridor for four minutes because of me.”

“I do not know that and neither do you,” said Wenna Raut. “You have been in that room with the door open every working morning since the eighteenth of this month and you have said about nine things out loud at that doorway, and if a person came up those stairs on any one of those mornings and did not ask anything then that would be the same four minutes, and you would not have noticed it, and you did not notice it on the nineteenth.”

## The Twenty-Second, At About Two In The Afternoon

“And the other one.”

“The twenty-second at about two in the afternoon,” said Wenna Raut. “There is a man on the half landing between the second and third floors who has been on that landing at about two o’clock every working afternoon for about three years and has never once come up the last flight, and he is paid nothing by this office and this office did not put him there and cannot move him.”

“Is that all of it.”

“That is the whole of it and it is the reason I am telling you, because it is the only one of the two that has nothing whatever to do with this office or with any office or with anything anybody in this city has done since the second of December.”

“A man on a landing.”

“A man on a landing, in a building with a stair that turns twice and a corridor about four feet wide, in the eleventh month,” said Wenna Raut. “Anybody in this city who wants to ask this office anything after the second of April will come up that stair and find a man sitting on the half landing at the place where the stair turns, and will go back down again.”

“And not one of them will have said why to anybody, not even to themselves, and nothing in this city can find out why and nothing ever will.”

## The Half Landing Between The Second And Third Floors

He went down to it at about two in the afternoon, after she had finished, and he did not go to the room and he did not go to the corner of the ground floor where a bin stands and he went up one flight from that floor and stopped.

The stair turns twice and the half landing is about five feet by nine and there is no window on it. There is a man on it with his back against the wall and his knees up and the heels of his hands in his lap, and he has been there at about this hour on every working afternoon for about three years.

He is not on any rota in this city that anybody in this office has ever seen. He is not paid by this office and he is not paid by any office in this city, and the clerk in the ground-floor corner says he was in the yard the year before the office opened and has never given a reason and has never asked for anything.

He did not say anything to the man and the man did not say anything to him and they were on that landing for about four minutes together.

Then he went back up the one flight to the room and shut the door.

“Were you there for long,” said Wenna Raut.

“About four minutes.”

“Did you ask him anything.”

“No.”

“Will you.”

“No,” said Lin Hao, “and I am not going to stand at the top of that stair in the morning either, and I am not going to have anybody do it.”

“And I want it written down nowhere that I have said that, because the day a man in a white plate watches who comes up his own stairs is the day there is a list of askers, and I have spent about nine years refusing to make one and I am not going to make one out of a landing and a cold morning.”

“You have not told me what that man is doing there.”

“He is sitting on a landing,” said Lin Hao. “He has been for about three years. He has not asked anybody anything and he has not answered anybody and he has not cost this office a minute of anything, and if I went up there every afternoon to find out what he is for then he would become a person this office holds something about, and that is all it takes.”

## What The Number Did

“About nine came up in February,” he said, “and about nine have come up this month.”

“Yes.”

“That is the best piece of news this office has had since the second of December and it is not evidence of anything.”

“It is not evidence of anything and you are right to be suspicious of it,” said Wenna Raut. “You have spent about fourteen months finding out that every good number in this city turns out to be two numbers, and I am not going to sit here and hand you one.”

She squared the standing sheet on the shelf behind the door and did not mention the two again.

That afternoon there was about four people at that rail in the cold and the counterman read the six lines of the foot of that page to them at the same speed as he reads everything else, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for fifty-five days and the head of it has not moved.

“In about four years somebody is going to want to know whether those two were put off by this office,” said Lin Hao.

“And you will have one thing to give them,” said Wenna Raut, “which is a day and an hour and a clerk who has been told not to remember it, and it will be the true answer and it will not be worth anything to the person asking, and nothing else in this city they could be given instead.”

*Three thousand five hundred and thirty-four days. Less three thousand five hundred and twenty-seven, the eighteenth of this month, is seven, and about nine people came up four flights in this month as about nine came up four flights in February, and two of the nine went back down without saying a word.*

*Less three thousand four hundred and seventy-nine, the thirtieth of September, is fifty-five, and the thirty-ninth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-five of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is one thousand one hundred and fourteen days, which is thirty-seven months and four days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

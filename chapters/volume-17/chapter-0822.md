# The Man Who Wrote It In The Rain

Three thousand three hundred and seventy-six was the seventeenth of this month and the twenty-fourth is day three thousand three hundred and eighty-three, and three thousand three hundred and eighty-three less three thousand three hundred and seventy-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## The Fourth Of The Eleven

The man had a square of pencil paper in his hand when he came up the four flights, and he had been holding it in the hand he was not using to take the rail, which is a thing people do with a thing they have decided to have.

He was the fourth of eleven at a rail in a passage off Cordwain on an evening in the last quarter of the year before. He wrote the December line out in his own hand on his own paper, in the rain, while a man who is paid nothing read the page to whoever stood there.

He had it out on the sill of the window and he had four words on it, and he said, before he had taken the chair, that he had come to find out whether those four words were right.

“You brought a paper.”

“I brought a paper.”

“Then it is about your own paper and it goes to you in a fold with the flap shut and the date and the hour on the outside and nothing else, and I will read to you what I am about to say four times and then you will decide.”

## What Four Things Could Do And None Of Them Would Do

“There are four things in this city that could tell you whether those four words are right and I am going to name all four and I am not going to improve one of them.”

“The first is the page itself. It is in a flat file four floors down and about four hundred yards off and it goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and it is not going to come up four flights to this table.”

“The second is the woman who wrote the foot of it out in her own hand, and that is one copy in the world, and it is in the inside of a man’s coat on the left, and it may not be produced.”

She said the third one to him.

“It is me. I am the third thing. I have kept that book in this room since the second of December and I may only enter what I am given, and not one clerk in this building has ever given me a copy of that page and not one ever is going to.”

“And the fourth.”

“The fourth is a man who is paid nothing who reads it out to about nine people a week and who has read it out for nine years and who has never once been asked whether he read it right.”

The man in the white plate was standing at the end of the table with his hands behind his back and did not help.

“So this office cannot tell you.”

“This office cannot tell you,” said Wenna Raut.

## What He Wanted It To Be

He said the thing he wanted in about eleven seconds, the way people do, and she let him get all of it out.

“I have read those four words to two people in two kitchens,” the man said. “One of them wrote them out again. So there are two sheets of paper in this city in two hands with the same four words on them, and I do not know whether they are the same four words, and if they are not then one of the two people has told somebody in a kitchen something this office did not say.”

“That is the whole of what you are asking.”

“That is the whole of what I am asking.”

“And I am going to tell you what it is, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to soften it.”

## What A Copy Is And What It Is Not

“A copy is a thing a person did on purpose with their own hand,” she said. “That is all it is, and it is worth having, and it is not nothing.”

She put two fingers on the paper without touching it.

“You stood at that rail and a man read four sentences and you put a pencil to a piece of paper because you wanted to go home with it. That is the whole of what happened.”

“Then what is it a record of.”

“It is a record of him,” said Wenna Raut. “He read it the way he reads everything, at the same speed, to whoever was standing there. Nine years of it, and he does not know what any of it is for, and neither do you.”

She turned the paper a quarter round so that the four words faced him instead of her.

“You have got a man’s reading of a page, on the night, in the rain. If he read it wrong then he read it wrong nine hundred times, and you have that on a piece of paper and no way of finding out.”

She looked at the four words.

“And about four hundred pieces of pencil paper went up four flights in this city and came down them again, and not one of them came through a door. It had about four hundred and thirty rows in it on the first of February of the year before and it has had rows added to it since, every one of them a mouth with a day against it. I have not counted them and I am not going to.”

“And the two numbers are the same size.”

“The two numbers have nothing to do with each other and nothing in this city can compare them. That is not a tragedy. It is the arrangement. A copy is not a smaller line. It is a different thing, and about four hundred of them are on your side of a wall that this office has never once been able to see over.”

## What She Did With Four Words Out Loud

She read them back to him. All four of them, in the voice she reads everything in, because that is what she does with anything a person says in that room, and the door was open because the door is open.

About four people in a corridor about four feet wide heard four words of a page being read to a stranger in the voice of a clerk.

She got about nine words further than the middle of the second one before she stopped.

“No,” she said.

She put her hand over the four words on the sill and took it off again at once, and that was the whole of it, and neither of them said one word about it afterwards.

“If I read those four words in this room then they are a sentence in that corridor,” said Wenna Raut, “and you have been carrying a piece of paper for about ten months and I have just taken the paper away from you, because a sentence in a corridor is not carried by anybody. It stands in about nine people and it is theirs.”

“Can you un-say it.”

“No,” she said, “and you will notice that I have not written anything down, because there is nothing to write down. There is a row with a question in it and a hole under it where an answer would go, and I am not going to enter your four words in there, because they are not an answer and they are not a question and they are about a person who is not in this room.”

She pushed the paper back across the sill towards him.

“Take it out of here. Read it to a living soul on this floor.”

## What He Offered, And What Could Not Be Taken

“Then take it,” he said. “I will leave it here. It is only paper.”

“No.”

“It is a copy of your page and it may not be cited and you would never use it. Take it and it is in the building and it is a piece of paper and nothing in this city can ask me about it after this morning.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am going to tell you the true reason and it is not caution and it is not your good manners.”

She drew the book towards her by its edge and left her forearm lying across it.

“If this office takes one piece of pencil paper made by a member of the public then it has begun keeping copies, and the first one was taken on the twenty-fourth of this month from a man who was told it was worth nothing, and in about four years there will be a drawer. And a drawer in this building is a list. That is the whole of the reason and I have not improved it.”

“So it stays with me.”

“It stays with you, and it is yours, and it is not a record of anything, and in about four years you will be dead and so will everybody in the two kitchens and it will be a piece of paper in a house with nothing on it.”

He put it in his pocket.

He was at the door before he turned round.

“When does the next one go up.”

“The thirtieth of September.”

“On the rail.”

“On the rail,” said Wenna Raut, “face up, the same sheet, the same head, the same four sentences, and about nine people will be standing there and none of them will be asked anything.”

## What He Said He Would Do

He came back up the four flights again about four minutes later, which was not long enough to have gone down a lane and come back and he had not gone down a lane.

“There is one thing you can do,” he said, “and it is not a promise to me and it is not a row and it is not going in anything.”

“Tell me what it is.”

“The thirtieth of September,” he said. “That page goes up at four and it is face up on that rail and I am allowed to stand at it and read it with my own eyes, the same as anybody. And I am going to stand there and read the foot of it and compare it to the four words on this, and if mine are wrong then mine are wrong and I will know by myself, and not one person in that building will ever have to tell me.”

She picked the pen up and then put it down again without writing.

“That is the first sensible thing anybody has said at that counter since the second of December,” said Wenna Raut, “and you are not going to tell me what you find out, because if you tell me then you have told me and I will have wanted to know.”

“I will not tell you what I find out.”

“You will tell me whether you were right.”

“I will not tell you that either.”

*Three thousand three hundred and eighty-three days. Less three thousand three hundred and seventy-six, the seventeenth of this month, is seven, and a man carried a piece of pencil paper up four flights to ask whether four words in his own hand were right, and could not be told, and is going to stand at a rail on the thirtieth of September and read a page with his own eyes.*

*Less three thousand two hundred and ninety-nine, the thirtieth of March, is eighty-four, and the thirty-seventh sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them, and it comes down on the thirtieth of this month.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and sixty-three days, which is thirty-two months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Month Clock Holds

Three thousand four hundred and two was the thirteenth of this month and the nineteenth is day three thousand four hundred and eight, and three thousand four hundred and eight less three thousand four hundred and two is six, and nineteen less thirteen is six by the month count.

## The Slate Was Face Up

Two streets off Cordwain there is a lane that has no name anybody uses. It is about nine feet wide, with nine stools on one side and nine districts behind the other, and a man at the head of it with a slate under his arm.

Emil Brack had the slate face up.

He turns it face down on a morning when somebody has been down that lane inside four days, because a man who is paid nothing does not like to be seen keeping a count while he is talking to a man in a white plate.

On every other morning in nine years it has been face up, and on the nineteenth of this month it was face up, and it had been face up for four days, and the lane was empty on all four of the days.

He had nine figures before eleven. There were about four figures at eleven on the nineteenth of this month and then nine at a quarter past it, and about four days is not a rhythm, and a lane with nine stools in it has a rhythm the way a clock has a tick.

“Not one person has been down this lane in four days,” said Brack. “I am going to say that first because you have come down it and I have had the slate face up for four days and I want you to know what I was doing with it.”

## The Month In His Own Mouth

“Now the month. It began again at nothing on the first and I did the whole sum over and I am giving you what I have, in my own mouth, and you are not going to write it down.”

He did not turn the slate over.

“Nine districts. Nine stools. Four kitchens by the end of the first week and about nine by the thirtieth, the same two every month, and I have said them both to you before and I am saying them again because the month is mine and I give it when it is given.”

He wiped his hands down his coat.

“And the standing cost, which I have given you once in this city and I am not going to charge you for again. Nine people a month who had one place in this city where they could put down what had been done to them, and have stopped putting it down, and are going to keep not putting it down. That is the whole of what a month costs and it is charged to none of them, which is the ordinary way of a price.”

“You have said it once.”

“I said it once, on the thirteenth of July of the year before, and you have been carrying it about for a year as though it were news, and it is not news. It is a month. There is one of them every month and it does not improve and it does not get worse, and I have never once seen a month come in under its weight and that is the only good news I have ever had about any of it.”

## About Sixty People And Not One Of The Nine

“Sit down.”

“I have nine figures before eleven.”

“Stand up, then, because this part is about you and it is the fourth time I have had to say a number at you and it is the first one that has come out wrong.” He put the slate against his hip and did not turn it. “You have had about nine questions a month through that door for about seven months. Call it sixty people. Call it sixty mouths with a day against every one of them.”

“Yes.”

“Now call the nine a month for the same seven months and tell me how many of the sixty are one of the nine.”

“Not one of them.”

“Not one of them,” said Brack, “and I have checked, and I am going to tell you how I checked, because the checking is the whole of what I have got and it is worth nothing.”

“I count faces. I have counted faces at that lane for nine years and I have never once written a name down anywhere. I could not tell you this morning which of the sixty has been at your four flights, and you could go up those stairs and look at every row and you would not be able to tell me either.”

“So neither of us knows.”

“Neither of us knows and we have each got half of it and the two halves do not meet. You have got sixty people who asked. I have got nine a month who stopped. And there is no instrument in this city that can put a face at the head of your stair next to a face at the head of this lane.”

## Four Days, And The Thing It Does To The Other Clock

He said the other one himself, without being asked, and it was the same sentence he has been saying at that lane for about a year, and he said it flatly, and then he added nine seconds that were not in the old version.

“In about four years the space under the mantel in about four hundred kitchens will be empty, and about forty people will say where it came from, and about four hundred will believe them, and not one of the four hundred will ever be told why it went quiet.”

He turned the slate over, wrote one figure on it, held it out, and wiped it out with his sleeve.

“That is four years and it does not start today and nothing you do in the next four years of it moves it. It is not a clock that you wind. It is a thing that happens because about four hundred people get quiet in their own time and in the same weeks, and every one of them is doing it alone and none of them can see the other three hundred and ninety-nine.”

“Then what has four days done.”

“Nothing has happened,” said Brack, “and that is what frightens me. For nine years that lane has had a pulse in it. Somebody comes down it and there is no telling why.”

“Four days of nothing is either the best thing that has ever happened to me or the fourth year arriving early, and I have got nine figures a morning and not one of them tells me which. I am not going to stand here and tell a man in a white plate that I cannot tell him, because I have told him something every month for nine years.”

## The Thing He Worked Out On A Pavement In Nine Minutes

He worked it out outside a market end that is shut on the nineteenth of a month and he worked it out standing up in about nine minutes and his chest was in its usual condition by the fourth of them.

The only way to put a face at the head of that stair next to a face at the head of that lane is for one person to be at both, and to keep a way of telling which face he has already seen.

There are about nine figures a morning in the lane and about nine questions a month in the room. The two numbers are the same size and the two sets have never once been made to overlap by an instrument of any kind.

And there is exactly one person in this city who walks both distances. He has walked both of them about nine times in four years and has taken nothing out of either in his hand.

The reason he has not was given to a stairwell on the third of June of the year before and it has not moved since. A man who keeps a tally of faces between two places is a man who has a list, and a man with that list is a man who has been to about four hundred doors.

He had gone up four flights in June and had read two days off the top of nine sheets from a doorway and had cost a woman in a laundry the only thing she had been standing in that room on for nine years.

He was not going to do it again in July.

## What He Wanted And Was Not Going To Be Given

“You came down here for a number about the nine.”

“I came down here to ask you whether any of the sixty is one of the nine.”

“You asked me and I have answered and you know more now than you did and it is no use to you.” Brack got the slate against his ribs. “Here is the thing I want and you will not like it and I am not being clever.”

“Go on.”

“The practice from the thirtieth of March,” said Brack. “A man goes down four flights with a fold and it stops at one door and the rest of this city never finds out whose it was. That is the only thing I have ever seen in nine years at that lane that made a person put something down again, and I have seen it twice, and both of the times were because the fold reached one hand and not one person saw whose it was.”

“If it stops.”

“If it stops then the month goes back to what it was in the ninth year, and it goes back without me, and the four years come back with it, and I will be standing at the head of this lane telling people a thing that has stopped being true, and there is no correction available to me in this city, because I have not said it to a living soul.”

He put the slate down on the stool beside him, which he has not done in nine years in front of anybody.

“I am not asking you to make it true. I am telling you that I have said what I am going to say whether it is true or not, in about three years, and I am not going to be asked to stop, and if you came down here in three years to tell me it had stopped I would say thank you and go on saying it.”

He picked it up again.

“You will not come down here in three years. I have watched you for nine years and you do not come down here to stop anything.”

*Three thousand four hundred and eight days. Less three thousand four hundred and two, the thirteenth of this month, is six, and a man gave a month out in his own mouth at the head of a lane and said the standing cost of a month in words.*

*And he said that not one of about sixty people who have come up four flights in seven months is one of the nine a month who stopped, and that a lane which has had a pulse in it for nine years has had four days of nothing in it and he cannot say why.*

*And a man stood on a pavement outside a market end that is shut for about nine minutes with his chest in a bad way and worked out that the only person in this city who could put a face at the head of one stair next to a face at the head of another lane is himself, and that he is not going to.*

*Less three thousand three hundred and eighty-nine, the thirtieth of June, is nineteen, and the thirty-eighth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last nineteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is nine hundred and eighty-eight days, which is thirty-two months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

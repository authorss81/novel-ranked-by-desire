# A Sheet Carried For A Year

Three thousand and twenty-nine was the thirtieth of June and the sixth of July is day three thousand and thirty-five, and three thousand and thirty-five less three thousand and twenty-nine is six, and six off the thirtieth, which is the last working day of that month, is the sixth of July.

## A Man With A Sheet He Has Not Opened

He came up the stair on the sixth of July with a piece of paper folded in four and it had been folded and unfolded and folded again so many times that the folds had gone through in three places and come soft.

It is about the size of the foot of a printed page and it is in pencil, in his own hand, with a date at the top of it in his own hand, and it is a copy of the bottom of a sheet he saw face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain about a year ago.

He has carried it up and down a street about eleven times a month for about a year. He has never once opened it in front of anybody, and when Lin Hao asked him about that on the second landing he gave the answer in four words and did not improve it.

“Because there is nothing in it I could use.”

## Why A Man Carries A Page For A Year

There are about four hundred people in this city who have carried a piece of pencil paper up four flights to a bench in a laundry off a street two streets wide and carried it on again, and about four hundred people who have stood at a kerb at the foot of a hill and got down low enough to write nine words in the bottom two inches of a thing they made themselves, and the two of those numbers are not the same number and are not anybody’s business.

A man carries a sheet for a year because he has heard a thing about it and not about himself, and because the hearing costs nothing and the carrying costs him a walk.

What he had heard was that a building puts people’s names on a sheet.

That has been going round since the third of December, and a man with a cap in a passage said it out loud in front of eleven people on the thirtieth of March, and about two hundred people in this city believe it, and most of them believe it the way they believe weather.

He believed it. He had the copy. He had carried it for about a year without knowing what was on it, because a sheet that cannot be used can still be carried.

## He Opens It At The Counter

“May I see what this office holds about you,” said Wenna Raut.

He put it on the wood and did not open it.

“There is nothing on it about me.”

“There is one row in that book with a date against it and an hour against the date,” she said, “and it is the seventeenth of June, and you brought a piece of paper through that door on it, and that is what this office holds about you. I may not read you anything else, and I am telling you what the row is before I tell you what is on the paper.”

Then she said the other half of it out loud before he asked her for it, which is what she does.

“Now open it. You have carried that thing for about a year because you thought it was about you, and there are about four hundred people in this city doing the same arithmetic with a different piece of paper, and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic for all of them, and it comes out the same way.”

He opened it on the corner of the counter, on the wood, and the four sentences at the foot of a quarterly page were in his own pencil in his own hand, and he read them standing up, and it took him about nine seconds.

He read them twice and the second time he read them out loud, about nine feet of dressed stone in four sentences, in a corridor about four feet wide, in the voice of a man who has never read them aloud before and does not know how a person is supposed to do it.

About four people behind him heard it. Not one of them wrote anything down.

## What Four Sentences Do Not Say

The fourth of the four sentences says that a building off a third court has for nine years kept a name at the head of a line on a page that goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters.

His name is not on that page.

There is no version of that page on which his name could be, and there has never been, and the fourth sentence is the one that got him, and he had been carrying it about a year in his coat believing it had his name on it somewhere further down.

“That is about four hundred people,” he said. “It is not about me.”

“It is not about you,” said Wenna Raut, “and you have just carried that piece of paper up four flights and across a courtyard to be told so, and it has cost you about a year of walking up and down a street to be told so, and there is no instrument in this city that could have told you so any sooner.”

## Two More Lines Than He Had

“Does it still say that.”

“It says more.”

She turned the bound Register a quarter on its own axis so the rows were the right way up for a man standing at the end of the table, which she does about four times a year, and she did not open the standing sheet, which is under the window and does not come out of the flat file until about half past two on the last working day of a quarter.

Instead she read him the bottom of the page from memory, which she can do, and it took her about forty seconds.

The four sentences are the same four. The six lines under them are the same six and the fourth of them is struck from the first letter to the last with a name under the striking.

And under the four sentences and above the striking, in this office’s hand, with a man’s name under each, there are now two lines that were not there when he made his copy at that rail.

The first is from the thirtieth of March and says that when a person says a thing at that counter this office writes it down in the words that person used, with a date and an hour.

The second is from the tenth of June. It is the shorter of the two and it is the one about the counter, and it is nine words longer in the original than it sounds when a clerk says it, and it ends with a clerk promising not to make a thing better.

“Those are eleven weeks old and four days old,” she said, “and yours is about a year old, and there is no form anywhere in this city on which a copy is recalled from anybody it has been copied into.”

## About Nine Of Them A Week

He put his fingers on the edge of the counter and then took them off it, which is what a man does when he has understood something and has not got a place to put it.

“How many people have come here since the tenth of June for the reason you have just given me.”

She had it, because she has counted every day of the instruction of the tenth of March since it went into that book, in a clerk’s hand, at that counter, and because she counted it before he asked and had been waiting about four seconds for somebody to ask her.

“About nine a week,” said Wenna Raut. “It has been between four and nine and it has been nine for four of the last eleven days, and it is a count of people at this counter and not of papers and not of rows and not of claims.”

“And what do you tell them.”

“The same thing I have just told you.”

“All of them the same.”

“All of them the same thing, and the same thing is about four hundred people and a page that goes out four times a year, and there is not one line on that page in which any one of those nine is named, and not one line in that book in which any one of those nine is named except in their own row on the day they came up.”

He put the sheet back into his coat, folded, in four, on the left, the way it had been for about a year.

He said the thing he had walked down four flights to say, and he said it to the door and not to the room.

“I have carried that for about a year because I thought it was about me.”

“It is not about you.”

“No,” he said. “That is what I have come down four flights to hear and I have heard it and I am going to carry it anyway, because it was true before I could read it and it is true now, and there is not one fact in that piece of paper that I did not have before the eighth of July.”

The door at the bottom of that stair comes off its latch about nine times a week and he heard it go about four seconds after he said it.

## What Happens To The Ones Where It Is Wrong

Wenna Raut squared the book to the edge of the table with both hands.

“Then the wrong one is in this office’s hand and it is on a page and the person it is about does not know that there is a page,” she said. “And she is not going to be told by anybody in this building, because this office does not go out of this building, and she will hear it from somebody else in about four weeks from a person who was in a corridor and believed she was helping.”

She put the pen across the book.

“And in about four years there will be about nine of those every week, and about four hundred people will have been told a thing about themselves that this office wrote down because they said it out loud in a room, and not one of the four hundred will have been able to answer a word of it.”

*Three thousand and thirty-five days. Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is six, and a man opened a sheet he had carried for about a year at a counter and found that the fourth of four sentences was not about him, and that the page now carries two more lines at the bottom of it than it carried when he made his copy at a rail.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is six, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and fifteen days, which is twenty months and fifteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

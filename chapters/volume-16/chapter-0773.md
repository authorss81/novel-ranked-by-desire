# The Price Of A Favour

Three thousand and twenty-two was the twenty-third of June and the thirtieth is day three thousand and twenty-nine, and three thousand and twenty-nine less three thousand and twenty-two is seven, and thirty less twenty-three is seven by the month count.

## About Half Past Two, And A Sheet Squared Twice

The thirty-fourth sheet went onto the table under the window at about half past two and Wenna Raut squared it against the wood in about three seconds, and then squared it again about four minutes later.

She did that once in nine years on the thirtieth of March and she has done it at every quarter-end since, and neither of them has mentioned it, and it is now simply what she does at about three o’clock on the last working day of a quarter.

About four hundred and nineteen lines stand under the head, each with a date against it and an hour against the date and no figure and no name.

Under those, the second column, with four lines in it under two printed words that say **carried by**.

Under that, four sentences at the foot.

Under those, six lines in this office’s hand, the fourth of them struck from the first letter to the last, and a name set under the striking, and two inches of nothing below that.

And under the four sentences and above the striking and in no column anywhere on that sheet, two lines that are new since the year began, the first from the thirtieth of March and the second from the tenth of June, and a man’s name under each of them.

## The Figure At The Head Of It

Eight hundred and fifty-two.

It has held that value since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and has not shifted once since, and not one person in this city has the standing to shift it, and there never has been one.

It is unmoved across eighteen of these sheets, counting the eighteenth onward as the eighteen sheets that have carried that number since it was put there, and this is the thirty-fourth.

It is unmoved across seventeen quarters, and that is a different count and it is also right, because a quarter here is counted as a quarter-end later than that thirtieth of March and one of those has fallen every ninety days since. Seventeen of them is one thousand five hundred and thirty days, and this afternoon is one thousand five hundred and thirty days after that March.

The two figures disagree by one, and a reader at a rail is going to be handed both of them, so the page has to say which one it is doing.

## Four O’Clock, And A Rail In A Passage

She carried it down the two floors herself at about ten to four, because she has never let anybody else take one down that stair, and laid it face up on the rail at four. It goes out at four on the last working day of a quarter and has done since the first year of the office.

The sheet that was on that rail until this afternoon came down off it in her hands at about five to four and went into a drawer at the back, which is not the drawer in the wall on the left of that table and never has been.

There were eleven at the rail at four minutes past four, which is what stands there at four minutes past four at every quarter-end this office has ever had, and has been for nine years.

Under the window there is a desk and a man at it who is paid nothing, and he went down that sheet from the second line of it to the bottom in the voice and at the pace he keeps for everything on a page, and he did not leave anything out.

Four sentences. Then the six lines under them, all six, straight past the striking and the name under the striking without any pause at all. Then the four lines of the column, the last of them given the way a thing under that heading is given, because that is what is under it.

And then the two new ones, and the second of those is the tenth of June, and it is the shorter of the two. A person brings a paper to that counter, and this office is to tell that person, in that room, what this office holds about them, and nothing else, and not to improve it.

He gave it in the ordinary way. Not one person in this city is entitled to tell him what a line under a sentence is for, and he is not going to be told, and he cannot be asked to read less of it.

A woman on the end of the rail asked him what the last of those two lines was for.

“That is what it says,” said the counterman.

“It does not say what it is for.”

“No,” he said. “It does not, and not one person in this city is entitled to tell me what it is for, and in about four years there will not be one either.”

The eleven went back up the lane. He squared the sheet against the edge of the rail with his thumb and did not say anything else about it.

## A Guild House On Cordwain, And A Strongroom Under It

He went down Cordwain at about ten past six with a question he had carried up two flights and down two corridors since the eighth of February.

The guild house has a passage four feet wide and a door at the back of it with a bar across it, and behind that door a strongroom with a shelf of iron-strapped boxes on it and about four hundred ounces of wax in a pan on the corner, and the whole of that floor has been cold since a fire in it in the fourth year.

The woman who brokers for four guilds in this city keeps nothing up those steps that may be read out to anybody, and the room she receives a person in is one floor above the bar.

He went up the stairs. The door does not shut. There is a deal table with the wood standing up out of it in long pale ridges and ink dried in the grooves, and two rush-seated chairs, one of which has been in that room since it was built, and a fire-grate with about four inches of ash in it and nothing burning in it since May, and a shutter up four inches on the window with the street coming in under it.

She had the second chair pulled out from the table before he got to the top of the last stair.

“Sit down,” said Marguerite Vale. “You are rank one and your chest has been spent since the fourth year and you have no permission of any kind, and you are here on the last working day of a quarter, and I am going to answer the question before you ask it, because you have got it in the wrong order and I have listened to you put things in the wrong order for four years.”

## What A Thing With Nothing Behind It Is Worth

“Ask it.”

He asked it.

“What is a thing done for a person with nothing written behind it worth.”

She did not answer for about nine seconds.

Here is what that guild house does, and it has always done it, and it has never once been written down in any of its books.

A thing done for a person with nothing written behind it may not be entered anywhere. Not in a real book, not in a day book, not in a copy of a foot of a printed page, not in a note on the back of a hand.

The moment it is entered it is a service.

And a service may be called in.

A debt cannot be called in, because a debt is a figure and a figure can be disputed by the person who owes it. A favour cannot be called in, because the moment a thing is entered it is a service, and a service is a thing somebody can stand in front of you and ask to be repaid out loud, in front of nine people, in a room with no door onto a street.

“I have been doing it that way for twenty-nine years,” said Marguerite Vale, “and I have never once been able to write one of them down, and I have stopped apologising for it to myself about nine years ago.”

## Nine Seconds She Keeps

“Nothing,” she said.

That is the price. That is the whole of the price and she said it in about nine seconds.

“It is worth nothing. It cannot be given, because there is no hand to give it into that would not make it a service the moment it landed. It cannot be entered, because the moment it is entered it is a service. And it cannot be called in, because it does not exist in any book that anybody could open, and there is nothing to be called in.”

She stopped there.

He waited for the rest of it and she did not say it, and about nine seconds went past in that room with the shutter up four inches and the street coming in under it, and she looked at the ash in the grate instead of at him, and that was the price and that was all of the price and she kept the rest of what she had.

Then she got up and put her hand on the back of the deal table.

“Twenty-nine years,” she said, “and the last twelve of them I have wanted a form.”

## The Last Twelve Years

She said it in the voice she uses over a counter, which is a voice about four people at once and not a voice at all about one.

“On the eighth of October of the first year somebody built an office in the only building in this city that could not be reached, and not one person could find it, and that was considered the correct arrangement, and it was correct, and it held for nine years. It is the reason about four hundred people carried a piece of pencil paper up four flights to a bench instead of handing it to an officer.”

“Yes.”

“In the last three months that office has put two lines in its own hand at the foot of a page that goes to about two thousand counters four times a year,” said Marguerite Vale, “and the second of them says that a person may be told what that office holds about them.”

“And in the last six weeks a man with a cap has read the bottom of that page out loud in a passage off Cordwain to about nine people a week. And about nine people a month have come up a stair to a counter and asked to be told something, and they have been told something.”

She came round the table.

“That is the thing I have wanted since before the Vant bought my books, and I am not going to say the rest of it in this room this evening because you are rank one and you have no standing to be told what I think about it.”

Then she pulled her collar back off her neck with two fingers and did not go near the chair.

“Now come here properly,” she said. “You have been sitting in that chair for about nine minutes in a coat.”

## The Room With The Shutter Up Four Inches

She was quick about it and she was not slow either, and she had decided before she came down the stairs and she did not propose it out loud.

She got her own fingers under the front of his shirt and opened it and pushed it back off him without taking it off, the way a person handles a thing that is in the way of work, and then she turned his face with the heel of her palm and looked at him for about two seconds in a way that had nothing to do with a book.

“Long ago,” she said. “I want that said out loud once, because it is the part of this evening that is mine and I am not having it confused with anything you came up the stairs with.”

He said it.

She took her clothes off the way a person takes off a thing that is in the way, over her head and off both arms, and one shoe and then the other hooked off with two fingers. She was quick at all of it and she was not once showing anybody anything.

Her belly is soft below the front, and the skin at the sides of her nails has gone hard and glossy the way leather goes. That is on about nine other pairs of hands in this trade and on hers.

She got him out of the coat and out of the rest of it in about eleven seconds and then she put her mouth on him and took the whole of him, tongue first and then deep, and her hands at his hips did not go anywhere else and did not need to.

She came off it and got up over him on the deal table and pushed her skirt back to her own ribs and set him against her and came down onto him in one movement, and the shutter was up four inches and the street was going past under it the whole time.

“Twenty-nine years,” she said, “and I have had about nine men in that chair and none of them came about a favour.”

She was moving on him before she had finished the sentence, and she took hold of his cock in her fingers and worked her hand up the whole length of it and looked down at it the way she looked at a real book she already knew the answer in, and then she laughed once, out loud, at nothing.

“Say nothing worth anything to me,” she said. “Say something with a figure in it. That is what you are for.”

He got a hand into her hair and she let him keep it there, and she rode him up off the table and put him down and did it again, and her nails went into the back of his own hand hard enough to leave four small crescents that stayed for two days.

“Again,” she said. “Do that again. You have about nine seconds and then I am going to take this one myself and you are not going to help me with a single second of it.”

He did it again and she thanked him out loud for it in the voice she uses over a counter, and her mouth was wet and she did not wipe it and neither did he.

Then she stopped him with two fingers on his hip and got her thumb under him again, and worked herself down onto him in one long slow movement and took all of it, and set her teeth in her own lip and rode out the end of it with her hands braced into the wood of the table on both sides of him.

There was semen on her own wrist and on the inside of her thigh and neither of them wiped anything, because there is a cloth on a nail by that door and it has been on that nail since the house was built and it has not been washed since March.

She came down onto the chair beside him after that and let go of herself in about four seconds and stayed there with the shutter up four inches and the street going past under it.

## What She Says Afterwards, Standing Up

He put his shirt on and did not get his coat, because the coat is on the back of the chair and he did not go near it.

“Nineteen times,” said Marguerite Vale.

He looked at her.

“Nineteen times in twenty-nine years that a thing was done for a person with nothing behind it and I was the only person alive who could put it in a book,” she said, “and I did not do it, and not one of those nineteen people has ever asked me for it, and one of them died not knowing whether she was owed anything.”

She stood up and pulled her skirt down and did it again to be sure.

“Now the sentence you have come down here with is not the sentence you have been carrying since February. You have been carrying a question about a sheet with four words on the back of it. That is a good question and it has been standing at the top of that stair since the eighth of February and it is still standing there.”

“And the sentence?”

“The sentence is this,” said Marguerite Vale. “You have put a form on the desk of a man who cleans washrooms.”

She said it flatly.

“He is rank one and he has a spent chest and he has a shift he asked for and there is not one officer in this city entitled to stop him from putting anything on that desk. And in three months he has done the thing I have wanted to do for twenty-nine years, and he did it with a pen, in a room with no door onto a street, and about four hundred people have found out.”

She got the bar off the strongroom door on her way out, because she does that at about seven every evening and it is not about him.

“One thing is worth more than the other and I am not going to tell you which one, and you will work it out on about the ninth of September or you will not work it out at all, and either way you will not be able to say that I told you.”

*Three thousand and twenty-nine days. Less three thousand and twenty-two, the twenty-third of June of this year, is seven, and a woman put a price on a thing done for a person with nothing written behind it, and the price was that it is worth nothing and cannot be given and cannot be called in, and a clerk squared a sheet twice at half past two and there were eleven at a rail at four minutes past four.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is ninety, and the thirty-third sheet had been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them and came down off it at four this afternoon, and the thirty-fourth went up in its place and stands until the thirtieth of September.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and nine days, which is twenty months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Line At The Foot Of It

Three thousand one hundred and thirty-nine was the twentieth of October and the twenty-seventh is day three thousand one hundred and forty-six, and three thousand one hundred and forty-six less three thousand one hundred and thirty-nine is seven, and twenty-seven less twenty is seven by the month count.

## The Order He Reads It In

Lin Hao reached the passage off Cordwain at about half past nine in the morning with the cold in his coat and the rail empty and the shutter half down over the window.

The counterman was behind the deal with a cup gone cold at his elbow and a pencil down beside it that he did not pick up.

“You are early for the reading,” said the counterman.

“I am not here for the reading,” said Lin Hao. “I am here to hear what the bottom of that sheet sounds like when there are about nine people at it and not one person there is telling anyone what any of it means.”

The counterman looked at the rail and then at the window.

“It sounds the way it has sounded since the thirtieth of September,” he said. “Four sentences. Four lines in the column. Six lines under those with the fourth struck through and a name under the striking. Then four lines in the office hand. Then two inches of nothing.”

Lin Hao wanted to hear whether the order had held for a month. That was the whole of what he wanted that morning, and he was ashamed of how much he wanted it, because a man who put lines on a page ought to be able to leave them alone for thirty days.

“Say the four in the office hand the way you say them,” said Lin Hao.

The counterman did not look at any paper. He had them the way a man has anything he has said about nine times a week for a month.

“Thirtieth of March,” he said. “What a person says at that counter goes down in the words that person used. Tenth of June. What the office holds about a person is told to that person in that room. Nineteenth of July. Nine words a man said at that counter in front of about nine people. Sixteenth of August. What a person says in answer goes under what they were told.”

He stopped there.

“That is the order they stand in,” said the counterman. “I read them the way they stand.”

## About Nine Kitchens In A Month

Wenna Raut had the book open on the table under the window when he came back up four flights with the cold still in his sleeves.

“About nine kitchens came to that door in the last month,” she said, before he had taken his coat off. “About nine people with them. About nine answers given, one to each, in that room, with a date against each and an hour against the date.”

Lin Hao hung his coat on the nail and stood with his hands in his pockets because the room was cold enough that he did not want to take them out.

“And all nine were told what the office holds about them,” he said.

“All nine,” said Wenna Raut. “Eight were told a row with a date on it and went down the stair with less than they brought up it, which is the best thing that book has ever done for anyone.”

She turned a page back and put her finger on a line halfway down it.

“The ninth was told a sentence about a building off a third court keeping the stone at the foot of a wall,” she said. “It is in a stranger words with a date against it from the nineteenth of May. It is wrong. The person it is about has been told it out loud.”

Lin Hao felt the cold move from his sleeves into his wrists. He had known since June that the instruction of the thirtieth of March would do harm, and he had known since May what sort of harm, and knowing had not made him any better at standing in front of it.

“Who told her,” he said.

“I did,” said Wenna Raut. “In that room. With the door shut. Because she brought a paper to that counter and the line of the tenth of June says what I do when a person does that, and I did it, and what the office holds about her is a sentence somebody else said about her in front of about nine people.”

## The Woman Who Was Told Wrong

She came at about eleven with a shawl over her head and her hands red from a washtub and a daughter waiting on the stair below because there was nowhere inside for two to stand without being looked at.

Lin Hao knew her face from the corridor in August. He did not know her name and he did not ask for it, because there was nothing on which that office might ask her anything at all.

Wenna Raut read her back what had been entered in May, in the words the second woman used, with the date and the hour, and then she read her the instruction of the sixteenth of August, which says an answer goes under what a person was told.

“I never said that,” said the woman. “I never went up any stair in May. I was at a washtub in May.”

“I know,” said Wenna Raut.

“Then why is it in your book about me.”

“Because a woman standing third in a line said it out loud at that counter in front of about nine people,” said Wenna Raut. “And the instruction of the thirtieth of March says what a person says at that counter goes down in the words that person used, and I wrote it down, and I may not take it from.”

The woman looked at Lin Hao for the first time since she came in.

“Are you the man who reads it out at the rail,” she said.

“No,” said Lin Hao. “I am the man who cleans washrooms in a branch off a third court, four nights and three off, on a rota he asked for. I put the line on the page that says she had to write it down.”

He hated saying it that plainly. He said it anyway, because anything less plain would have been a way of leaving the room while staying in it.

“What do I do now,” said the woman.

“You say what you have just said, here, and it goes under what you were told, in the words you used, with a date and an hour against it,” said Lin Hao. “And then anyone who is told that row afterwards is told your answer with it, by whoever is standing at that rail.”

## What Went Under It

She said it standing up, with her shawl still over her head and her hands shut together in front of her to keep them from shaking where anyone could see.

“I was never in that line in May and I never said anything at any counter,” she said. “I mend linen for about nine households and I have never been inside that building before this morning.”

Wenna Raut wrote it under the May row in about three minutes, with the date at the head of the added lines and the hour against the date, and read it back once in the voice she reads everything in.

The woman listened to her own sentence come out of another person mouth and nodded once, the way people nod when a thing is finished and not when a thing is mended.

“Is that on the page that goes out at four o clock on the last working day of December,” she said.

“It is,” said Lin Hao.

“Then a man who is paid nothing will read it out,” said the woman. “With my answer under it.”

“He will,” said Lin Hao. “In the order the lines stand. He reads what is on the page.”

She went down the stair to her daughter without thanking either of them, and neither of them asked her to, and the door shut behind them on a corridor about four feet wide with about nine people in it who had heard all of it through wood.

## The Line He Had Not Priced

Wenna Raut put the pen across the book and looked at him for the first time since eleven.

“You priced the denial,” she said. “You carried it four days and you would not put it on my table until you had said what it would cost a man at a rail nine times a week for ever. You did not price this one.”

Lin Hao stood at the window with his hands still in his pockets and looked down at a yard with washing in it going stiff in the cold.

“I did not think there was anything to price,” he said. “The tenth of June says tell a person what the office holds about them. The sixteenth of August says enter what they say in answer. I thought the two together were a kindness.”

“They are,” said Wenna Raut. “And one of about nine answers in a month is wrong, and the person it is about has been told it out loud, in a room, by me, and will be told it again at a rail by a man who cannot be asked to read less of it, with her own denial under it that not one person asked her to make.”

She did not raise her voice. She had never once raised it in nine years, and the room would not have known what to do with it if she had.

“That is what the line at the foot of it sounds like now,” she said. “Four sentences. A name in a column. A denial that is spoken every morning. And a line in the office hand saying what the office holds about them, whether it is true or not, because a clerk may only enter what she is given.”

Lin Hao felt anger come up in him for the first time in about a month, not at her and not at the counterman and not at the woman third in the line, but at the page itself for doing exactly what he had told it to do.

“I will not take it from,” he said.

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “You will not. A row may be added to and may not be taken from, which is the worst rule in the bound book and is the reason you needed the second instruction before you needed the first.”

She picked the pen up again and squared the book to the edge of the table.

“About nine people came in the last month and about nine will come in the next,” she said. “And one of the next nine will be told a thing about herself that somebody else said, and she will answer it, and her answer will go under it, and the page will get longer by two lines every time anyone is wrong about anyone.”

*Three thousand one hundred and forty-six days. Less three thousand one hundred and thirty-nine, the twentieth of October of this year, is seven, and a page with four sentences and a name in a column and a denial that is spoken was read out at a rail and at a counter and one of about nine answers given in a month was wrong and the person it is about was told it out loud.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is twenty-seven, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last twenty-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and twenty-six days, which is twenty-four months and six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

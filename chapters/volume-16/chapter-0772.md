# A Copy Made From A Copy

Three thousand and sixteen was the seventeenth of June and the twenty-third is day three thousand and twenty-two, and three thousand and twenty-two less three thousand and sixteen is six, and twenty-three less seventeen is six by the month count.

## A Front Room Off A Street That Runs Down To The Laundry

She has mended umbrellas in that front room for about nineteen years and the room is eleven feet by nine and has a board floor with the boards warped in a curve towards the door, and there are about forty umbrella ribs on the wall on nails and about nine canes in a bundle standing in the corner that is never moved.

There is a frame on the bench, a wooden clamp, and a bed of nails with the shine worn off two of them. There is a lamp with a green shade and no bulb in it because the lamp is for looking through a canopy at night and the bulb in it went in the third year and she never replaced it.

She learned her writing in a schoolroom and had not needed it for about six years until the middle of last year, and she charges nothing, and she has never asked anybody for anything in that room.

He came down the street at about half past two because there was a thing he had been carrying for nine days and he could not get it out of the room he had carried it through.

## She Knew Before He Arrived

She had the ninth sheet of nothing in front of her — which was not paper, which was an umbrella with the canopy off it and the shaft in a vice — and she did not look up, and she said it.

“You are not going to ask me to stop doing it.”

“No.”

“You came down here to ask me to stop doing it,” she said, “and you have got about nine seconds in which you were going to do it, and you have spent them on the stairs, and I have been sitting here since two o’clock.”

He put his hands in his coat pockets.

“I have got something to tell you about the foot of it.”

“Go on.”

## What She Is Right About, The Second Time

She has been right about this once before, in a passage off a street two streets wide, holding a sheet up against the light off the shopfronts and reading the bottom four inches of a page out loud slowly twice because a man asked her to.

What she was right about then was that a copy holds what the page carried on the day the copy was made.

What she is right about now is the thing underneath that, and she has had it since the thirtieth of March and has not said it out loud to a person until this afternoon.

“A copy of a line is not the line,” she said. “It has been that since the year before last and everybody knows it. But look at what a copy of the bottom of that page is now.”

She set the shaft down in the vice and took her hands away from it.

“Four months ago, when a woman brought me a copy, there were four sentences at the foot of it and that was the whole of what was at the bottom. In March there was a fifth line and the fifth line was in a clerk’s hand with a man’s name under it, and not one person had ever heard of it, and not one person was asking for it.”

“And in June.”

“In June there are six things at the bottom of a copy instead of four sentences,” she said, “and the two extra ones were never written by a person about a person, they were written in a room by a clerk off what people said out loud, and then a man who is paid nothing read them out loud in a passage, and then somebody’s grandmother made a pencil copy at that rail, and then that copy comes in here to me.”

She looked at him for the first time.

“So there are three copies between that room and the kitchen, and none of the three has ever seen the room, and the kitchen has got a thing on it that was said out loud by a man who was reading something else.”

## A Word Crossed Out In Pencil, In June

There is a piece of paper in this city with a word struck through it in pencil, and a day written beside the striking in a hand that is not the hand of the woman who brought it, and it does not exist in any book and cannot be produced again.

She said it while she was putting the canopy back over the frame and she did not stop while she said it, because it is not a thing that requires anybody’s attention.

“A woman came in on the twenty-second of June with a copy of the bottom of the page and there was a word crossed out on it in pencil and a day written beside the striking in an office hand.”

“And you wrote it out.”

“I wrote it out the way she told me to write it out,” said the woman who mends umbrellas, “because that is what I do with a thing somebody brings me, and she told me it had been fixed for her and she did not say by whom, and I did not ask, and I wrote the fixed version because that is the version she wanted written.”

“Was the fixed one right.”

“I do not know and that is the honest answer and it is the whole of what I have got,” she said. “I write out what a person says. I have never once in about nineteen years gone and found out whether the person was right, and I have never once been asked, and if I had asked, they would not have come again, because a woman who corrects you is a woman you can be corrected by.”

She put the canopy down and squared a rib against the frame with her thumbnail.

“About four people in this city cannot read a line of that page. I write the bottom of it out for any one of them who brings me a copy and I charge nothing and I keep nothing.”

“So when she told me it had been fixed, I wrote it out fixed, and that fixed version is now the only one of the two in about nine kitchens. The one that was struck is the only version anybody can check, and it is in a drawer under a bench in a room I have not been in.”

## Four Other Versions, All Wrong In A Different Direction

“You said there would be four more.”

“I said there would be four other versions and that every one of them would be wrong in a different direction,” said the woman who mends umbrellas, “and I said it on the stairs before you had got to the top of them, and it is still the twenty-third of June, and there are three of them that have been brought to me by people who found out by themselves, and I have not been asked about the fourth because the woman who has it cannot read it either.”

She turned round and looked at him properly for the first time that afternoon.

“One has the two lines the wrong way up. One has the column copied in between the sentences, because whoever made it read it out at a rail in the order a man reads it and not the order it is printed in. One has the second of the two dates as a date of a division and not a date of this office, because she got it from a man at a window and not from a rail.”

She said the whole of it in about forty seconds without stopping and without any weight on any part of it, the way a woman who repairs other people’s things for a living reads out what is wrong with a seam.

“I am not one of the four and I have never met the other three,” she said. “There are about four hundred people in this city who can write and I am one, and a woman in a front room on this street is another, and there are two more and I do not know what they do and they do not know what I do, and that is the whole of why the four of us cannot go wrong about each other.”

## Why There Is No List

He asked her about the list, once, in the third year, and she gave him the answer then and she gave it again now and it did not change.

“It is not a principle,” she said. “It is that a list has to be put somewhere, and I have a bench and a floor and a wall with forty ribs on it and a bundle of canes in the corner.”

“If I had a book in this room then somebody would come and ask me about the book, and on the day they asked me about it I would have to say yes or no. And a woman who says no four times a week about a small thing gets asked once about a large one.”

“So I mend umbrellas, and I write out what people bring me, and I put my mother’s clothes away at the end of the week, and that is the whole of what this room is for.”

She picked the frame up off the bench.

“You have been right twice about this and wrong about it every time you thought you had found a person who could fix it.”

## Nine Seconds He Does Not Use

He had about nine seconds on the pavement outside after that and he had said nothing in them.

The thing he had come down to say was that a line in a room was going into about nine kitchens a week through a front room with forty ribs on the wall, and that there was a form in a division building two streets away that could have stopped it, and that the division had never had a paper from that room in nine years.

None of that was true. The division had nothing to do with it, and the line was not his to stop, and she was not carrying it. She was writing out what people brought her, in the words they brought it in, and the day she stops is the day she begins keeping a list.

He went up the street about nine feet and then turned back and said the only true thing available.

“You are right and I am not going to ask you.”

“I know you are not,” said the woman who mends umbrellas, “and I have known since about two o’clock, and you have cost me eleven minutes, and they are the cheapest thing you have had off anybody in two years.”

She put the frame down on the bench and turned the lamp shade a quarter, which she does at about three o’clock because the light comes round that room and there is a board across the middle of it.

“And there is one more thing you did not come for and I am going to say it because you are standing in my light.”

“Say it.”

“In fifty-three days there is going to be a third line at the bottom of that page,” said the woman who mends umbrellas. “I have known that since the tenth of June, the same way I knew the other four, and I cannot tell you how I know it, and I have been wrong about two things in nineteen years and one of them was about a winding.”

“Then you are right about that as well.”

“I am right about that as well,” she said, “and about four people in this city are going to bring me a copy of the three of them in the autumn and read it out to me, and I am going to write down what they say.”

“And there is going to be four more of them after that. And in about nine years there will be a woman in this city who has never heard any of it and is going to be told that a page said it.”

She put her hand on the frame and left it there.

“I will write it out because that is what this room is, and I will not keep one of them, and you can do what you like with that.”

He was about three streets off it before he took the pencil out, and he wrote it on the back of his own left hand at about three o’clock on the top landing of a branch office, and there were seven other things on that hand by then, and one of them had been there since the eighth of February.

*Three thousand and twenty-two days. Less three thousand and sixteen, the seventeenth of June of this year, is six, and a woman who mends umbrellas was right about a second thing about the foot of the page, and there is a word crossed out in pencil in this city on four different copies and a word struck in ink on a fifth, and she keeps none of them and has never had a reason to.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is eighty-three, and the thirty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and two days, which is twenty months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

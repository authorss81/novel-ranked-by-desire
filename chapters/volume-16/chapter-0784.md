# He Carries It Down Four Flights

Three thousand and ninety-four was the fifth of September and the twelfth is day three thousand and one hundred and one, and three thousand and one hundred and one less three thousand and ninety-four is seven, and twelve less five is seven by the month count.

## Down Four Flights And Along Four Hundred Yards

He did it at about eleven in the morning on the twelfth with the sheet in his hand and not in the inside of his coat, because he wanted a clerk to see that he was carrying something before she had to ask him what it was.

He put it on the wood under the window and squared it against the edge of the table.

“That is the second column,” he said. “All four lines, on a woman’s own paper, in her own hand, with her own date on it. It is the only copy of it in the world.”

Wenna Raut looked at it and did not touch it.

“That is a piece of paper with a date on it that did not come through that front door,” she said. “I cannot enter it.”

“You are the only person in this city who can enter it.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “I can only enter what I am given, and nobody has given me that. A man carried it in his own hand off another person’s table and put it on my table and told me what it is, and that is you telling me and not her, and I have spent nine years on the other side of that line and I am not going over it for you.”

## What She Did Do With It

She turned it round and read it where it lay, which is what she does with everything, and she did not write one word of it down.

“There is a word at the head of that column,” she said. “Two of them. I have had them in front of my eyes about four hundred times this year and I have never once asked anybody what they mean, and there is no form on which the question may be put.”

“I know.”

“You do not know because you have never spent a whole quarter looking at a corner of a page instead of at the lines.” She squared the book against the edge of the table. “And you cannot have it entered, and you cannot have it cited, and I cannot enter it, and I am going to say the rest of that sentence out loud before you ask me the question at the end of it.”

“There is one person in this city who could tell you.”

“There are three,” said Wenna Raut, “and I know that because you have told me, and I am not going to ask you about any of the three, and you are not going to go and ask any of the three, and I know that as well.”

## The Four Flights Back Up

He went back up them at about half past one in the afternoon, which is a stair he has gone up five times in six years, and his chest has been spent since the fourth year and he stopped twice on the second landing and once on the third and did not sit down on either of them.

Perla Quist had the fourth sheet out and was going down a column with a pencil and a ruler squared against the bench and the calendar coming away from the plaster by a hair every time a machine changed load.

He put the sheet down in front of her, squared, with the date at the top and her handwriting on it, and she looked at it for about four seconds.

“You carried it down four flights.”

“I carried it down four flights and along about four hundred yards and I tried to enter it in a book and I was refused, and I am not going to tell you who refused it or what she said.”

“All right,” said Perla Quist. “Then read it out.”

He read the four lines out loud in the order they stood on her sheet, at the pace she reads a column, and she went back to her column and the pencil lay on the bench the whole time he was reading.

## What The Two Words Are For

She waited until he had finished and then she went down one flight and came back up with about eleven things in both arms and put them on the bench and sorted them with the side of her hand the way a person sorts a drawer they know the whole of.

She stopped with her hand on the seventh of them.

“A docket,” she said. “A works department. Two streets from here. That is where that paper comes out of and it has been coming out of there for nine years and I know it because I have been printing my own dockets on the same stock on the same press in the same room for about nineteen years.”

She turned it round and put her finger under two printed words.

“Carried by,” said Perla Quist. “That is what it says, and it is about half an inch down from the top of a works department docket, and there is a line ruled under it, and the line under it takes the name of the person who is carrying the sheet from one counter to another.”

She took her finger away.

“That is the whole of it,” she said. “It is not a list of anybody. It is a routing line. It is the name of whoever has the docket in their hand at that hour.”

## Since The Seventh Of December

She put the docket back on the bench with the other ten and squared it against the edge.

“I have known that since the seventh of December of last year,” said Perla Quist. “I had a docket in that drawer and I turned it over and I read two words and I went up those four flights to a different kind of counter and I came down again, and I have not told anybody in this city in eight months.”

“Why have you not told this office.”

“Because of the fourth line,” said Perla Quist.

She did not point at anything and she did not need to.

“That line has got a woman’s name in it and a man’s name under it and a date at the top, and it went in under two words that belong to a works department’s routing book, and every part of that line is in a clerk’s hand and correct, and it cannot be taken out, and it cannot be improved.”

She picked the pencil up and turned it over twice without marking anything.

“If a woman’s name is in a routing column in a works department, then she is a person on a sheet that a man carries,” said Perla Quist. “And a person on a sheet that a man carries is a person who can be asked where she has been.”

## The Whole Of Why She Said No Five Times

“That is what you have been refusing me for,” he said.

“That is what I have been refusing you for, and I am going to say the other half of it out loud, and then you will have the whole of it.”

She put the pencil down across the bench.

“You have never once asked me a thing about myself,” said Perla Quist. “In six years. You have come up these stairs five times in six months with nothing in your hands and stood at the end of that bench for nine minutes and gone down again, and every one of the six times I said no to you, not one of them was against you.”

“Yes.”

“And the seventh time I said yes, and I have thought about that every day since, and I did not do it because you asked me.”

She turned round and put the ten other things back into the drawer under the bench and shut it with her hip.

“I did it because you asked me about the paper.”

## What She Said, And What She Did Not Say

“You could have told this office in December,” he said.

“I could have told this office in December and you would have written a line under a line, and the line would have gone out at four o’clock on a quarter-end to about two thousand counters, and it would have named a works department and a laundry and a street two streets wide.”

“And that would have been the whole of my name.”

“That would have been my name and my street and the fact that I keep nine sheets,” said Perla Quist, “on the bottom of a page that about nine people a week have read out loud to other people for nine years.”

She straightened up and looked at the bench rather than at him.

“You have not written it down anywhere,” she said. “I want that said out loud between us and I am going to leave it there and not improve it.”

“It is not written down anywhere.”

“No,” said Perla Quist. “It is in the head of a woman who mends other people’s sheets for nothing, and that is a worse place than a book and it is the only place in this city it is in.”

She picked up her pencil and squared the ruler against the bench.

“I am not going to thank you for carrying it down four flights and back up again with a spent chest,” she said, “and if you thank me for giving it to you I will not be here when you come up the stairs next time.”

## Where A Piece Of Paper Like That Can Live

He was on the second landing for about four minutes on the way down with the sheet in his hand and not in his coat.

There are three places in that room off a third court where a thing can be put and each one of them is a place a thing dies.

The drawer in the wall on the left of the table was shut on the twenty-first of October of the seventh year under an instruction with his own name under it and cannot be opened again by anybody, including him.

The bundle on the table beside it has twenty-six things on it and nothing is ever going to be taken off it, and the twenty-sixth of them is the empty sheet he carried out of that drawer in the inside of his coat in the seventh year.

And the column in the bound book cannot be improved and cannot be taken from, and everything this office learns to say from this year forward is permanent the first time it is said, and goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters.

There is a fourth place. It is on a bench four flights up a street two streets wide, and it is in the head of a woman who cannot be cited, and she told him about nine seconds ago where a piece of paper like that can live in this city and she did not use the word *person* and he did not either.

He got it out of the inside of his coat and looked at her handwriting on it under a window about eleven feet by nine with the steam going past.

*Three thousand and one hundred and one days. Less three thousand and ninety-four, the fifth of September of this year, is seven, and a man carried a sheet down four flights and along four hundred yards and was refused a book, and carried it back up four flights, and a woman told him what two printed words are for.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is seventy-two, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-two of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and eighty-one days, which is twenty-two months and twenty-one days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

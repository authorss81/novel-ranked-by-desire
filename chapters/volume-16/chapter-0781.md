# Two Records And A Name

Three thousand and seventy-five was the sixteenth of August and the twenty-third is day three thousand and eighty-two, and three thousand and eighty-two less three thousand and seventy-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## Four Hundred Yards, And Both Of The Dates

There are two records in this city of about four hundred people and he is the only person alive who has read both.

One of them stood at about four hundred and seventy rows on the third of June, which is the last day anybody counted them, and every row of it has a date against it and an hour against the date. That is a count of rows in a bound Register and not a count of anybody.

The other is nine sheets under a third table four flights up a street two streets wide, with about two hundred lines under a heading a woman ruled on her own paper in the eighth year, and about four hundred and thirty pieces of pencil paper have gone up those four flights and come down them again over six years.

Four hundred and thirty is a count of pieces of paper. It is not a count of rows and it is not a count of people.

He has not gone up those four flights since the third of June and he is not going to this week, and the reason is nine words in pencil on the back of his own right hand that he has not improved and has not said to anybody.

## A Piece Of Pencil Paper With Nothing On It

He understood it on the first landing at about nine in the morning and he stood there for about two minutes with the thing in his hand.

The rule has been standing in that book since the tenth of June of last year and it has never before had to be held up to a person. When a person brings a paper to that counter, this office is to tell that person, in that room, what this office holds about them.

He is the only person in Ostara entitled to put one question to that office. He has used it twice in nine years.

There is one way to find out what an office holds about you and the way is to arrive at its counter with a piece of paper in your hand, and he has understood that about four hundred and seventy times and has never once done it, and there was never any reason on any of the four hundred and seventy days.

What he had in the inside of his coat was a piece of pencil paper about the size of the fourth of a page and it was blank, and he had put it there on the sixth of April because a new pad comes in a bundle with the sheets and he had not thrown the offcut away.

He went back up the four flights with it.

## At The Counter On The Second Of About Eleven

He put it on the wood and squared it against the edge of the table, which is what everybody does in a room with a counter in it, and he did not sit down and he did not say anything for about nine seconds.

Wenna Raut took the pen up and then put it down again.

“I have not got a question this morning,” she said, “and I had one and I have spent it, and that was at about ten and you were not here.”

“Then what do I do with it.”

“You brought a paper to a counter,” said Wenna Raut, “and there is a line standing in the front of the bound book since the tenth of June of last year that says what I do when a person does that, and I am going to do it, and it is going to be the first time in nine years that obeying it has produced nothing at all.”

She opened the Register to the last entered page and put the date at the top of the line and the hour against the date.

“There is no form in this city on which I may ask you a second question about it,” she said, “and if I ask you one you will answer it and it will be on a page that goes out at four o’clock on the thirtieth of December, in this office’s hand, with a date on it and an hour against the date, and about nine people a week will hear it read out loud by a man who is paid nothing.”

He did not say anything.

“That is not me refusing you,” said Wenna Raut. “That is me telling you what the question costs before you decide whether to pay it, and you have had it since the second of December and you have not paid it once.”

## What This Office Holds About Lin Hao

“Read it back,” he said.

She turned the book a quarter on its own axis and read it in the voice she uses over that counter.

“There is no row about you,” said Wenna Raut. “Not one, in nine years, of a door that has come off its latch four hundred and seventy times.”

“That is the whole of it.”

“That is not the whole of it, and I am not going to read less of it than is on the page.”

She put her finger on the front of the book without opening it.

“There are four lines in the front of the bound book in a man’s own hand and a man’s name under each of them.”

“The first shuts a drawer in the wall on the left of that table and cannot be shut again by anybody. The second says every paper through that front door is to be entered as received with a date against it.”

“The third says every paper is to be entered with a description of the person who brought it, in that person’s own words. The fourth is a denial about a page and a stone, and a man’s name is under it.”

“That is four lines.”

“That is four lines and one date and one hour and one piece of pencil paper with nothing on it,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have put the date and the hour against a piece of paper that a man carried up four floors to give me, and there is a space in the middle of that row where your words would go, and the one that is in the row of the twenty-first of October has been sitting there since last year, and there are two of them now.”

## The Name Under The Heading

He looked at the second column for a while.

There are four lines in it under two printed words that say **carried by**. The first three are older than the third line’s own date. The fourth has a date at the top of it and a sentence in a clerk’s hand, and in the middle of that sentence there is a woman’s name, and at the foot of the line there is a man’s name, and the man’s name is his.

“You are in there once,” he said.

“You are in there once and it is the only name of yours in nine years that is not under a line you wrote yourself,” said Wenna Raut, “and I am not going to improve that for you, and I will tell you what a heading is because I am a person who reads things aloud in this room now and it is the only thing I have got.”

“The heading says carried by.”

“It says carried by,” she said, “and there is not one form in this city that says what a box on a form is for, and nobody has ever asked me, and I have had the word in front of my eyes about four hundred times.”

## The Thing Neither Of Them Can Do

“There is one page,” he said. “One. It goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters and the original is in this room and it has been in this room since the first year of this office.”

“Yes.”

“There is nothing in this city that can take that page and make a second one of it.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am going to tell you why there is nothing, because you have brought me a piece of paper and I owe you a shape.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table with both hands.

“A page is printed by a works department two streets from the laundry, on the same stock that laundry prints its own dockets on, and every sheet of that form in this city comes out of one press in one room, and the press is not this office’s and the stock is not this office’s and the room behind that press has never had a paper out of this building in nine years.”

“So there is one.”

“There is one,” she said, “and there are about nine people a week who make copies of the foot of it by hand in this city, and there are about two thousand originals of the whole of it, and not one instrument in Ostara will let anybody set one of those against the others and call the answer a result.”

She closed the book on her finger.

“I am a person who enters what she is given. I cannot enter a second copy of a page that has not been made.”

## Two People, And Neither Of Them

He had it by eleven and he had it in three parts and none of the three was about rules.

There is a woman who mends umbrellas in a front room and has written the bottom of that page out by hand, for nothing, for anybody who cannot read, since about the thirtieth of March.

She keeps none of it, and he has been down that street four times in seven weeks and has never once asked her for anything.

And there is a woman with a pencil on a bench four flights up a street two streets wide who has nine sheets under a third table and has said no five times about anything at all and once more on the twenty-sixth of May, and he is not going to make it six.

There is a third person in this city who can write the bottom of that page out from memory, and she is not allowed to be asked about it by anybody in any book this office holds.

Three people. Two of them he could walk to in about nine minutes and the third one is not to be spoken of at all.

## The Counter Off Cordwain, At About Half Past Eleven

He went down at about half past eleven and the rail had nothing at it in the middle of a month, because a page only goes up four times a year, and the counterman was under the window with the shutter down and a cup on the desk with the handle turned away from him, which is how he keeps it.

He had been down to that counter four times in nine years and had put one question in all of them, in December, and the answer had been no, and that had been the first question anybody had ever put to that man.

Not one person was entitled to tell that man anything about the page he read out loud to about nine people a week. There never had been. He was not going to be told and he was not going to be asked, and he had not asked in nine years.

He put two fingers on the deal of the counter, which was the only thing he touched in that room, and he asked him whether the line under the four sentences was part of the four sentences.

*Three thousand and eighty-two days. Less three thousand and seventy-five, the sixteenth of August of this year, is seven, and a man carried a piece of pencil paper up four floors to a counter in a building with no door onto a street and did not say one word at it, and a row went in with a space in the middle of it, and there are two of those now.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is fifty-three, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and sixty-two days, which is twenty-two months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

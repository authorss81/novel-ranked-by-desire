# Any Person May Ask

Three thousand one hundred and seventy-four was the twenty-fifth of November and the second is day three thousand one hundred and eighty-one, and three thousand one hundred and eighty-one less three thousand one hundred and seventy-four is seven, and five out of the thirtieth, which is the last working day of that month, is the second of December.

## The Sixth Line In The Office Hand

He carried it eight days and did not show it to anyone, not to Wenna Raut and not to Yusuf Adeyemi and not to any of the nine, because he had carried four lines before it and every one had been priced in a room before it went on a table and this one had to be priced alone first.

It was the fifth instruction that office ever made and the sixth line in the office hand at the foot of the page. The July line and the August line and the October line were already on the thirty-fifth sheet. This one would go out at four o clock on the thirtieth of December with the thirty-sixth.

What he wanted was to open the only office in Ostara that could not be asked anything, and it had been in him since March, when eleven pieces of paper came through a front door in eight days with about nine people behind them and nowhere to wait that was not also being looked at.

He was afraid. He had been afraid at the thirtieth of March and at the tenth of June and at the sixteenth of August and at the twentieth of October, and each time he had done it anyway, and each time the fear had been right.

## What It Closes

Yusuf Adeyemi came at about ten in the morning on the second with rain on his shoulders and the cold in his hands, and shut the door of the room off a third court behind him for the first time in nine years.

“Read it to me,” he said. “Once. Before she touches it.”

Lin Hao read it once, standing up, in the order it stood, with the date first and his name last.

*From the second of December of this year, any person may put one question to this office about any paper this office holds, and this office shall answer it in writing, and the question and the answer shall be entered in the Register with a date and an hour against each, in the words the person used, and may not be taken from. Lin Hao.*

Adeyemi stood at the window for about four minutes without saying anything, with the yard below going white with rain.

“Nine years,” he said at last. “For nine years this office could be asked by exactly one man. You. Twice you asked. Now anyone may. That is what it closes.”

“And a building with no door onto a street,” said Lin Hao. “Which is the whole of what I believed made it safe. A queue with nowhere to wait that is not looked at. A counter where sentences are said out loud. Safety was never letting anyone in. Safety was never being asked.”

Adeyemi turned round. He was forty-four and had been Registrar of Unrecorded Merit through all of it, and his face showed everything those nine years had cost him and none of it showed as blame.

“You know what it costs,” he said. It was not a question.

“About four hundred people papers become answerable to anybody who asks,” said Lin Hao. “Including a person asking whether a neighbour claim is there. The Register fills with questions in strangers words for ever. About forty people will say it came from the office off a third court.”

He stopped there, because the last half belonged to another mouth and he would not say it himself.

“Say the rest,” said Adeyemi. “You carried it eight days. Say all of it.”

Lin Hao looked at the floor, at the boards worn smooth by nine years of feet that had never once queued until March.

“And the second time the two inches go quiet they will not come back,” he said.

## What It Costs, Said By Her

Wenna Raut came in at about half past ten with about nine people already in the corridor and the cold coming in under the door every time it opened.

He put the sheet on the table face down and told her to read it back and tell him whether he had got it right, the way he had done on the tenth of June, because he had learned that a clerk who may only enter what she is given is the only person who can tell him whether he has written what he means.

She read it back standing up, in the voice she reads everything in, word for word, with the date and the name.

“You have got it right,” she said. “And I am going to say no to it, and then I am going to explain that it is not the same no as the other ones, because the other ones were about entering what I was given and this one is about answering.”

She put the pen across the book.

“I am the hand and since March I have been the mouth,” she said. “From the second of December I will be the hand that writes answers in writing to anyone who asks about any paper, and every question and every answer goes in the bound book with a date and an hour against each, in the words the person used, and may not be taken from.”

She looked at Adeyemi and then at Lin Hao.

“That means about four hundred kitchens will have their papers asked about by anyone with legs to climb four flights,” she said. “It means I will write all winter. It means a man at a rail will read questions as well as answers, about nine times a week, for ever.”

Lin Hao felt love for her so sharp it hurt his chest in the spent place, because she had been the mouth of about forty people a quarter since March and had never once refused to be it, and now she was agreeing to be the mouth of anyone at all.

“Give it to me,” she said.

He gave it to her. She wrote it in about five minutes with the date at the top of the line and his name at the foot of it, and did not improve one word of it, and it was the sixth line in the office hand at the foot of one of its own pages and the last one that office will ever make.

It goes out at four o clock on the thirtieth of December to about two thousand counters. He told the counterman at about half past eleven that morning, standing at that counter with nothing at the rail, and the man listened the way he listens to everything, with his thumb on the edge of the wood.

“I shall read it,” said the counterman. “In the order it stands. With the other five.”

## A Man Already Waiting In The Corridor

When Lin Hao came back up four flights at about noon, a man was already waiting in the corridor about four feet wide with a cap in his hands and two others behind him, all three having climbed before the second had well begun.

Wenna Raut looked up from the book and then down again, because her one question of the morning was spent and the door had to be managed without it.

“He wants to know whether his neighbour claim is there,” she said quietly, when Lin Hao reached the table. “He said it out loud to the corridor before I could stop him, and now about nine people have it, and his neighbour is one of them, standing third in the line.”

Lin Hao felt cold go through him that had nothing to do with rain. Eight days he had carried the sixth line alone, and within hours of its entering, the very asking he had priced was standing in his corridor.

“Tell him the line is entered but does not take effect until the thirtieth,” said Lin Hao. “Tell him to come back after the thirty-sixth goes up. Tell him in the voice you read everything in, so that all about nine hear it.”

She did. The man stared at the floor and put his cap back on and went down the stair with the two behind him, and the neighbour stayed, white-faced, with her hands shut on a piece of pencil paper she had not yet put across the wood.

“I am not asking today,” she whispered to Lin Hao as she passed. “But I will. After the thirtieth. I have waited six years to know whether he asked about me first.”

He stood at the window long after the corridor emptied, with the sixth line drying in the book and the cost of it standing where three men had stood, and he understood for the first time that opening the office meant letting neighbours ask about neighbours for ever, with dates and hours against each, and no hand able to lift any of it after.

## The Night Shift With Nadia

Nadia Ferrous came on with him that night at eleven with her hair tied back and her hands already red from cold water, and they worked the third-floor washrooms side by side without speaking for about an hour, the way rota nights go when both know the work.

“You put the sixth line in,” she said at last, wringing a cloth into a bucket. “Petra told me. Isolde told me what it closes.”

“Nine years in which exactly one man might ask,” said Lin Hao. “And a building with no door onto a street.”

Nadia nodded. She was one of the three who never live with him again and come in on the rota, and she knew better than most what lists do to people.

“Are you afraid,” she said.

“I am,” said Lin Hao. “I was afraid at the thirtieth of March and at the tenth of June and at every line since. Fear has been right each time.”

She put a hand briefly on his sleeve, wet and cold through wool, and then went back to her bucket.

“Then be afraid and leave it standing,” she said. “You have never once taken a line from, and that is why all of them trust you with the ones you put in.”

*Three thousand one hundred and eighty-one days. Less three thousand one hundred and seventy-four, the twenty-fifth of November of this year, is seven, and a sixth line in the office hand says any person may put one question to that office and it closes nine years in which exactly one man might and a building with no door onto a street.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is sixty-two, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-two of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and sixty-one days, which is twenty-five months and eleven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

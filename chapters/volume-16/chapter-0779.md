# The Umbrella Woman Is Right

Three thousand and sixty-one was the second of August and the ninth is day three thousand and sixty-eight, and three thousand and sixty-eight less three thousand and sixty-one is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## She Was Right On The Twenty-Third Of June

On the twenty-third of June a woman in a front room off a street that runs down to the laundry told him that there would be four other versions of the foot of that page in this city and that every one of them would be wrong in a different direction.

She had it on the twenty-third of June and it is the ninth of August.

He did not go and ask her how she knew, because she mends umbrellas and charges nothing and has never asked anybody for anything, and because she said it at about half past two in the afternoon and he was there.

A man came up the stair at about a quarter to eleven on the ninth with a piece of pencil paper in his hand and the two lines at the bottom of it were in the wrong order.

Both of them are in this office’s hand. Both of them have a man’s name under them. One is from the thirtieth of March and one is from the tenth of June.

On the page they stand the thirtieth of March first and the tenth of June under it.

On his copy the tenth of June is first and the thirtieth of March is under it.

## What A Copy Made By Ear Is

“It is not wrong,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the answer and it is not an answer and I am going to give you both halves of it.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table.

“A man behind a counter reads the bottom of a page out loud. He reads the four sentences, then the six lines under them, then the column, then whatever is under the sentences. That is the order he has read it in for nine years and he is not going to change it for two lines that were added to it in March, because he is not permitted to be told what an added line is.”

“Which is not right.”

“There is no right one,” said Wenna Raut. “The page is not an instrument of this office. The page is a printed sheet that goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and about nine people a week stop at a rail.”

“It has two thousand copies in this city and it has one original, and the original is in a building with no door onto a street. And nothing in Ostara will let anybody take nine kitchen copies and set them against two thousand originals and call the answer a result.”

## What A Copy Made By Ear Does In A Kitchen

A copy made by ear is not a copy. It is a second account of a thing, given by a person who was not there when the thing was printed, to a person who cannot read, in a room, out loud.

About nine of those a week leave the rail off Cordwain. About nine of them are carried up four flights to a bench and written out. About nine of them end up in a kitchen in about four years.

Three hands. Nine feet of dressed stone and a voice, and a pencil in a lane, and a woman with a pencil, and a kitchen.

“Eleven days ago there was a woman at that rail who asked me what I was copying and I told her,” the man said, “and she went away and came back with her husband, and they both write, and about four days after that I had two versions of the same bottom of the same page and they do not agree, and I did not know which of them to give my wife and she did not know either and we are not going to find out.”

“You could ask the man at the counter which one.”

“I have,” said the man. “I stood at that rail on the twenty-third of June with both of them and I asked him which one he read on the day of the thirtieth of March and he told me that he reads whatever is on the page every time and he does not keep a copy and he is not going to start, and he was quite right, and I did not put the other one back in my coat and leave it.”

He put the paper down on the wood and squared it against the edge, the way everybody does in a room with a counter in it.

“So I have given the wrong one back and I have thrown the other one away, and that is what I have done and I have not told anybody until this morning.”

## Four Kitchens In About Nine Weeks

“How many are wrong.”

“I do not know and I am not going to work it out for you,” said Wenna Raut, “because when I worked out a version of that arithmetic in the spring it cost somebody a fortnight. What I have is the standing sheet, and about nine people a week at this counter, and about nine of those nine carrying a sheet they cannot read.”

The man put his paper on the wood.

“I cannot read it either,” he said. “My wife reads it. She took it to a woman in a front room with about forty ribs on the wall and had the bottom of it written out off my copy, and the woman wrote what my wife read out of it, and that is the one I have got.”

Lin Hao understood it in about four seconds and he was on his feet before he had finished understanding it.

## The Front Room With Forty Ribs On The Wall

There is one person in this city who could tell him which kitchen the wrong copy went to.

She wrote it out. She wrote it out on about the second of August, off a copy brought in by a woman who cannot read her own, in a room eleven feet by nine with a board floor warped towards the door and a lamp with a green shade and no bulb in it.

She has been mending in that front room for about nineteen years and she charges nothing and she keeps no list, because a list has to be put somewhere and there is a bench and a floor and a wall with forty ribs on it.

And he had been down that street four times in seven weeks and had never once asked her for anything.

He did not go.

He stood at the end of the table in a room off a third court and he put his hands in his coat pockets and he wrote the reason on the back of his own right hand under the five sentences that were already there, in pencil, in about nine seconds.

*She keeps no list. Going would be making one.*

He did not improve it and he did not say it out loud to anybody and it stayed nine words on the back of a hand on a landing with no water on it, and it is the only sentence in nine years he has written down and obeyed on the same night.

And the other half of it, which he did not write down, is that a woman with a pencil on a bench four streets away could probably find the man who brought a copy in a front room on the second of August, because about nine people a month go up that bench and she has kept them in her head for six years and about nine people cannot be nine hundred.

He did not go up that bench either, and that one had been closed since the third of June for a different reason, and the reason has never once been a rule.

## What He Said To The Man Instead

“I cannot find out which one is wrong,” he said. “Not this month.”

“That is a strange thing to say to a man who has come up four flights with a piece of paper.”

“It is the true one and you have had a clerk’s version of it already,” said Lin Hao. “There are about nine of those copies in about nine kitchens and about two thousand of that page in this city.”

“And about nine people a week stop at a rail and hear the bottom of it read out loud, and every one of those nine has got a woman in a different kitchen who has heard it and read it back. There is not one instrument in this city that can stand in nine kitchens at once.”

The man squared his own paper against the wood, the way everybody does in a room with a counter in it.

“Then what do I do with it.”

“Keep it.”

“It is wrong.”

“You cannot read it and your wife can, and your wife took it to a woman who wrote out what your wife said,” said Lin Hao. “In about seven weeks that same page goes out at four o’clock on the last working day of a quarter with two more lines at the bottom of it than it has now.”

“And it will be read out loud to about nine people a week, and you will hear it, and not one person in this city will be able to tell you which of the two copies in your own kitchen is the one he read.”

He put the paper back into his coat.

“I am going to put it in the drawer with the other one,” said the man, “because I cannot read either of them and they are the same piece of paper.”

He said it as a joke. He went down four flights laughing about it. And the wrong one will be carried on from the wrong one, in about nine kitchens, for about four years, by about nine people who cannot read, and not one person will ever know that there was a difference.

*Three thousand and sixty-eight days. Less three thousand and sixty-one, the second of August of this year, is seven, and a man came up a stair with a copy of the foot of that page with two lines in it in the wrong order, and the only person in this city who could have found out which one was right was not asked, in nine words, and the wrong one is going back in a drawer with the right one in it.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is thirty-nine, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and forty-eight days, which is twenty-one months and eighteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

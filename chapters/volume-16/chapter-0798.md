# A Pencil Across The Bottom

Three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight was the ninth of December and the sixteenth is day three thousand one hundred and ninety-five, and three thousand one hundred and ninety-five less three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

## She Did Not Come Up The Stair

She did not come up the stair that week. The stair in the building off a third court stayed empty of her the way it had been empty of her for eleven months except twice, once in February and once in May, and Lin Hao did not expect her and did not send for her and did not ask anyone about her.

What he wanted was for her to stay wherever she was until she chose. That was the whole of what he wanted, and it was the hardest wanting he had ever held, because the sixth line was now standing and anyone might ask and the one person it was made for might never come.

Wenna Raut spent her one question of the morning on whether any question had been entered under the sixth line, and the answer was none yet, and she went back to her column with the book open and the pen ready, because readiness was all that office had left to offer.

## The Corner At About Five

At about five in the afternoon Lin Hao stood at the kerb at the foot of the hill with a boy with a paste brush nowhere in sight, because the board had been up since the seventh and would not be touched again until the thirtieth.

The month form was face up on the board with rain softening one corner and both ruled squares at the foot of it empty. One was for the figure of the thing at the address. One was for the standing of it. The printed words under each were worn nearly smooth.

Under the two ruled squares, held against the board by the lower batten where the paste had gone thin, was a piece of office paper with the date at the top in a clerk hand and four words on the back in ink that could not be seen from the front.

Anyone bending low enough could see the front. About nine people a week get down low enough to read four inches of dressed stone under the striking, and some of them glance at the board on the way up. Not one of them could read the four words on the back of it, because the back was against the wood.

Lin Hao knew the paper. He had seen its like once before on a bench under a third table, on top of about eleven things that had never been able to be used. He did not touch it. He stood eleven feet away with his hands in his pockets and looked at it the way everyone looks at anything left at that board, briefly and without stopping.

## With A Line Across The Bottom

She had left her own sheet under the two ruled squares, with a pencil line across the bottom of it in a hand of her own.

It was not a figure. It was not a standing. It was not an entry in any book. It was the oldest convention Ostara has for I was here, a line in a hand of her own across the bottom of a sheet, and it had been the way of saying it for about twenty years.

She was sent by not one person. That was plain from the hour and the manner of it, because the board stands unwatched at all hours and anyone may leave anything under a batten without being seen, and the boy who tends it looks at neither squares nor sheets.

Who told her is not established. Lin Hao did not ask Brack, who had said she would come to the corner and not up four flights. He did not ask Perla Quist, who had read her four words once and written nothing down. He did not ask Wenna Raut, who may only enter what she is given. Establishing it would have meant asking her something, and nothing on which that office might ask her anything exists.

He stood there until the light went. About nine people in a week get down low enough to read those four inches, and the hour he was standing in was not a week, so he could not say how many of them were his; four came down while he was there and went up again without glancing at the sheet, and one old man glanced at it and did not stop, because a sheet under two empty boxes is not a thing anyone was ever taught to read.

## The Batten Coming Loose

Rain came harder and the lower batten holding the form began to lift at one end where the paste had gone thin, and the sheet under it stirred in the wind coming up the hill.

Lin Hao stepped forward without thinking and put two fingers on the wood beside the sheet, not on it, and held the batten down against the gusts until the worst passed.

His fingers were inches from her pencil line. He did not look at it directly. He looked at the batten and the paste and the wet wood, because looking would have been a kind of reading and he had no right to read anything she left where anyone might see.

A girl coming down low to the stone watched him hold the board and said nothing, and went back up the slope without glancing at either square, and he stayed until the rain eased and the batten held again on its own.

He had steadied a board and nothing more. The sheet stayed where she left it. The line stayed across the bottom of it. His hands shook afterward in a way they had not shaken since his first quarter-end, not from cold but from how close he had come to touching something he had vowed to leave alone.

## What The Mark Is Not

Wenna Raut said it that evening when he told her, with the book shut between them so that neither would be tempted to enter anything.

“A piece of office paper under two ruled squares with a pencil line across the bottom of it is not a figure and not a standing and not an entry,” she said. “The office has no instrument that can receive it and is not asked to. A mark is a thing with a hand on it. It stays where it was left until a boy takes the form down on the thirtieth.”

Lin Hao nodded. He felt tenderness so strong it hurt behind his eyes, because a woman who cannot read had gone to the only place in Ostara where a person who cannot read can leave anything, and had left the one thing such a person can leave, under boxes she could never fill, with a line she could make.

“She did not come up the stair,” he said.

“She did not,” said Wenna Raut. “She went to the corner, where a form has been face up on a board since the seventh. About nine people a week get down low enough to see it and not one of them can read the four words on the back of it. That is as safe as anything left anywhere in winter.”

He did not go back that night. He went to the branch and cleaned washrooms until half past five in the morning with the pencil line across the bottom of a sheet he had not touched sitting in his head like lamplight, and he was glad he had not touched it, because touching it would have made it into something carried and it was better as something left.

## At Dawn The Next Morning

He went back at dawn on his way home to sleep, with the cold sharp enough to show breath and the slope empty except for a woman sweeping a step two doors up who did not look at him.

The sheet was still under the lower batten, damp at one corner but holding, with the date at the top in a clerk hand showing plain and the pencil line across the bottom hidden against the wood where only someone bending low would guess at it.

The old man from the evening before came down the slope with a girl beside him and went low to the stone again, and this time the girl glanced at the sheet and then at Lin Hao, and he shook his head once, barely, not to forbid but to ask for leaving alone, and she looked away.

Brack came up with his slate at about seven with rain still on his cap from the night before and stopped eleven feet from the board the way Lin Hao had.

“It held the night,” said Brack quietly.

“It did,” said Lin Hao.

“Then it will hold until the thirtieth,” said Brack. “Paste goes soft and battens lift and hands leave lines, and boards hold them all the same. That is what boards are for, and why no book will ever do instead.”

Lin Hao walked home to sleep with those words in him, and he dreamed of nothing, which was the best sleep he had had in about a month.

## What Wenna Entered That Week

No question was entered under the sixth line that week, because the second had to pass first in the book before anyone might use it, and the sixteenth was still six days before it.

Wenna spent her one question of the morning on whether the sheet at the corner needed anything from the office, and answered herself before he could speak.

“It needs nothing,” she said. “A mark under two empty boxes stays where it was left. The office has no instrument that can receive it. A boy takes the form down on the thirtieth with the sheet under the batten, and both go wherever old forms go, which is nowhere anyone can cite. That is the whole of what the corner teaches, and it is enough.”

He nodded, glad to hear her say it, because her saying it meant the office would not reach for the one thing in Ostara that could only live by being left alone.

*Three thousand one hundred and ninety-five days. Less three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight, the ninth of December of this year, is seven, and she did not come up the stair and went to the corner where a form has been face up since the seventh and left her own sheet under two ruled squares with a pencil line across the bottom of it in a hand of her own.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is seventy-six, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and seventy-five days, which is twenty-five months and twenty-five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

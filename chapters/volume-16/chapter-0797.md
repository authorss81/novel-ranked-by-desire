# The Cost In Three Rooms

Three thousand one hundred and eighty-one was the second of December and the ninth is day three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight, and three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight less three thousand one hundred and eighty-one is seven, and nine less two is seven by the month count.

## The First Room, The Office

Wenna Raut said it first, in the office, at about nine in the morning with the book shut between them and about nine people already in the corridor.

She had held the sixth line for seven days without entering any question under it, because the second of December had to pass before anyone might ask, and now the ninth had come and the door was open in a way it had never been.

“From this morning I am holding answers as well as rows,” she said. “A question comes in a person mouth and goes in the bound book with a date and an hour. My answer goes under it in writing, with a date and an hour against it. Both stay. A row may be added to and may not be taken from, which is the worst rule in the bound book.”

Lin Hao stood at the window with his coat still on because he had come straight from the night shift and had not yet been home to sleep.

“Say what it will do to you,” he said.

“It will make me into a person who writes all winter,” she said. “About four hundred papers are now answerable to anyone who climbs four flights. Anyone may ask whether a neighbour claim is there. Anyone may ask what the office holds about anyone. I shall enter every one in the words the person used, and I may not improve one word, and I shall be wrong in public about nine times a month the way I was wrong about one woman in October.”

She put her hand on the cover of the book without opening it.

“The thirtieth of March made me the mouth of what people say. The tenth of June made me the mouth of what the office holds. The sixteenth of August made me the mouth of what people say in answer. The second of December makes me the mouth of anyone at all. That is the cost in this room, and it is mine to carry and I will carry it.”

## The Second Room, A Laundry

Perla Quist said it second, in a laundry, at about eleven with steam under the door and the ruler squared against the edge of the bench and nine sheets under the third table.

Lin Hao had not gone up to ask her anything. He had gone to tell her, because she had given him four lines on her own paper with her own date and he owed her the knowledge of what those lines would now be open to.

“The sixth line is in,” he said. “From the second of December anyone may put one question about any paper. The question and the answer go in the bound book.”

Perla wiped the pencil on the cloth on the nail by the door and did not pick it up again.

“Then what I keep does not reach,” she said. “Nine sheets under a third table. About two hundred lines under a heading I ruled myself. A copy of a nine-year ledger in a pigeonhole. A fourth column with a heading on it since the twenty-first of February of the eighth year. None of it is a paper this office holds. None of it may be asked about. None of it goes in any book.”

She looked at him steadily, the way she looks at anything she means to be exact about.

“That is the cost in this room, and it is not a cost,” she said. “What it does not reach keeps me safe. A woman who keeps an index that may not be cited is safe for exactly as long as not one person can ask about it, and your sixth line does not change that, and I am glad of it.”

She picked the pencil up and squared it against the bench.

“You asked about the paper once and I answered once,” she said. “Do not ask about me now. Anyone may ask your office. Not one person here will answer.”

He smiled at that, tired to the bone after a night of washrooms and bins, because it was the closest she had ever come to telling him she trusted what he had built.

“I will not,” he said.

## The Third Room, At A Rail

The counterman said it third, at a rail, at about half past three in the afternoon with nothing at it and the shutter half down and the passage empty enough to hear rain on the window.

Lin Hao had come down to tell him face to face, because a man who reads the bottom of a page to about nine people a week ought to hear what is coming before it is on the page.

“From the thirtieth of December there will be six lines in the office hand at the foot of that sheet,” said Lin Hao. “Five instructions and one sentence a person said. The sixth says anyone may ask. I shall not read it to you now because you will read it at four o clock with the rest.”

The counterman put his thumb along the edge of the deal and left it there.

“Then I will have to read a line to people who will ask me what it means,” he said. “About nine a week now. More, after. They will ask what any person means. They will ask what any paper means. They will ask whether a neighbour claim is there, and I will read them the question and the answer in the order they stand, and I will not know what any of it means and not one person is entitled to tell me.”

He looked down the empty passage toward the window with rain on it.

“That is the cost in this room, and it is mine,” he said. “A claim in a city hand, spoken out loud, about nine times a week, for ever, and now questions with it. I have read four lines since the year began and could not tell you one thing about any of them. Now there will be six, and I will read all six.”

Lin Hao felt shame and gratitude together, the way he always did in front of that man, because the man asked for nothing and was given more to carry every quarter and carried it without praise.

“I am not going to thank you for it,” said Lin Hao, “because you told me once not to do it twice.”

“Then do not,” said the counterman.

## He Gave It Without Improving It

He went back up four flights in the evening and gave the sixth line to the clerk at about seven, with about nine people gone from the corridor and the lamp lit over the table.

Wenna Raut took it and read it back once, standing up, in the voice she reads everything in, with the date and the name, and entered nothing new because it was already entered on the second.

“Five instructions now, and the sixth line in the office hand,” she said. “Four of the earlier five are instructions and the fifth of them is a sentence a man said out loud in a room and is not an instruction at all. The July line and the August line and the October line are already on the sheet that stands on the rail. This one joins them on the thirtieth, and it is about anyone at all.”

She said the five apart the way the book separates them itself, and he listened without stopping her, because a clerk who may only enter what she is given is the only person in Ostara who can tell a record from a decision and she had just done it again.

He did not improve it. He had not improved any of the five, and he would not start on the last one, though improving it would have been easy and would have spared all three rooms a part of what they had just priced.

## What The Three Said Twice

Before he left, Wenna Raut put the pen across the book and said the one thing she had not yet said, with the lamp lit and the corridor empty enough to hear rain on the yard glass.

“About forty people will say it came from the office off a third court,” she said. “That is what Brack said would happen in about four years, and he was right about every figure he ever gave. When they say it, they will mean the questions as well as the answers, and they will be right, and not one of the four hundred will ever be told why the two inches went quiet twice.”

In the laundry that morning Perla Quist had said her second thing after he turned to go, with her hand on the bench edge and steam going past the high window that is never cleaned.

“The ninth sheet will stay nine sheets,” she had said. “Whatever your book fills with, mine does not grow. Anyone may ask your office and no hand here will copy any of it. That is how an index stays an index and not a list.”

And at the rail the counterman had said his second thing after the cost, with his thumb still on the wood and the passage empty.

“I will read the questions the way I read the answers,” he had said. “In the order they stand. Whoever is standing there will hear both, and whoever said the question will hear her own words out of my mouth, and that will be the first time most have ever heard themselves read back.”

Lin Hao carried all three second sayings down four flights with the first three, six sentences in all, and he knew he would hear them again every morning at that rail for ever, once the thirty-sixth went up.

He left the office at about half past seven and walked down four flights with the stair empty and the cold coming up from below, and he knew all three costs by heart and could do nothing with any of them except carry them into the thirtieth.

*Three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight days. Less three thousand one hundred and eighty-one, the second of December of this year, is seven, and the cost of a sixth line was said out loud in three rooms before it is done by three people and he gave it to the clerk at the end and did not improve it.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is sixty-nine, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and sixty-eight days, which is twenty-five months and eighteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

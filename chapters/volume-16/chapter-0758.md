# The Front Door Fills Up

Two thousand nine hundred and twenty was the eleventh of March and the seventeenth is day two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six, and two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six less two thousand nine hundred and twenty is six, and seventeen less eleven is six by the month count.

## Eight Working Days, And A Clerk Who Counts

Wenna Raut counted them herself, at the counter, and she did not ask anybody to count them with her, and she did not say the number out loud until the eighth day, and then she said it out loud twice.

Eleven pieces of paper came through that front door in eight working days.

Eleven is a count of pieces of paper and nothing else. It is not a count of claims, it is not a count of people, and it is not a count of kitchens; and about nine people came through that door carrying those eleven pieces, which is a different count of a different sort of thing, and the day somebody in this building carries one of those numbers inside one of the others is the day that door is finished and so is this office.

“There are four hundred and thirty rows in that book that were in it on the first of this month,” said Wenna Raut, “and there are eleven more in it now, and the eleven are the only rows in nine years of that book that have anything else near them.”

“What else.”

“The hour.” She put the pen across the book. “On four of the last eight days somebody stood at that counter for between nine and about forty minutes and said things, and on four of the last eight days there is a second hand-mark against the hour in that book that has never been there before and is not a description and is not an address and is not a figure.”

“Then somebody has been in the room.”

“Somebody has been in the room four times in eight days and each time they have not left anything that may be put in a field and each time they have not asked for anything and each time they have been told, correctly, that they have not been told anything.”

## The Instruction Working For The First Time In The Wrong Direction

He got it wrong for three days and then he got it right on the eighth, and what he got right was this.

It is worth reading out again in full, because it has been standing in that book for nine months and not one person in this building has read it since the morning it went in. It says that every piece of paper received through that front door is to be entered as received, with a date against it, and with a description of the person who brought it, written in the words that person used.

It has been standing in that book for nine months and it has been obeyed every day of those nine months.

And on the eighth day of this month, for the first time in nine years, the instruction was being obeyed in a way that produced a person.

Not a description of one. A person, standing at a counter, for about twenty minutes, saying things out loud into a room where none of it is being written down.

And there is a field for it. The fifth one. About what was done to a person, filled out of the mouth of the person it is about, and it has been empty for nine years and it is empty now, and it is going to stay empty, because the only way to fill it is to ask a person a question, and this office may not ask anybody anything, and has never asked anybody anything, and there is nothing in this city on which it could.

So the rows multiply and the field stays empty and about nine people stand in a corridor in a building where about nine were standing a month ago.

“You have built a door,” said Wenna Raut.

“I have built a room with a counter in it and a corridor and a stair at the back.”

“You have built a door in the sense that there is a thing people now come to, and a thing people come to is not a room any more.”

She turned the book a quarter so the rows were the right way up.

“I will say the rest of it and I am spending nothing on it, because it is not a question. Four hundred and thirty rows in nine years and a clerk who only enters what she is given. Eleven rows in eight days and a queue. Those two numbers are the only argument anybody in this city will ever be able to make with me about it, and when somebody does make it I am going to hand them the book and let them read it.”

## A Queue Is A Public Room

There is no door on this building onto a street.

There is a door at the top of a stair off a third court and the stair comes out of a court and the court goes to two more courts, and for nine years that has meant that not one person could get in here by accident and that was the whole of the design and every part of it was correct.

On the sixth day there were three people in the corridor. On the eighth there were about nine, and the corridor is about four feet wide and goes past the counter and goes on to a fire door that is never opened, and there is nowhere to stand in it that is not also being looked at by a person waiting behind you.

He worked that out on the eighth day at about eleven in the morning, standing at the counter with a piece of paper in his hand, and it took him about nine seconds and it has been true for every other counter in this city for about nine hundred years.

An office with a queue is a public room. There is no version of this building in which a queue is not a public room, and about nine people who have found this place out from each other, in a fortnight, from a name that was read out in a passage by a man who is paid nothing, are going to stand in a four-foot corridor about twice a week for the rest of their lives.

He did not build that. He built the opposite of it, on purpose, over four years, with a drawer and a bundle and a column and a woman with a pencil and twenty-six things on a table that nothing is going to be done with.

## Which Counter

There is a man at the end of the corridor at about twenty past eleven on the ninth day with a piece of paper in his hand, and he is not holding it up, and he has been standing there since about eleven, and he is not going to come to the counter because there are about six people between him and it and he has come once before and did not want to be looked at in front of anybody doing it twice.

He came before, in the November, and he stood at that counter for about four minutes and a clerk told him — correctly, and in a clerk’s voice, and without any pleasure in it — that nothing had happened to him.

He had not told anybody that. He had taken it away with him and had not put it down anywhere and had not written it in the bottom two inches of anything, and had told no person.

“You did that,” he said, to the room in general, and not to anybody in it. “That building. That is this building. I know it is this building because I have been at that counter before and the woman at it has got the same voice as the woman who said it to me.”

“You have been at this counter,” said Wenna Raut.

“In the November. And she told me nothing happened to me, in this room, at that counter, and I have gone home about nine times since and told no one, and I am not angry at anybody and I have come up four flights to find out which counter it was so that I know which one it was.”

“The counter under the window in a passage off Cordwain,” said Wenna Raut. “It is not in this building and it does not belong to it and the man who runs it is paid nothing and has been there nine years.”

He stood in the corridor and worked out how a sentence travelled from a room with no door onto a street to a desk in a passage two streets away, and he could not, and he had been getting it wrong for five weeks.

It was not a sheet and it was not a column and it was not a page. It was a woman at a counter in November saying no to a man correctly in a clerk’s voice, and there was no instrument anywhere in the chain between that and a man standing in a corridor in March with a piece of paper in his hand, and there never had been, and there was nothing to withdraw and not one person to blame.

Every claim in this city waits to be told where it came from. That has always been true and it was never a problem, because it meant that a person could sit on a thing for as long as they liked, and take it to a counter, and put it down themselves, in their own hand, and no person could take it off them by knowing.

And this one has already been told. Somebody has said out loud, in a passage, about nine times a week, for five weeks, where to go.

What is standing in that corridor is not a claim. It is the telling.

*Two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six days. Less two thousand nine hundred and twenty, the eleventh of March of this year, is six, and eleven pieces of paper came through that front door in eight working days, and about nine people came with them, and a man stood in a corridor at the end of it asking which counter had told him nothing happened to him.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is seventy-seven, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seventy-seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and six days, which is sixteen months and twenty-six days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

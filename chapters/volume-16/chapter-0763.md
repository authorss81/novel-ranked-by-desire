# The Fortieth Off A Person

Two thousand nine hundred and fifty-three was the fourteenth of April and the twenty-first is day two thousand nine hundred and sixty, and two thousand nine hundred and sixty less two thousand nine hundred and fifty-three is seven, and twenty-one less fourteen is seven by the month count.

## Half Past Five, And Four Bays

He came off shift at half past five and got to the bottom of that stair at about ten past six in the morning carrying nothing, which was deliberate and took him most of the night to arrange.

The light was coming up grey and cold off the yard and there was not another person in that corridor except the two of them and a woman in the third bay who had been asleep since about eleven the night before.

“Two flights,” said Tessa Okonkwo.

“I counted them.”

“You have counted it three times and every time you have said the number and never the seconds,” she said. “I keep the seconds. I have kept them since the sixth year and nobody has ever wanted them and they are the only figure in that building that has never once been wrong.”

She had a blue apron on with the ends of the strings gone and a pen behind one ear and her sleeves up past the elbow, and she was standing at the door of the sluice at the end of the corridor with her fingers round the edge of it.

“Do not wake her,” she said. “Not for anything. She came in three nights ago and has not slept since, and if she is asleep now then she is asleep. I have got about forty minutes before the round comes round and I am going to spend about nine of them on you and I am telling you what for before I start.”

## A Stone Sink At The End Of It

The sluice is about eight feet by six and the floor falls towards a drain in the middle of it, and there is a stone sink along one wall with two taps that go into a lead pipe and come out again in the corridor.

There is a shelf of boiled linen folded to the height of two hands and a bar across the window and grey light coming in over the top of it. It smells of carbolic and wet cloth and it is about eleven degrees warmer in there than in the corridor, and through the floor there is the sound of a building that has not stopped.

The skin is gone from the base of the nails on both of her hands and has been gone for years, which is what soap and hot water do to a person who does not wear gloves indoors in a city where the top landing of a branch office has never had water on it.

It is the only mark she has and it is not a mark anybody in this city would think to call one.

“Sit down if you want to sit down,” she said. “I am not going to hurry you and I am not going to be tender and I am not going to do this slowly to be kind.”

He took the upturned crate by the door, which is where anybody sits in that room, and the two of them could hear the woman in the third bay breathing.

## What A Fortieth Is

“I take a fortieth off a person,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and I keep it.”

“That is all of it.”

“That is all of what it is. Not all of what it costs.” She turned a tap and let it run and shut it off again, the way a person does while they are talking. “It goes in as a figure on a chart in a book in this building, and the book goes in a drawer in this building, and the figure is a figure and it is not a name and there is no line under it with a name on it.”

“You do not itemise it.”

“I do not itemise it and I have never itemised it and there is not going to be an itemised column anywhere, and if anybody in this city ever invents one I will take it off the wall myself.”

“You have done it about four hundred times.”

“About four hundred and something, and I have counted them the way I count everything, and I am not going to give you that number this morning because you would write it down somewhere and then it would be a figure about four hundred women in a city that has no idea what it is carrying.”

“Does it keep.”

“It keeps,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “That is the part people get wrong. It keeps, and it goes on keeping, and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and that is why I will not have a conversation about it at ten past six in the morning.”

## The Rule Of That Corridor, Which Is Not Named

Here is what that building does, and it has always done it, and it has never once been written down.

A thing that is taken from a body is taken before anybody asks anything about it. It is not itemised. The person it is taken from is the only person in that building who is not told about it.

Not the ward, and not the round, and not the clerk who writes the figure, and not the man who comes up two flights at ten past six in the morning.

Her.

And there is one remedy for that in this city, and it is not a form and it is not a column and it is not a thing anybody in a division building can be given a piece of paper about.

The only remedy is another body.

“There are four of us in this city who can take a share off somebody and hold it for them,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and all four of us are in a ward, and two of them are over sixty, and one of them is me, and I have been doing it for longer than that building has been mine, and I have never once offered it to a woman who came in off a street.”

## The Question He Is Allowed To Ask

He had it by the second landing of his own building at about eleven o’clock the night before, and he had it in the order it was going to be said.

He is the only person in Ostara entitled to put a single question to that office. He has used it once, in December, about a page.

He used it now.

“Can a person be told what was taken out of them.”

Tessa Okonkwo did not answer it for about nine seconds. In those nine seconds she shut the tap off properly and dried her hands on the back of her apron and looked at the drain in the floor.

“No,” she said. “Not in this city. And not in any city I have been in.”

“That is the whole of what I came up two flights for.”

“That is the first half of it and you have not got the second half and you are going to want it, and I am going to give it to you, because you will work it out badly on your own and then I will have to listen to it for a year.”

She turned round.

“The second half is that telling somebody puts it in a second body,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “That is all of it. It comes out of one and it goes into the next one, and the next one carries it, and that one has to hand it on. There is not a person alive who has taken a share and then put it down again anywhere else. That is not a rule. That is a body.”

## What She Would Not Let Him Do

“You could ask her.”

“No.”

“You could ask the woman in the third bay whether she would like to know. You have said her name four times in nine years to about four people and there is not one officer of this city entitled to object to you asking her.”

“I would object to me asking,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “You have not thought about the second woman. If I ask her then she knows. And if she knows then it is in a room with two other women in it, and she has not slept for three nights, and by the spring she will be on a round list of people who know.”

“A list.”

“A round list is a list, and a list is the only thing in this city that has ever gone after anybody who could not read, and I am not putting her on one, and neither are you going to, and if you ask me again I am going to say no in a different voice from this one.”

He had not thought about the second woman.

“No,” he said.

“No,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “And you will not ask me again, and I have not said no to you about anything until this minute, and I am aware of what that costs and I am going to price it once and then never again.”

“I am not going to ask you again.”

“Then here it is. There is a man in this city who keeps an office where nothing happens, and he is good at it, and he has been at it nine years, and I have watched him from a distance for four of them.”

She put the pen out from behind her ear and put it back again.

“He cannot ask anybody anything. I cannot ask anybody anything. Between the two of you this city has about four hundred women carrying something that was taken out of them, and not one of them knows, and the two of you are the only two people alive who could change that, and you are both forbidden, and neither of you is going to break it for about the same reason.”

“What reason.”

“Because we are both frightened of being the person who did it,” said Tessa Okonkwo. “That is the whole of it. There is no other reason and it is not noble and I have never once pretended it was.”

## The Woman In The Third Bay

The corridor light came up off the yard while she was saying that and put a grey edge along the four bays, and in the third bay a woman who came in three nights ago turned over on her side and said four words of it in her sleep.

They both stopped.

Not one person in that building said anything at all for about four seconds, and in those four seconds Lin Hao understood the whole of that corridor better than he has understood anything in nine years, and what he understood was that it was about her.

That woman has been asleep since about eleven. She will wake at about half past seven and she will be asked how she slept, and she will answer that question, and the answer will be entered in a chart, and she will go out of that building by a different door from the one she came in by.

And she will never in her life know that a woman in a sluice at the end of that corridor took a fortieth off her on some night in the last year and wrote a figure in a book about it.

“Do not go in there,” said Tessa Okonkwo, without turning round. “She is awake now and she will want to know why there is a man in the sluice at ten past six in the morning, and I will have to say you are here about a laundry, and then in four weeks she will ask me what laundry.”

“Then let me go.”

“No. Stay where you are. I have got about nine minutes and I have not spent them on an argument.”

She came back across the corridor and stopped in the doorway about two feet from him, and she was not being tender and she was not going slowly.

“You have been up two flights with nothing in your hands and you asked me one question and I have answered it and you did not like the answer, and you are not going to be able to do one single thing about it, and I can see that from here.”

“Yes.”

“So we are going to do this, and you are going to stop having a face about it,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “because I have been tired for four years and this is the part of my work that is mine and not anybody else’s.”

“I am rank one,” he said. “I have had a spent chest since the fourth year. I have no permission of any kind and a shift I asked for and there is not one instrument in this city you can point at me with.”

“Good,” she said. “Do not talk about your chest.”

She had the apron off over her head in about four seconds and dropped it on the shelf of linen, which is what everybody in that building does, and then she got his shirt open at the front with two fingers of one hand and then with both hands and did not take it off him.

She got her own fingers round him below the belt and pulled, and looked down at what she had hold of the way a person looks at a chart they already know the answer to.

“Same as a fortnight ago,” she said. “You have got slower and you have not told me about it and I am not going to ask you about it.”

Then she went down onto one knee on the drain cover and took all of him into her mouth, and it was not quick, and her tongue went along the underside and back and stayed there a while, and she swallowed what was in her mouth before she took any more of it, and she looked up at him over him twice while he could not do one thing with either hand.

He got a fist into the bunch of the apron at her throat and she did not stop for it and she did not slow for it either.

## Nine Seconds She Would Not Let Him Help With

She came off him and got up over him on her knees and pushed her trousers down with one hand and got them off one at a time and did not bother with the rest of what she was wearing, and she was naked from the waist up on a wet floor in a room eight feet by six with a woman asleep forty feet away, and neither of them had woken her and neither of them was going to.

“Now,” she said.

She set him against her and came down onto him in one movement and took all of it, and the noise she made in that room was not a small one, and the tiled wall gave it back twice.

“That is about four hundred and something,” she said. “Not the figures. The other count. You want the number of it and I am not going to give you the number of it, and you know exactly why I am not going to, and you have been trying to get it out of me with your face for the last four minutes.”

“I have not asked you for anything.”

“You have been asking me with your face since about half past six.”

She rode him with one hand at his hip and the other braced against the side of the sink and worked him up off the drain cover and put him down and did it again, and twice in about nine seconds she changed the angle of it without warning him first, and both times she looked up first to see whether he had worked out what she had done.

“There,” she said. “That is a man who has asked me nothing for nine minutes.”

“I came up two flights about a woman asleep in the third bay.”

“You came up two flights about a woman in a ward being told what was taken out of her,” said Tessa Okonkwo, “and you have had that answer and it is no. You are the only man in this city who can be given an answer by a person, and what you have just been given is the one that everybody in this city has and not one person had ever said out loud.”

“You may lie to yourself about the other nine things you came up here wanting. You cannot lie about that one.”

He put his cock back into her and she thanked him for it out loud, in the voice she uses over about nine people on a round, and she took him deeper than the drain cover allowed and put her forehead against the tiled wall.

“Stay,” she said. “Do not move. I am going to do about nine seconds of it myself and I do not want any help with any of it.”

She did about nine seconds of it herself, and he stayed where he was and did not move, and when she had finished she took all of him and then rode him out to the end of it with her hips and her teeth set in her own lip.

He went in her with her name in his ear and she held him there and counted the whole of it out loud, the way she counts everything, and she took all of that too and did not let go of him for about four seconds afterwards.

There was semen on the inside of her left thigh and on her own wrist and neither of them wiped anything, because there is a cloth on a hook by that door and it has been the same cloth since the building was put up.

## What He Is Left Holding At Ten Past Seven

She rinsed the sink and dried her hands and put her apron back on and the pen went behind her ear again, and it had taken about nine minutes in all and she had said she would use about nine.

The woman in the third bay was awake and had been for some time, and Tessa Okonkwo went in and asked her how she had slept and the woman said it had been all right.

The whole of that took about four minutes and he stood in the corridor and listened to it, and it is the ordinary sound of an ordinary morning in that building, and it goes on four times a day in four wards.

He went down the stairs in five and had to stop on the second landing, because his chest has been spent since the fourth year and there is nothing anybody in this city can do about that.

And he stood there about four minutes with what she had given him.

A thing taken out of a body goes into another body, and telling somebody does not end it, it moves it.

The only two people alive who could put that right are two people who will not, and neither of them is doing it for a reason anybody could defend at a hearing.

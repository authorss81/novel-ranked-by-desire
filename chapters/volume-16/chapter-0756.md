# A Man With A Slate

Two thousand nine hundred and seven was the twenty-eighth of February and the fourth is day two thousand nine hundred and thirteen, and two thousand nine hundred and thirteen less two thousand nine hundred and seven is six, and four less twenty-eight is six by the month count.

## Nine Stools And Nine Districts Behind Them

The lane is two streets off Cordwain and it is about nine feet wide and it is not a street anybody has a name for, and there are nine stools down one side of it and nine districts behind the other side of it, and a man sits at the head of them with a slate under his arm and twelve books on a shelf behind him that do not belong to any district in the city.

Emil Brack is paid a grain a head. A grain is nothing. He has been sitting at the head of that lane for nine years and he takes about nine figures a morning before eleven and he has never once been paid for anything he said to anybody off the stools, and he has never once asked to be.

Lin Hao came down the lane at about ten past ten with his hands in his pockets because it was raining and because he had come down that lane four times since the thirtieth of December and had gotten the same answer four times.

He got it a fifth time.

## The Fifth Time, And What Has Changed

“You have not changed your mind,” said Emil Brack, before he had got to the second stool.

“No.”

“Good. I never expected you to and I told you in the lane in the third month of last year and I told you on the twenty-fourth of December and I told you twice in between and I have been right four times and paid nothing for any of them.”

“You have been right four times.”

“I have been right about every figure I have given you this year and that is not a boast, it is the arrangement.” Emil Brack put the slate against his ribs the way he does when he is about to say the whole of something in one go, and then he took it off his ribs again, which he does not often do. “Sit down. No. Don’t sit down, you have forty seconds and I have nine figures before eleven and I want you standing up for this part.”

“Why standing up.”

“Because I am going to say a thing I have never said at that lane and I am not going to say it sitting down with a slate on my knees.”

## Nowhere Left To Put It

“The thing you told me in the third month of last year was that you did not want it printed,” said Lin Hao. “And the reason was that a claim everybody knows came from somewhere waits to be told, and it waits about four years, and by the time anybody is told there is no one left who needs telling.”

“That is about right and I have said it better than that.”

“You have said it four times.”

“I have said it four times because you have given me four chances to say it and every one of you was standing at that head of that lane hoping I would say it a fifth time in a different direction,” said Emil Brack. “Here is the fifth direction. Here is the whole of what has changed since the thirtieth of December and I have been sitting here for six weeks with nothing to do about it.”

He turned the slate over and did not write on it.

“There is nowhere left to put it,” he said. “That is all. That is the entire sentence and it has taken me six weeks and I am giving it to you free.”

“Say the rest.”

“The second column is full and you have said it will stay at four lines.”

“The bound Register has your name at the head of it and you write whatever you like in it. The counterman in Cordwain reads out whatever is under his hand and he is not yours to ask. The woman with the pencil will not take it and has said so five times and is right five times.”

“And the woman who cannot read has had four words on the back of a sheet in her coat for about a year, and there is nobody in this city who can put a second sentence underneath them, including her, because she does not know what they say.”

He stopped and looked up the lane.

“There was a month in which I could have told you one of those was a place to put it. It was not there in the end. It is not one of the nine things you have listed and it is the only one that mattered.”

## Both Clocks, In Two Sentences, In His Own Mouth

“Now the two numbers,” said Emil Brack, “and I am going to keep them apart, because the last time a man in a white plate put one of my numbers into the other one’s sentence I heard about it a week later and it cost him a fortnight.”

“All right.”

“By the end of this week it will be in about four kitchens, and by the end of the month it will be in about nine.”

“And the other one.”

“The other one is four years and it is a different figure and it is the only one I have ever actually been frightened of.” He turned the slate over again, in his own hands, and did not write on it that time either.

“In about four years the bottom two inches under that striking will have gone quiet in about four hundred kitchens. About forty people will say that it came from the office off the third court, and about four hundred people will hear it in a kitchen and believe it, and not one of the four hundred will ever be told why it went quiet.”

“They will believe it because it is printed.”

“They will believe it because a man with a desk and no wage read it out of a page to them in the ordinary voice he reads everything in,” said Emil Brack. “That is not printing. Printing is what this office does to about two thousand counters four times a year. What that desk does is more than printing and it costs him nothing, and I have watched him do it for nine years and there has never once been anything on that page he could not read out.”

## The Thing He Wants Said Out Loud

“Do you want it printed,” said Lin Hao.

“No,” said Emil Brack. “I have never wanted it printed and I have said so four times and I will say it a fifth, and I am going to tell you the reason one more time and then it is finished, and the reason is that I have been at a market end nine years and I have watched what happens to a street when a street is told where a thing comes from.”

He put the slate down on the stool beside him and left it there, which he has not done in nine years in front of anybody.

“Not the rule. Everybody can ignore a rule. The place. A rule you decide about. A place you go and look at.”

“And when people are told that a place is where a thing happens, about forty of them will say it in about nine days and about four hundred will hear it in about four weeks. Every one of those four hundred will go and look for the building, and the building will be there, and it will not do anything for them, and they will not be told that.”

He picked the slate up.

“That is why I did not want it printed and it is the only reason I have got.”

## What Lin Hao Is Left Holding

He stood at the head of nine stools with the rain coming off the eaves into the lane and did not say thank you, because a man with a slate is not thanked for being right and has never once asked to be.

What he wanted, standing there, was for one person in this city who is not behind a counter and is not a clerk and is not a woman with a pencil to tell him that the two inches at the foot of that wall are going to open again.

About nine people a week get down low enough to read them. About four hundred people have ever written in them. And for five weeks there has been nothing in the bottom two inches at all.

There is no one in Ostara to tell him that. Not the woman who sweeps them at ten past seven, who has never been asked and is not going to be. Not the boy who puts the board up. Not the counterman, who is paid nothing and would say yes to a question about anything, because that question is not one anybody is entitled to put to him, and because a man behind a counter answers what he is asked and is never asked what he thinks.

“You are going to stand at that corner again this evening,” said Emil Brack.

“Yes.”

“You are going to stand at that corner again for about four more weeks and then you are going to stop, because a man who stands at a corner five evenings a week for nine years is a man somebody will ask about, and there is a version of this where you are the one with a name in a column and I would not wish it on you and I have never wished you anything.”

“I will stop when it opens.”

“You will stop when you have to,” said Emil Brack, “and you know the difference and I know the difference, and that is the whole of what there is between us.”

He got the slate under his arm and stood up off the stool.

“And one more thing, free, because I have not taken a grain off anybody today and it is a wet morning.” He did not raise his voice, because a man at the head of nine stools does not raise his voice. “About nine people will come up that stair before the month is out.”

“What stair.”

“The one you go up four times a week behind a girl with linen,” said Emil Brack. “About nine people, in about nine kitchens, and they will come because they have run out of anybody else to ask. They will not come for you. They will come because there is nothing left between them and that counter except the stair.”

He went down the lane with the slate under his arm.

*Two thousand nine hundred and thirteen days. Less two thousand nine hundred and seven, the twenty-eighth of February of this year, is six, and a man with a slate said in a lane that there is nowhere left to put a thing he wants unprinted, and gave both clocks in two sentences without borrowing one figure from the other.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is sixty-four, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last sixty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is four hundred and ninety-three days, which is sixteen months and thirteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# A Row May Not Be Taken From

Three thousand and one hundred and seven was the eighteenth of September and the twenty-fourth is day three thousand and one hundred and thirteen, and three thousand and one hundred and thirteen less three thousand and one hundred and seven is six, and twenty-four less eighteen is six by the month count.

## The Sentence He Brought On The Twenty-Fourth

He did not say it at the counter.

He wrote it on a piece of pencil paper at about half past nine in the morning and put it on the wood face down, and he had thought about that for nine days and the thinking was about a rule that says a person’s words are entered when a person says a thing at that counter, and he is a person, and he was not going to be a sentence on a page four times a year because he had been careless with his mouth in a room.

There were about nine people in the corridor behind him, which is what the corridor holds most mornings now.

He turned the paper round.

“That is not what I want entered,” he said. “That is what I want entered.”

It was about four lines long. It said that the two words at the head of the second column on the standing sheet are printed on a form made by a works department two streets from a laundry and belong to that department.

It said that a line under those two words on a works department docket takes the name of the person carrying the sheet and not the name of a person who has been hurt, and that the fourth line in that column was entered by a clerk in good faith under a heading that does not mean what this office has been reading it to mean since the eighth of October of the first year.

It had no name in it. He had taken about four days to write a sentence with no name in it.

## Nine Words In A Clerk's Mouth

Wenna Raut read it twice and then put it down on the table at an angle and did not square it.

“No,” she said.

“You have not read the whole of the reason.”

“There is no reason that makes that sentence safe to put in that book,” said Wenna Raut. “What goes in that book stays in that book.”

That was nine words and she did not add anything to them and she did not take anything off them.

“And you cannot take it out afterwards,” said Lin Hao.

“No.”

“You said what the rule was, not what it does.”

“What it does is that it is permanent the moment my pen touches it,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am the hand, and I cannot put a thing in and come back in about four years and take it out, and if there were an instrument in this city that could, it would not be mine.”

## Why That Sentence Cannot Go In At All

“The fourth line has a woman’s name in it.”

“I know whose name is in the fourth line,” said Wenna Raut.

“If that goes on that page, then the page says, in this office’s hand, that a works department’s routing column has been in use in this city for nine years, and about two thousand counters will hold the paper, and about nine people a week have the bottom of it read out loud to them.”

“Yes.”

“And every one of those nine people who can read will be able to work out that the fourth line in it is a person, and the only person who can tell them which person is a woman with a pencil about four hundred yards up a street two streets wide.”

“Yes,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have been the mouth of this office since the thirtieth of March, and I am not going to be the mouth of that.”

## The Six Words He Found Instead

He had one more piece of pencil paper in his coat and he put it on the wood without looking at it.

He had been carrying it since the eighteenth and he had written it at about eleven at night on the second landing of his own building, and it was the only version of the sentence he had found that carried no name anywhere.

The two printed words at the head of a column on that sheet are the words of the department that printed the form.

Six words and a period and a date at the top of it, and nothing else, and no name, and no works department, and no laundry, and no street.

Wenna Raut read it and then read the other one again, side by side, for about nine seconds.

“You have found the only thing that could go on that page,” she said.

“I have found the only thing that could go on that page.”

“And it is useless.”

“It is useless and it is permanent,” said Lin Hao, “and there is a difference and the difference is that useless can be taken back in about four years and permanent cannot.”

## Why She Was Not Going To Write It Down

She put the pen down across the book and did not pick it up.

“No,” she said. “I am not going to enter that.”

“You entered four lines this year that I wrote and you were not asked to check any of them.”

“I entered four lines this year because a person said four things out loud at that counter and I may only enter what I am given,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the whole of my trade and I have done it nine years and I have never once entered a sentence this office decided to say.”

She turned the six words round on the wood until the date was at the top again.

“There are five lines in that page in this office’s hand and I could put the number on it now. Four of them have a man’s name under them and the fifth is a sentence a man said out loud in a corridor on the nineteenth of July, and three of the four are decisions and the fourth is a record, and there is a difference between those two things that this city has never written down and that I have had a pencil in my hand for nine years.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It is the reason and I have given you it out loud before you asked me for it,” said Wenna Raut. “If I write those six words then there are six lines in that page in this office’s hand and I cannot tell anybody afterwards which of them a person said and which of them this room decided, because I am the hand and the mouth and the two have been the same woman since the thirtieth of March.”

She put two fingers on the paper.

“You have brought me a sentence with no person in it,” said Wenna Raut, “and that is the only reason I cannot put it in, and I have never in nine years refused a line for that reason and I am not going to start today with four days to a quarter-end.”

## Read It Back And Tell Me Whether I Have Got It Right

“Then I am not going to have it on the page at all,” he said, “which means I am going to have to be sure of the wording, and I wrote it at eleven o’clock at night on a landing with no water on it.”

“That is not a reason I can act on.”

“It is the only one I have got.”

He put it on the wood in front of her.

“Read it back,” he said. “Not for the book. For me. Tell me whether I have got it right, and then it is yours and I am not carrying it round this city on the wording of a man I am not allowed to check.”

Wenna Raut looked at the corridor behind him and then back at the paper.

“There are about nine people in that corridor,” she said.

“There are about nine people in that corridor.”

“That is your doing and not mine and I am going to do it anyway, because you have asked me for the reading and it is what I do.”

## What About Four People Did With Six Words

She read it standing up, in the order it stood on the paper, with the date, in the voice she reads everything in and at the volume she reads everything at, and it took her about nine seconds.

Four of them had it.

Not all nine, because four of them do not read and had gone down the stair at the sound of a pen. Four of the nine had six words about the printing of a form in their heads by the time that room emptied, and about four of them had heard it twice because she had read it back a second time to be sure.

She put the paper face down on the table and squared it and did not enter it.

“That was me,” she said.

“That was you.”

“I read everything back. I have read the thirtieth of March back and the tenth of June back and the sixteenth of August back and I am going to read whatever else comes down that counter for as long as I am in this room, and there is nothing in this city that lets me stop and nothing in this city that would let you stop me either.”

She put the pen across the book.

“And you have not got it on two thousand counters. You have got it in about four heads, in about eleven seconds, in a corridor, for nothing, and one of the two of us wrote the six words and neither of us chose the room.”

## Four Hundred Yards Down A Hill

There is nothing in this city between a corridor about four feet wide and the bottom two inches of a wall.

He worked it out on the second landing of his own building that afternoon with the six words still standing under the four sentences and above the striking and about four people carrying them down a slope in their heads.

They will be at a counter within about a week and at a table within about a month, and there is about nine people a week in this city who get down low enough to read four inches at the foot of that wall, and the four inches have had nothing new in them since the eleventh of March.

Nothing in this city could stop any of those four people, and a person cannot be asked to put six words back into their head, and he wrote the six himself, and he gave them to the only person in this city who could not refuse them.

He went down and did the third floor and then the fourth and then the stairwell and the bin room, and he stopped twice, and the chest is the same as it has been since the fourth year.

*Three thousand and one hundred and thirteen days. Less three thousand and one hundred and seven, the eighteenth of September of this year, is six, and a clerk refused to enter six words about the printing of a form and read them back standing up to about nine people in a corridor, and about four of them have them, and there are still four lines in this office’s hand at the foot of that page and not five.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is eighty-four, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and ninety-three days, which is twenty-three months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

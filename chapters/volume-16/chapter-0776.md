# The Second Denial

Three thousand and forty-two was the thirteenth of July and the nineteenth is day three thousand and forty-eight, and three thousand and forty-eight less three thousand and forty-two is six, and nineteen less thirteen is six by the month count.

## Nine Feet Of Dressed Stone And About Two Thousand Counters

Since the sixteenth of August of the eighth year this office has carried a sentence in its own hand at the foot of the page that goes out four times a year.

It says that no page of this office and no stone in this city is connected to any other, and that a resemblance is not a figure.

It is the fourth of four instructions in that book and it is the only one of the four that was written to put a thing right rather than to get a thing done, and a man’s name is under it, and it has been face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain at four o’clock four times a year since, and a man behind a counter under a window has read it out loud to about nine people a week every time.

About two thousand counters hold a page with that sentence at the bottom of it.

It is the only denial this city has ever printed, and it is printed in a building with no door onto a street, and it has never once been asked for.

## A Man At The Counter On The Nineteenth

He came up the stair at about twenty past ten on the nineteenth of July and he had a piece of pencil paper in his hand with about nine words on it and he did not sit down, and he said it at that counter before anybody had asked him anything.

“A building off a third court keeps the stone at the foot of the wall.”

There were about nine people in that corridor behind him and the sentence went past all of them and came to the counter in about nine seconds.

Wenna Raut took the pen up.

“No,” said Lin Hao.

“I have to,” said Wenna Raut. “She said it here, at that counter, in front of nine people, and a line in the front of that book is standing up and has been standing up since the thirtieth of March, and it is the only line in it that has ever asked this office for a sentence.”

“It is not true.”

“That line has never asked whether a sentence is true,” said Wenna Raut. “It never did and there is no version of it that could. If I leave it out I have improved it, and improving it is the one thing I am not allowed to do.”

## The Word He Left Out

He had it in his hand for about four seconds and then he asked his question, which is the only one he is entitled to put to that room.

“Where did you get it.”

“From the foot of the page,” said the man. “A man read it out in a passage in the last week of March and in the last week of June, and I have it written down here in my own pencil, and I have had it in my coat since March, and I have said it to about four people and not one of them has told me I have it wrong.”

He put the pencil paper on the wood.

“There is a denial on that page about the bottom two inches of a wall,” he said, “and I have written down the version of it that was in the passage, and the man at that desk read it out with the building in it and not with the wall, and I have carried that for sixteen weeks and I would like it stopped if it can be stopped.”

There is nothing in this city that can stop a sentence that a man has heard out loud.

## She Writes It In His Words

She wrote for about forty seconds in the words that were used, with the date at the top of the line and the hour against the date, and she did not change one word of it and she did not add one, and she did not put in the words *no page* or *no stone*, because neither of them had been said.

Then she squared the book to the edge of the table with both hands.

“It is under the four sentences and above the striking and in no column anywhere on it,” she said, “and the page goes out at four o’clock on the thirtieth of September, which is seventy-one days from now, and about two thousand counters will hold it, and a man who is paid nothing will read it out loud at a rail to about nine people a week.”

“I know what you have done.”

“What I have done is write down nine words a person said out loud at that counter in this office’s hand and put a date on it,” said Wenna Raut. “That is the instruction of the thirtieth of March and it has been standing in that book since March and it is not mine and I could not have refused it.”

## The Only Answer That Was Available To Her

“Why could you not have read him the denial.”

Wenna Raut did not pick the pen up.

“I could have,” she said. “I have the denial in this room in the front of that book and I could have said it out loud at that counter to that man in about nine seconds and it would have been correct and it would have been in this office’s hand that somebody said it.”

“That would have been better.”

“That would have been me putting a sentence into the mouth of a person who had not said it,” said Wenna Raut, “and you have spent nine years making sure that I never do that, and you have not once done it yourself, and if I started doing it this morning you would take that book away from me and you would be right to.”

She squared it again.

“You want the denial and what this office has is a room where people say things out loud and I write them down. Those two have been in the same city since the eighth of October of the first year and there is not one form in Ostara that puts them in the same sentence.”

## Four Other People In Four Feet

Four of the people in that corridor repeated it back to each other while she was writing, which takes about forty seconds, and they repeated it in the wrong order.

It went out of that room in a form that is not the form it came in with: a building and a stone and a wall, with the building first where the man said it last, and about four people have it now who did not have it a minute ago.

The man at the counter noticed it happening and did not say anything about it, because a man who has just said something at a counter does not stand there and argue with a corridor.

“That is about nine lines on that page by the end of September,” he said, “and about four of the nine were said in rooms and not by me.”

“Not in my room,” said Wenna Raut.

“In rooms,” he said. “That is what I mean. There are about four people in this city who know how to say that sentence and every one of them has a copy of the page, and every one of them is going to say it at a table to somebody, and in about four years the wall is going to have it on it nine times in about nine different hands.”

He put the pencil paper back in his coat.

“Can you tell me it is not true.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “I can tell you that this office holds about four hundred pieces of paper and a page that goes out four times a year and that the office is not a stone and does not keep one. That is not the same sentence and I am not permitted to improve yours into mine.”

## Seventy-One Days

He went down to the passage off Cordwain at about ten minutes to five and stood nine feet back from the rail with his hands in his coat pockets and watched a man who is paid nothing read the bottom of a page to four people.

The page in front of that man is the thirtieth of June of this year. It has four sentences at the foot of it and a denial and two lines in this office’s hand.

The denial has the word *no* in it twice and it is about nine feet above the four lines of the column, and the two new lines are in this office’s hand with a man’s name under each of them, and about four hundred and nineteen lines run down the middle of it with a date and an hour against every one.

The man read all of it. He did not stop at the striking and he did not stop at the column and he read the last line of the last sentence and then went back down to the top of it in the way he has been doing for nine years.

Here is what a denial is on a page that a man reads out loud nine times a week.

It is the only kind of sentence anybody repeats. Every other line on that page is an address, a figure, a name or an instruction, and a person carrying one of them home repeats the thing they came for. A denial is the only line on it that names two things and then says they are not connected, and what goes out of a passage is the two things.

The building. And the stone.

About nine people a week go into kitchens with that, and about nine people a week go down to the bottom of a wall with that, and about nine people a week come up four flights to a bench with that, and not one of them in this city has ever heard the word *no*.

And there is no instruction anywhere in nine years that could take it off the page, and the only person in this city who could read less of it is a man behind a counter who cannot be asked.

He stood there until the four had gone and there was not one person left at the rail, and then the counterman came out from under his window and squared the sheet against the edge of the rail with his thumb, which he does every time.

“You have been standing there a long time,” the counterman said.

“I have.”

“That is the third line that has come off that page since the year began and I have read two of them and I could not tell you one thing about either,” said the counterman, “and the second of the two was about what a person said in a room and went out on the thirtieth of June, and this one is about the same thing and it goes out on the thirtieth of September, and about nine people a week are going to hear it.”

He put his thumb on the edge of the rail.

“Do not ask me what they are for.”

*Three thousand and forty-eight days. Less three thousand and forty-two, the thirteenth of July of this year, is six, and a man said out loud at that counter that a building off a third court keeps the stone at the foot of the wall, and a clerk entered it in his own words with a date and an hour against it, and it goes out at four on the thirtieth of September to about two thousand counters.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is nineteen, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last nineteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and twenty-eight days, which is twenty months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

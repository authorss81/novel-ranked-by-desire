# She Gave One Sheet

Two thousand eight hundred and eighty was the first of February and the eighth is day two thousand eight hundred and eighty-seven, and two thousand eight hundred and eighty-seven less two thousand eight hundred and eighty is seven, and eight less one is seven by the month count.

## Four Flights Up A Street Two Streets Wide

He went up in the middle of the stream of linen and the stair is narrow enough that two people going the other way have to agree about it, and he used about ninety steps to be certain he was not going to turn round on the second landing.

Steam came under the door and the whole room was about eleven degrees warmer than the stair.

Bench along the wall. Three tables. A ledger on the middle one with a week of mending in it in two hands, one of them not a clerk’s and never going to be. Pegs by the door. A calendar from a works department on the wall that this laundry has never written to in nine years, for the same reason this office has never written to one.

A bench with a ruler squared against its edge, and the ruler had been squared against that edge so often that the paint had gone through to the wood under about four inches of it.

About four hundred people have carried a piece of pencil paper up those four flights and put it on the third table and carried it on again, and not one of them knew the name of the woman at it, and none of them has ever been back.

About nine sheets were under the third table, and he did not count them out loud, and he has never once counted them out loud, and Perla Quist does not let people count things in a room where they are being kept.

Under the bench there is a drawer with about eleven things in it that have never been able to be used. A docket from a works department. A receipt for a coat. A sheet with a date at the top of it in Wenna Raut’s hand and four words on the back of it in ink with a nib ground down twice in that year, and a clerk’s signature at the foot of the front.

It has been on top of the eleven since the twenty-sixth of November.

## Somebody Was Going To Come Up Those Stairs Today

He had worked out on the fourth that she was coming, and he had worked it out the same way four times before.

A thing said out loud in a passage in front of eleven people in the first week of December is in about four kitchens inside a week and in about nine by the end of the month, and at a counter or a queue or a kitchen table by the middle of the next one. There is a woman in Ostara who cannot read and has been carrying a piece of paper with four words on the back of it for about a year.

He had five weeks of that arithmetic in front of him and every one of the five weeks said the same thing, and the same thing was that he was not going to do anything about it, and he went up four flights anyway.

This office has never sent anybody to anybody in nine years. It did not send anybody in December. It has told no person where any person lives. And on the first of February a man who is not an officer of this city stood at the foot of a stair two streets wide and found somebody coming up it, and did not arrange it, and cannot prove he did not arrange it, and is not going to ask.

She came up four flights without stopping, which is not a thing that everybody can do, and she was not out of breath at the top of them, and she had the sheet in her hand.

## The Four Words, Read Out Loud

Perla Quist looked at the sheet and then at the hand it was in and did not take it.

“That is the second one,” she said.

“I know.”

“I can tell by the fold,” said Perla Quist. “Turn it over.”

He turned it over. There was a date at the top of the front and a signature at the foot of it, and on the back, in ink, four words.

She read them out loud.

*She gave one sheet.*

That is all of it. That is the whole of what the second true sentence this office has ever printed about that woman says, and it is true, and it is about a piece of paper, and it is not what she came up four flights for.

Perla Quist put it down on the bench, face up, where anybody coming into the room could see it, and then picked it up again and gave it back, because a sheet belongs to a hand.

“That is what this city says about you,” she said. “That is the only sentence anybody has written down about you, and a woman wrote it in a room off a third court and put it in your hand, and it says you gave one sheet of paper to a counter and the sheet went on to somebody else.”

## What Comes Back Is Not An Answer

The woman said it standing at the bench. She said it in about nine seconds, the way a woman says a figure at a market end, and she had said it eleven times in eleven months and had been told no eleven times, and the eleven times were eleven different days and the sentence was the same every time of them.

“They said it didn’t happen.”

That was nine seconds and it was the whole of it.

Perla Quist did not pick the pencil up. She put her hand on the bench beside the ledger instead and left it there, curled, the way a woman leaves a hand that is not working.

“No,” she said. “They did not. I have not written it down and I am not going to, and I am going to tell you why out loud before anybody asks me, because you have come up four flights and you are owed a reason rather than a nod.”

She said it in about nine seconds.

“A woman who keeps a list is a person who can be sent.”

And then she stopped, and neither of them improved it, and it is the sixth reason she has given anybody and it is the same reason she gave a man in this room four times in the autumn.

## The Instrument Reaching The Person It Is For

That is what the room is.

Nine sheets under a third table, about two hundred lines under a heading a woman ruled on her own paper in February of the eighth year, a copy of a counterman’s nine-year ledger in a pigeonhole, and a fourth column with a heading on it since the twenty-first of February that has never once been cited by anybody in any division in this city.

It is the only instrument in Ostara that can put a claim true against a day.

And it is the only instrument in Ostara that has just been used, correctly, in the only way it can be used, on the one person in this city it was built for, and the result is that she has been told the truth about a sheet of paper.

For about a year this office has printed the truest sentence it owns, twice, and has never once put it in her hands in a way that said what it was. It has been in her hands since the seventh of December. She has turned it over twice at a time about four times and has never once been asked what is on the back of it, and she has never asked, and there was no reason in the world for her to.

And now there is a woman standing at a bench who has read it out loud to her, and the sentence is true, and it is about a piece of paper, and it is not an answer.

There is no second sentence. She has come four flights to be told whether the thing that happened to her happened, and what she has been told instead is that she gave one sheet, and the rest of her life is standing on the other side of that.

## What He Is Not Going To Do

He did not ask her anything.

He had about nine questions and he had been carrying them up the stair behind a girl with linen, and he put all nine of them down on the way, on the second landing and on the third, and he let them lie there.

This office may not ask a person what she has done with a piece of paper.

Nobody was sent. Nothing was sent. He did not ask her one thing, and he did not ask her where she lived, and he did not ask her who she had shown the sheet to, and he did not ask her whether she wanted it taken back.

That is four sentences and it is the whole of what he is allowed to do about her. He arrived at it in nine years, it is correct, and it is the worst thing he has ever been correct about.

There is a fifth thing he could have done and he did not do, which is that a woman who cannot read is a woman who cannot be asked a question in a corridor either, and that he could have stood in that corridor on some evening in nine years and said a sentence out loud in it and she would not have heard it, and that would have been the same as saying it to a wall, and he wanted to say it to a person.

## The Fourth Piece Of Paper Left On That Bench

She put the sheet on the bench before she went, and squared it against the edge of the wood with two fingers, which is what everybody who comes into this room does to a bench and almost no person does to a counter.

Then she stopped at the door and did not turn round and said one thing, in about four seconds, to the doorframe.

“The four words are not what I came for,” she said. “I asked whether it happened. It did not happen, and it has not happened eleven times, and I have been told so eleven times and I came up four flights this morning because somebody in a queue said this was the place a person goes.”

She did not say who.

She went down four flights and the steam shut the door behind her, and a woman who keeps the only instrument in this city that can date a claim stood in a room with nine sheets under a third table and did not write one word of it down, because a woman who keeps a list is a person who can be sent.

It is the fourth time a piece of paper has been left on that bench. The first three were the ones in the drawer, and she has never once seen the drawer.

And it is the first time one has been left there by a person who came up four flights to put it down.

*Two thousand eight hundred and eighty-seven days. Less two thousand eight hundred and eighty, the first of February of this year, is seven, and a woman came up four flights in the first week of the ninth year and was read the only sentence in this city about her, and left it on a bench.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is thirty-eight, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is four hundred and sixty-seven days, which is fifteen months and seventeen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

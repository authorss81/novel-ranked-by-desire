# The Reading Back Is Wrong

Three thousand and sixty-eight was the ninth of August and the sixteenth is day three thousand and seventy-five, and three thousand and seventy-five less three thousand and sixty-eight is seven, and sixteen less nine is seven by the month count.

## The Man Who Came Back About The Nineteenth Of July

He came up the stair at about twenty past eleven on the sixteenth of August with a piece of pencil paper in his hand with about nine words on it in his own pencil, and he had been carrying that paper for four weeks.

Under the instruction of the tenth of June, when a person brings a paper to that counter this office is to tell that person, in that room, what this office holds about them, and nothing else, and not to improve it.

So Wenna Raut turned the bound Register a quarter on its own axis and read him the row.

The nineteenth of July. The hour against the date. Nine words in his own writing, entered in this office’s hand without the improvement of one of them.

She read it all back and then she put the pen across the book and waited, which is what a clerk does when she has read a thing to a person.

“That is not what I said.”

## What He Actually Said

He said it in the ordinary voice and there were about nine people in that corridor behind him.

“You have got it wrong.”

“I have entered what I was given at this counter on the nineteenth of July and I did not improve one word of it,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have never said to you at this counter that what was given to me was what you said. It was what you said in the room, and this office does not hold what happened outside this room and has never claimed to.”

“I said a sheet.”

“You said a building and a stone and a wall and you said them in that order and in about nine seconds, and about nine people heard you, and four of them repeated it back to each other while I was writing, in the wrong order.”

“Then it is wrong and I want it out.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am going to tell you what I can do and then I am going to do it, because you have come up four flights and you are owed a shape rather than a refusal.”

She turned the page.

“I can add a line under it in this office’s hand with a date and an hour against it, in your own words, at that counter. A row may be added to and may not be taken from. That is not my rule and it is not this office’s rule and it is the shape of every book in this city. And there is no line on any form in Ostara on which anything you have said at this counter in the last four weeks may be taken out.”

## Nowhere For An Answer To Go

“I want it out.”

“I have told you that I cannot do it and I have not improved that.”

“Then say what I said.”

Wenna Raut did not pick the pen up.

“That is the whole of it and it is nine words long,” she said. “You have asked me to enter the sentence that you did not say that nine words, and there is nowhere in this building for it to go.”

“The field for what was done to a person was filled for the first time in nine years on the twelfth of May, out of the mouth of the person it is about, and it is about a window. It is not this.”

“The foot of the standing sheet takes what a person says at this counter, and there is no instruction standing in that book that says what this office does when a person says the entry is wrong. There is no field for an answer. There has never been an answer anywhere in this city and no instrument in this city that could hold one.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table with both hands.

“So you can say it, and I will write it under the nine words, and I will not be able to do one single thing else with it, ever.”

## What He Prices It For, And What She Says About That

“I have to tell you what it costs before I give it to you,” said Lin Hao.

“No,” said Wenna Raut.

“That is the thing I do.”

“You did it once and I let you, and you stood at the end of this table for four minutes holding a sheet while I waited,” said Wenna Raut. “And once you did not do it at all and I have not forgiven either. You do not get to choose which of those two it is going to be today, because the choice was made by the second one and not by you.”

He put the sheet on the table face down.

“Then tell me what it costs.”

“You know what it costs and you have known for about eleven weeks and you came up four flights anyway,” said Wenna Raut. “Say it out loud in this room so that it is in two mouths and not one, and then give it to me and do not hold it.”

He said it. She listened to all of it and did not improve any part of it.

## The Third Line In The Office’s Hand

*From the sixteenth of August of this year, whatever a person says in answer to what this office has told them is to be entered in the same row, under what they were told, in the words that person used, with a date and an hour against it, and a row may not be taken from.*

*Lin Hao.*

She wrote it in about four minutes, which is twice as long as it takes her, and she wrote the date at the top of the line and his name at the foot of it. Then she read it back to him standing up, all the way through, with the date.

It is the third instruction this office has made in its own hand since the year began, and it is the fourth line in this office’s hand at the foot of one of its own pages, because the nine words a man said at this counter on the nineteenth of July are down there as well, in the same hand and on the same sheet, and they are a line and they are not an instruction.

The instruction of the thirtieth of March is about what a person says. The instruction of the tenth of June is about being told a thing about yourself. This one is about what a person says in answer to being told, and nothing in this city has ever done that for anybody.

## The First Answer, Entered In His Own Words

“Read it back,” said Wenna Raut, and she put the pen on the book.

He said it at that counter in about nine seconds and there were about nine people in that corridor and about four of them repeated it back to each other before he had got to the stairs.

“I said a building off a third court keeps a sheet. I never said a wall.”

She wrote it under the nine words, in this office’s hand, in the words that were used, with the date at the top of the line and the hour against the date.

She did not improve one word of it and she did not put a stop in that had not been said, and she did not add that she believed the nine words above it to be a different sentence, because a clerk may only enter what she is given and not one person in this city had given her that.

Then she squared the book and stood looking at the shelf of that room as though the shelf were going to tell her something.

“There is your answer,” she said. “In your own words, under a row in a clerk’s hand, with a date on it and an hour against the date. And it cannot be improved, and it cannot be taken from, and nothing anywhere in this city will ever do either of those two things to it.”

## What It Costs, Said By A Person

“From today I am a person who writes down what people say about what they said to them,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am going to say the rest of it and you are not going to price it, because you have already had your four minutes in this room once and you are not having them twice.”

She put the pen down across the book.

“About four hundred people are answerable. Not four hundred rows. Four hundred people, in public, in their own words, for what they said out loud in a room with a clerk in it between the thirtieth of March and now.”

“Every one of those people can now stand at this counter and say that this office has it wrong, and this office will write that down in the words they used. And it will be on a page that goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and a man who is paid nothing will read it out loud to about nine people a week at a rail in a passage.”

“And he cannot be asked to read less of it.”

“No,” said Wenna Raut. “He cannot, and he will not be told what any of it means, and about nine people a week will hear the answers as well as the questions, and they will be about people standing in a corridor, and that is what this office has bought with four lines in nine months, and there is no form in Ostara on which it can be given back.”

He stood at the end of that table and understood the whole of it in about nine seconds.

There is not one instrument in this city that will now let anybody say a sentence and have it not heard.

## The Two Before This One

“You did the four minutes once.”

“I did the four minutes once,” said Lin Hao, “in the spring, and you let me, and it bought nothing at all because the thing I was pricing went out at four o’clock either way and a man read it out to eleven people either way.”

“Yes.”

“And in June I did not do it at all, and you were right about that as well.”

“I was right about that as well,” said Wenna Raut, “and I have been right about both of those for eleven weeks and about nine of them a week have been standing at that counter, and you may put the price down wherever you like because there is nothing left to put it against.”

She turned the page and squared it and left the pen across it.

“I have one more thing to say and it is not about cost.”

“The instruction of the thirtieth of March asks this office to write down what a person says at this counter. The instruction of the tenth of June asks this office to tell a person what it holds about them. Neither of those two says one word about what happens when the second one is wrong.”

“And you have just written the first sentence in nine years that does, and you wrote it in about nine seconds, on the same afternoon that a man came up a stair with a pencil paper and said *that is not what I said*.”

She looked at him.

“I want that written down somewhere that it was the nineteenth of July, and not the second of December, and not the fourth of April, and not this morning because you had nine minutes.”

## Outside The Door On The Third Court

Lin Hao came out of the stair door at about twenty past twelve and the man from the nineteenth of July was on the step below it with his hat still on.

He had not gone out of the yard.

“So what do I do now.”

“Come back up four flights on the thirtieth of September,” said Lin Hao. “Any morning before four. She will read it back to you at that counter in the order it stands — your nine words first, then the eleven underneath them — and she will not improve one word of either.”

“And then.”

“Then a man who is paid nothing reads the whole of the bottom of that page out loud in a passage to whoever is standing there. Both of yours are on it and he reads them in that order, and he cannot be told to read less of it.”

The man turned the paper over in his hand.

“I can read it myself.”

“You can. You have never once read the bottom of that page out loud to anybody in your life.”

“That is not a thing I would do.”

“Then that is the whole of what you have been left,” said Lin Hao.

The man put the paper back inside his coat and took a while over it.

“So I came up four flights and said it was wrong and she wrote it down, and nothing has changed.”

“Nothing has changed for you,” said Lin Hao. “That is not what it was for.”

He waited.

“It is for the next one,” said Lin Hao. “There is nowhere in this city to stand at a counter and say *you have got it wrong* and have it written down in the words you used. In about four years a man will be able to say that about a thing of his own and not about one word of what anybody has written down about him.”

“You have cost me something and I do not know what it is yet.”

“You have not been charged anything,” said Lin Hao, “and I am not going to let you go out of this door thinking otherwise. From the thirtieth of September about nine people a week will stand at that counter and be told a thing about themselves that they did not come in to ask about, in this office’s hand, in public, off the bottom of a page. It costs a person nothing. It is going to cost a person nothing for as long as that page is on that rail.”

The man went down the step and out through the archway and did not look back.

Lin Hao stood in the door and listened until he could not hear him, and then went back up the four flights. There were about nine people in that corridor and Wenna Raut was writing down what somebody else had said at that counter.

Two lines at the foot of that page have never yet been on a page that went out. In about six weeks there will be four, and they go out two by two — a man’s nine words, then a sentence answering them — and this city has nothing that will take either of them off it.

*Three thousand and seventy-five days. Less three thousand and sixty-eight, the ninth of August of this year, is seven, and a clerk entered the first answer ever entered in this city under a row in her own hand with a date and an hour against it, and a third instruction went into the foot of the page as its fourth line, and about four hundred people written down in public since the thirtieth of March are now answerable in their own words for what they said in a room.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is forty-six, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-six of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and fifty-five days, which is twenty-one months and twenty-five days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

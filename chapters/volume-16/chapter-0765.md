# She Says It Once

Two thousand nine hundred and sixty-seven was the twenty-eighth of April and the fifth of May is day two thousand nine hundred and seventy-four, and two thousand nine hundred and seventy-four less two thousand nine hundred and sixty-seven is seven, and five less twenty-eight is seven by the month count.

## The Steam Came Under The Door

He was at the end of the bench by about a quarter to eleven and had been for about four minutes, and the room was about eleven degrees warmer than the stair and the steam came under the door from the washroom front in a slow push every time a machine changed load.

There were pegs by the door and a wet umbrella on one of them that had been on that peg since the previous morning.

A calendar from a works department hangs on the wall above the third table and has hung there since before the street was paved, and a laundry four streets from a division building has never once put a piece of paper in an envelope and posted it, and the two facts have never been compared in either building.

She was on the second landing when Lin Hao heard the door at the top of that stair open, and she came the last flight with her right hand shut inside her coat and she took it out at the bench and put a piece of pencil paper down and squared it against the wood with two fingers, and she had not asked anybody to do anything and had told nobody that she was coming.

That was in February. It is the fifth of May.

She had said the same eleven words eleven times in eleven months and been told the same thing eleven times in eleven different months, and then a woman at that bench had told her that a piece of office paper could not be read by anybody in the world except the two of them, and she had believed her, which no person in this city had ever had cause to do about anything.

Lin Hao was at the end of that bench and had been for about four minutes and had not asked her one thing, because there is not one form on which this office may ask a person anything, and because about four hundred people have trusted this room precisely because not one person in it ever asked.

## The Sentence, A Second Time

“They said it didn’t happen.”

Nine seconds. It is the same nine seconds and the same eleven words and it is the first sentence anybody in this city has heard from her out loud since the eighth of February.

Perla Quist did not pick the pencil up. The pencil was lying across the bench where it had been lying when the door opened and it stayed exactly where it was.

Perla Quist put the pencil down across the bench and squared the sheet twice.

“A sheet is not a person,” she said, “and I am going to write it on a sheet because a sheet may be written on, and I am not going to head it and I am not going to rule a line under it, and when I have read it back to you you are going to take it off this bench and put it in your coat and that will be the end of it in this room.”

“Again,” said the woman.

“I am not going to write it in a column and I am not going to head it and I am not going to put it in the drawer under the bench,” said Perla Quist. “A sheet is not a person and a sheet may be written on, and that is the whole of the difference between the two things and there is not one form in this city that will ever give me the other one.”

She took a sheet out of that drawer, one of about eleven things in it that have never been able to be used, and squared it against the edge of the wood and wrote on it in about nine seconds, in pencil, in her own hand.

She wrote the eleven words. She wrote them in the order she had heard them, which was the order they had been said in eleven months and eleven rooms and eleven counters, and she did not put a stop in that was not there.

Then she turned it round and read it out loud.

“They said it didn’t happen.”

## Say It Again

“Say it again.”

“That is the third time anybody has said that sentence to me and the second time I have read it back.”

“Say it again.”

Perla Quist read it back four more times.

She read it at the pace she reads a column, which is about nine words to a line and a line a second and a half, and she did not change anything about it between one reading and the next, and she did not put any of her own weight on any part of it, and the room did it five times in about ninety seconds.

The woman stood at the other side of the bench with her hands in her sleeves and listened to a sentence of hers come out of the mouth of another person for about a minute and a half.

At the third reading she put one hand out flat on the bench beside the sheet, not on it, beside it, and left it there for the fourth reading and took it away for the fifth.

That was the only thing she did. She did not thank the woman and she did not apologise and she did not ask what the writing looked like, and the eleven months of a sentence going into a room and coming out of it as nothing were not lifted by the fifth reading, because nothing had been done about them, and only the sentence had been made to stop.

That is the longest that sentence has ever existed in this city. She had said it eleven times and each time it had gone into a room and come back out of it as nothing, and this was the first time anything had held still for it, and the thing that held still was a woman with a pencil and a sheet that was not a column and a heading that was not a heading.

## Take It

“Take it,” said Perla Quist.

The woman looked at it for about four seconds and did not touch it.

“You keep it,” she said.

“I do not keep it. I keep about nine sheets under that table and this is not one of them, and if I put it in with those then it becomes part of a list of things I have decided to hold and I have not decided anything.”

“Then what do I do with it.”

“Carry it,” said Perla Quist, “and do not ask anybody what it says, because the two people in this room are the only two people alive who can read it and one of us is not going to tell you.”

The woman looked at the bench for about four seconds, and then she took it up and folded it in half and put it into the inside of her coat against her body on the left, and she did it the way a person does a thing they are going to keep.

The other one of those is lying squared against the edge of that bench with four words on the back of it that she has still never once been able to read.

## What He Had In His Mouth And Did Not Say

Here is what he wanted to say and did not say.

He wanted to say, at that bench, in the ordinary voice, *there is a field in a book two streets away that has stood empty for nine years for exactly that sentence, and it is filled out of the mouth of the person it is about, and it is in your own words, and I am the only man in Ostara who may write into that book and I am standing here.*

He did not say it.

Because the moment he said it, this office had sent a person to that room. Not a letter and not a form and not anything that could be refused at a counter in about nine days.

A man, with a sentence in his mouth, walking four flights up a street two streets wide and saying it out loud to a woman who cannot read. And there would be a thing that came after that, and after the thing that came after that there would be about nine more, and in about four years about forty people would know that a woman with a pencil kept nine sheets under a third table because a man in a white plate had said a sentence at a bench.

Perla Quist did not ask him anything about it, then or afterwards, and he did not tell her, and she has never once asked him what he is thinking about, and that is the whole of what she has given him since the seventh year.

She squared the ruler against the edge of the bench and turned to the ninth sheet and went back down her column at about nine lines a minute, and the door shut behind the woman on the stair, and the room was about eleven degrees cooler inside four seconds, and he was standing at the end of a bench in a room that had just been the only place in the city where a sentence was true.

He went down the four flights with the sentence still in his mouth, which was about thirty words long and was the best sentence he has had in nine years, and it got no further than a stairwell.

And the eleven words were on a sheet in a laundry and not in any book in this city, and there was a field in a book two streets away standing empty for that sentence, and not one person in this city is entitled to say to that field what it is for.

*Two thousand nine hundred and seventy-four days. Less two thousand nine hundred and sixty-seven, the twenty-eighth of April of this year, is seven, and a woman came up four flights for the second time and said the same eleven words in nine seconds, and a woman at a bench wrote them on a sheet in her own hand and read them back five times and did not head them, and not one person was sent anywhere.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is thirty-five, and the thirty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-five of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and fifty-four days, which is eighteen months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

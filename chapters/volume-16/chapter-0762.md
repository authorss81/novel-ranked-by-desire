# A Question With No Answer

Two thousand nine hundred and forty-six was the seventh of April and the fourteenth is day two thousand nine hundred and fifty-three, and two thousand nine hundred and fifty-three less two thousand nine hundred and forty-six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## A Quarter Past Eleven, And A Piece Of Pencil Paper

She had the piece of pencil paper in her hand and had been holding it in front of her for about four minutes before she put it down, and she did not put it on the counter, she put it on her own knee, which is what a person does with a thing they have not finished deciding about.

“Did it happen.”

That was the whole of it and she had it ready, and she had it ready the way people have things ready when they have been carrying them up a stair.

Wenna Raut asked her one question, which was hers to ask and costs her nothing, and the woman answered it in about four seconds and still did not put the pencil paper on the counter.

“Have you been to this counter before.”

“Eleven times,” said the woman. “In eleven months. And I have been told the same thing eleven times and it was always correct and it was always said in this voice.”

“That is a clerk’s voice.”

“It is a voice,” said the woman, “and I have never once had a reason to think anybody in it was doing anything else.”

## What A Clerk May Be Given

“I cannot tell you whether it happened,” said Wenna Raut. “That is not me not knowing. This office does not hold what happened to anybody. It holds pieces of paper. Rather more than four hundred and thirty rows of them, every one with a date against it and an hour against the date and nothing whatever against the hour.”

“Then what is in it about me.”

“One row,” said Wenna Raut. “And I am not going to read you the date and the hour at a counter with nine people standing in the corridor behind you, and I am not going to say one word else about that row, and that is not me being careful with you. That is the shape of a book.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” she said. “It is a correct statement of what a book can do and it is not an answer, and I have about nine of them and every one of them sounds like that one and not one of them is any use to anybody.”

She turned the book a quarter so the rows were the right way up for a man standing at the end of the table, which she does about four times a year.

“The answer you have had eleven times is the correct answer,” she said. “I have never given you an answer that was not correct, and that is not an accident, and it is not me being careful with you either. It is that this office cannot do anything else, so the honest thing to do is to say it correctly and say it quickly and get back to the counter.”

“Then why did it take you about four minutes.”

“Because it is your twelfth time and because you have carried that pencil paper about four hundred yards to find out, and those are two different things and I can count them separately.”

“I am not going to stand here and pretend otherwise to a woman holding a piece of pencil paper on her knee in a corridor with a queue in it.”

## The Fifth Field, Named Before Anything Goes In It

“There is a field in that book that is about what was done to a person,” she said. “It is the fifth one. The words go into it from the mouth of the person it is about, in that person’s own words, and it has stood there nine years with nothing in it and nothing is going into it this morning.”

“And you could put it in.”

“I could put it in if you asked me to, and you are not going to ask me to, and if you did ask me to I would have to refuse, and I would refuse correctly and you would leave this counter knowing exactly as much as you knew when you came up the stair.”

She squared the book to the edge of the table.

“The rule I work under is that I may only enter what I am given, and cannot improve it, and cannot be asked to hurry. And there is not one person in this city entitled to hand me a question. A question is not a thing that may be given. Everything in that book this morning arrived in a hand.”

“Who handed you the last thing that went in it?”

“A man off a branch night shift,” said Wenna Raut, “in about nine seconds, on the thirtieth of March, and he may not be asked to hurry either and he does not hurry and neither of us has ever tried to make the other.”

## A Man In The Corridor Who Will Not Say

There was a man in the corridor who had been standing there about twenty minutes without coming to the counter, and at about a quarter to twelve he said it out loud to the room and to nobody in it.

“I have been told that before.”

“You have.”

“The eighth month of last year. A different building and a different counter, and a man read it out of a sheet to about four of us.”

He did not look at anybody while he said it, which is what a man does in a corridor when he has said a thing out loud and has decided to get through the rest of it.

“I am not going to tell you who, because I do not know who. I have wanted to be able to put a name in that sentence for about four months and there is not one anywhere in it, and there is not one in the other three sentences either, and all four of us were told the same thing on the same afternoon by a person none of us had seen before or since.”

The corridor was about four feet wide and there were about nine people in it and the whole of that went past in about four seconds.

“That is the part that stays,” said the man in the corridor. “Not that it did not happen. That I cannot tell you who said it.”

## The First Question In That Book In Nine Years

The woman on the stool folded the pencil paper in half and unfolded it again, which was the only thing she had done with it in about four minutes.

“Then what do I do.”

She said it in the ordinary voice and she was not asking Wenna Raut. She was asking the corridor, the way somebody asks a room.

And Wenna Raut took the pen up.

He understood about two seconds later what she was doing, and understood about two seconds after that she was going to do it correctly and for the rest of it.

The instruction of the thirtieth of March says that when a person says a thing at that counter this office is to write it down in the words that person used, with a date against it and an hour against the date, on the standing sheet and not in the second column.

Not one person had said a question at that counter until this morning.

She wrote six words. She wrote them exactly and she put the date against them and the hour against the date in about eleven seconds, and she did not improve one of them and she did not put a full stop in that was not there.

Then she squared the book, which is what she does at the end of every entry whether or not the entry has changed anything, and stood with the pen across it.

“That goes on the standing sheet and not in the second column, under the four sentences and above the striking,” she said. “It is not in the bound Register. It is a different book and it goes to about two thousand counters on the thirtieth of June, and there is one line of mine already on it from the thirtieth of March and a man who is paid nothing is going to read that line out loud to about eleven people in a passage.”

## What Cannot Be Answered And Cannot Be Taken From

“There is your question,” said Wenna Raut. “In your own words, with a date on it and an hour on the date, in a book in this city that has never had anything like it in it.”

“Does that do anything.”

“No.”

“You do not know that. You have not looked.”

“I do not have to look to know that,” said Wenna Raut. “I am the only person in this city allowed to write in that book and there is nothing in it that answers anything. The six words are in it now and they cannot be improved and they cannot be taken out, because a row may be added to and may not be taken from, and that has been the rule of every book in this city for longer than anybody alive.”

She squared the book again.

“There is nowhere in this city to be told,” she said. “There is now, for the first time in nine years, somewhere to have asked.”

The man in the corridor had not moved from where he was standing.

“Write that one down as well,” he said. “Whichever of you it is that can write anything down at all.”

“There is nothing to write it on,” said Wenna Raut. “You have said one thing to me this morning and you did not say it at that counter, you said it in a corridor, and what goes in that book is what a person says at the counter.”

*Two thousand nine hundred and fifty-three days. Less two thousand nine hundred and forty-six, the seventh of April of this year, is seven, and a woman said a question out loud at that counter for the first time in nine years, and a clerk entered it in six words with a date against it and an hour against the date, and there is nothing anywhere in this city that will answer it and nothing anywhere that will take it out.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is fourteen, and the thirty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fourteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and thirty-three days, which is seventeen months and twenty-three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

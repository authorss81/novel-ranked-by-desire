# The First Line On The Page

Two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three was the twenty-fourth of March and the thirtieth is day two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, and two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three is six, and thirty less twenty-four is six by the month count.

## Four Instructions Standing In One Hand

He had it by about nine on the morning of the thirtieth and by ten he had it in the order it was going to be said and by a quarter to eleven he had it on the back of his own left hand in pencil on the top landing, where there is nowhere else in this city he is permitted to put a thing down.

There are four instructions standing in the bound Register in this office’s hand.

The first shuts a drawer in the wall on the left of that table and cannot be undone. The second says every piece of paper received through that front door is to be entered as received, with a date against it.

The third says every piece of paper received is to be entered with a description of the person who brought it, in that person’s own words, whether or not it is true, whether or not it is complete, and whether or not anybody is able to check it.

The fourth is a denial, entered on the sixteenth of August, saying that no page of this office and no stone in this city is connected to any other, and that a resemblance is not a figure.

Not one of the four is about a person.

Three of them are about a piece of paper and one of them is about a drawer, and about four hundred and thirty rows have been written under them in nine years, and about four hundred and thirty times this office has asked what a thing was and not once what happened to the person who brought it.

And there is nothing anywhere in this city on which an instruction may be undone.

That is not a rule anybody made. It is what a book is. A row may be added to and may not be taken from, and an instruction is a row, and a line under a printed heading on a quarterly sheet is exactly the same instrument, and the only thing anybody in this city can do about a sentence they have entered is add another one underneath it in about nine years.

So whatever he entered at about two o’clock this afternoon was permanent the moment Wenna Raut’s pen touched it, and it would be on a page at four o’clock, and about nine people a week would hear it read out loud in a passage by a man who is paid nothing and cannot be asked to read less of it.

## What It Is Going To Say, In The Order It Stands

*From the thirtieth of March of this year, when a person says a thing at that counter, this office is to write down what that person said, in the words that person used, with a date against it and an hour against the date, on the standing sheet, and not in the second column.*

*Lin Hao.*

That is the whole of it. He had written it out four times in about eleven seconds each and he had cut about nine words out of the fourth version and had not written the date at the top of the fifth because the date goes on when the clerk puts it in.

“The second column,” said Wenna Raut.

“Does not go in the column.”

“It does not go in the column, and the column will have four lines in it at four o’clock this afternoon and four lines in it at the thirtieth of June, and I have said that out loud every quarter for about two years and I am saying it again because you have just written an instruction that says so in a sentence that is going to a counter that is not this one.”

## About Half Past Two, And The Thirty-Third Sheet

The thirty-third sheet went onto the table under the window at about half past two and Wenna Raut squared it against the wood, and then squared it again about four minutes later, which is what she does.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the head of it.

It has held that value since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year and has not shifted once since, and not one person in this city has the standing to shift it and never has, and only one of the two counts under this is being read out this afternoon.

It is unmoved across **seventeen of these sheets**, counting the seventeenth onward as the seventeen sheets that have carried that number since it was put there, and this is the thirty-third.

And it is unmoved across **sixteen quarters**, and that is a different tally and it is also right, because a quarter here means a quarter-end later than that thirtieth of March and there has been one every ninety days since. Sixteen of them is one thousand four hundred and forty days, and this morning is one thousand four hundred and forty days after that March.

The two figures disagree by one, and a reader at a rail is going to be handed both of them, so the page has to say which one it is doing.

And under the head: about four hundred and nineteen lines, each with its date and its hour and nothing whatever in the line after the hour.

Under those, a second column with four lines in it under a heading of two printed words that say **carried by**.

Under that, four sentences.

Under those, six more lines in this office’s hand, the fourth of the six struck end to end, and beneath the striking a man’s name.

And then the paper stops, and there are two inches of it below the name, and a person who crouches at a rail in a passage can read what is in them.

And under the four sentences, and above the striking, and in this office’s hand, with a man’s name under it, and in no column anywhere on that sheet, the first line this office has added to the foot of one of its own pages in nine years.

## What It Costs, Said By A Person, Before He Gave It To Her

He had it in his hand and he did not give it to her for about four minutes, and he understood in those four minutes that he was doing it for her benefit, and that there was no reason whatever to suppose she wanted the four minutes.

“Read it back to me,” he said, “and then I am going to tell you what it costs, and then I am going to give it to you.”

She read it back. She did not improve one word of it and her voice did not change on any part of it, and when she had finished she put the pen across the book and waited.

“From this afternoon I am the hand,” said Wenna Raut, “and from this afternoon I am the mouth. About forty people a quarter will stand at that counter and say a thing, and I will write it down in their words, and I will not know whether any of it is true, and a clerk who may only enter what she is given has become a person who is given sentences.”

“That is the whole of it.”

“That is the whole of what I have just said and it is not the whole of what it costs.”

She did not pick the pen up.

“I have spent nine years being a person who writes down what she is given and cannot be wrong. That is the best position a person in this building can hold and I built it, and I am not going to be able to unbuild it after this afternoon.”

“Everything this office learns to say from today goes on a page that a man behind a counter reads out to whoever is standing there. If one of the four hundred and thirty people in this city says a wrong thing about himself at that counter in a bad week, it will be on the page in a clerk’s hand with a date against it and an hour against the date, and I will have written it.”

“And it cannot be taken from, and there is nothing in this city that improves a sentence after a person has said it.”

“I know that you know,” he said.

“Of course you do,” said Wenna Raut. “Give it to me.”

He gave it to her.

She wrote it in about four minutes, with the date at the top and his name at the foot of it, and read it all back in the order it stood, and she did not improve one word, and then she squared the sheet to the edge of the table with both hands and left it there.

## Two Floors Down, And A Rail In A Passage

She carried it down two floors herself at about ten to four and laid it face up on the rail at four, which is four times a year and has been since the first year of it, and the thirty-second sheet came off that rail where it had stood for ninety days and went into a drawer that is not the drawer in the wall.

There were eleven at the rail at four minutes past four.

That is what stood there at four minutes past four at the last quarter-end and what stood there at the one before it, and the man at the desk under the window read the bottom of it out loud to all eleven of them in the voice and at the pace he keeps for everything on a page.

He read the four sentences. He read the six lines under them and did not stop at the striking. He came down to the second column and read the four lines in it, with a name at the head of the fourth, as though it were an address, because that is what is under that heading.

And he read the new line.

He read it in the ordinary way, because that is the ordinary way, and the passage did not tell him that the thing at the foot of that page was an instruction and not a notice. Nobody in this city is entitled to tell him what a line under a sentence means, and there is nothing on which a man behind a counter may be asked what he has just read.

There was a silence of about two seconds, and then a woman asked the man beside her a question, and he said he did not know either, and eleven people looked at about nine feet of dressed stone at the foot of a wall across the road and went back up the lane.

At about half past five he stood at a table in a room off a third court with a drawer shut in the wall on his left and twenty-six things on a bundle beside it and an account in a book that belongs to no living person and cannot be drawn against and has not moved since the first of October in the fourth year.

And he had put one sentence into the only instrument in this city that goes out four times a year to about two thousand counters, and there was nothing on which it could be taken back.

*Two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine days. Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three, the twenty-fourth of March of this year, is six, and a clerk entered an instruction on the standing sheet in this office’s hand with a man’s name under it, and the thirty-third sheet was squared at about half past two and went down two floors and was face up on a rail at about four, and there were eleven at it.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is ninety days, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last ninety of them and came down off it at four this afternoon.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and nineteen days, which is seventeen months and nine days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

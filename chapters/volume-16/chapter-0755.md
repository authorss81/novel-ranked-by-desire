# Four Inches Since December

Two thousand nine hundred was the twenty-first of February and the twenty-eighth is day two thousand nine hundred and seven, and two thousand nine hundred and seven less two thousand nine hundred is seven, and twenty-eight less twenty-one is seven by the month count.

## Half Past Five, And An Hour Of Counting

He counted them himself, in the hour, because there was nothing else to do with the hour.

About two hundred and forty people went past that corner between five and half past five. That is a count of one hour of one day at the busiest hour of the day at that corner, and it is not the count of a day, and the count of a day was printed once, in the autumn, by a man who had to know it, and he was not going to stand here on the twenty-eighth of February and produce a second version of it out of his own head.

About a hundred of them stood still at the board long enough to read it. About nine of them in a week get down low enough to read the four inches at the foot of the wall, and not one person does both.

He knows that last one from the same source and has never verified it and cannot verify it, because verifying it means standing at that kerb for six hours at a time, which he has done four times, and counting the people who go down on their hands and knees in a street in the cold at half past five, which no person does and which no instrument in this city provides for.

The month’s form is on the board. Two ruled squares at the foot of it, one for the figure of the thing at the address and one for the standing of it, and neither has held anything on any day since the eighth of October of the first year, when a clerk was given a sheet and a form and nothing to go on.

That is the standing of a thing. Nine years of a city’s addresses going past a kerb twice a day and not one figure and not one standing entered into two boxes that are there, ruled, four inches deep, waiting.

## The Boy With The Paste Brush

The boy came at about five with the board under his arm and the brush in his fist and put it up the way he puts it up, and the two squares at the foot went up empty, the way they go up.

He did not look at them. He has not looked at them in seven years.

He was asked nothing on the eleventh, or on the eighteenth, or on the twenty-fifth, or this afternoon, and he has been asked nothing at all this month by anybody, and it is not because anybody is being careful with him. It is because there is nothing anybody in this city wants to know, and the two boxes have been empty for nine years because there is no form anywhere for asking what a box is for.

He took his board and went up the slope and did not look back at it, which is what he does.

## Four Inches, And Nothing In Two Of Them

Four inches of dressed stone under a wall at that corner, at the depth of two fingers, with a course of it about a foot above the kerb, and they have been under a wall for longer than anybody alive has been reading them.

The two inches at the bottom of that stone are the bottom two inches of a printed page, and a person at that kerb can get down to them with a hand on the kerbstone and read what other people wrote in pencil on a piece of paper about themselves in a room with a door on a court.

About four hundred people have done it.

That is a count of pieces of pencil paper. It is not a count of claims, it is not a count of people, and it is certainly not a count of kitchens, because a kitchen and a piece of paper are two different objects and he has had that written on the back of his own hand since August so that he stops doing it in his head.

He had been out to that corner four times in four weeks to look at the bottom two inches, and on the fourth of this month there had been one mark on them, and on the eleventh there had been one, and on the eighteenth and on the twenty-fifth there had been none.

Nothing has been written in them since the thirtieth of December. That is five weeks and it is the first time in nine years that two inches have gone five weeks with nothing in them.

## The Woman At Ten Past Seven

She was on the slope at about ten past seven with the short-handled one, working down to the kerb and back up again the way she has worked that corner every morning for longer than the boy has been carrying boards up it.

She has never once mentioned what she has seen done down there. She has not been asked this month and she is not going to be, and she has not asked him anything either, and that is the only conversation either of them has had in nine years.

He has got it into his head over five weeks that the two inches have gone quiet because of something he did on the third of December, and he has been out to that corner at half past five and at ten past seven and at the hour before he starts to try to make it true, and the sweep at ten past seven is the one thing that has happened at that corner every single morning of his life and it has nothing to do with him.

He put it down the way he puts everything down, in pencil on the back of his own hand at about ten to twelve at night on the top landing of a branch office, because there is nowhere else in this city he is allowed to leave a thing he is thinking about.

Four dates. Nothing in the two inches on three of them. One mark on two of them. A mark on a piece of rag paper is not a person and two marks is not a falling off and one mark is not a rate and three of nothing is not a figure.

The arithmetic does not come to anything he is able to say out loud to a person.

## About Four Paragraphs In A Branch Office

He comes in at eleven and goes out at half past five. There are three floors of washrooms above him, a stairwell, a bin room, and a room at the end of the passage that belongs to the branch office and not to him, and on a board in that room is a page ruled into seven squares with four names on it: his, and Nadia Ferrous’s, and Petra Ilyin’s, and Isolde Marchetti’s.

All three of those women are sworn to him. Two of them do not live in his house any more and all three of them come in on the rota, and the arrangement was granted by a man who found it convenient and has not thought about it since, and he asked for the shift and there was nothing in this city on which a man of rank one could ask for one.

He does the second floor first. The chest has been spent since the fourth year and there is nothing this building can do about it and nothing to sell it for.

He has got about nine minutes on the top landing before half past eleven, and there is no water on the top landing and there never has been any, and he has carried it up four floors for six years on the word of a man who left this building in the second year.

At about eleven he stops on the top landing and does the thing he has been doing for five weeks, which is to put down the arithmetic again, differently, to see whether it comes out the same way.

Five weeks with nothing in two inches. About four hundred people have ever written in them. About nine of them a week get down low enough to see them.

Five weeks is about thirty-five days. About nine a week is about forty-five in five weeks.

So there should be about forty-five pieces of pencil paper with something written in the bottom two inches of them between the thirtieth of December and tonight, and there is not one.

He cannot find out why. Not without asking about forty-five people a question that this office is not permitted to ask anybody, or send anybody to ask, and it has never in nine years sent anybody to anybody, and could not have done it in December either.

There is a form in a drawer in a division building two streets from the laundry that would carry a person from a bench to a door and back again with a date against it, and it is not this office’s, and this office has not sent that division a piece of paper in nine years.

## What He Wants It To Be

He wants it to be something he did.

He put a woman’s name in a column on the third of December and about four minutes after four o’clock on the thirtieth a man behind a counter read it out in a passage to eleven people, and it is in about nine kitchens by now and by the end of this month it will be in about nine kitchens, and the man who said those figures has been paid nothing for saying them.

If it is him, then the two inches have shut because a person said out loud that a building keeps a woman’s name, and a person who has written down what was done to them once is now standing at a kerb holding a piece of pencil paper and thinking about a building, and not writing.

And he cannot prove that. Proving it would mean asking about forty-five people whether they had stopped writing in the bottom two inches of their own paper, and there is nothing in this city on which that question may be put to any of them, and no instrument anywhere that could put it, and no answer that would be any use even if it were put.

It might be the weather. It might be February. It might be that about four hundred people got tired, or got a new landlord, or stopped believing in the bottom two inches of a piece of paper the way you stop believing in anything, without anybody’s name on it.

He wrote that on the back of his hand too, on the top landing, at about ten to twelve at night, and stood looking at it, and did not cross it out.

## The Last Mark In The Two Inches

It is eleven weeks old and it is on a piece of rag paper about the size of the foot of a printed page, and it is nine words long, and it is about a thing that happened to somebody else in a doorway.

That is the last thing anybody in this city wrote in the bottom two inches of a printed page before the thirtieth of December.

And those two inches are the only place in Ostara where about four hundred people have ever written down what was done to them, in their own hands, with a pencil, on a wet evening or at three in the morning, and he has been standing at a kerb on his own time on five evenings out of seven for five weeks watching the place where it happens stop happening.

*Two thousand nine hundred and seven days. Less two thousand nine hundred, the twenty-first of February of this year, is seven, and about two hundred and forty people went past a corner between five and half past five, and nothing has been written in the bottom two inches of that wall since the thirtieth of December.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is fifty-eight, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fifty-eight of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is four hundred and eighty-seven days, which is sixteen months and seven days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

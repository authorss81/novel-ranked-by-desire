# The Eighty-Second Form

Two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine was the thirtieth of March and the seventh of April is day two thousand nine hundred and forty-six, and two thousand nine hundred and forty-six less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine is seven, and seven out of the thirtieth of March, which is the last working day of that month, is the seventh of April.

## Five O’Clock, And A Board

He was standing at that kerb at about five because a board goes up at about five, and he had been to that corner about nine times in six weeks and had never once been there at the hour the board goes up.

There is a form a month and it goes up on the seventh and it comes down on the seventh of the next, and the seventh of this month is the eighty-second of them.

The boy came up the slope with the board under his arm and the paste brush in his fist and set the bottom edge against the kerbstone and squared it with the toe of his shoe and pushed it up flat, the way he does it, and went back up the slope without looking at it.

He has been carrying boards up that slope for seven years. There are about two hundred people a day who stop at that corner and about ninety who read the board, and not one person in this city has ever asked him anything about any of it.

## Two Ruled Squares, Going Up Empty

The form has two ruled squares at the foot of it. One is for the figure of the thing at the address. One is for the standing of it.

Both of them went up empty and both of them are going to come down empty, because they have gone up empty and come down empty on every one of the eighty-two.

The reason is back at the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, and it is the same reason for both of the two and it has not changed in seven years, and he has stopped going and looking at it.

He did not look at them. He has not looked at them in seven years and he did not look at them this afternoon.

That is the part that took him six weeks to get to. There was not one person standing there to see whether he looked or did not, and there has never been anybody there, and the two boxes have been empty for seven years for want of anybody in this city who wants to know what a box is for, and this office has never sent anybody anywhere to find out.

## Four Inches, And The Sixth Week

Under the board is four inches of dressed stone, with a course of it about a foot above the kerb, and the bottom two inches of those four inches have had nothing written in them since the thirtieth of December.

The bottom two inches of that stone are the bottom two inches of a printed page, and it is the only ground in Ostara where a person can get their own chest against a kerbstone and read what a different person wrote about themselves in pencil, in their own hand, about a room with a door on a court.

About four hundred people have done that in nine years. About nine of them in a week get down low enough to read them, and not one person who stops at that board goes down to them.

This is the sixth week he has been out here looking and there is still nothing in them, and there was one mark on the fourth of March and one on the eleventh and none on the eighteenth and none on the twenty-fifth, and one mark is not a rate and two marks is not a falling off.

He wanted to say one word to the boy on that slope. He did not say it.

Wanting is not a reason, and he wrote that on the back of his own left hand at about ten to twelve at night on the top landing of a branch office, in pencil, which is the only surface in this city he is allowed to deface with a thought.

## Four And Nine, In A Clerk’s Mouth

She counted at the counter on the seventh and said it out loud before she had her coat off, which is what she does when she has decided a thing is going to be said in that room and is not going to be improved.

“About four kitchens inside a week and about nine by the thirtieth,” said Wenna Raut. “That is Emil Brack’s figure and it is not mine, and I am not going to argue with a man who has been right four times and paid nothing for any of them.”

“I am not going to argue with him either.”

“I am not going to do arithmetic on it either, because the last time anybody in this city did arithmetic on one of his numbers it cost somebody a fortnight.” She squared the book to the edge of the table. “It is his and it is right and it is a count of kitchens. It is not a count of claims and it is not a count of people and it is not a count of pieces of paper.”

“You can set all four of those numbers on one line in a book,” he said, “and about four hundred people will read that line and understand every figure on it to be a different figure.”

“They are not. One of them is kitchens and the other three are things that have happened inside kitchens, and the line is the most dangerous object in this city and about nine people a week are getting close to it.”

She turned a page.

“There were about four hundred and thirty rows in that book on the first of February and there are rather more than that in it now. The difference is pieces of paper that came through a door. It is a count of rows and it is not a count of anybody.”

## Seven Days Of A Line

The thirty-third sheet went face up on a rail in a passage off Cordwain at four on the thirtieth of March, and it has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven days.

At the foot of it, under the four sentences and above the striking, and in no column anywhere on it, there is a line in this office’s hand with a man’s name under it.

A man who is paid nothing read the whole of the bottom of that page out loud to eleven people at about four minutes past four on the afternoon it went up, because that is what a man behind a counter does with what is on the page and there is no way anybody may ask him where the end is.

And now it happens on ninety mornings of every quarter, and about nine people a week stop at that rail.

## Four People In Seven Days

The instruction went into that book on the thirtieth of March and this is the seventh day of it.

Four people have stood at that counter in seven days and said a thing out loud. Wenna Raut has entered all four in the words each of them used, with a date against every one and an hour against every date, under the four sentences and above the striking.

The first was a man at about eleven o’clock. It was nine words long and not one of them was a claim, and he read it back before he went down the stair.

The second was forty minutes about a doorway and a figure, and it is wrong in two places, and there is not a person in this city able to check any part of it, and it is going out on a page to about two thousand counters on the thirtieth of June.

The third said one sentence and then stood at that counter for about nine minutes while she entered it.

The fourth came in at about a quarter past two on the sixth of this month with a piece of pencil paper in her hand and did not sit down, and stood there until it had been written down.

## What A Room That Keeps Words Becomes

“You are going to ask me what the fourth one said,” said Wenna Raut.

“Yes.”

“I am not going to tell you what she said, because she said it to me and not to the room, and a clerk enters what she is given and does not repeat it to a man afterwards, and that has been the arrangement in this room for nine years and it is the only arrangement here that has never once been broken.”

“Then tell me what sort of thing it was.”

“That is not the question you should be putting to me and you have not got another one until tomorrow.”

She squared the book and left her hand on the edge of it.

“They said it to me because somebody told them to,” she said. “That is the whole of what I have got and I have had it since about a quarter past two yesterday, and there is nowhere in this building to put a thing like that, and I am not going to invent a place to put it and then have to defend the place for the next four years.”

He stood at the end of the table and understood three things in about nine seconds, and he understood the third of them last.

About four hundred and thirty people have walked into that room in nine years to hand it a piece of paper.

In seven days, four people have walked into it to hand it a sentence somebody else had written for them before they came up the stair.

And he had put the first sentence this office has ever asked a person for at the foot of his own page seven days ago, and a room with no door onto a street had become the sort of room a sentence is carried to.

*Two thousand nine hundred and forty-six days. Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is seven, and a form went up on a board by a kerb with two ruled squares empty on it, and four people said a thing out loud at a counter, and the fourth of them had been told what to say by somebody else.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March, is seven, and the thirty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last seven of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and twenty-six days, which is seventeen months and sixteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# A Woman Who Keeps A List

Three thousand and eighty-eight was the twenty-ninth of August and the fifth of September is day three thousand and ninety-four, and three thousand and ninety-four less three thousand and eighty-eight is six, and five out of the thirtieth, which is the last working day of that month, is the fifth of September.

## Four Flights Up A Street Two Streets Wide

He went up at about half past ten in the morning with a piece of pencil paper in the inside of his coat, which was the sixth time in six years, and the stair was full of light from a landing window at the second floor and had about nine people on it in about eleven minutes.

There was a high window on the back room's outside wall that had not been cleaned in six years and that no linen ever went near, and about nine people a week go up those stairs and not one of them in six years has looked at it.

Steam came under the door. Bench along the wall. Three tables. A ledger on the middle one with a week of mending in it in two hands. Pegs by the door. A calendar from a works department on the wall that this laundry has never written to in nine years, for the same reason this office has never written to one.

A ruler squared against the edge of the bench with the paint gone through to the wood under about four inches of it. Nine sheets under the third table. About eleven things in the drawer under the bench that have never been able to be used.

Perla Quist had the ninth sheet out and was going down her column at about nine lines a minute, and she did not look up when the door opened, and she did not look up when the door shut.

## The Third Question In Nine Years

He stood at the end of the bench for about four minutes and then he asked it.

It is one question and he is the only person in Ostara entitled to put it, and he has used two in nine years, and this is the third.

“Will you write out the four lines that stand under the two printed words at the head of that column.”

Perla Quist put the pencil down across the bench.

She did not look up for about nine seconds. The room did the thing it does when a machine changes load, and the calendar came away from the plaster by a hair and went back the way it had been.

“No,” she said.

He had expected that. He had it on the stair and on the second landing, and he said nothing at all and did not improve it.

“That is the seventh time I have said no to you about something,” said Perla Quist, “and I am going to tell you the reason for the seventh one, and it is not the reason for the other six, and you have not asked me for it and I am giving it to you because you have come up here five times in six months with nothing in your hands.”

## What She Said Before She Said Yes

She squared the ruler against the edge of the bench and did not pick the pencil up.

“The other six were about me,” she said. “What I keep. Whether I would send it. Whether I would let anybody see it. What my name costs. Every one of those six was a question about the woman and not about the paper, and I have said no to all of them and I was right six times.”

“Yes.”

“And this one is not about me,” said Perla Quist. “This one is about four lines on a page in a building four hundred yards from this room, and you are the only person in this city entitled to ask that office one question, and there is no instrument in Ostara that can copy them, and I am the only person in this city who can write them out in a hand.”

She stopped there and left it.

“A sheet is not a person,” she said. “I have said that to you before and I am not going to say it again in a different way to make this afternoon sound different.”

## The Way She Did It

She took a sheet out of the drawer under the bench, out of about eleven things that have never been able to be used, and squared it against the edge of the wood and wrote the date at the top of it in her own hand.

Then she wrote the four lines out in about four minutes, and she did not use the bound ledger and she did not use any of the nine sheets and she did not rule a line under them.

There are two printed words at the head of the column and she wrote them first, because they are on the page, and then a date, and then a name, and then a sentence, and then the name of a man at the foot of it.

There is no figure on any of the four lines and there is no figure on her sheet either, and the reason is that no figure appears anywhere in that column and she was not going to invent the shape of one.

She turned it round and pushed it across the bench with two fingers and did not let go of it until he had it.

“That is yours,” said Perla Quist. “It is my paper and my hand and my date and it is not a copy of a page anybody can check, and I have written down what is on that page because you asked me in a way that was about the paper.”

She wiped the pencil on the cloth that is on a nail by that door and has been on that nail since the room was built.

“I have not written anything on the ninth sheet,” she said, “and I have not put a heading over it, and there is no place in this room where a thing like that could be kept twice.”

## What She Wanted

For about four minutes she was not asked anything.

She picked the pencil up and put it down again.

“Six years,” said Perla Quist. “Nine sheets under that table and about two hundred lines under a heading I ruled myself, and a copy of a man’s nine-year ledger in a pigeonhole, and a fourth column with a heading on it since the twenty-first of February of the eighth year. And in six years not one person in this city has come up those stairs and asked me a question I could answer by writing something down.”

She turned the bench stool round and got onto it, which she does not do.

“I did not want a favour and I have turned down six of them and I would turn down a seventh,” she said. “I wanted to be asked one question by a man who was allowed to ask his office anything at all. That is the whole of what I have wanted in six years and it is about nine words long.”

“Take the sheet.”

“I have given it to you. You are holding it.”

“I am going down four flights with it.”

“Then take it down four flights,” said Perla Quist, “and do not fold it, because a fold on a sheet with a woman’s name on it is a thing a person can recognise in about nine seconds in a corridor.”

## The Two Ways A Thing Gets To A Person

He put the sheet down on the bench and squared it against the edge, the way everybody does at a bench and hardly anybody does at a counter.

“There is a thing I have been carrying since the third of June,” he said, “and I am going to say it here and then I am going to do something about it, and I am telling you first because you have never once had a person do a thing at that bench without knowing.”

Perla Quist did not look up.

“In this city there are two ways a thing gets from where it is to a person,” he said. “A hand, and a window. Everything else is a door onto a street, and not one office in this city has one.”

He looked up at the high window with the steam going past it.

“That window has not been cleaned in six years and no linen goes near it and about nine people a week go up these stairs and not one of them in six years has looked at it.”

“A window is not a form,” said Perla Quist.

“A window is not a form and it is not a book, and nothing in this city has ever recorded a thing that arrived through one.” He put two fingers on the sheet on the bench. “And this is the first thing that has ever left that room in a hand, and it is your paper, and you gave it to me and not to her, and I could not enter it in anything if there was a form for it.”

She was on the stool with her hands in her lap and looked at the bench for about four seconds.

“You are going to do something about it,” she said.

“I am.”

“Not now.”

“Not now.”

## The Room At Half Past Eleven

It went quickly and neither of them treated it as a thing that had been negotiated.

She got up off the stool and pushed it back under the bench with her hip and did not put the pencil down first, which is the only piece of carelessness she committed in six years.

Her hands are the whole of what she is in that room and she has never once been able to make them be anything else. The skin at the knuckle of the middle finger of her left hand splits every winter and she has never had it dressed and there is a burn about the size of a coin on the inside of her right forearm about four inches up from the wrist where the steam got in when the door was propped in February.

She had the front of his shirt open and her fingers under it and then she had it off him over her head and off both arms, and she did not go round the room turning anything down.

“You are rank one and your chest has been spent since the fourth year and it is going to cost you more than it costs me,” she said. “I have had about nine men in that room in six years and every one of them came up four flights for something and stayed too long, and you have never once asked me a thing about myself, and that is why you are getting up off that stool with your coat still on.”

She got him out of the rest of it in about eleven seconds and then she turned him with two fingers at the back of his neck and took him into her mouth, tongue first and then deep, and her hands stayed out of it entirely, which was not modesty and was not showing off.

The steam came under the door in a slow push every time a machine changed load and the calendar came away from the plaster by a hair and went back the way it had been.

She came off him and got him turned and put him down onto the second table with her hip and climbed up over him, and she was quick about all of it and she enjoyed it, and she was not one degree less direct about that than she was about anything else in that room.

He got his hand round the back of her head and she let him, and then she told him to take it away again because she was not going to have a thing done to her in the middle of it that she had not asked for, and he took it away.

“Six years,” she said. “Nine sheets and a pigeonhole and about eleven things in a drawer and a woman who cannot read has been up these stairs twice. I have been the only person in this city who can write a thing down properly since the seventh year, and I have had about nine men in here and not one of them ever asked me for anything.”

She got hold of his cock in her hand and worked her fingers up the whole length of it and looked down at it the way she looked at a ledger she already knew the answer in, and then she laughed once out loud at nothing at all, which she had not expected to do. Her mouth was wet and she did not wipe it and neither did he.

“Now tell me what you are going to do about it,” she said. “Say something with a sheet in it. That is what you are for.”

## What She Did With Her Own Hands

She kept her fingers dug into the wood of the table on both sides of him and rode him out the end of it, and he got a hand up under her and found her hard and slick at the join of her and went on working the same two fingers until her legs started to shake, and there was semen on her own wrist and on the inside of her thigh and neither of them wiped anything.

He got a hand into her hair and she let him keep it there for about four seconds and then she moved it to the back of her neck, which was not doing anything.

“Again,” she said. “And this time I am going to do all of it myself and you are not going to help me with one second of it, and you are going to lie there and be told about it, and you have about nine seconds before I start.”

He was on his back on the second table with his coat on and the top landing of his own building in his head, and he had to stop twice, and she waited both times without looking at him and without saying one word about either.

When she was finished she climbed off and stood in the middle of that room about eleven feet by nine with the steam going up past a window that is never cleaned and the pencil lying on the bench where it had been when the door opened.

“Right,” said Perla Quist. “Now sit down and read it.”

## What She Said Afterwards, Standing Up

She gave him about nine minutes on the stool at the end of the bench and did not sit down herself and did not look at the sheet.

“Nine years,” she said. “Four lines under two printed words and not one figure on any of them, and about four hundred pieces of pencil paper that went up those stairs and went down them and were never in a book anywhere in this city.”

She got the pencil and squared it against the bench.

“You have taken a sheet out of that drawer and you are going to carry it down four flights and along about four hundred yards and you are going to put it somewhere, and there is not one place in this city for a sheet like that to live except a person.”

She did not say *I* and she did not say *you*.

“There is a bench in that room with a drawer in the wall on the left of it that was shut on the twenty-first of October of the seventh year and cannot be opened again by anybody, and there is a bundle on a table in that room with twenty-six things on it and nothing is ever going to be taken off it, and there is a column in a book four feet from that drawer that nothing may be taken out of.”

She put the pencil down.

“So you will put it somewhere. And if you put it in the drawer it is gone for ever and that is a clean answer. And if you put it in the column it can never be improved and it will be read out loud to about nine people a week by a man who does not know what any of it is for.”

“I know.”

“You know it because a man told you so on the twenty-ninth of August over a counter,” said Perla Quist, “and I am not a person who is told things and I am asking you to say it again at that bench.”

He said it again. She listened to all of it and did not improve a word of it.

“I am not going to thank you for carrying it,” she said, “and I am saying that out loud so that you do not do it twice.”

*Three thousand and ninety-four days. Less three thousand and eighty-eight, the twenty-ninth of August of this year, is six, and a man carried a piece of pencil paper up four flights with a question on it that was about the paper and not about the woman, and she said yes, and she wrote four lines out in her own hand on her own paper, and nothing in this city will take any of it.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and seventy-four days, which is twenty-two months and fourteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

# The Twelfth Question

Three thousand one hundred and ninety-five was the sixteenth of December and the twenty-third is day three thousand two hundred and two, and three thousand two hundred and two less three thousand one hundred and ninety-five is seven, and twenty-three less sixteen is seven by the month count.

## Four Flights On The Twenty-Third

She came up four flights on the twenty-third of December the way she had come eleven times in eleven months and a twelfth time, with her hand shut inside her coat and the stair full of winter light from the landing window at the second floor.

Lin Hao was not in the building. He was two streets away at the branch with a bucket in his hand, because rank one gets assigned work and he had asked for that shift and the morning was one of his four nights made up elsewhere.

Wenna Raut was at the table under the window with the Register open and the pen ready, and about nine people were in the corridor about four feet wide, and the cold came in under the door every time it opened.

The woman stood at the counter with her shawl over her head and her hands red from cold and put a piece of pencil paper across the wood with a pencil across the bottom of it in a hand of her own.

She was sent by not one person. She was traced by not one person. She came on her own, the way she had come in February and in May, and no reason was given for her coming and none was asked for.

## The Sentence She Had Said Eleven Times

Wenna Raut took the paper and read it where it lay, because she may only enter what she is given and a sheet at a counter is a thing given.

On it, in a hand of her own, uneven and pressed hard enough to furrow the paper, was the sentence she has said eleven times in eleven months at a bench and at a counter, asking whether what happened to her happened.

Wenna Raut looked at her for about nine seconds without speaking, and then she did what the instruction of the thirtieth of March says to do when a person says a thing at that counter.

She opened the bound Register to the page with the row of the twenty-first of October of the eighth year, with a date on it and an hour against the date and nothing whatever after the hour and a space in the middle of it where the words of the person who brought the paper were to go.

It was the older of the two spaces left in that book, and it had stood empty for twenty-six months and two days, which is seven hundred and eighty-two days, and it was about her, and out of her mouth it had to be filled, and no other mouth would do.

“Will you say it again,” said Wenna Raut, “here, so that I can put down what you say in the words you use.”

The woman said it again, standing up, with her hand on the edge of the wood but not on the paper, in about nine seconds, the way she had said it eleven times before.

Wenna Raut entered it in the space in the middle of that row, out of the mouth of the person it was about, in her own words, with a date and an hour against it, and it cannot be improved afterwards and it cannot be taken from.

The row of the twenty-first of October of the eighth year was complete, and it had been filled out of her mouth and not his, which is the only way that space was ever going to be filled.

## The Answer That Is A Permission

Then Wenna Raut did what the instruction of the tenth of June says to do when a person brings a paper to that counter, and what the instruction of the second of December says to do when any person puts one question.

She told the woman what the office holds about her, in that room, with nothing added and nothing improved.

One row, the twenty-first of October of the eighth year, now filled. One sheet squared against the edge of a bench four flights up, with four words on the back of it in a clerk hand, entered as a row about a non-event because it has not been sent to anyone and cannot be. That was the whole of it.

And under the question the woman had put that morning, Wenna Raut entered the only true answer available, in the office hand, with a date and an hour against it.

*This office is not permitted to ask you anything.*

It is a permission and not a fact. It is true. It is the only true answer that office has ever had for her, and it went in without improvement and may not be taken from.

Not one question was put to her by anyone that morning. That was plain to all about nine in the corridor, who heard all of it through wood, because no question was put to her at any hour that morning except the one she was asked in order to enter what she said.

The woman listened to her own sentence read back once and her answer read once, and nodded the way people nod when a thing is finished.

“Is that in the book,” she said.

“It is,” said Wenna Raut. “With a date and an hour against each, in the words you used. A row may be added to and may not be taken from.”

The woman put her hand inside her coat where the office sheet with four words on the back of it has been carried up and down a street for about a year, and left it there, and went down four flights with empty hands and a full row, and not one person followed her and not one person thanked her.

Lin Hao learned it at about six in the evening when he came up with washwater still on his sleeves, and Wenna Raut read him both rows where they lay and wrote nothing new while he stood there.

## What The Corridor Heard

About nine in the corridor heard the permission read out through wood and stood a while after she went down, because most had come to ask about papers and none had ever heard an answer that was not about a paper.

A woman with a basket asked Wenna Raut whether the permission meant the office would never ask her anything either, and Wenna said it meant exactly that for the woman who had asked, and for others the sixth line says anyone may ask and the office shall answer in writing.

A man with rain on his cap asked whether his own row from May could be answered the same way, and Wenna said any answer he gives goes under what he was told, in the words he uses, with a date and an hour against it.

Lin Hao stood at the end of the corridor with his back to the wall and listened to all of it without stepping forward, because stepping forward would have made him into a man answering in a corridor and answers belong in the book.

He wept then, once, with his hands over his face, because eleven denials had become one permission and the one person that office had asked the most would never be asked a twelfth, and the book had finally done the one thing it was opened for, which was to listen.

## What He Carried To The Laundry

The next morning on the first of his three off he went up four flights to a laundry with steam under the door and told Perla Quist what had gone into the book, because she had read four words once and written nothing down and was owed the knowledge of what those words had become.

“The row of the twenty-first of October of the eighth year is filled,” he said, standing at the end of the bench with his hands out of his pockets so that she could see he carried nothing. “Out of her mouth. In her own words. With a date and an hour. The answer under it is a permission and not a fact.”

Perla squared the ruler against the edge of the bench and did not pick the pencil up.

“Then the second sheet stays where it is,” she said. “Squared against the edge of a bench four flights up, with four words on the back of it. The row in the Register is still the only true sentence that office has published about her, and now there is a second true sentence under a blank that had stood empty for twenty-six months.”

She looked at him steadily.

“You did not ask her anything,” she said. It was not a question.

“I was two streets away with a bucket,” said Lin Hao. “Wenna asked her to say it again so that she could enter what she said. Nothing else was put to her at any hour.”

Perla nodded once and wiped the pencil on the cloth on the nail by the door.

“Then the stair worked the way it should,” she said. “She came on her own. She said her sentence. She got a permission for an answer. No hand sent her and no hand will follow her. That is the whole of it, and it is enough.”

He went down four flights lighter than he had come up, with the permission in his head like something read back once in a clerk voice, and he knew the book had taken the one field it was ever going to take, out of her mouth and not his.

His own blank row from August, with a date and an hour and nothing after the hour, stood untouched in the same book. No hand filled it that winter. It waits still, with its space in the middle of it, and the book holds it the way it holds everything, without hurry and without release.

*Three thousand two hundred and two days. Less three thousand one hundred and ninety-five, the sixteenth of December of this year, is seven, and she came up four flights on the twenty-third of December and put the sentence she has said eleven times into a book in her own words and the answer entered under it is a permission and not a fact.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is eighty-three, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-three of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and eighty-two days, which is twenty-six months and two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

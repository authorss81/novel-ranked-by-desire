# A Door With People In It

Two thousand nine hundred and eighty-one was the twelfth of May and the nineteenth is day two thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight, and two thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight less two thousand nine hundred and eighty-one is seven, and nineteen less twelve is seven by the month count.

## About Nine In A Corridor Four Feet Wide

There were about nine of them at about eleven on the nineteenth, and the corridor is about four feet wide and goes past the counter and on to a fire door that is never opened, and it has been that same four feet since the day the building was put up.

Nine is what it was at the beginning of May and nine is what it was on the twelfth. It is not nine because anybody arranged it.

She could hear the door at the bottom of the stair coming off its latch from about nine feet away, and that is a sound this building has made nine times in eight working days and then twice a week since, and it is not a person, and it has never once been a person.

The woman who had come on the ninth was not one of the nine.

He had looked for her on the way up at about a quarter to eleven and had known before he got to the second landing that he would not find her, because this office may not ask a person anything, and because there is not one form in this city on which it could ask her where she goes, and because he has never sent anybody to anybody in nine years.

## What A Public Room Is For

“An office with people waiting at it is a public room,” said Wenna Raut. “There is no version of this building in which that is not so, and I worked that out in about nine seconds on a morning in March and I have not said it out loud since, because a fact of that shape does not want saying out loud.”

She put the pen across the book.

“And in a public room people talk to each other in front of you. There are about nine of them standing in here at this moment waiting to be told a thing about themselves, and there is nowhere in four feet for a person to say a thing about somebody else quietly.”

The corridor went on doing exactly what a corridor does.

That is the part of it that has not changed in the nine days since the ninth of May, and it is not going to change, and there is nothing anybody in this building can do about it and nothing in the city that would let anybody try.

Two women in a line at a counter in a room with one door are going to talk to each other whether there is a book in Ostara or not. The only question anybody ever gets to answer is whether there is a book afterwards that says what they said.

“And I am not able to stop it,” said Wenna Raut, “and neither is the man at the end of the table, and neither of us is able to say a word about it, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the eighth of October of the first year.”

## The Second Woman

The second woman was standing third in the line. She had been behind the first one on the ninth while that sentence was being written, and at about eleven she said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to the counter and to the corridor and to about nine people who did not know her:

“That is the woman who came in on the ninth. She has been going round that building for three weeks telling everybody that somebody in the second office went through her mother’s papers and took four months off her standing for it. She told me in the queue. She has told about nine of you by now and not one of you has read a word of what she actually said, and what she actually said was about a window.”

There was a silence in that corridor of about two seconds.

It is not one word of that true.

He did not know that and could not have known it. It was about a window and an office and a figure and a mother’s papers, and he is not entitled to a word of any of it.

But he knew the shape of it, because a woman had stood at that counter on the ninth and said nine words about a window and about not sleeping, and Wenna Raut had written down whatever she had actually said, and he had been standing in the room for it.

A man further down the corridor said, “Is that right.”

“Ask her,” said the second woman. “She is here. She is the one who is here on the ninth every week.”

## Entered In Her Words

Wenna Raut took the pen up.

“No,” he said.

“I have to,” said Wenna Raut. “She said it at that counter. It was said out loud in this room in front of nine people, and the instruction of the thirtieth of March says that when a person says a thing at that counter this office writes down what that person said in the words that person used.”

“It does not say what is true.”

“It has never said what is true and there is no version of it that could, and if I leave it out then I have improved it, and improving it is the one thing I am not allowed to do.”

He had used that up in February.

She wrote for about forty seconds, in the words the second woman had used, with the date at the top and the hour against the date, while about nine people in a corridor four feet wide listened to a clerk being accurate.

She did not change one word of it. She did not add that she believed it to be false, because a clerk may only enter what she is given, and not one person in this city had given her that.

“You could have put a line under it,” he said. “You did it in February, with me. You told me the shape of an answer is not an answer and you asked me what happens if somebody else may.”

“I am not writing a correction in a row that has been written nine minutes ago,” said Wenna Raut, “because a row may be added to and may not be taken from, and the second one of those two things has just happened to a woman who said four sentences in a corridor about somebody who was not in the room.”

## Two Of Them, Nine Days Apart

There are two rows now in two different books in two different buildings about four hundred yards apart and they are both in a clerk’s hand and both have a date and an hour against them.

The first says that a man at a window in the third month of last year told her that nothing had happened to her, and that it did happen, and that she has not slept since, and that she is not asking for anything.

The second says that she has been going round that building for three weeks telling everybody that somebody in the second office went through her mother’s papers and took four months off her standing.

Both of them are in that office’s books for ever. Neither of them can be improved. Neither of them can be taken from.

There is not one form anywhere in this city on which a person may be shown what a book says about her. That was true on the ninth of May and it is true now, and the ninth of May is the first day in the history of this city that it was ever untrue in a way anybody could put a name to.

He could ask one question in that room. He has one.

“Was it her own,” he said. “The second one. Was it her own words you were given.”

“It was her own words and I was given them by her at that counter,” said Wenna Raut. “And I wrote them, and they are in the book, and I could not take them out, and I have got about nine questions of my own and I get one a morning and not one of the nine is any use to anybody.”

## The Two Women, And What Neither Of Them Will Be Told

There is a woman in this city who was in that corridor on the ninth of May and is somewhere else in it now, at a counter in a different division, in a different street, with a piece of pencil paper in her hand.

She is going to be told, by somebody, in about four weeks, that she has been saying things to nine people in a corridor that she has never said. She is going to be told it by a woman who was standing third in a line and who believed she was being helpful.

She will not be told that it is in a book. There is nothing on which she can be told that, and a clerk cannot improve a sentence after a person has said it, and a book cannot be asked what it means.

And the woman who said it is going to be told that she was right, by nobody, for ever.

And a man further down that corridor is going to go on believing her, and she is going to hear about that in about four weeks, and she is going to say thank you for it, and the thank you will be the truest sentence anybody has said about any of it.

Two women. One sentence each. Nine days between them. One of them is true and neither of them can be taken from, and about four hundred people in this city will have been told where a thing comes from before either of them is told anything at all.

*Two thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight days. Less two thousand nine hundred and eighty-one, the twelfth of May of this year, is seven, and a woman said out loud in a corridor four feet wide in front of about nine people what another woman had done, and a clerk entered it in her own words because the instruction says this office writes down what a person says, and neither of the two of them will ever be told.*

*Less two thousand nine hundred and thirty-nine, the thirtieth of March of this year, is forty-nine, and the thirty-third sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last forty-nine of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and sixty-eight days, which is eighteen months and twenty-eight days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

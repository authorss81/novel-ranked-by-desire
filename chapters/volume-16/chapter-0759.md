# Eleven At The Rail

Two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six was the seventeenth of March and the twenty-fourth is day two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three, and two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three less two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six is seven, and twenty-four less seventeen is seven by the month count.

## Half Past Two, And A Sheet That Was Not Squared Again

The thirty-third sheet has been lying on the table under the window since the morning of the twentieth, when he took it out of the flat file himself and squared it to the edge of the wood, and a sheet that has been squared early goes crooked in the fold over four days, and at about half past two this afternoon Wenna Raut brought the edge of it true with the edge of the wood in about three seconds, in the ordinary way.

And then she did not square it again.

What she does on a quarter-end is square it, and then square it again about four minutes later. She did that on the thirtieth of March and on the thirtieth of June and on the thirtieth of September and on the thirtieth of December, four quarters running, and she did not do it on any of those four afternoons, and she did not appear to notice that she had not done it.

In six days it is four o’clock, and there is a piece of paper going to two thousand counters.

He noticed. He has noticed it four times and he had said nothing about it on any of them, because it is a sheet that comes down off a rail four times a year and it does not come back, and because a woman who squares a sheet of paper twice at half past two on a quarter-end is a woman who has always done it and is not going to explain herself to a man on a branch shift.

The figure at the head of it is the figure it has carried since the thirtieth of March of the fifth year, and nothing in this city can move it and nothing ever has.

Below the head, about four hundred and nineteen lines, every one of them carrying a date and an hour and nothing at all after the hour and nothing along the line.

Below that, the second column, with four lines in it under two printed words that say **carried by**. Three of them are older than the third one’s date. The fourth was entered on the third of December and it has a date at the head of it and a name and a sentence in a clerk’s hand and a man’s name under that, and no figure of any kind anywhere on the line, and it is the first line in nine sheets and nine years that is a claim.

Below that, the four sentences at the foot, and about nine feet under them six lines in this office’s hand, one of the six struck right through with a name set underneath where it was struck.

And two inches of nothing at the bottom of it, where a person at a rail can get down low enough to read.

## Four Minutes To Four, In A Corridor, Asking Himself

He got to the passage off Cordwain at about four minutes to four and stood in it and did not go down to the rail.

He had asked a man behind a counter under a window a question in December and the answer had been no, and the answer had been the whole of the question, and it had been the first question he had ever asked that man in nine years, and he had carried it for eighty-four days.

He asked himself the same question at four minutes to four, in a corridor, on his own.

Is there anything on that sheet I should not read.

The answer came back in about four seconds and it was no, and it came back for two reasons and neither of them was about the fourth line in the column.

The first was that he did not know which part of the sheet he meant. He had been carrying “that sheet” for eighty-four days as though it were a particular thing, and it is a page about eleven feet long when it is open, and it has a number at the top of it and four hundred and nineteen lines and four names and four sentences and six lines under them and two inches of nothing, and a question about a page that size does not have an answer.

The second was that he did not want the answer to be yes. He had wanted a no badly enough to fly down four flights and ask a stranger for it, and he had been told no and had been right to have been, and he was not doing it again.

## Eleven At The Rail

There were eleven at it at four minutes past four.

That is what was there at the last quarter-end and it is what was there at the one before that, and the difference between eleven then and eleven at the one before is nothing at all, and it will be eleven again in six days, and about nine people a week stop at that rail and two thousand counters hold the rest of it.

He had never before stood still for the whole of it. He stood still for the whole of it this time, nine feet back with his hands in his coat pockets.

The man read the four sentences and the six lines under them and went straight past the striking, because a man behind a counter reads what is on the page. Then he came down to the column and read the four lines that stand in it, in the voice he reads everything in, at the speed he reads everything at, and he read the last of the four as though it were an address, because that is what is under that heading.

There was a silence in that passage of about two seconds. A woman put a hand on the arm of the man beside her and said something, and the man beside her said something back, and neither of them said anything about a building.

## What A Heading Does To A Name

A heading is printed once a year by a works department two streets away and sent out in a bundle to every division in this city, and it cannot be taken off after it has been printed, and no office in Ostara alters a heading on a form.

**carried by** means whoever carried the thing.

It has meant that on every sheet this office has ever printed and it will mean it on the one in the autumn.

The woman whose name is at the head of the fourth of those four lines carried about four hundred pieces of pencil paper up four flights to a bench and carried them on, and that is what she is on the page for. And there is no form anywhere in this city on which a person at a rail may be told that the sentence under her name is not about her.

He stood nine feet back with his hands in his coat pockets and let a man who is paid nothing read a woman’s name out of a column about what she carried, to eleven people, at the speed he reads everything at.

And he understood the thing about the quarter-end, standing there, which he had not understood in the eighty-four days he had been carrying the question.

He had been asking whether the counterman might read the fourth line in a way that made it about a person.

There was no way to ask him that.

No person was entitled to tell him to read it differently, and there never will be one, and the only thing in this city that could have changed how it sounded was a different heading on a different form. The heading is printed in a building this office has never sent a piece of paper to in nine years.

## Ten Minutes To Five

He was still standing there when a clerk came up the passage from the stair at the back of the counter at about ten minutes to five, holding a sheet of office paper in her hand, and she came the whole way down the passage to find him, which she has done twice in nine years.

“There is a person at the counter,” said Wenna Raut.

“I know. I heard the door.”

“No. I have been at the counter for an hour and she came in at about a quarter past and she has been talking for about forty minutes and she has not left.”

She held the sheet up, not at him, at nothing in particular, the way she holds a thing she has been given.

“She has said four things out loud. Not to me. Out loud, in the room, to the room, the way somebody talks when they have worked out that not one word of it is going to be written down.”

“What did she say.”

“That is not the question you should be asking me and you know that, and I have spent my one question of the morning on something else.”

Wenna Raut turned the sheet over and put it against her own arm.

“What I am going to tell you is that there is a field in this office for what people say to it, and it is the fifth one, and it is about what was done to a person, and the words go in it from that person’s own mouth, and nothing has ever gone into it in nine years.”

“Yes.”

“And there is no instruction,” said Wenna Raut. “That is what I have come down four flights and two corridors to tell you.”

“There is a field, and there is a person saying four things into a room, and there is not one word of standing instruction in this office that says a clerk may put one of them in it. I may only enter what I am given, and there is no other person in this building you may ask that question of.”

“I have already given you the shape of an answer to it once this month, and you told me that you knew it was a shape.”

He did not have anything to say to that.

“I am not going to stand here with a sheet in my hand until you have one,” said Wenna Raut. “I am going to take it back up two flights and I am going to put it on the table under the window and I am going to leave it there until the thirtieth of March at about half past two, when that sheet is squared and signed for, and then you are going to tell me whether this office enters sentences.”

*Two thousand nine hundred and thirty-three days. Less two thousand nine hundred and twenty-six, the seventeenth of March of this year, is seven, and a sheet was squared at about half past two and not squared again, and there were eleven at the rail at four minutes past four, and a man read the four lines of a column out loud to all eleven of them.*

*Less two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, the thirtieth of December of the eighth year, is eighty-four, and the thirty-second sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last eighty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is five hundred and thirteen days, which is seventeen months and three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

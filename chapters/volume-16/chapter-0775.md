# Four Kitchens Go Quiet

Three thousand and thirty-five was the sixth of July and the thirteenth is day three thousand and forty-two, and three thousand and forty-two less three thousand and thirty-five is seven, and thirteen less six is seven by the month count.

## The Lane At Ten Past Ten, And Nine Figures Before Eleven

The lane is about nine feet wide and it is two streets off Cordwain, and there are nine stools down one side of it and nine districts behind the other side of it, and the man at the head of them has a slate under his arm and twelve books on a shelf behind him that belong to no district in this city and are not going to.

Emil Brack is paid a grain a head, which is a small thing to say and the whole of what there is. He has taken about nine figures a morning before eleven for nine years and has never once been paid for any of them and has never once asked to be.

He had the slate face down on his knees when Lin Hao came down the lane, which is not the way he keeps it.

“Sit down,” he said. “No. Do not sit down, because I have had this for about nine days and I have been waiting for somebody to put in front of me.”

## About Four In The Last Eight Days

“In the last eight days it has been in about four kitchens,” said Emil Brack. “By the thirtieth it will be in about nine. That is the same nine you had in June and the month before it.”

“And it is a count of kitchens. It is not a count of claims, it is not a count of people, and it is not a count of pieces of paper, and I am not going to have a man in a white plate put one of those four numbers inside another one in front of me again.”

He turned the slate over and did not write on it.

“Where I am going to stop being right about that is on the wall, and you know the wall better than I do.”

## A Hundred And Twenty-Two Days

The bottom two inches of that wall under the striking at the foot of the hill have had nothing new in them since the eleventh of March.

That is a hundred and twenty-two days on the thirteenth of July, and a piece of rag paper about the size of the foot of a printed page, and about nine words in it, and it is about a thing that happened to somebody else in a doorway.

About nine people in a week get down low enough to read those four inches. About nine of them did it in the last seven days and about nine of them did it the week before that and about nine of them did it the week before that.

They have been going down there since March and finding nothing, and they have gone down there again, and they will go down there again this week.

That is the part of it that is new and it is the part he had not expected.

He had expected the reading to stop with the writing.

## About Nine Of Them Went Down Last Week

He had it from the lane, because a man at the head of nine stools sees about nine figures a morning and about nine people a week who are not figures.

“Seven of them went down in the last seven days,” said Emil Brack. “And they did not go down and look for something. That is what I want you to hear first. A man who has stopped writing does not stop going down. He goes down and gets his chest against a kerbstone in the cold and reads four inches of pencil that is a hundred and twenty-two days old and he gets up again and goes home.”

“Seven.”

“Seven, and the week before it nine, and the week before that nine, and I have not counted a man going down at that corner since I was on that lane nine years ago because no one counts it and I only did because you have been standing there.”

He turned the slate over again and wrote nothing on it.

“You have spent six weeks trying to make the two inches go quiet on your own and they went quiet on their own, and they are not going to open again because you have stopped looking, and they are not going to open because of anything either of us can do about it in about four years.”

## The Other Clock, Which Is His And Is Not This Month

“Now the other one, and I am going to keep it in its own sentence because the last time a man put one of mine inside the other’s sentence it cost somebody a fortnight.”

“I am not going to put anything anywhere.”

“Good,” said Emil Brack. “In about four years the bottom two inches under that striking will have gone quiet in about four hundred kitchens. About forty people will say that it came from the office off a third court, and about four hundred people will hear it in a kitchen and believe it, and not one of the four hundred will ever be told why it went quiet.”

He put the slate down on the stool beside him, which he has not done in nine years in front of anybody.

“That is four years. That is not this month and it is not this year, and there is nothing whatever you can do about it between now and then, and I have never been frightened of anything else in nine years at that lane and I am frightened of that.”

## The Price, And Who Pays It

The pieces of pencil paper do not come up four flights to a third table because there are none.

That is the second half of the month and it goes the way the first half goes. A woman in the fourth kitchen wrote in the bottom two inches of her own paper for three years and went down to that kerb about four times a year, and she has not written anything in the last nine weeks, and she has not come up four flights, and she is not going to be told anything about either of those facts by anybody.

The going quiet costs nine.

Not nine claims, and not nine pieces of paper, and not nine kitchens. Nine people a month who had one place in this city where they could put down what had been done to them, and have stopped putting it down, and are going to keep not putting it down.

And the price is charged to none of them, which is the ordinary way of a price.

Not one person in this city can say to any one of those nine: there is a counter about four hundred yards from your table where a clerk will write down anything you say out loud, in your own words, and never improve a word of it, and there has been one since the eighth of October of the first year.

That is the whole of what the month costs and it is printed here once because it is a month and there is one of it every month and it is not going to be added up in front of a reader.

## The Woman In The Fourth Kitchen

He asked about the fourth one and got it in about nine seconds, because Emil Brack takes about nine figures a morning and has never pretended for nine years that a figure is not a person.

“Kitchen four is a woman mending other people’s coats in a room with two tables in it,” said Emil Brack, “and she has a table and she has a lamp, and there is a piece of paper in a drawer in that room with nine words on it in her own hand that she wrote in the third month of last year about a room with a door on a court.”

He said it without any weight on it at all.

“She wrote it down because there was nowhere else to write it down, and she has carried that piece of paper down to a kerbstone about four times a year for about two years, and she has not written anything in the bottom two inches of it for about nine weeks.”

“Is that a thing she decided.”

“It is a thing she is doing,” said Emil Brack. “She is not going to tell you why. She has not told me why and I have not asked her and I am not going to, and she is not one of my nine figures and I have got about nine of those a morning and I have never once turned one of them into a person I go and see.”

He got the slate up off the stool.

“That woman is going to go on carrying that piece of paper down that hill about four times a year for about four years, and she is never going to be told that there is a counter about four hundred yards from her table where a clerk would write down anything she said out loud. That is not a cruelty.”

“Not one person in that office has done anything to her. She has simply stopped. And the reason she stopped is a thing about somebody else that she was told in about nine kitchens, and she will never know that that is why.”

## What A Man With A Slate Is Not

“You have been asked,” said Lin Hao.

“I have been asked four times since the fourth of May,” said Emil Brack, “by men who come to the head of that lane on their own time and stand about nine feet off, the way you stand, and every one of them asked me the same thing and I have said the same answer to all four of them and it has not worked one single time.”

“What do you say.”

“I say no,” said Emil Brack. “I have said no four times and I am going to say it a fifth about the same width. And I would like you to understand that a man with a slate is not an instrument. A slate is a thing you put figures on.”

“I have no standing, no form and no page, and there is no book in this city with my name at the head of anything. About four hundred people trusted that about a woman with a pencil, and not one of them will ever trust it about a man at the head of a lane.”

He got the slate up off his knees and put it under his arm.

“Say the denial to them yourself, then. Say it in that room with the clerk in it, and it goes on the page, and a man behind a window in a passage reads the bottom of that page out loud to about nine people a week, and he cannot be asked to read less of it, and he is not going to be told what any of it means.”

That is what he said, and it is what he has said four times in four languages of the same weather, and it was correct.

Lin Hao stood at the head of nine stools and said nothing, and the answer went past him about four hundred yards away into a corridor about four feet wide where nine people were waiting to be told something about themselves.

“I am not asking you to do anything,” said Emil Brack. “I am telling you that I said no four times and it did not work once, and that is the only news I have had in nine days and I have given it to you for nothing.”

*Three thousand and forty-two days. Less three thousand and thirty-five, the sixth of July of this year, is seven, and in the last eight days the bottom two inches under that striking have not been written on in about four kitchens and by the thirtieth it will be about nine, and about nine people a week are still going down there to read a hundred and twenty-two days old pencil, and a man with a slate has been asked four times and has said no four times.*

*Less three thousand and twenty-nine, the thirtieth of June of this year, is thirteen, and the thirty-fourth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is six hundred and twenty-two days, which is twenty months and twenty-two days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

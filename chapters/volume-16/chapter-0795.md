# The Figure And The Standing

Three thousand one hundred and sixty-seven was the eighteenth of November and the twenty-fifth is day three thousand one hundred and seventy-four, and three thousand one hundred and seventy-four less three thousand one hundred and sixty-seven is seven, and twenty-five less eighteen is seven by the month count.

## Two Ruled Squares At The Foot Of A Form

The month form had been face up on the board by the kerb at the foot of the hill since the seventh, with the rain getting under one corner and the paste going soft along the top edge.

Lin Hao stood eleven feet from it at about five in the afternoon with a pencil in his pocket nine inches from his hand and about two hundred people going past between five and half past five along the kerb.

Both ruled squares at the foot of it were empty, as they had been since the morning of the eighth of October of the first year, when a clerk was handed a sheet and a form and no instruction and put two words into a box because the box was there.

One square was for the figure of the thing at the address. One was for the standing of it. The words were printed small under each, worn nearly smooth by seven years of weather and fingers.

He wanted to know what a person who cannot read would do with them. That was the whole of what he wanted, and it had been in him since September, when a woman at a bench told him there was not one place in Ostara for a sheet like hers to live except a person.

A boy came up the slope with a paste brush in his fist and a fresh corner of paste on his cuff, squared the board with the toe of his shoe the way he always does, and went back up without looking at either square. Lin Hao did not speak to him. He had nothing to ask him and not one person in Ostara is entitled to ask it.

## A Mark And Not A Figure

Emil Brack found him there at about twenty past five with his slate under his arm and rain on his delivery coat, on his way down with the last of his round.

“You are looking at the boxes,” said Brack.

“I am,” said Lin Hao.

“A person who cannot read cannot write a figure,” said Brack. “That is the whole of it. Figures are for people who were taught them. A person who was never taught can make a mark, and the mark has been the way Ostara has said I was here for about twenty years.”

Lin Hao took his hands out of his pockets because the cold had got into them and he wanted Brack to see he was listening with all of it.

“What sort of mark,” he said.

“A line,” said Brack. “Across the bottom of a sheet. In a hand of her own. It does not say what was in the boxes. It does not say what the figure is or what the standing is. It says a person stood at that board on that day and left something of her own under them.”

Brack shifted the slate under his arm. Rain ran off the brim of his cap and onto his coat and he did not wipe it.

“Is there any form anywhere for asking what a box is for,” said Lin Hao.

“Not one that was ever filled in,” said Brack. “There is a form in a division building that asks a person what a box on a form is for, and it has stood empty since the first year, because a person who writes in a box is a person who has been asked, and asking costs the asker more than the writer.”

Lin Hao felt something ease in his chest, not relief but the smaller thing that comes before it, when a fact fits into a place that has been empty for months.

“The mark is not a claim,” he said.

“It is not,” said Brack. “A claim is a thing with a date on it. A mark is a thing with a hand on it. The two are different the way a door and a window are different, and Ostara has been mixing them for seven years because the boxes are there and the hands are there.”

## What The Corner Teaches

About nine people came down low enough in the hour Lin Hao stood there, old and young, wet to the knees, bending to read four inches of dressed stone under the striking where about forty words in about four hands had stood since early October about a building off a third court.

Not one of them looked at the two ruled squares. Not one of them put a hand toward them. The squares belonged to a month form and the stone belonged to everyone, and everyone knew which one was theirs without being told.

A woman with grey hair knelt with difficulty and traced one line of the stone writing with her eyes and then got up again with help from a girl beside her, and Lin Hao looked away because watching felt like asking, and there was nothing on which that office might ask anyone anything.

Brack watched too, and said nothing, because a man with a slate is not an instrument and has said so four times since spring to no effect whatever.

“She will come here,” said Brack, quietly, after the two had gone up the slope. “Not up four flights. Here. Where a form has been face up since the seventh and a board takes anything put under it without entering it anywhere.”

Lin Hao did not ask him who. He did not ask him how he knew. He had learned in June that a man with a slate says what he knows when he knows it and will not be hurried before or after.

“If she does,” said Lin Hao, “that office has nothing that can receive it.”

“There does not need to be,” said Brack. “A mark under two empty boxes is not received. It is left. The board holds it until the thirtieth and then a boy takes it down with the form and it goes wherever old forms go, which is nowhere anyone can cite it.”

Rain came harder and the board dripped along its lower edge and the two empty squares shone wet and pale under the light from the lane.

Lin Hao put his hand in his pocket and touched the pencil there, nine inches from a box, and left it where it was, because the moment a word went into one of those boxes somebody in a works department would come down that hill and ask what the word was, and he had spent nine years learning what asking does to the person whose name goes on a list.

He walked back up the slope with Brack as far as the turning, and neither of them spoke about figures or standings again, because both had said all of it and improving it would have made it less true.

At the turning Brack stopped and looked back down at the board with the form on it going soft at the corner.

“About twenty years,” he said. “That is how long a line across the bottom has meant I was here. Longer than your office. Longer than my slate. It will outlast both.”

## What An Old Mark Looked Like

Brack took his slate from under his arm and turned it over and showed Lin Hao the back of it, where the wood was scored with short lines in many hands, one over another, worn nearly smooth by years of thumbs.

“Every winter some hand adds one,” said Brack. “Drivers. Sweepers. Women carrying washing. About nine a year, most years. Not a figure among them. Not a standing. Each one says a person stood here on that day with a load too heavy to sign for.”

Lin Hao ran his finger lightly over the oldest of them, a deep cut across the grain with black in it from years of dirt.

“Did anyone ever ask what they were for,” said Lin Hao.

“Once,” said Brack. “A district officer, about six years back. She asked me whether the marks were claims. I told her a mark is a thing with a hand on it and a claim is a thing with a date on it, and she wrote nothing down, because a woman doing her job knows when writing would make a true thing into a false one.”

Rain ran off the slate and onto Brack coat sleeve and he turned it face down again to keep the chalk dry.

“Your two ruled squares will stay empty,” said Brack. “And hands will go on leaving lines under them. The two have stood side by side for seven years without touching, and they will stand so for seven more, because the city needs both and can only read one.”

Lin Hao walked on up the slope alone after Brack turned down toward Cordwain with his slate, and he thought about hands and dates and the difference between them all the way to the branch, where the night shift waited with buckets and keys and work that asked nothing of anyone except strength.

## What He Told The Clerk That Night

He told Wenna Raut about it at about nine the next morning, with the book shut between them and the cold coming under the door.

“A mark under two empty boxes is not a claim,” he said. “Brack said it plainly. A claim has a date on it. A mark has a hand on it. If a sheet with a pencil line comes to that board, it stays where it was left and no hand here lifts it into any book.”

Wenna nodded once. She did not pick the pen up, because there was nothing to enter and both of them knew it.

“Then the board can hold what the book cannot,” she said. “Two ruled squares empty for seven years, and under them a line in a hand of her own. The office will not receive it and will not be asked to, and that is the safest thing anything left anywhere has ever been.”

He stood at the window after with washwater on his sleeves and felt the small ease from the slope still in him, because at least one place in Ostara could be left alone without being entered, and he had not built it and did not have to defend it.

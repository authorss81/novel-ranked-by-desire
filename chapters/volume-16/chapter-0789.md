# A Man Who Reads The Bottom

Three thousand and one hundred and twenty-six was the seventh of October and the fourteenth is day three thousand and one hundred and thirty-three, and three thousand and one hundred and thirty-three less three thousand and one hundred and twenty-six is seven, and fourteen less seven is seven by the month count.

## Somebody Came Up Four Flights

He came up the stair at about ten past ten in the morning on the fourteenth and he came up it wrong, twice, because the stair in that building is not the stair he comes up every working morning of his life and he has never once been in the building.

He stood in the corridor outside a room with a door on it for about two minutes before he knocked, and about nine people were in that corridor, and none of them had ever seen that man in that building in nine years.

Lin Hao took him in and shut the door, which he had never done for anybody in nine years either, and Wenna Raut looked up from the book and then looked back down at it, because she was the mouth of this office from the thirtieth of March and she had about nine seconds to decide whether a man who is paid nothing is somebody who has been told a thing about himself.

She decided he was not, and went on with her column.

## What He Had Come To Say

“In two weeks,” said the counterman, “about four of them out of about nine have come back.”

“Come back to the rail.”

“Come back to the rail and told their household what I read them, and two of them have brought somebody with them who was not there on the day.”

He put his thumb along the edge of the table without touching it.

“I have read them both halves every single time. I read the line about the building and then I read the line that says the two things are not connected, and then I read that one again because somebody asked me to, and they go away still saying the first one.”

“How many times have you read it twice.”

“Nine times in two weeks,” said the counterman. “I have read it twice nine times for about four people and once for the rest, and it makes no difference whatever which way round I do it, and I have tried it both ways.”

## What He Was Told, Flatly

He was told the true thing in about nine seconds because he had asked for it and he was not going to be made to wait for it.

“A clerk at a window in a division building has had the address in a book since the eighth of October of the first year,” said Lin Hao. “There is no stone on those premises. There never has been one. He looked at the entry twice and read the wax in the corner of the ground floor out twice, because it is the only thing in the entry that is worth reading out.”

The counterman took that in about four seconds.

“Right,” he said.

“You did not know that.”

“No,” said the counterman. “In nine years not one person has come up that stair and told me anything about that page except that it was four sentences and a column and six lines under them, and not one of them has ever said the word no to me.”

## The One Of The Four Who Brought A Woman Who Writes

The worst of the four is not the one who shouts.

He is a man of about fifty who came to the rail on the eighth with his wife, and the wife had the same piece of pencil paper in her hand that he had, and it is a copy, and it was made by ear off a rail in the last week of March in about nine different hands and it is about eleven feet long when it is opened.

He has it because a woman who mends umbrellas wrote the bottom of it out for his wife in about nine seconds and kept none of it, and because his wife cannot read and he can, so he is the one who holds it.

On the eighth of October he asked me to read the ninth sentence again and I did, and his wife wrote it out in front of him on the rail with a pencil, in about eleven words, and took it away folded in the inside of her coat.

That is the third kitchen in this city with the ninth sentence of that page in it in a hand, and I did not know it was the third and there is no instrument in this city that could tell me whether it is the third or the ninth.

## Why It Does Not Help Him

He stood at the end of that table for a while with his hand on his own hip.

“You have just told me a fact,” he said, “and it is the fact I have wanted for about two weeks, and I cannot use it.”

“No.”

“I cannot use it because of what I am,” said the counterman. “I am paid nothing, I am in that passage four hours a day, and about nine people a week stand at a rail and hear the bottom of a page come out of my mouth. And if I say to a woman on the twenty-second of October that there is no stone on those premises, then what I have said that day is not on the page.”

“That is correct.”

“It is not on the page, and no instruction in this city lets a man behind a counter say anything that is not on the page, and there never has been one, and I have never once asked for one and I am not going to start.” He took his hand off his hip. “I am permitted to read all of it or less of it, and what there is, is what there is.”

## The Thing He Could Have Asked For And Did Not

“Then I will say it to you once,” said Lin Hao, “and then I will not say it again. This office can put an instruction at the foot of that page and it goes out at four o’clock on the last working day of a quarter, and a man behind a counter reads it out with everything else and cannot be asked to read less of it.”

“I know what an instruction is. I have read four of them this year.”

“You have read four of them and you could not tell me one thing about any of them, and there is not one person in this city entitled to tell you.”

“That is the arrangement,” said the counterman. “I have said so twice in nine years and I am not going to say it a third.”

He did not ask for anything. That is the whole of what he came up four flights for, and he did not ask for it, and he stood there about four minutes afterwards not asking for it.

He did not ask because he worked it out on the stair on the way up, and it took him the whole of the second flight: there is nothing a man who is paid nothing could ask for that would not have to be put on a page, and the page is the thing that is wrong.

## What The Clerk Did While He Was In The Room

She wrote for about nine minutes while he was standing at the end of that table, and that is longer than a row takes, and he did not stop her and she did not explain herself.

A man who is paid nothing came up four flights and shut a door that had been open for nine years and stood in a room with about nine people in the corridor outside it. There is a line in the front of that book that says a person brings a paper to that counter and this office is to tell that person what this office holds about them.

He did not bring a paper. She worked that out in about four seconds and she went back to her column, and that is the correct answer, and if she had read anything out to that man in that room it would have been the mouth of this office saying a thing about a person to a person who has not asked for it, and she has been that mouth since the tenth of June and she is right about the line of it every time.

Afterwards she said one thing about the door and one thing about the light in the corridor and neither of them was about the building or the stone, and both of them were about nine words long.

## What He Said On The Second Stair

He went down about eleven minutes later and got himself halfway down the second stair and stopped and turned round and came back up four steps, which is not a thing a man does in nine years in a building he does not work in.

“The ninth sentence on that page,” he said. “It is the one about the building. And the tenth one is the denial. And they are in the same hand about nine feet apart, and I read them in the order they stand because that is the order they stand.”

“Yes.”

“I have been reading them in the order they stand since the thirtieth of September and about four people in this city have gone away with the ninth one.”

“Yes.”

He put his hand on the rail of the stair, which is a banister and not a handrail and has been in that building since it was built.

“If I read the tenth one before the ninth one, nobody in this city would believe the ninth,” said the counterman, “and nothing in Ostara tells me whether I am allowed to do that or not, and if I am not allowed, then somebody has to make the tenth one a different kind of thing from the ninth, because at the moment it is not one.”

*Three thousand and one hundred and thirty-three days. Less three thousand and one hundred and twenty-six, the seventh of October of this year, is seven, and a man who is paid nothing came up four flights to a building he does not work in for the first time in nine years, and was told a fact he could not use, and did not ask for anything, and said one thing about the order of two lines on a page on the second stair.*

*Less three thousand and one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is fourteen, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last fourteen of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and thirteen days, which is twenty-three months and twenty-three days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man’s name under it.*

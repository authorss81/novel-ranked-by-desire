# The Second Column Is Four Lines

Three thousand one hundred and forty-six was the twenty-seventh of October and the fourth is day three thousand one hundred and fifty-three, and three thousand one hundred and fifty-three less three thousand one hundred and forty-six is seven, and three out of the thirtieth, which is the last working day of that month, is the fourth of November.

## The Copy In His Coat

He had carried it for seven weeks in the inside of his coat on the left, folded once along the short edge so that the date at the top did not rub against the seam.

It was her paper and her hand and her date, with four lines on it and no figure on any of them, because no figure appears anywhere in that column and she had not been going to invent the shape of one.

Lin Hao took it out on the second landing at about nine in the morning and looked at it for the first time in about nine days, not to read it but to see whether he still had any right to be carrying it.

He had asked about the paper and not about the woman, after six refusals in six years in which every question had been about her. She had given it because of that. She had told him not to fold it and he had folded it once. She had told him not to thank her for carrying it and he had not thanked her.

What he wanted that morning was to put a fifth line under the four. That was the whole of what he wanted, and he had wanted it since the twenty-fourth of September, when a clerk in a room off a third court refused to enter a correction about two printed words and read it back instead to about nine people in a corridor about four feet wide.

A fifth line would have said the two printed words at the head of that column belong to a works department two streets away. It would have had no name in it. It would have gone out at four o clock on the last working day of December to about two thousand counters.

## The Fourth Line

Wenna Raut was at the table under the window with the Register open and a ruler along the edge of it when he came in.

He put the sheet on the wood face up and squared it against the edge, the way everybody does at a counter, and stood back from it so that she could see he was not going to push it toward her.

“Read it where it lies,” he said. “Write nothing down.”

She read it where it lay, all four lines, in the order they stand, with the date first and the man name at the foot last.

“The fourth has a woman name in it,” she said.

“It has,” said Lin Hao.

“She was paid once,” said Wenna Raut. “In public. In a building with no door onto a street. On the third of December of the eighth year, by a clerk in good faith who entered what she was given.”

Lin Hao felt his throat go dry. He had not said any of that to her, and she had said it the way she says anything that has been in a book for eleven months, without looking up.

“I know,” he said.

“And you are considering a fifth,” said Wenna Raut. “With no name in it. About two printed words. To go under the four sentences and above the striking and in no column.”

He did not ask her how she knew. She knew the way she knows anything in that room, by having entered every line that went near it for nine years.

“I am,” he said.

## Why Not One Person Is Paid Twice

He walked to the window and stood with his back to the table because he did not want to look at the sheet while he said the next thing.

“The woman whose name is in the fourth line has been paid once,” he said. “In public. In a building with no door onto a street. About nine people heard it read out at a rail. About nine more heard it in a corridor. She did not ask for it and she did not refuse it.”

Wenna Raut did not pick the pen up. That was how he knew she was listening in the way she listens when a thing is not going to be entered.

“Any correction that names the department names her on about two thousand counters,” said Lin Hao. “Any correction that does not name her cannot be entered, because a clerk may only enter what she is given and a sentence with no person in it is a decision and not a record.”

He turned round.

“I have had that answer since the twenty-fourth of September,” he said. “I had it read back to me standing up, with about nine people in a corridor, and about four of them have it still. I am not asking you to improve it.”

“What are you asking,” said Wenna Raut.

“Whether there is any fifth line with a person in it that does not name her twice,” said Lin Hao.

She looked at the sheet for about nine seconds. Then she looked at him.

“No such line exists,” she said. “Any line that says where that column came from says who went through it. Any line that says who went through it says her. She has been paid once and the page cannot pay her again without putting her on every counter in Ostara.”

Lin Hao felt something go out of him that had been in him for seven weeks, ever since he carried four lines down four flights and along about four hundred yards and learned what two printed words were for.

He had wanted to mend the column. He had wanted to put the truth under it the way he had put the truth under everything else since February. He understood, standing in that room with her paper on the wood, that mending it would cost exactly the person it was meant to spare.

“Not one person is paid twice by me,” he said. “Not in that column. Not on that sheet. Not while I am the hand behind it.”

## What He Did With The Sheet

He picked the sheet up off the wood and put it back in the inside of his coat on the left, folded once along the short edge, where there was nothing else in it.

“I will not ask you to enter it,” he said. “I will not ask you to read it back. I will not carry it down to any rail.”

Wenna Raut nodded once. She had refused him twice in September and she did not refuse him now, because there was nothing to refuse.

“The column is four lines,” she said. “It was four lines on the thirtieth of December of the eighth year and it is four lines now and it will be four lines on the thirtieth of December of this year.”

He stood at the end of the table for about four minutes without saying anything, with the sheet against his ribs through wool and linen, and he thought about a woman on a bench four streets away who had written four lines out in about four minutes and pushed them across with two fingers and told him there was not one place in Ostara for a sheet like that to live except a person.

She had been right. He was the person. He would go on being the person. That was the whole of what carrying meant, and he had not understood it when he asked for it.

“I asked about the paper,” he said at last. “She answered about the paper. The paper is in my coat and it is not in any book and it will not go on any page that goes out at four o clock.”

He went down four flights with it still in his coat and it got no further than a stairwell, the way another sentence had once got no further, and he was glad of the likeness because it meant he had learned at least one thing twice and kept it both times.

At the foot of the stair he stopped and took the sheet out once more and looked at the date at the top in her hand, and then he put it back, and he did not show it to anyone in the corridor about four feet wide who stood aside to let him pass.

## What Adeyemi Said On The Landing

Yusuf Adeyemi was on the first landing with rain on his shoulders and a bundle of dockets under his arm from a division building two streets away, and he stopped Lin Hao with a hand half raised and then lowered.

“Show me nothing,” he said. “I know what you are carrying without seeing it. A woman hand with four lines and a date. I am Registrar of Unrecorded Merit and I have known for eleven months that such a sheet exists without ever having seen it, which is exactly how it should be.”

Lin Hao kept his coat shut. “I considered a fifth line and I am not entering one.”

“I know,” said Adeyemi. “Wenna told me before you came down. She was right to tell me, because a fifth line with no name in it would have gone into the Register as a decision, and the Register holds papers received and words said at a counter, and a carrier name from another office book is neither.”

They stood on the landing with about nine people moving below them and the cold coming up the stairwell from the yard.

“The fourth line stays because it was given in good faith,” said Adeyemi. “The fifth stays out because you would have to pay her twice to put it in. That is the whole of it, and it is also the reason the drawer stays shut and the bundle stays at twenty-six. Some things are kept by being carried and not by being entered.”

Lin Hao felt gratitude sharp enough to hurt, because Adeyemi had priced the refusal without being asked and had done it in words that left her out of it.

“Will the Register hold under the sixth line,” said Lin Hao.

“It will hold because she will hold it,” said Adeyemi. “She has held every line since March. Go home and sleep. You look like a man who has been carrying paper too long.”

Lin Hao went down the last flight with the sheet against his ribs and the landing words in his head, and he knew the column would stay at four lines through December, not because any rule said so but because paying twice was a thing he would not do.

*Three thousand one hundred and fifty-three days. Less three thousand one hundred and forty-six, the twenty-seventh of October of this year, is seven, and a column with four lines in it was read where it lay and no fifth went under it because the woman whose name is in the fourth has been paid once in public and not one person is paid twice by him.*

*Less three thousand one hundred and nineteen, the thirtieth of September of this year, is thirty-four, and the thirty-fifth sheet has been face up on that rail for every one of the last thirty-four of them.*

*Less two thousand four hundred and twenty, the twenty-first of October of the seventh year, is seven hundred and thirty-three days, which is twenty-four months and thirteen days after them, and a drawer in that room has been shut for every one of them under an instruction with a man name under it.*

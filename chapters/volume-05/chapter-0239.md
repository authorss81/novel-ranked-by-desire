# The Frame And Her Hand

He went to the Reading Square on the twenty-ninth in the morning with Petra Ilyin, because she had asked him to carry the bundle while she looked at a wall.

It was Thursday and the Thursday crew was at the ranks wall at nine with two ladders and three pots, painting the week off it before the figures went up at ten. The board at the side stood untouched under its shade with the schedule on it. The schedule had gone up on the twenty-sixth. The certificate had been dated on the twenty-seventh and received on the twenty-eighth. The board would be repainted on the first of next month. Today was not its day.

He felt empty. The receipt was in his pocket with the head line. The house had three papers that agreed. He had wanted to refuse the certificate and had signed for it instead, and the steadiness under the grief had held for a day and then gone thin.

Petra stood at the foot of the wall with her bundle under her arm and watched the painters work. Eleven years an officer and a fortnight discharged, and she was on no roll of any kind. She watched the way she had watched counters for eleven years, for the hands and not the paper. Two men up, one below. Paint laid at the top and drawn down. Wrists steady. No haste.

"You wanted to see the paint," he said.

"I wanted to see the frame," she said. "The schedule hangs in a frame. The frame is the office's. The hand that put it there is a clerk's. I have spent eleven years looking at hands."

A woman beside them read the number aloud to a child and moved on. A clerk from the Reading came out with a book, wrote the hour under the frame, went in. The square filled and emptied the way it did between hours. The lamps on brackets hung dark on their iron. In daylight every figure was exact. Petra watched it all without writing anything down, because she carried no notebook and kept nothing, and that was still the strongest position in the city, to look and not to hold.

At ten the Reading door opened and the same two clerks came out with a docket frame and a sheet. They did not touch the schedule. They hung a notice beside it about hours. The clerks squared the frame, stepped back, went in. Their hands were quick and plain.

An old man at the wall end asked him whether the number on the board was a street number. He said it was a household number, and that it stood for the house and for everybody on the roll of it. The old man looked at the schedule above the number and said six, and he said six, and the old man said that was a great many for one street and went off to find his daughter.

That was the first time a stranger had asked him a question about the house that he could answer, and the answer had cost him nothing, and he stood there afterwards with the thought arriving sideways. A board argued with nobody. It only made the question easy to ask and easy to stand next to.

Petra watched the hands, not the paper.

"There," she said quietly as they went in. "That is what a hand does when it is not answering for anything. Mine did that for eleven years. I marked a hundred and thirty-two dockets on the second of the month at four seconds apiece and never asked who produced them. A hand can do anything for eleven years if it never asks."

They walked the length of the wall slow. The paint was fresh and the tile shone under it. Low at the end where the crew had started there was the place Beatrix had shown him, where the paint went thinner over tile touched often.

They came to the far end, where the ranks stopped and the street began, and Petra stood with her bundle against her hip and looked back along the whole run of it.

"That is the entire of what a city knows," she said. "Not what anybody did inside it. That a street holds so many and the ground holds so much. Everything past that number is paper. I signed returns off that number for eleven years and I never once asked whether the number was right, because it was not my number. It was above me."

"Wren found your name here in the dark," Petra said. "On the eighteenth. With her hand. In about two seconds. Darkness kept it off for five years and the city switches darkness off at eleven. Now it is on a side board in daylight with a schedule over it. That is what paper does. It moves a thing from dark to light without anyone deciding."

He nodded. She was frightened of her own hands, and he could see why. On the first her hand would have to watch Marguerite mark a book, and a mark at the foot of a docket was the only thing standing between a plate and the dead list.

They rode the tram back standing with the bundle between them. The car was full. A guard checked seasons without being asked. A woman gave her seat to an older man and stood holding the strap. The market below the hatch spilled noise through the windows. Two hundred and three people moving through a morning that ran to two. Petra held the bundle steady against the sway. She had ridden trams with bundles for eleven years.

Her body knew the sway the way Beatrix knew a line. He watched her keep her feet without holding on and thought about discharged officers, women who had been instruments and then were not, and what they did with hands that had marked a hundred and thirty-two dockets and would never mark again.

At Ferrier Street Petra stopped at the bottom of the sixty-one steps, counted them under her breath the way she had counted them since the first of June, and looked up.

"On the first," she said. "Ten to nine. I will be here with the bundle. You be where you are told. Do not come down to watch. A marking is not a thing to watch."

"I will be where I am told," he said.

They climbed together. The nail held nothing. The rectangle stayed pale. The Thursday pass at the square had taken the week off a working wall and left the side board standing, and the landing wall at Ferrier Street stayed unpainted with its pale shape, because no crew painted a stair.

He stood on the landing with his hand flat on the rail and looked at the pale shape for the first time since the fourth. Twenty-five days of it. He had a receipt in his pocket that could not be given back and a shape on a wall that could not be painted over, and only one of the two was his to have done.

Somewhere in the middle of June he had decided that the wall would be the harder of the two to live with. It was not. Sun had told him on the twenty-seventh that a fourth paper in four places would have turned them into one document. The pale rectangle was the only thing in the city that could not be copied, and he looked at it and found he was glad of it, which he had not expected to find and did not entirely trust.

In the kitchen Tessa had the entry sheet on the table, filled in her own hand, face down. She would carry it to the Vestry office on the morrow and put it into the lines. It described a clinic, not a person. It gave hours, not names. Nine years of a quarter of eight. Two hundred and three a morning.

"Read it," she said to him.

He turned it over and read it. Hours. Counts. A hatch. A room with one window. No name but hers.

"It is true," he said.

"It is true," she said. "And from the morrow it is counted. That is the price. A hole in a wall is safe while nothing is looking at it. From the morrow something is."

He carried her crate to the hatch and set it down where two hundred and three people would pass it in the morning. The hatch was plain wood with a counter worn smooth by nine years of hands. He put his palm flat on it a moment, feeling the smoothness. A place got smooth by being used truly. That was the whole of its instrument.

"You have carried enough crates," Tessa said. "Go up. Eat."

Ines came in at six and ate standing and went to her room. She had not discussed her review and had not been asked, and the extract that had taken her off the wrong paper was under the salt dish. Petra watched her go with the look of an officer watching another officer carry weight alone. Petra had refused twice in five weeks to hand paper round a kitchen table. She understood carrying alone.

Runa came in late from Cordwain with ink on her cuff and ate bread with her boots on. Her answer was in. Her hearing had a paper. She sat beside Beatrix and the two of them talked tram talk, seats and seasons and guards, the way people talk who do not want to talk about the thing in the middle of the table.

Toward evening Agnete stayed in the doorway longer than usual, with her cup, watching the certificate on the table. The house ate bread round it. Nothing touched it. It was received. It stayed.

He wanted to be the one who answered. Not tonight, and not the morning after. But somewhere in the next week, when the thing that had been made visible came asking to be told about, he wanted to be the man standing at the bottom of sixty-one steps.

He wanted to be able to say yes, I am the head of that house, and here is what I will tell you and here is what I will not. He had wanted it since the twenty-seventh. On the morrow it stopped being a want and became a plan.

He went to bed with the empty feeling still in him and the frame and the clerks' hands behind his eyes, and the wall at the bottom of the city dried under its paint for another week, and the side board kept its number and its schedule in the dark.

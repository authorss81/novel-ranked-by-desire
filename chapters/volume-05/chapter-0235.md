# The Bare Line

He spent the twenty-fifth with Agnete Lindqvist, and nothing happened, and it was the quietest day of the month.

He had woken wanting one hour without a figure in it. The finding was on a head's desk. Runa's answer was in a fourth office. The schedule would go up on the morrow and the certificate after it. Tessa had decided to enter her clinic into the returned lines and had not yet done it. Every paper in the city was moving toward Ferrier Street. He wanted to sit in a room where nothing could be compelled to say anything and simply be there.

Agnete was at the back door with a pail when he came into the kitchen at eight. She had slept in the building for eleven years and was on no list of any kind, and the door at the back stayed open. She was washing the step.

"Help me with the pail," she said.

He carried the pail to the yard and back. The yard was small with a wall round it and washing on a line two floors up. No office had ever entered it. No returned line had ever come back from it.

"You are kind to ask nothing," she said.

"I was told there is no provision for a person to write her own line," he said. "On a premises return. You were told on the thirteenth."

"I was," she said. "I have known it since May. That is not what I meant."

She wrung the cloth and laid it straight. Her hands were red from the water. Eleven years indoors had not softened them. Work kept them.

"You wash this step every day," he said.

"I wash it when it needs it," she said. "A step gets dirty from feet. Feet are honest. Paper is not honest. I like the step."

He carried the second pail out to the yard and emptied it at the drain. The washing on the line two floors up moved in the wind. The yard wall kept the wind partly off. It was a small place with nothing in it but a drain and a line and a door that stayed open, and it had never been entered in anything, and no line had ever come back from it.

He stood there a minute with the empty pail, feeling the quiet the way other men felt sunlight.

When he came back she was wringing the cloth again, and he took an end without being asked, and they wrung it together. The water ran into the pail. Her end held steady against his. That was the whole of the morning, and it was enough to stand beside what the morrow would bring.

"Eleven years indoors," she said. "People think the cost is the walls. The cost is not the walls. The cost is that a room where I stand is a room that cannot take testimony out of me, and I have kept that room for eleven years by never standing in a room where anyone wanted me to speak. The moment I stand in a wanting room the eleven years end."

He listened. He had learned from Tessa to let a clinician finish, and from Petra to let an officer finish, and Agnete finished in her own time.

"Your head's answer says six," she said. "An index says seven. A district return of the third has six names and a blank. I am the blank. Three offices hold me without holding my name. That is a new shape. In eleven years no office has held even the shape."

He felt grief then, plain and heavy. Not shame. Grief for a woman whose whole gift was that no instrument could hold her, and who had just been put into an instrument by proxy, in his handwriting, on a page she had never been shown.

He wanted to give her back the outside. There was no outside to give. The door stayed open and the street went past and every step on it was counted somewhere.

"What do you want from this house," he said.

"Nothing you have not given," she said. "A door that stays open. Food. No questions. On the thirteenth I asked for one impossible thing.

"That if a name ever goes on that line it is not written by the Office and not by any of you. You told me there was no provision. I am holding you to the telling, not to the thing."

They washed the step in silence, and the brush went on the stone and came off again, and the water went down the gutter the way it did every day of his life before any of this began.

"You are going to ask me what I am frightened of," Agnete said, at the second bucket, "and you are going to ask it in that voice, so I will answer it before you do. I am not frightened of being found. Being found is what I have wanted for eleven years and I have been too proud and too careful to arrange it, which is a long way of saying I am a coward about the only thing I actually want."

She wrung the brush out and set it against the wall.

"That is the whole of what I have today. Go and do your landing. Do not carry anything for me."

At ten Tessa came down with a crate and he helped her carry it to the hatch without talking, because she had asked him on the sixteenth to help on a day and not to talk about the fourth page or the certificate or the correction or the board, and he kept to it. At eleven he swept the landing round the nail without touching the rectangle.

At noon Agnete made soup and they ate it in the back room with the door open to the yard, and the rest of the house stayed out of it. The soup was plain and hot. He had not eaten a hot meal sitting down across a table from somebody since April, and neither had she, and they did not say so.

"You have a brother," she said after a while.

"I do," he said.

"You have not spoken about him in six months," she said. "Tessa told me."

He told her a little. That his brother had said a number was wrong in public and by morning was gone. That he wanted nine because nine opened a file. That the want had cost him a plate and a true answer and now a roll. He did not tell her what Sun had told him the sentence was that would unlock the eighth year. Some things stayed in their rooms.

She listened the way she did everything, without compelling.

"Thank you," she said when he stopped. "That is the first history anyone has given me in this house without being asked for it in return."

In the afternoon the district bag came with nothing for Ferrier Street. Nadia wrote down that there was nothing, with the hour on it, because four mornings of nothing had become five and a record of nothing was still a record.

At four Runa's counsel came back to him. Tell Tessa to wait until after the twenty-sixth before entering the clinic. He found Tessa at the hatch counting sheets.

"Runa says wait," he said. "Until after the schedule goes up. So your line does not sit with my six in the same office on the same days."

Tessa counted to the end of her pile before answering.

"Tell Runa I heard her," she said. "I decided on the sixteenth and I have not done it. I will not do it before the twenty-sixth. After that I do it, because a hole in a wall is only safe while it is not looked at, and they are looking."

He nodded and carried an empty crate back up for her. That was the whole of the afternoon, and he was glad of the weight of the crate and the ache in his arms, because his want for one hour without a figure had been answered by work and not by thought.

On the way back they passed the stair window where the light came in and stopped above nothing now. The seventh row had lived in two inches of dark under the light since the twenty-ninth of last month. Now the row was in a sewn pocket and the light stopped above a nail. He looked at it and looked away, because Sun had built the agreement of wall and file for eight days and then cut it, and the house was lighter for the cutting and heavier too.

Sun was mending a shirt at the table when they came in. She showed him the stitching without being asked.

"My mother taught me eleven for a pocket," she said. "I put twenty-two. A pocket that holds a row holds the house."

"It holds," he said.

Beatrix came in from the yard tram with dust on her boots and sat down with her notebook shut. She had ridden the line all day with her good ear to the window, listening for what a panel might hear. At noon the guard had checked her season without being asked, the way he did every day, and nodded her to her seat. Third seat. The one she could hear from. Small kindnesses like that held a life together.

She ate bread with both hands and did not write, because five mornings of nothing were enough record for one day. Then she took the bread knife and washed it, because Runa had cooked nothing and Sun had sewn and someone had to wash, and ordinary work was the only answer anyone in the house had left to extraordinary paper.

In the evening Agnete stood in the doorway of the kitchen with her cup and watched the house eat bread. She had come further out of the back room than she had any day that month, by about the width of one hand, and she was counting the people at the table and not the papers on it.

Ines came in at six and ate standing and went to her room. Petra mended her bundle strap. Marguerite read the thirteenth docket without touching the empty foot.

He went to bed at ten with the grief still in him and the quiet of the back room under it, and the bare line on the district return of the third stayed blank in the dark in three offices, and nothing was written on it that night.

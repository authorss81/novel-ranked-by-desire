# Off The Strength

Runa Sato went to the Field division in Cordwain at nine in the morning on the twenty-sixth because a division opens at eight and closes its counter to anything but returns at half past three, and she wanted a woman at the counter who had been there all night.

The building had a wet floor and a smell of oil and eleven people in it who were not there about her.

Her service file was on the counter in about four minutes, in a grey folder, with a string on it, and it had been opened and closed a great many times because the string had frayed and somebody had retied it twice.

"Read it at the counter or take it out," said the clerk.

"At the counter," said Runa Sato, because a thing you carry out of a building does not come back.

The correction was on the second leaf, exactly as she had written it, in her own hand, initialled by a clerk on the seventh of this month at ten past one in the afternoon, and the paragraph said what it had always said.

Field division, Cordwain. Entered as refusing a count on the twelfth at ten past two. Struck from the strength on the thirteenth by a duty clerk over a reconciliation the building had owed since the middle of February. On the strength of a standing household at the above premises since the twenty-seventh of April. Not on a roster. Available to a division under a document she has not yet signed.

"That is right," she said.

"It has been produced," said the clerk.

"To whom."

"To an intake, on the twenty-second. One sheet, one item, called for on the twenty-first, produced on the twenty-second, and returned on the twenty-third."

Runa Sato read the second leaf twice.

"Produced and returned," she said. "Then it is a copy."

"It is a copy."

"Where is the copy."

"The copy is on a district sheet," said the clerk, "in the ordinary way, and the district sheet is not mine and I cannot tell you which week it is filed in and I have never had a reason to want to."

Behind her a man in a field coat had been standing for about two minutes.

"You have got a minute," said the man.

"I have got until half past three," said Runa Sato, without turning round, and the man waited, and after about half a minute the man said, "I am here about a set of boots."

"There are boots," she said, "and there are eleven people in this corridor and one of us is reading a file, and I am going to be about nine more minutes, and you may sit down on the bench."

The man sat down on the bench.

"Ma'am," said the clerk, "I will say this once and I will say it because I am required to say it, and I am telling you before I am told to tell you.

"Everything on a page in that file has been read by somebody in this building since the seventh of this month. That is normal.

"What is not normal is that on the twenty-first somebody in an audit intake asked this division for a document about a person, and we gave it them, because we were asked for it in the form. I have never known this division to be asked for a document before by anybody who was not owed money by one of ours.

"That is the whole of what I know and it has been sitting on this counter since the twenty-second and I have not mentioned it to anybody because you were not here and I do not have your number."

"Thank you," said Runa Sato.

She read the rest of the file standing up, and it took about four minutes, and she found nothing else, because there is not much else. A form of enlistment from four years ago. A refusal of a count. A reconciliation from the middle of February with a duty clerk's name on it that she did not know.

She put the file down.

"What is on the strength of that document," she said. "Say it to me as a fact and not as a sentence you have learned."

"Nothing," said the clerk. "There is no standing on it. It says you are on the strength of a standing household. That is a standing you have got somewhere else, and this division does not hold it and has never held it and cannot check it."

"I have not got one," said Runa Sato. "I have got a man's answer at a counter. He signed a sheet on the nineteenth of this month and he is a washroom attendant and he has eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight grains a week and a margin of three."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And the household's standing is a guild broker's, because the examination is entered against the highest standing in the house and the highest standing in that house is rank two of a guild of brokers who holds four books and is about to be replaced by a woman called Rask in about four years."

"I could not tell you that," said the clerk.

"Then that is what it is," said Runa Sato, "and I have just told you a thing that is in three offices and it is now in a corridor with eleven people in it, and that is the second time in nine days that this house has put something into a stranger's ear, and the first one was a card with a question on it and I did not even have to speak."

She went back up the sixty-one steps and got in at about half past ten with wet feet, and the kitchen table had the declaration on it, and she did not sit down.

"Read the second question out loud," she said to Ines Duarte. "The instrument. Go on."

"You have written it," said Ines Duarte.

"Read it anyway."

"Instrument evidencing the standing held."

"Right." Runa Sato put her hands flat on the table. "I have been a member of a Field division for four years and I have a form of enlistment and a reconciliation and a refusal of a count, and the standing of this household stands on a broker's book, and the broker's book is going to a woman called Rask, and every one of us in this house is standing on a document that belongs to a woman who will not be here in four years.

"That is not a threat. It is a load order, and I am the one who carries things, so I am going to say it out loud and there is exactly one thing I can do with it and I am going to do it before anybody asks me."

"Say the load order."

"Bottom to top. A book. The examination entered against a book. The broker who holds it. The broker's rank two. The head of the household, whose standing is nothing, whose figure is on page one, and who answers in his own name for every member of it. Then the six of us, and the six of us are on it because of a signature and a form and a letter with a form number in a margin printed too small to read.

"Five of us can be traced. One of us cannot, and it is the one who is not on the roll, and she is the only one of the six who does not need to be."

"Sun Meiling does not need to be," said Sun Meiling, from the stair, "and you may stop saying her name in a sentence where it sounds like a favour."

"I am not saying it as a favour."

"Then say it as what."

Runa Sato turned round.

And there was nothing at all in the room for about four seconds.

"I am asking to be at the bottom of the top," she said. "Under the head, above the broker's book. I am asking that if anything in this house comes off, it comes off me, and it does not come off Sun Meiling, and it does not come off Ines Duarte, and it does not come off anybody who has spent a month building a position.

"I have spent four years getting off a division's strength and I have done it in ninety seconds at a counter, and it is the only thing I have ever done that I would write again exactly as it is.

"And the instrument that does it is a document with my name on it saying I am on the strength of a household. That document went out of this building on the twenty-second and it went to a division and it came back as a copy, and the copy is on a district sheet, and I do not know what week.

"So I am the load-bearing member and I am volunteering to be the one that is tested. And if you put me on a sheet with a figure against my name, put the instrument in it that says where my standing comes from, and let it say all of it, including the broker, including the book, including the woman who is going to take it in four years.

"Write the whole chain down. Do not write a woman's confidence. Write her book."

Ines Duarte read the foot of the sheet where the small print was, which took her about a second and a half.

"The form does not have a box for a chain," she said. "It has three lines for each member. Standing. Instrument. Office, standing or entitlement in a body corporate.

"There is nowhere on it to write a woman called Rask, and I have looked for a box and there is not one, and there is not one because this city does not expect an instrument to fail four years from now, and a rule book only prints what somebody expects.

"So the chain goes in the instrument column or it goes nowhere, and if you put the whole of it in that column then you have written a paragraph about a guild succession into a document a district clerk will read off a card on a landing."

"Then it does not go in," said Runa Sato.

"Then you stand on a document with a broker's name on it and no reason behind it, and if the guild sits in four years and the examination passes to a woman called Rask, there is a stranger on a schedule with nothing under her, and there is a great deal you will never be able to prove, because the only place it was ever written down was a kitchen table on the seventh of this month."

The kitchen was very quiet for a moment.

"Nine years," said Runa Sato. "It holds nine years."

"It holds while she holds the book," said Ines Duarte. "And she told this kitchen on the seventh, in ninety seconds, that she is being replaced at the annual sitting, and she has told you four years, and four years is the figure she has been working with since the seventeenth, and I do not think she has told you she is wrong.

"Which of those two figures do you want in the instrument column."

Runa Sato did not answer it.

She put her wet coat over the back of a chair and went down to work, because she had a shift at eleven and had been at that division since eight, and the twelve instruments on the kitchen table stayed where they were for four more hours while the six of them argued about a number.

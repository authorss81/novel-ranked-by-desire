# The Fifth Line

He read the week's sheet off the rail at the junction on his way in, the way he has read it off that rail once a month since December without once stopping.

There was one slot on the Reading wall that was bare and had been bare since the twenty-sixth of April, and a hole in that wall is not a list, and the men who paint it come at nine, and he stands at the bottom of the ladder four times a month and has never once gone at nine.

There was no repaint due on that wall for another two days, and he had known that since the twenty-first.

The card by the time clock on the ground floor was still the old one, because the card turns over on the first and the last entry on it is the seventh of next month and the seventh of next month is not the day those men come.

"The last line is out by six," he said, out loud, to the basement. "The twenty-fourth is the last one they come in this month. After that they are not due in this building until the first of next month, and the first of next month is a Thursday, and it is the day the card turns over, and that is why the last line of one has never once been read by a living man."

He took the card down off its nail and read all five lines again and put it back, which he did not need to do.

Then he went and did the second floor and the third, and the fourth, and the fifth, and the sixth, and washed his hands at a quarter past two, and told himself for about a minute and a half that there was no reason whatever to say any of that out loud in Ferrier Street, and finished at six, and came up the sixty-one steps at half past six.

The declaration was on the kitchen table face up where he had left it. All seven of them around a kitchen table in a building with a chop-house under it is a great many women for a kitchen, and the room had got used to it somewhere in the last month without any of them deciding.

Sun Meiling had the lamp off on the landing and was standing at the bottom of the stair with her coat on over her arm and nothing else in her hands, which is where her hand goes when a person knocks at the door of the ground-floor flat, and which is what she does.

"Tenth past seven," she said. "I have not opened it and I am not going to open it on my own, because the instruction on the doorframe says a paper comes to me and I answer it, and this is not a paper, this is a woman, and I do not know what I am allowed to do with a woman."

They went down together.

The woman on the step was perhaps forty-five, in a coat that had been new in a different decade, and she had a satchel and a card about the size of a call slip, and she did not come up sixty-one steps for anything so slight.

"I am from the district," she said. "Marit Kolb. I have a card and I am entered to ask for a roll kept at those premises and to enter the hour and the person I asked and the number of names upon it and the number of spaces upon it.

"That is the whole of my business and I am not going to give you my business beyond that, and I have not got a form for anything else and I am not asking for anything else."

The district had sent her, and the woman who had gone up the same sixty-one steps on the tenth of this month had been entered to ask a roll kept at premises and had read that board from the top down without touching it and had entered five names and two spaces, and a man with a folded sheet had come the day after her and entered six names and one space because he read a rule through a line as though the line were not there.

The figure had not moved since the second of this month, which was twenty days ago.

Sun Meiling answered the third question out loud, on the bottom step, in plain words, before she could be asked it, because her mother had taught her that a child who volunteers an answer does not get asked the question.

"Six names and two spaces."

And it was six names and two spaces, and it was the first time the figure had been right, and she had been carrying five and two around in her head for four days the way you carry a stone in your mouth.

The woman on the step said, "You are the seventh row."

"You are the third person to come up these steps for that board this month and you are the first one who has said it out loud before being asked, and I want you to know that I heard it, and I am not going to say one word about it, and I am not going to be civil to you about it either."

The woman on the step looked at her for about two seconds and then wrote on the card, and her hand did not change at all while she did it.

That is a thing about Kolb. She wrote six names, two spaces on the card without changing her hand, because if you change your hand when you write a number that somebody has corrected, you have told the number something about itself.

Then she said, "Thank you," and went down to the pavement, and was gone before anybody had thought of a second thing to ask her.

"That is three," said Sun Meiling.

"It is three," he said. "The tenth, the eleventh, and tonight."

"Then they will come again at the end of the week, because it goes in at the end of the week, and if there is a fourth of them then this will be a district and not an address."

They went up. The declaration was on the kitchen table with the small print at the foot of it where he had left it, and there were two other papers beside it now that had not been there in the morning, and Ines Duarte had one of them by the corner and was standing at the table the way she stands when she has read something twice and is going to read it a third time from the bottom up.

He did the arithmetic in the doorway on his way past, because he had promised himself on the first of this month that he would do every figure out loud in that house, and the promise does not have a clause in it for the days when a card comes up.

"Twenty-five of the sixty are gone," he said. "Thirty-five are left. The men are on that wall next in two days and then not again until the first of next month, and the certificate is dated the twenty-seventh of June, and there is not one of those three dates that will move because I have read a card."

"Twenty-eight gone and thirty-two left," said Sun Meiling. "That is the twenty-fifth."

"That is the twenty-fifth, and I have not said that figure out loud in this house and you have, and neither of us has asked the other where it came from, and I would like that said before the month is out, because there is a form on this table with this address on it."

"Ines," he said. "The four questions on the covering sheet. Read them out."

"Ines" did not do it. Ines Duarte read them out.

They were ordinary. What standing does each person named on the schedule of record hold. What instrument evidences it. Does the person hold any office, standing or entitlement in a body corporate, guild, exchange or office of this city. And: state the number of persons of reading age keeping an address at the premises.

The fourth was the composition return's own question, word for word, and it had been asked of this address since the twenty-second of March and had been answered six both times.

"There is a column on that board that is not on the wall," said Beatrix Halm, at the table, reading the sheet upside down. "I have stopped writing mine in. Somebody who can read a document should tell me whether an empty column in my own hand is a forgery or a preference."

"It is neither," said Ines Duarte. "A roll kept at premises is not an instrument. Nothing on that wall has force and I have said so since the tenth of this month and it has not changed. You may leave that column empty for thirty years and there is no office in this city that will send anybody for it.

"The instrument is the schedule of the fourth of this month. That is where your standing is and it has been since the fourth, and Doyle filled it in from the plate, and there is not one line in it about a roll on a landing in Ferrier Street."

"Then I will leave it empty with my name on it," said Beatrix Halm. "And when the next one of them comes up sixty-one steps and asks me what it says, I will tell her it says what is written on it. That will be the second one in a week who has come up those steps."

"Two more came," said Ines Duarte. "Both addressed to you by name, both in the same type, both off the same machine.

"One of them is a covering sheet and it says the declaration may be completed by the head and returned at the counter named on the reverse, or that the premises may be verified at source by an officer of the audit intake, and that where a premises is verified the counter is not called at again."

"And the other one," he said.

"The other one is four questions," said Ines Duarte, "and they are not difficult.

"And the third one asks whether the person keeping an address at those premises is a member of an office of this city, and I am the answer to that one, and I am going to answer it in my own hand. It will go up to the audit intake at the end of the week inside a district envelope, and there is not one person in that office who is going to tell me about it before I read it in the file."

She put the sheet down and squared it.

"And you are going to say something about that, because you are going to see what it costs, and I want it said once and then never again."

"What does it cost you?"

"That is not your sentence," she said, "and you are going to find the right one or you are going to say nothing, and either is better than that one."

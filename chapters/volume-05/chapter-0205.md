# The Sheet She Would Not Complete

She had sent him a message on the twenty-fourth that said four words and a time and explained neither.

READ IT ALL. TWENTY-FIFTH. ELEVEN.

Her rooms were two floors above a laundry on Lantern Row, eight feet wide, with a table under the window and a chair on each side of it and a shelf of ledgers she had bought herself over eleven years.

She had not lived under his roof since the first week of April and had given no reason. He had not asked, because he had promised himself on the first of this month that he would do every figure out loud in that house, and asking was not a figure.

He came up at two minutes to eleven with the declaration in a coat and a bottle of something cheap.

She had the whole of it on her own floor.

Not the household's notebook. Six women keep that under a table at Ferrier Street and keep it well. Two instruments in this house were in neither the notebook nor the house, and she had both of them laid out flat on her own table in date order, with a sheet beside each one with the date and her opinion of it written on it in a small even hand.

"Twelve," she said.

"Twelve?"

"Twelve instruments across eleven dates, and one card from a district clerk that went into a building two floors above my office at the end of the week and was read back to me the next morning by a woman in a corridor who would not give me her name."

"Sit down. You have eleven minutes to say all of that and then you are going to read, and I want to read, and it will take an hour, and then we are going to talk about one line of it and nothing else."

She read.

She read the composition return of the twenty-second of March first, because it was the oldest, and she read it the way she reads everything, which is the way a person reads a document she intends to be cited from — slowly, once, without going back.

"Six persons of reading age keeping an address at those premises. One not on the roll, permitted answer. Head, blank.

"That is the beginning of all of it, and the head's space on it was empty then and stayed empty for two months, and that is the reason everything after it happened."

"Six," he said. "There are seven women in that house."

"There are six women keeping an address at Ferrier Street," said Ines Duarte, "and a seventh woman who sleeps in the building and is on no list of any kind, and a man on the landing at the top of sixty-one steps. The answer to that question has been six since the twenty-second of March and it has never once been wrong."

She went on.

The re-issue of the first. The schedule of the fourth, four pages, in order. The correction Runa Sato signed at a counter on the seventh at ten past one in the afternoon, entered by a clerk who initialled it and dated it and could not take it back. Sun Meiling's four hundred words, which she did not read aloud but held up for about three seconds with her thumb on the paragraph, and then set down.

The invoice of the sixteenth, RANK TWO in the value column, and the second line under it.

"That is the one that frightens her. Not the first line, the second. And the second line does not apply to the woman signing a sheet about herself. It applies to whoever holds the book after her, and there is a woman called Rask with four guilds who has never met one of you, and on the day the guild sits she will be named on that document as the person answerable for six members, and she will not know one thing about any of you."

"And that is in a public book as of the twenty-fourth."

"That is in a public book as of yesterday," said Ines Duarte. "You have not read it. I have not read it. I am telling you because I am the person in that house who reads things, and if I do not hand it to you it will sit in my coat for a month and come out at the wrong time."

Then the assumption of the nineteenth, one side of a sheet, and the printed clause at the foot.

She read it out loud in the flat voice of a clerk reading to a man who has not read it, because that is how it had last been read, and because she wanted to hear what it sounded like in a room where it was not being read at him.

"THE HEAD OF A HOUSEHOLD ANSWERS IN HIS OWN NAME FOR EVERY MEMBER OF IT."

She put it down.

"And the district sheets."

"There are two and they contradict each other and one of them is wrong."

"The tenth says five names and two spaces. The eleventh says six names and one space, and the eleventh is the wrong one of the two, and I want that written down before somebody in this city settles it the other way round.

"A man came up those steps the day after her with a sheet folded in four, and he read that board the way a man reads a page, and the rule I put through the sixth row on the second of this month was in his way and he counted straight through it, and the woman who went before him read it from the top down without touching it and counted round it. Neither of them did anything wrong.

"That is not a roll changing between two visits. That is a roll that changed on the second of this month, eight days before the first of them came, and after that this board can honestly be counted three ways by three people who are all doing their job properly on the day."

"And the card."

"The card says six names and two spaces, and it says it in this house's hand, and I want that written down, because the next one of them is going to be a different figure again, and one day one of those numbers is going to be the number on a certificate, and after the twenty-seventh of June there is not one instrument in this city that can change any of them."

She stopped.

"And I know what is underneath all of it," she said. "I can read any record in this city and I know when it has been touched, and I am going to tell you the one thing I found, and then I am going to read my own line, and I am not going to stop."

She did not stop.

"Your division raised these premises on the twenty-first. There is a form number on that declaration, and the run of form numbers on the machine is printed in the margin in a typeface too small for anybody to look at, and this one is the ninety-first of a run that began on the fourth.

"So it was raised on the twenty-first at an office two floors below the room where I eat my lunch, by people who index every schedule a member of our division appears on, and one of the people it appears on is me, and there is a rule saying an officer is not told about her own division's index sheets, and I had never once thought about that rule until the card came back."

"Who is the auditor of record on it," he said.

"I am," said Ines Duarte. "Which is the only reason I have not been told anything, and which is the ugliest thing about my working life, and I have known it for two years."

She turned the sheet round and put a finger on page two, sixth line up from the foot.

"Now my line."

Rank two. Auditorate.

And under the column headed INSTRUMENT EVIDENCING THE ABOVE, a space with nothing in it.

"There," she said. "Ten days old. It is a member form with my name on the corner lying face down in a drawer at Ferrier Street, because I turned it over on the fifteenth and did not turn it back.

"Four women in that house have seen the corner of it and one of them has not mentioned it once, and she is you, and you have not mentioned it for about a month, and I want you to know that I know you are not going to."

"Ines—"

"I am not going to tell you why." She said it without heat. "I said in a corridor in April that you were not to ask me, and I have not discussed it since, and I am not discussing it on a night like this one either.

"It is not about this house. It has never once been about this house. And you are going to let that stand for a long time, and I am asking you for it now so that we do not have to have it twice."

"All right."

"Thank you."

She got up and put her coat over the rail, which is a thing she does before she reads and before she cooks and before she does anything else, and he had seen it four times in four months and had never asked.

"What do you want," he said. "Say it plainly. I have been putting figures on a kitchen table for a month, and this is the first question anybody has asked me in that kitchen that I can actually answer."

She stood at the table with the twelve instruments on it in date order and did not look at one of them.

"I want you to enter me on that declaration on your word," she said. "Not on mine. Not on an instrument. Your answer, in your hand, at the foot of a sheet, with a figure beside it that is you.

"Do you know what that is. It is the weakest standing available to a person in this city. It is voidable and it goes the day your plate does. Any standing I put there myself is mine and it is mine whatever happens to you, and no district can take it by removing a man from a post.

"And if you enter me on your word, then I am a member of this household whose standing is a man who cleans six floors, and the day that standing is questioned the certification goes with it, and the certification is worth every permission any of us holds."

"Yes," she said. "That is the arithmetic and I have done it on the tram and I have done it again four times since you knocked.

"I am asking you for the worst standing in this house because it is the only one I have ever wanted. I want it because for four years the one thing I have had that cannot be taken off me is that I am in nobody's book, and I have been so proud of that for so long that it did not occur to me until the twenty-third that being outside a book is not the same thing as being safe in a room.

"You are the first person who has ever offered to carry a piece of paper with my name on it. I would like to be carried. I have never wanted anything else in my life, and I am asking you for it at about midnight, and I am not going to be embarrassed about it."

He stood up.

"Yes," he said.

"Do not do it tonight."

"No."

"Because if you do it tonight it is a kindness, and I want a decision, and I want it to cost you something in daylight in front of people. You are good at daylight and you are bad at midnight, and so am I."

She said, "Come here," and he came across a room eight feet wide, and she put her hand flat on his chest over the plate he has carried in a coat pocket for four years without once pressing it into a socket, and she said, "I want to say one true thing before anything else happens tonight, so listen."

"I am listening."

"You are not going to keep me off that sheet." She said it without any weight at all. "You have been trying to keep me off every document you have signed since the nineteenth of May and you have done it very well, and I have watched you do it and I have let you, and it stops tonight.

"If I am on that declaration I am on it with my name and my rank and the word that says what I am, in my own hand, and you are going to certify a household that contains an officer of the office the certificate will be audited by. And the office is going to have to decide what to do about it in a room with a door on it, at their table, on their afternoon, with my name in front of them.

"Let them find out about me the way everybody in this city finds out about everybody else."

He put his hand over hers.

"You are asking me to walk into a building and be a fact about somebody," he said.

"Yes," said Ines Duarte, "and you are going to do it badly, and it is going to cost you the only thing you have ever wanted, which is to not be findable. And I am going to be worth it. And you are going to have to keep me worth it for about nine years, and there is not one grain of margin in this house for that either."

It took them about twenty minutes to reach the point where neither of them was reading anything, and about four of those were her hands on the collar of his coat, and he stood still for the first minute of them the way he stands still for everything she does, which she told him once is not patience.

"You are going to say something about the margin of three," she said.

"I was going to say something about the margin of three."

"Do not." She undid the second button. "There is not a form in this city that governs what I do with my hands and you have read enough forms this month."

He got the coat off her shoulders, and she got his shirt open over the ribs on the left, four of them, and put two fingers flat under the fourth without pressing, because she knows exactly where it is and has decided not to, and she gave him the reason for that in April in a washroom at the Fourth Branch and he has not asked since.

She got him out of the rest of it standing in the window, where there was nothing on the other side but a laundry's back wall and a strip of sky, and she did not hurry and did not ask him to help and he did not offer.

"Four years," she said. "You have not asked me one thing in four years that you did not have a reason to ask."

"That is not true."

"It is nearly true and I am counting it as true," said Ines Duarte. "And I have been counting it since the sixth of this month, when you stood in a kitchen and priced an entire household in ninety seconds and then waited about four minutes to see whether anybody would move. Six women watched you do it and not one of us said a word, and I have been taking it personally ever since."

She came back to the table and put a knee between his.

He got a hand into her hair and she let him have it for about four seconds and then took his wrist and put his hand where she wanted it, flat on her back, low, and held it there, and said, "There. Do not be careful with me. I am not the clinic."

And it was not careful and it was not fast and it went on a great deal longer than either of them had expected, in a room eight feet wide above a laundry, on the one night in the month when there was no repainting and no counter and no clerk and no roll on any wall in the world, and for a while the only sound in it was the window and the building and the two of them.

Near the end she got her mouth against his ear and told him what she wanted, all of it, in the order she wanted it, and she told him the order by counting on her own ribs under his hand the way she counts everything, and when he got the third of them wrong she stopped him and made him do that one again.

He did it again, and it took about nine minutes, and neither of them said anything else in it.

Afterwards she lay with her cheek on his chest and counted out loud, and he asked her what she was counting and she said, "Seconds. Do not look at me like that. I do it at the office as well."

"How many."

"Eleven," said Ines Duarte, "and it was nine last time, and it will be nine again by the end of the month, and I am going to be extremely annoyed about it."

"You can stop counting."

"I cannot stop counting," she said. "It is the only thing I do. It is the whole of what I am for, and I am telling you that at half past two in the morning because I asked you for something enormous an hour ago and I am entitled also to be the woman who counts seconds."

They lay there a while. Then she got up, and took her own member form out of her bag where it had been folded in four for ten days and had been through a reading and a corridor and a room, and held it up for about four seconds with her name on the corner and every other square of it blank.

"I am not filling this in," she said. "I have thought about it for ten days and I am not, and this is not me deciding in front of you, because I decided it at a table on the fifteenth and I have not changed my mind once since.

"And I am not handing you a sheet with my name on the corner and nothing else in it, because you would keep it, and it would sit in a drawer in Ferrier Street for four years, and one day somebody would open the drawer and find a piece of paper with Ines Duarte on it, and the only true sentence available about it would be that a woman could not fill in a piece of paper."

She tore it once, across, and gave him the two halves.

"Now that is settled, one way or the other, and I did it in my own rooms at half past two in the morning on my own, and you did not ask me to and you were not in the room for the deciding, and I want that written down too, because somebody is going to read about this in nine years and they are going to get it wrong."

Then she sat at her own table with her own pen and her own ledger and copied the whole of it out. Twelve instruments, every date, every figure, in her own hand, from the top of the first page to the foot of the printed clause on the assumption. It took her about an hour and ten minutes and she made one mistake, which was a comma, and she rubbed it out and wrote it again and initialled the correction because she is a woman who initials corrections.

She put the copy into a book that was not the household's notebook and that had nothing on its cover.

Opposite page two, sixth line up from the foot, in the column where the instrument goes, she wrote one word.

WITHDRAWN.

"That means the instrument," she said, before he could ask. "Not the member."

She put the original back in an envelope for Ferrier Street, and the envelope on top of a stack by the door with her key on it, and the copy on the shelf with the other eleven, and turned the lamp out.

"The first copy of that record ever to exist outside that house is on a shelf in a flat on Lantern Row," she said, "and it is mine, and I am the only person alive who can read either of them properly.

"And you are going to sign a sheet on the thirtieth that says six, and I am going to be one of the six, and that is the whole of what I wanted on the twenty-fourth and I got it, and it has cost both of us exactly what I said it would."

He was awake a while after that, on his back, in a room eight feet wide, listening to a building settle.

"Six," he said.

"Six or five," said Ines Duarte. "That is the only question left in this house, and you are going to have to answer it in daylight in front of all of them, and you are going to answer it wrong, because the right answer is whichever one keeps the most people safe, and there is not one person in that kitchen who is going to tell you which one that is.

"They will tell you what it costs them. It is not the same thing. It has never once been the same thing."

# A Paper With His Name On It

The card by the time clock on the ground floor of the Fourth Branch still had the old month on it, because the card turns over on the first and it had been the twenty-first for about three hours.

He read it standing up with the bucket in his hand. He read the first four lines, which he had read since the first of the month, and then he read the fifth line again, which was the one he had been carrying around since the first.

MAY. THIRD. TENTH. SEVENTEENTH. TWENTY-FOURTH. AND THE SEVENTH OF NEXT MONTH.

Four of those five entries are days in this month. The fourth one is the twenty-fourth, and the four men who wrote the card do not put a day on it that does not exist, and this month has thirty.

Which means the day after the twenty-fourth entry is not in this month at all. The next day they are due in this building is the first of next month.

The card says the seventh.

He had had twenty days with that and had not said it out loud to a single person, and the reason was that he could not think of anyone in this city to whom he could say it without sounding like a man who has found a fault in a wall.

Then he went up at seven, and the second floor and the third and the fourth and the fifth and the sixth took him until a quarter past eleven, and then he washed his hands.

He stood at the bottom of the sixty-one steps with his bag on his shoulder and did the arithmetic out loud, because he had promised himself on the first of this month that he would do every figure out loud in that house from now on, and he was going to do it until the day he did not.

"Twenty-four of the sixty are gone," he said. "Thirty-six are left. The return was filed on the twenty-seventh of April and the certificate is dated the twenty-seventh of June, and there is nothing whatever in between that anybody can do about, and it is not due today and it will not be due tomorrow and there is no version of this month in which I touch it.

"And the twenty-fourth is the last day the men come in this month. Tomorrow week they come, and they will bring the next card, because the card turns over on the first and a man who paints on the twenty-fourth knows on the twenty-fourth what the first is."

Sun Meiling was on the landing with the lamp off and had been there since about nine, because the light stops about two inches above the seventh row at that hour and she had been looking at it.

"Say the other one," she said.

"The last line of that card is six days out," he said. "The first of next month is a Thursday and the card says the seventh of next month, and it is not a Thursday, and the seven days off the twenty-fourth is the thirty-first, which is not a day, so it is the first.

"The first of next month is a Thursday because the twenty-fourth is a Thursday and there are six days between the twenty-fourth and the thirtieth and one more day after that."

The landing was quiet for a moment.

"June," she said.

"June. And the first is a Thursday, so the others are the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second and the twenty-ninth. Five Thursdays and the sixth of July after that, and I have not got a card for July and I do not need one until the twenty-second of June."

"You have just worked out next month off a piece of paper in the basement," she said, "by counting."

"That is what the piece of paper in the basement is for. It has been for nineteen years. Four men come here with a ladder and paint a wall and they leave a card, and the card is the only thing in this building that says what day it is, and everything else in Ostara is worked out by somebody who did that first and then guessed."

"Then the card is wrong," said Sun Meiling.

"The card is right four times out of five and wrong on the line that never gets read," he said. "The line that tells them when to come back. They have never had to read it, because the card turns over before they do. Write that down or do not, it is not going to be the thing that ends anybody."

The post had come up over the chop-house before he got to the door and there were three things in it and two of them were for the women.

The third had his name on it.

Not the address. His name, in a typed hand he had never seen, at the top left, and beneath it a line of smaller type with an office in it and a floor, and beneath that a second address that was not Ferrier Street and was not the Fourth Branch.

He stood in the doorway with it in his hand and did not open it for about a minute and a half.

Not one person in four years had written to him. Four years of being the least findable man in this city, and it had not cost him anything, and it had cost five women in his house something every month for four months, and on the nineteenth of this month he had put his name at the top of a sheet in a clerk's office and a clerk had read a printed sentence about him out loud and asked him four questions.

A paper could now reach him by writing to him first. Doyle had told him that at the counter and had told him it was the last time being on a pavement would be true of him.

He opened it.

One side of a sheet, cream weight, a little heavier than office stock. A form number at the top right. In the Office's own type, across the head of it:

DECLARATION OF MEMBERSHIP IN A STANDING HOUSEHOLD.

Under that, the premises, filled in, in the same type, not written: THE PREMISES AT FERRIER STREET. And under that, THE HEAD OF THE ABOVE, and a space with a name already in it, and it was his.

Below the heading, printed, and he read it twice because it took him a while to see what it was asking:

WHERE THE CERTIFICATE OF THE HOUSEHOLD ABOVE IS ISSUED, EVERY PERSON NAMED IN THE SCHEDULE OF RECORD SHALL STATE, IN THAT PERSON'S OWN HAND — the standing held; the instrument evidencing it; and whether the person holds any office, standing or entitlement in a body corporate, guild, exchange or office of this city.

Then, lower down, on the same side, under a rule:

TO BE COMPLETED BY THE HEAD. The head shall certify the members of the household, and the number of persons of reading age keeping an address at the premises, and shall sign.

And at the foot of the page, in the same type, in a size about half of the rest of it, so that a man reading quickly would go past it, which is what it was set that size for:

WHERE THE HEAD'S CERTIFICATE DOES NOT AGREE WITH THE SCHEDULE OF RECORD, THE HEAD SHALL STATE THE FIGURE HE CERTIFIES AND THE REASON, AND THE SCHEDULE OF RECORD STANDS.

On the reverse, which he turned over in the doorway and read standing up, four lines: the counter, the floor, the hours, and the date.

RETURNABLE AT THE COUNTER NAMED ABOVE ON OR BEFORE THE LAST WORKING DAY OF THIS MONTH.

He stood in the doorway of a chop-house under six flats for about a minute.

There is no thirty-first in this month. The last working day of this month is the thirtieth, and the thirtieth is the day the counter named on the reverse shuts its intake at four in the afternoon, and it does not take anything at four o'clock on the first of next month, because the first of a month is when the first of a month is dealt with and everything else waits.

So he had nine days and a shift he would have to finish early and a form with his name already typed into the top of it.

He went up the sixty-one steps with it in his hand and put it on the kitchen table face up, and the six women who keep an address there came and looked at it, and the light on the landing was on because it was the middle of the day and the seventh row had nothing above it at all.

"Read it out loud," said Ines Duarte. "All of it, including the small print at the foot, and I will tell you when you have got to the part that matters."

He read it out loud. It took him about a minute and a half and he read the small print at the foot.

"That is the whole of it," said Ines Duarte.

"That is the whole of it," he said, "and it has got my name in it in somebody else's hand, and it came to Ferrier Street addressed to me, and a man in an office decided between the nineteenth of this month and this morning that a piece of paper should go into a post with my name at the top of it."

He put two fingers on the sheet above the three questions and not on any of them.

"It is not a demand for anything," he said. "It does not ask me for a grain and it does not ask me for a rank and it does not ask me for a plate or a form of identification or a signature in front of a clerk.

"It asks me for the six names on the schedule of record and the instrument that stands behind each of them, and it asks me to write it down, and it asks me to do it in nine days, and it is dated this month and it does not have a number on it that means anything to me."

He looked round the room at the six of them who keep an address there.

"And the part at the foot of it is the part that matters, and Ines told me where it was before I had read it out, which means she had read the whole sheet in about four seconds standing behind me, and that is her gift and it is not a thing you can be taught."

The room went quiet for a moment.

"What does it mean," said Sun Meiling. "In a sentence. I am the woman who reads the post in this house and I am not the woman who reads forms and I would like it in a sentence."

"It means," said Ines Duarte, "that on the twenty-seventh of June a certificate will issue on a schedule of the fourth of this month with six members named on page two of it, and that from the moment it issues, the office that audits certificates will hold a second copy of the membership of this household, and it will hold it whether anybody files anything or not.

"And it means that every one of us will be in that copy under her own standing and the instrument that evidences it, and that four of the six of you can say what your instrument is in about four seconds, and that two of you have never once been asked for it."

She did not look at anybody while she said that, and she had put it in the shape of a count, and the count was four and two, and everybody in the room understood that she had put it in that shape on purpose.

"Twenty-four of the sixty are gone," he said, "and thirty-six are left, and this form is due on the thirtieth, and there is nothing in this house that anybody else in this city has to do about."

He picked it up and held it out.

"So I would like it to stay on this table until every one of you has read it, and I would like it to be read out loud by the woman who can read the record correctly, because it is her office's paper and it is my signature at the bottom and I have never been any good at a form in my life."

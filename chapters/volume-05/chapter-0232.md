# What The Board Carries

He woke on the twenty-second before the stair light came on and heard the ladders in the street below the Reading Square before he saw them.

He had gone down to the square at half past eight because Beatrix Halm had asked him to walk with her to the tram stop at the yard end, and the painters were already at the foot of the ranks wall with two ladders and three pots and a sheet. It was Thursday and the Thursday crew painted the ranks wall at nine, before the figures went up at ten, and the board at the side of the wall was not theirs.

He felt tired in a way that sat behind his eyes. He had slept badly with the shame and the fear in the room, and he wanted this morning to see the wall plainly once before the week closed over it, because on the twenty-sixth a schedule would go up on a board and on the twenty-seventh a certificate would be dated, and after that nothing on a wall would be plain again.

Beatrix walked beside him with her notebook in her bag and her good ear to the street. She could hear a tram three streets off and she could not hear out of the other ear since the winter, and the panel on the first of next month would ask her what a signal operator who could not hear in one ear was doing in a cab.

"You do not have to come," he said.

"I am going to the tram anyway," she said. "And I want to see what they paint over."

The man at the foot of the ladder was the one who had talked in the tea hut about a hole at the low end of the standing-household board about a yard long, where a name had come off with a correction and not with paint. He nodded to them without stopping his stirring.

"Morning," he said. "You are early for the figures."

"We are early for the ladders," Beatrix said.

The crew worked fast. Two men up, one below, the paint cut in at the top of the ranks wall and brought down in long strokes. The wall carried the ranks in tile and the figures under them, and the Thursday pass took the week off it and laid the ground for the next.

The board at the side, eleven feet of painted wood with the number 34-6-118 and a name typed small at the head line, stood untouched under its own shade.
Another crew had put it up on the first, in a shade of their own choosing, ahead of the figures, and on it stood the schedule of the fourth of last month. It came down for repainting on the first of next month. Today was not its day.

He watched the paint go over the tile and thought about darkness. Wren Kallo had found his name on that wall with her hand in the dark on the eighteenth in about two seconds, and had told him that everything kept off the wall for five years had been kept off by darkness, and that darkness was a thing a city switched off at eleven. In daylight the wall was exact. There was no darkness on it at nine in the morning.

Beatrix put her hand on his arm.

"There," she said quietly.

Low on the ranks wall, near the end where the crew had started, there was a patch where the paint went on thinner, over tile that had been touched often. It was not the pale rectangle on his landing. That rectangle was nine inches by fourteen on a stair wall at Ferrier Street, behind a nail that held nothing since the nineteenth. This was a working wall, wetted and painted every Thursday. The pass took whatever the week had left on it.

The rule was simple enough. What he could not get past was that a woman beside him was watching paint cover tile, because she wanted to know whether a house could be painted off a wall the way a week could.

"It does not take names," he said to her, low. "The man said so. A name comes off the side board with a correction. Paint takes the week."

"I know," she said. "I wanted to see it do it."

They stood until the first ladder moved. At nine the crew chief called the time and the second man came down for more paint, and the square began to fill with clerks going to the Reading door two hundred yards off. The lamps on brackets that lit the wall until eleven at night were dark on their iron.

On the way to the tram stop Beatrix told him about the notebook she had kept since the seventh. One line on the seventh. Four mornings of nothing after it. A weight at the top of Ferrier Street that had gone into the line at twenty to four on the seventh and had not got lighter, and about forty operators on that line who could find it.

"I have not written the weight down," she said. "I have written that I have not written it. That is the line. My panel will read it and ask me what I mean, and I will say I mean a plate lodged in a chained book is a place, and a place can be found, and I am the only one in this house who knows how finding sounds."

"You want to be the only one," he said.

"I want to be useful before the first," she said. "That is my want. It is not large. I have had eleven years of being useful to a line and now the line is going to ask me whether I am fit to ride it."

He left her at the tram with her season in her hand and went to the Fourth Branch and worked the washrooms until three. At half past three the Field division counter at Cordwain closed to returns, and he thought about Runa behind it, counting out loud so a count did not live in one head.

At four he mopped the ground floor corridor and Lode came past with a ladder and said the valve on the fourth floor was still weeping. They stood together a minute the way men stand who have worked a building for years.

"They will get to it when the month turns," Lode said.

"They have said that since March," he said.

"They have," Lode said. "A weeping valve is not a finding. It is a sound. You hear it when the corridor is quiet."

Lode shifted the ladder on his shoulder.

"Your landing is quiet too," Lode said. "I hear it from my sister. She says the wall is bare and the house eats bread standing up. She says it kindly."

"Tell her kindly back," he said, and Lode nodded and went on with the ladder.

He finished the corridor and changed the paper and wiped the tile where the cleaners signed. On the second floor a man he had never spoken to asked him whether the third-floor landing light was meant to be on all night, and he said it was on the same clock as the stair lamps and had been since he came, and the man said right, and went down. He held the ordinary exchange the rest of the hour because there had been so few ordinary exchanges in June.

He came home at six and found Wren Kallo on the step below the sixty-one steps, sitting with a folder on her knees, not coming up.

She was the eighth partner and was on no roll at any time, and she had been off the page for a year before the eighteenth, and now she was back with a finding that went to the head of her division on the morrow.

"You are not coming up," he said.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tomorrow my paper goes in. Tonight I am sitting on a step that is not in any file."

She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. Four years of reading the shape of edits in everything she touched had taught her to see removal where other people saw paper, and his file had four edits and every one a removal and not one addition in five years.

"Are you frightened," he said.

"Yes," she said. "I have been frightened since the sixteenth and I have not sent it and I have not taken it off. Tomorrow I send it because there is no version of my work in which I do not."

He sat down on the step below her because she had not asked him to stand.

"Four hours and a half," she said. "I am worse at my work than I was. I would do it again."

"Will they ask you who you have told," he said.

"They will not ask me," she said. "I wrote it. The last person anybody goes to is the person who wrote it. That is the protection and it is the sentence."

She stood and picked up the folder.

"Tomorrow," she said. "After that it is a division's file."

She went down the street toward the Reading without turning round. He went up the sixty-one steps with wet hands and found the kitchen quiet and the nail still bare and the rectangle still pale, and Ines at the table reading a sheet with a form number on it that had come in the district bag that morning.

She looked up when he came in and put the sheet face down before anyone could discuss it, because the standing instruction in that house was that whoever opened a thing with a form number told her first, and Nadia had opened it on the landing.

"It is for tomorrow," Ines said. "Not tonight."

He nodded and washed his hands and ate bread standing up. Petra came in with the bundle string in her pocket and Marguerite with ink on her fingers from the thirteenth docket, and Sun with eleven stitches showing white on her coat pocket, and they ate without discussing anything, because the sheet lay face down under Ines's hand and the instruction held.

After supper Beatrix showed him the back of her notebook page, where she had written the morning down. Ladders. Two up, one below. Paint cut at the top and brought down. Side board untouched. Schedule of the fourth of last month still standing. Her handwriting was small and level.

"That is the whole of it," she said. "If my panel asks where I was, I was with the head looking at paint. That is a true sentence and it has nothing in it they can use."

He sat at the table until nine with the house round him, writing nothing, because there was nothing left that anyone in it controlled enough to write down, and at nine the house settled, and the stair light went off on its clock, and the wall at the bottom of the city dried under its new paint for another week.

# The Weight At The Top

Beatrix Halm had been aimed since the seventh of June and the notebook had five mornings in it, and four of them said the same word.

07:58. Off the north line. Satchel. Slow.

08:06. Nothing.

08:04. Nothing.

08:11. Nothing.

07:59. Nothing.

She had ruled the columns herself in March and she wrote the hour first because the hour was the part she could be wrong about least, and she had not missed a shift in four years, and the Division had stopped sending her anywhere she could be replaced from.

"That is one," said Lin Hao. "That is one in five days."

"One on the seventh and nothing on the four mornings since," said Beatrix Halm. "Nine a day from the first to the fifth, nine on the sixth, one on the seventh, and then the street went quiet."

"Quiet how."

"Not quiet. Empty." She turned the notebook round and put a finger on the four lines. "On the ninth I sat between Cordwain and the junction by the Vestry for the whole of the morning rush and I felt about forty thousand people go past me and not one of them was walking to a counter.

I have been wrong about that twice in four years and both times I was tired, and I was not tired, because I have been in bed since the sixth and I have been doing nothing at all except being rested."

Runa Sato came in with her coat still on and stood at the end of the table and did not sit down, because Runa Sato sat down when she had decided to stay.

"Somebody stopped sending them," she said.

"Somebody stopped sending them," said Beatrix Halm, "or somebody told them not to come. Those are different and I cannot tell you which one it is from a tram, and I have been not telling you which one it is for four days and I have been not telling you because I did not have anything else to give you."

She shut the notebook.

"There is something else."

## Twenty To Four

They went up the street at half past eight in the evening because she would not do it at a table.

It was a narrow street with a chop-house at the bottom and six flats over it and sixty-one steps, and the lamps were on at the bottom and not at the top, and she stopped at the corner where the line comes round from the north and put her hand flat against a railing that was not hers.

"Here," she said. "This is where I felt it. Not on the seventh. On the seventh I felt the man with the satchel at four minutes to eight in the morning and I wrote it down and it was the best morning I have had in four years."

"And on the seventh at some other time."

"On the seventh at about twenty to four in the afternoon." She took her hand off the railing. "Something went into that street at about twenty to four in the afternoon and it has not come out, and it is not louder, and it is not a person.

I want to be very careful about how I say the next part, because I have been careful about it for four days and I have not said it out loud and I am saying it out loud on a corner at half past eight in the evening because you are the only person in this house who would know what I am talking about if I said it."

"Say it."

"It got heavier."

Nobody said anything.

"Not heavier as in louder," said Beatrix Halm. "Heavier as in more. There is a difference and people do not believe there is a difference and there is. A crowded street is loud.

A crowded street with one person carrying more than the rest of it is heavy, and it sits in the line like a knot, and I felt it go in at Cordwain and I felt where it stopped, and it stopped at the top of this street, and then I walked up this street to find out what was at the top of it."

She turned and looked up at the first landing.

"Nothing," she said. "That is what is at the top of it. Sixty-one steps and six flats and a woman who keeps a notebook."

## What He Felt

He had been thinking about it for four days and had not said so, because saying it was the same as making it true and there was no version of making it true in which he came out of it better than he went in.

He had felt it too. Not in a line. In a stairwell, at about twenty to four on the seventh, on the way down sixty-one steps of the Exchange with a sheet of paper in his coat that was not his and could not be given back.

He had put his hand on the rail at the Exchange that afternoon and it had been a rail. He had put his hand on it at about half past five the same evening and it had been a rail.

And then on the eighth at about ten past seven in the morning, with the mop in his hand on the second floor of the Fourth Branch, he had put his hand on it again, and there was a thing on the other side of it, like a door in a wall that had not been there on the Thursday.

He had stood in that stairwell for about nine seconds and had felt sick and had not said anything, and had gone back to the sinks.

"Take me upstairs," he said.

"There is nothing upstairs."

"Take me up to the roll."

## The Roll

She did not understand and he did not explain, because the explanation was four days old and had a word in it he was not ready to say in a street.

Up sixty-one steps in the dark, past the landing where the seventh row was, past the door at the back that stood open the way it stands open in the daytime, to the wall at the top of the stair where the roll was on a nail under a coat.

He put his hand flat on the wall beside it.

"Do you feel that."

She stood very still for about four seconds, which was longer than she had stood still in his presence in four years.

"Yes," said Beatrix Halm. "That is it. That is exactly what it is and I have been feeling it from a tram four hundred yards away and you have just made me feel it with my hand on a wall."

"A standing lodged against an account is a weight," he said. "It goes into a chained book and it is answerable for a thing that is not in this house and it cannot be drawn on and it cannot be taken back, and it sits there until somebody who holds the book calls it."

"And the city can feel it."

"The line can feel it. You are the line."

He took his hand off the wall.

"That is the part I have not been able to say since the eighth," he said, "and I would like to say the rest of it out loud before I have to, because if I do not say it tonight somebody will work it out about eleven months from now and it will be in a file with my name at the top of it.

"There are about forty signal operators on the Ferrier line. You have told me that and I have not asked you how you know it and I am not going to ask. Every one of them feels the same current you feel. None of them is you.

All of them work the busiest line in this city, and any one of them can stand at a junction and work out that the heaviest thing on the Ferrier line tonight is at the top of Ferrier Street, and there is not one instrument in this city that says they are wrong."

## The Want

They came down to the second landing and she sat down on the step, which she had never done on that stair in four years.

"I want you to hear the rest of it," she said, "because you have given me a thing and it is the worst thing anybody has handed me since the eighth of June and I am going to be fair about it before I say the other half.

"You did not tell me. You were in this building four days ago and you knew, or you did not know, and either way there is a woman on the Ferrier line who has been sitting on it since the eighth with the answer in her hand and did not put it down."

"You did not write it down either."

"No," said Beatrix Halm. "And I did not write it down for the same reason you did not tell me, and the reason is that I have spent four years being told I have a condition, and I have made a decision about what I do with things I am not certain of, and the decision is that I do not put them in a notebook where a clerk can find them.

"So here is what I want and it is not aimed at anything, because there is nothing to aim at. You cannot un-lodge a standing. There is no second form that takes it back and I have looked and there is one desk in this city that does that kind of thing and you used it on the seventh.

"What I want is to be the only one who can find it. I want forty people on that line and one address at the top of it, and I want it to be me, and I have wanted that since you gave me four sentences on a kitchen table and I have not been able to ask for it in the right words until now."

She looked up the stair at him.

"So tell me what to do with the hours before the hearing. There are twenty of them. I am going to work every one and I am going to be on that line at eight every morning and I am going to write down where it is every time, and when the panel asks me on the first of July what a signal operator who cannot hear in one ear is doing in a cab, I am going to have an answer that is mine and not yours."

She stood up and went down the rest of the stair on her own, which she had never done either, and did not look back, and was at the bottom door before he had finished breathing.

# What The Line Does

She came up the sixty-one steps at about seven in the evening with a notebook in her hand and one ear still ringing from six hours in a cab, and she put the notebook on the kitchen table face down, which she had never done before.

"Sit down," said Nadia Ferrous. "You look like a woman who has been listening to a tram for a living and has finally got something to say about it."

"I've had four days to say something and I have not said it to one person in this house." Beatrix Halm sat down heavily. "It started on the first of the month at about eight in the morning and it has happened every day since.

"I have been sitting on the Ferrier line with a notebook and I do not know what to do with any of it. I am going to say it out loud and then I would like somebody to tell me what to do with it."

She turned the notebook round.

"I feel people," she said. "I have said that in this kitchen before and Sun Meiling has said the word condition about it in this kitchen before, and it is not a condition, and I have been proving that for four years on about nine hours a day and I am tired.

"Right. So: the first of the month is the busiest day in this city. Every counter in Ostara is busy on the first, because the monthly figures and the monthly calls and the monthly postings all land on the same day, and the whole city turns toward a counter on the first the way it turns toward a door in the rain. I know that. That is the first of the month to me and it has been for four years.

"And on the first, at about eight in the morning, there was a knot in it. Nine people, going the wrong way."

"How wrong," said Lin Hao.

"Not wrong as in lost. Wrong as in not going where the day's shape says they go." She put her finger on the page. "They were coming off the north line and walking, and they were walking down toward Ferrier Street, and they were slow about it. Not late for anything. Walking because they had a reason and the reason was not a counter with a queue.

"And then it happened again on the second, and on the third, and on the fourth. Same hour. Same size. Nine."

The room did not make a sound.

"I can feel about forty thousand people move on a good evening," said Beatrix Halm. "I cannot tell you a name and I cannot tell you a face. What I can tell you is a direction and a weight and whether somebody is in a hurry, and I have been wrong about those things perhaps twice in four years, and both times I was wrong because I was tired and I have been in bed for a day and a half and I am not tired."

"What weight," said Runa Sato.

"Heavier than a person on an errand." Beatrix said it slowly, because she had thought about this part for two days and she wanted it exactly right. "Somebody carrying a case. Or a satchel. Or a coat over an arm that is not a coat, because you carry a coat over an arm when you do not mean to put it on, and that is a document."

Nadia Ferrous put both hands flat on the table.

"That is what a woman with a docket looks like," she said. "I have watched two of them this week."

"You have watched one," said Beatrix Halm. "Hanne Voog came up those steps on the sixth at about eleven and she had a docket under one arm and a coat over the other, and I was on a tram at Cordwain at the time and I felt her go up that street like a coin going into a slot, and I have been trying to say since then what the other eight are."

Nadia Ferrous turned round and looked at her.

"You felt her come up this street from a tram at Cordwain."

"I feel the Ferrier line," said Beatrix Halm, "and Cordwain is on it, and a person coming up this street is on it, and I do not need to be in the room. That is the entire mechanism and it has never once let me down and I have never once been believed about it in a professional capacity in my life."

## The Ear

"Sit down," said Nadia Ferrous again, and pushed the notebook toward her. "You are not going to do the thing where you explain it to me for nine minutes and then apologise for explaining it. I have done that at a counter once and I would rather not do it in my own kitchen."

"Six," said Beatrix Halm.

"That is the shortest you have ever been in this room and I have known you four years. Go on."

"The cab is the loudest room in Ostara," she said, "and I am in it nine hours. That is not a complaint. That is the job. There is a line on the Ferrier that goes through nine junctions in four miles and every one of them puts metal on metal, and if you are listening properly you are not listening to the train, you are listening to what the train is about to do.

"And I have not had two ears since the Veil." She touched behind her own left ear without taking her hand off the table. "It is not deaf. That is the word they use and it is wrong. It is a switch. When it goes, the whole of the noise of that cab comes into the other ear and stays there for the rest of the shift and into the night, and a signal operator cannot work a line she cannot get out from under.

"I told that to a man in a corridor four years ago and he wrote the word condition on a card, and I have been an officer with a condition since, and do you know what a condition is in that building? A condition is a thing you are not tested for. It is not a thing you are excused from. It is a thing you are not *measured* for, which means you are never found to be all right and you are never found to be not all right.

"For four years I have been sitting in a cab I can hear through one ear, working a line I could work with my eyes shut, and not one person in that division has ever once found out what I can do, because the only instrument that could tell them is the one that is switched off.

"And then on the first of June, in the middle of the busiest morning of the month, there were nine people going the wrong way, and I knew it in the same four seconds I know a junction, and there is no card in this city that takes the reading of a woman who can feel nine people turning a corner."

She stopped, and looked at the notebook face down on the table.

"That is the part I was not going to say upstairs," she said. "That is the whole of what a thing done for a door gets. Sun Meiling has a better sentence than mine and I have never once been able to get near it, so I am going to go and sit in a chair and be quiet now."

## The Part She Will Not Do

"I am not going to be brave about the rest of it," she said. "I am going to be the opposite of brave. I am going to tell you the one thing I have been carrying since the second and then I am going to stop.

"It has got a shape. Nine people at eight in the morning, five days running, is a thing somebody has arranged. Arranged means a list, and a list means somebody has a list, and the reason I have not said it out loud until today is that the last time I said a thing like this to a man with a badge he wrote the word condition on it in front of me and gave it back.

"So here is what I want and it is the only thing I have come up here for." She put both hands on the notebook. "I do not want to be told I am right. I want to be aimed.

"Nine people, eight in the morning, going the wrong way, five days running, and a sixth day coming tomorrow, and by tomorrow it will be a pattern and a pattern is the only kind of warning that is worth anything, because one day is a coincidence and two days is a habit.

"You tell me where to point it. You tell me what to look for and I will look for it from a tram, and I cannot be in two places and I am one person and my hearing is going and I am not going to spend the rest of my life writing down things I felt and being pleasant about them."

Nadia Ferrous reached over and turned the notebook back face down.

"For the record," she said, "she has been in this building four years and not one person has ever once asked her what she wanted before she told them what she felt. I am not going to be the first. I am going to be the second and I am going to be extremely gracious about it."

"Nadia."

"It is true and it is graceless and I am doing it on purpose," said Nadia Ferrous, "and I would like the household to note that I said it."

Lin Hao looked at the notebook for a while.

"Tomorrow morning," he said. "Eight o'clock. The first person who comes up those steps with a case or a satchel, or who stands on that corner for four minutes without going into anything, you write down what time and which way they came and what they were carrying and you bring it here and you do not go in after them and you do not stand on a corner being looked at by anybody. Is that clear."

"That is clear and it is four sentences and they are the first four anybody has ever given me with anything in them." She did not pick the notebook up. "I will say one more thing and it is going to sound like a complaint and it is not one.

"For four years I have been the person this house finds out something from after it has already happened. Beatrix heard about it. Beatrix came. It is a useful arrangement and I have never minded it until this month, and what has changed this month is that the last four things anybody found out from me got in first.

"On the second I felt nine people and I did not say so until the sixth. That is four days. Four days is about the length of time it takes a letter to go somewhere and come back, and I have been running the fastest service in this city and I have still been four days late."

"You had a reason."

"I had four days of a notebook and not one address in this city to give it to," said Beatrix Halm, "and the reason is a good one and I am not going to use it in this room."

She stood up and picked the notebook up and went to the door and then stopped, because Agnete Lindqvist was standing in the doorway of the room at the back with the door open behind her, in the ordinary way, in the daytime, the way she had stood every day this month.

"You are the woman a district officer left a line blank for," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"I am."

"Good," said Agnete Lindqvist. "The other one made a man put his pen away, and the difference between the two is the whole of what I do and nobody has ever been able to write it down."

# The Day Book

On the eighth of July Tessa Okonkwo showed him the day book with his name on the first line of every month and asked him to hold to it.

He had woken wanting to be useful to someone he could still reach. The want pointed him down the market stair before seven. He felt shame that usefulness was all he had left to offer and offered it anyway.

Seven women on the cut roll kept their pieces in seven pockets. Six kept an address at Ferrier Street and six kept the notebook, one shut in an empty room. The seventh row stayed pencil sewn in a pocket with twenty-two stitches. Petra Ilyin was on no roll of any kind and walked with Tessa as far as the market. Wren Kallo was on nothing at any time and was the eighth partner. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and her door stood open.

The hatch ran two hundred and three people through the morning that ran to two. He carried the crate and set it down where they would pass it. The counter was worn smooth by nine years of hands.

Tessa took the day book from under the counter and opened it to the month. His initials stood on the first line with a date and no number. A standing clinical debt for the month her shelves ran out, so that when an office asked what she was holding and for whom she had a name and a date instead of a sentence made up at two in the morning.

"It holds," Tessa said. "Nine years of a quarter of eight and eight days counted in the lines. I worked it out on the sixteenth of last month and I am holding to it. I want you to hold to it with me."

"I hold to it," he said.

She shut the book and put it back under the counter. Her hands were steady. Nine years had taught them steadiness. Eight days counted had taught them something new about being read as a person when the line described a clinic.

He worked the washrooms floor by floor. At noon he ate on the back step. Lode sat down beside him with a bucket.

"One room empty," Lode said kindly. "The landing knows by lunch. A true report in a division. A flat still held on paper."

"Both true," he said.

"Then it is a household that can lose a woman and keep her name," Lode said. "That is the hardest kind to carry. A signed receipt is a door shut. A filed report is a door shut in another building."

He mopped the second landing after noon and changed the paper and chased the boiler the fourth floor had reported. The boiler knocked once and settled. Hot water came back by two. A woman thanked him and went down. The work steadied him the way exactness steadied the house.

He stopped at the Reading Square on the way home to look at the wall the Thursday crew had painted on the sixth. The week stood fresh in the Thursday shade. The side board stood untouched in the monthly shade with the schedule of the twenty-sixth under the number. Two crews and two shades. He could tell them apart now without stopping to think.

An old man at the wall end asked him whether the number on the board was a street number. He said it was a household number and that it stood for the house and for everybody on the roll of it. The old man said six was a great many for one street and went off. The cheap question cost nothing. It only made the dear one easy to stand next to.

He came home at six and found the house doing its own exactness back, in a kitchen, with bread. Runa washed the bread knife and put it away. Beatrix folded her gloves. Marguerite capped her pen. Sun folded her copy. Petra tied nothing and untied nothing. Nadia wrote the day with the hour on it.

Beatrix showed him her page before she shut the book. The day in her small level hand. Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. A bag gone from an empty room. A house that now existed on paper and still ate standing up. He read it and nodded, because a record of ordinary things was the only record left that anyone in the house controlled.

Runa did not show anybody anything, which was how he knew she was thinking about the Field counter and its half past three. Her answer was in. Her hearing had a paper. She sat beside Beatrix and talked tram talk to keep from talking about the thing in the middle of the table.

Wren stopped him on the step after supper with her folder shut and her hands empty.

"Fourteen days," she said. "Two weeks on Hadewig's desk. It has not been acted on. I am the person who wrote it and I am the last person anyone will go to. Tomorrow I go to her desk and ask what a division does with a file it has held two weeks. That is my own act and I will answer for it."

"Fourteen days," he said.

She sat down on the step beside him without being asked and set the folder flat on her knees. Fourteen days the finding had sat with four edits and every one a removal. Four days the true report had sat in another division with eleven minutes in her own hand. Two divisions held him between them without speaking to each other.

"I cannot keep a thing," Wren said quietly. "That is structural. I get a thing and I get rid of it. Four years I told three people about a thing sworn in a room. This time I told paper. Paper keeps better than people and worse. It sits."

He sat with her until the lamps came on. The step was cold. The folder stayed shut.

Agnete ate standing in the doorway with the door open behind her. The three papers lay squared on the table. The empty room stood made and straight behind its shut nothing.

He went to her with bread. She ate and thanked him. Her discipline held rooms still by standing in them. His presence let her give what no room could take.

Sun came out with her coat over her arm and checked her stitches under the stair light. Twenty-two held the seventh row. The white thread stood out against the dark. She bit a loose end off and showed him.

"It holds the house," she said. "Say it back to me."

"It holds the house," he said.

At nine he stood on the landing with his thumb in the cold middle of the pale rectangle. The nail held nothing. The paint stayed pale.

He oiled the stair rail after morning work with a rag from the washroom store. The rail took the oil dark and smooth. Hands would wear it lighter by evening. Work answered work. The house learned exactness from the office and did its own back.

Beatrix thanked him for the rail on her way up with her notebook. Her good ear caught his step. She climbed steady without holding on. Her gloves were folded square. Her season was squared in her coat. Small squares held her.

Marguerite wrote five lines pencilled on a fresh sheet after bread and set it beside the valuation copy. Five debts stood. The examination. The open security. The live account. The certified figure. The counted clinic. She did not uncap her pen. Pencilled lines could be read without being entered.

Sun showed Petra her twenty-two stitches on the stair after supper. Petra looked the way she had looked at hands for eleven years. She nodded once. Stitches held the way markings held. Both were made by a hand that did not ask.

Nadia wrote the day with the hour on it and read it back twice. The second reading was for the empty room. Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. A rail oiled. A house that existed on paper and still ate standing up.

He set the oil rag square on the washroom shelf before bed. The rag smelled of oil and work. The rail shone dark down the stair. Hands would wear it lighter by morning. That was the whole of its record.

Agnete ran her hand along the oiled rail in the morning and nodded. A shut door clicked behind her once on the first because she shut it. An oiled rail shone because he oiled it. Both were made by a hand that chose.

He hung the oil rag to dry on the landing hook before sleep. The hook held. The rail shone. The morrow would wear it lighter.

Beatrix touched the shone rail on her way down in the morning and smiled once. Small shines held her.

He walked the sixty-one steps down and up once before bed for the steadiness of it. Petra had counted them since the first of last month. He counted them now without moving his lips. The steps knew his tread.

Agnete listened to his steps from her doorway and smiled into her cup. Eleven years she had heard steps go down and not come up. Tonight every step came back.

He left the stair lamp burning till eleven as the office paid for. The light fell two steps down and stopped short of the landing. The pale rectangle stayed cool outside it. Light and pale held apart.

Petra turned the lamp off at eleven from the landing switch. Darkness took the stair whole. The rectangle went dark with it. Both waited for morning.

He tested the dark stair after eleven with one foot before trusting it. The stair held. The rail shone faint under his palm. Dark stairs held the way shut doors held. Both were learned.

He slept after eleven with the dark stair outside his door. The stair would lighten by five. The rail would shine. The rectangle would cool. Morning would square them all.

He went in and lay down with the head line in his coat pocket and the day book's first line in his head. The report sat filed. The finding sat fourteen days. The clinic stood counted. The plate stayed lodged. Five debts stood and the one woman who could price them was out of the room with a true document behind her.

He slept after eleven with that arithmetic in the house, standing, waiting for the morrow to bring the ninth and her reading.

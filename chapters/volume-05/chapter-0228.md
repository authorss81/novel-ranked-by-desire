# The Shape Of An Edit

The Reading Square empties at about ten and the ranks wall is lit until eleven by two lamps on brackets that the Office pays for and that go off on a clock in a hut at the end of the row, and on the eighteenth of June there were two people at the bottom of it.

She had been there since a quarter past nine, sitting on the kerb with a folder flat on her knees and her back to the wall, and when he came round the corner of the ranks wall and saw her she did not stand up.

"You came on your own," she said.

"You said on your own."

"I did, and I want to be clear that I did not do it because I do not trust your house. Your house has been extremely careful for a month and it is the only careful thing in this city that has not been an accident." She moved the folder along the kerb. "Sit down.

I am going to talk for about ten minutes and then I am going to stop talking, and I want you to be sitting down for the ten minutes because what I have got is a document and I am not a dramatic person and you should not be looking at my face while I hand it to you."

He sat down on the kerb with his back to a wall of tile.

## The Finding

"Your record is in my division four times over," said Wren Kallo. "I want you to understand what that means before I tell you what I found in it, because what I found is not about a household and it is not about your plate and it is not about a certificate, and I have been looking at it since the sixteenth and it has got worse every day and it has not once got better.

"Counter-forensics reads the history of a record. We do not read the record. The record is a thing you can lie to. The history is the thing you cannot, because every change ever made to a document leaves a shape behind it in the shape of everything that went before, and I have been trained to see that shape since I was twenty-six and I have never once been wrong about it inside a building.

"Open your file. Any file of yours anywhere in this city, your service file, your reading file, the schedule of the fourth, the index entry at Cobb Street that has six on it in your own handwriting now. Put them in any order. Look at the shape.

"There have been four edits."

She held up four fingers and did not look at them.

"In March. A figure comes off a band and a tolerance comes with it, and it is taken out of your own reading, out of your own record, by your own hand, and nothing goes in to replace it.

"The seventh of April. A line comes out of an instrument column. Not a figure — a line. The whole of a form is struck through and a half form is entered instead, and the half form says that a thing you did is an instrument, and that is an edit downward, because the long version was better for you and the short version is better for the file.

"The twenty-seventh of April. A quantity is detached from an account and the account is left open, and that is the biggest edit in any record I have ever read and it went in the direction of away from you, into a place that has no name on it.

"And the nineteenth of May. Six lines go onto a declaration and every one of them is somebody else, and your plate becomes answerable for six people it has never met."

She put her hand down on the folder.

"There is not one addition in it. Not one. Four edits and every one of them a removal, and I have been in counter-forensics for eleven years and I have read about nine thousand files and in every single one of them the edits go up. That is what a record does when it is used. People add themselves. They buy permissions, they answer for other people, they put a wife's name on a schedule because a house is better than a flat.

"Yours has only ever been made smaller," said Wren Kallo, "and a record like that is not about the man it is about. A record that has only ever been edited downward is a record somebody has been protecting, and in this city a protected record is a record somebody has had access to, and it is not the man."

## The Date

"How long have you had it," he said.

"Since the sixteenth, on my bench, with the correction that carries my name at the foot of it." She did not look at him. "I want to tell you the two halves of that separately, because one of them is my fault.

"The first half is that a correction came across my bench and I countersigned it because that is the standing order and because a countersignature is a check and not a signature. That is true and it took four seconds.

"The second half is that I read the history of your file afterwards, at about a quarter past eight in the morning, on my own time, because your premises number was on it and because in four years you have never once given me a reason to and I had built one out of nothing, and I have never hated anything about myself so precisely."

She put both hands flat on the folder.

"I have written it up. It is on my bench and it goes to the head of the division on the twenty-third of this month, and there is no version of my job in which it does not go, and I have known that since the sixteenth and I have not sent it and I have not taken it off, and I am telling you the date because you would work it out and I would rather you had it from me."

"The twenty-third."

"The twenty-third. And after that it is a division's file, and a division's file about a man whose record has only ever been edited downward is a question that every office holding a copy of it will ask, and none of them will ask me, because I am the person who wrote it and I am the last person anybody will go to."

## What She Wants

He had been sitting on a kerb in the cold for eleven minutes and he had not asked her one question, and he understood at about that point that he was not going to, and that the reason was that she had come here at a quarter past nine on a night when she could have written to him and had not, and she had told him a date.

"Here is the thing I have not said yet," said Wren Kallo, "and I have been in this city a long time and I have never said anything like it to anybody, and I am going to say it badly.

"I cannot keep a thing. That is not a figure of speech and it is not a condition and it is not a thing somebody wrote on a card about me when I was younger. It is structural. I get a thing and I get rid of it.

Four years and I have told three people about a thing I swore to in a room, and I have watched it travel, and every single time it has got somewhere I did not put it and I did not know the route, and I have never been able to sit on one and I have never once wanted to.

"And then you come along," she said, "and you are the only record I have ever opened that has an edit in it and the edit is the only thing about you I have ever been able to see, and it is not a thing I am allowed to keep, and it is not a thing I can put down, and I have been carrying it for four years and it has never once weighed anything and tonight it weighs about as much as that wall."

She put her thumb flat on the tile behind her.

"So I have come out here to ask you for a thing and it is the only thing I have ever wanted off another person in my life. I want one night with nothing in it.

Four years and every single thing about you has a shape, because everything about you has been taken off something or put on something by somebody, and I cannot look at a person and not see the editing, and it is the only thing I do, and I am so tired of it that I came and sat on a wall for three quarters of an hour before you got here."

"You want me to not have been altered."

"I want nothing to have been altered," said Wren Kallo. "Not for long. Not permanently. I know what I am asking.

I have been on the other side of a table from a person like you and been asked to hold still and hold still is not a thing anybody can do for another person, so I am asking for about four hours and I am telling you that I will not be able to explain what is happening to me while it is going on and that is the price and I have thought about the price."

The lamp at the end of the row made a sound and went off, and the one at the other end followed it, and the ranks wall went dark above them in about four seconds, and the square was black except for the Reading's own doorway two hundred yards off.

## Four Hours

"Yes," he said, and it was the quickest of the eleven minutes she had watched him spend on it.

She had been sitting on that kerb for three quarters of an hour before he came round the corner of the ranks wall, and she had asked him for the only thing she has ever wanted off another person in her life, and it had taken him eleven minutes to say it, and she had wanted it said in two, and she had to sit there and find out whether that was going to matter.

"Before any of it," he said. "I want to say it back to you, so that you have heard it said. Outdoors, on stone, about four hours, and you have told me twice that you will not be able to tell me what is happening to you while it is going on. If you want it to stop, say a word and I stop, and none of it goes in a book."

"Then I will not be able to tell you whether I am stopping or not." She put her hands flat on her knees. "That is the price. I have said the price out loud twice and you are the first man in this city who has offered to stop anyway."

"Yes," said Wren Kallo. "I want it. I am frightened of it. Those are the same thing tonight and I am not going to stand here and tell you they are two things."

She did not talk for the next hour and a half and neither did he.

That is the part of it that would not have been possible with anybody else in this city, and it happened because she had asked for it, and what she had asked for was the one thing a woman who reads the shape of an edit in every document she has ever touched can be given by a man who has had four taken out of him.

He took his coat off and put it on the kerb for her to sit on and she did not say thank you. He undid his shirt and she watched his hands do it and then she put her own hand flat on his sternum, low, over the fourth rib on the left, and held it there, and closed her eyes.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing."

"Four years," said Wren Kallo, "and I have never once touched a man and not found it. I found it in about four seconds on the eighth of June and I have been carrying it round like a stone in a shoe since and there is nothing. Take your shirt off."

He took his shirt off, and she got him up off the kerb and back against the tile with both palms flat on his ribs, and she was not looking at him.

She was looking at the place between his collarbone and his breastbone the way a woman looks at a page. Her fingers went down the line of him and stopped, and went on and stopped somewhere else, and he watched her fail to find anything and decide to look somewhere else.

"Stay still," she said.

"I am still."

"You are holding still." She did not look up. "Holding still is a different shape from not moving. I can see the difference between two documents that agree on every word and I can see it in your hands, and you are helping."

"Then tell me where to put them."

"Anywhere that is not on me."

He put them behind him on the cold stone and she made a sound with her nose that was almost a laugh, and then she got his belt open with two fingers and had to stop and look at what she had done to it, and he lay still and let her work at it, and when she had it she went on higher up, and after a while he stopped keeping track of what she had already read and stopped trying and simply felt it.

She went down the front of him with her mouth and came back up about halfway and looked at him from four inches away.

"Say something."

"Anything in particular."

"Anything. I am not listening to the words." She said it into him. "I am listening to what happens underneath them, and I have never once done that with a person, and I did not know until tonight that it was a thing a body could be made to do."

He told her about a valve on the fourth floor at the Fourth Branch that had been weeping since March, and about a tram he had sat on behind a man who was reading a document upside down for forty minutes without once turning it over, and about a woman in a queue who had let him keep her place and had not said why.

"Say something else."

He told her about a woman who had come into the washroom on the ground floor of the Fourth Branch once a week for four years and talked at him for twenty minutes about nothing, and had never once asked him a single question, and that he had understood about two years ago that she had not been talking to him at all and had come anyway.

She put her hand over his mouth and made him stop, and then took it away again.

"There," she said. "Do you know what that is."

"You have been listening to me the whole time."

"I have been checking you the whole time." She sat back on her heels.

"Eleven years in a division where the entire job is the shape a thing leaves behind, and a man who has been edited four times in five years ought to sound like four joins. You do not.

"You sound like a man who has spent five years taking things out of other people's records and putting them nowhere at all, and that is a worse thing than a join and it is not a join, and I have never heard it before tonight and I do not know what to do with it."

She took him apart with her hands and her mouth afterwards, in her own time, and did not hurry at one point of it, and he let her do it, which he had not believed on the walk over the square that he would be able to do.

She made him promise not to tell Ines Duarte, which was a thing she had done once before in her life and to a different man, and she told him about that afterwards, and he laughed, which she had not planned for.

He had spent four years being the thing other people edited him through — a division, a board, a man on a ladder with a trowel — and there was a woman on the stone in front of him with her hands on him going through him for what somebody had taken out, and there was nothing there, and he understood somewhere in the third hour that he had wanted this for a long time and had not known that it was available.

She read him with her hands for about an hour after that and found nothing, over and over, the way you check a door you have already checked. At some point in the second hour she started to shake, and it was not cold, and he held her through it.

Afterwards she told him that she had not done that since she was nineteen and had not told a living soul about it, and that she was telling him because she had asked for a night with nothing in it, and that this was the first thing that had come out of her in four years.

Afterwards they lay on the kerb under about four feet of dark tile with their clothes half on and she talked to the sky about her division, and her flat, and the woman she trained, and the exact number of years she had been pretending a condition was a discipline, and none of it was about him.

"There," she said, at about half past three. "Nothing. Four hours and a half, and one shape less than I came out here with, and I am going to be enormously worse at my job at the start of next week than I am now, and I would do it again."

## What She Left

She sat up and squared the folder on her knees and did not open it.

"Two things and then I am going home, and both of them are about you and neither of them is a request.

"The first is that the certificate is on the twenty-seventh and the schedule goes up on the twenty-sixth and the schedule cannot be certified by the auditor of record, and I have read that index entry, and the auditor of record is named on it, and her name is on your roll as well, and I am going to say the next sentence and then I am not going to say another thing about it in my life.

"You are going to have to lose somebody in the next nine days."

The square was very quiet and the Reading's doorway was two hundred yards off and the tile above them was black, and there was a name in it somewhere above her left hand, and she had found it in the dark.

"The second is smaller and it is the reason I came out here at a quarter past nine instead of writing to you, and I would like you to have it before you go to bed."

She stood up and turned and put her hand flat on the dark wall above them and ran it along, and found the place by touch, about four feet up, in the second rank.

"Stand up and look at it," said Wren Kallo. "You cannot see it. That is the other half of it and I have been thinking about it all the way down here. There is no light on this wall after eleven o'clock and there has not been for twenty years, and I have just found your name with my hand in the dark, in about two seconds, in a wall that a hundred and forty thousand people a week read.

"And that is the thing that is going to happen to you, and it is going to happen without anybody deciding to do it. Everything that has been kept off this wall for five years has been kept off it by darkness, and darkness is a thing a city switches off at eleven, and it has switched it off on you for the last four hours, and I was standing here when it did."

She came down off the kerb and picked her coat off it and gave it back to him.

"I am not going to say who I have told since the eighth," she said. "I said I would not and I am not. I want you to hear the reason, because the reason is not that I am protecting them. The reason is that the moment I say a name to you, you will go and stand in front of that person, and I will have made a road, and I have never once in my life been able to stop a thing once I have made a road."

Then she said it, once, and she said it as a clerk reads a line out of an index and not as a woman says a man's name in the dark.

"Lin Hao, premises Ferrier Street, number 34-6-118.

And on the first of next month there will be one of two dates on that board in four-foot type, either the fourth of last month or the twenty-seventh of this month, and it will not be your choice and it will not be mine, and you will walk past here at nine in the morning and read it with your own eyes, and you will be the only person in Ostara who knows which one it is before the people it is about."

She went up the square toward the Reading without turning round, and she did not say his name again, and the lamp in the hut at the end of the row did not come back on for about four hours.

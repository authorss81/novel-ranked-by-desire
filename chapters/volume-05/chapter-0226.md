# Nothing Moved In It

She saw him through the hatch at about ten past one on the sixteenth and she did not stop, because two hundred and three people went through that hatch on a good morning and a man standing at it was a man who wanted something, and then she looked again and put her hand flat on the counter.

"Come in," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Not through the hatch. Round the side and in the trade door. I have got the room until two and you have got the rest of your life, and one of those two things is going to waste itself if you stand out there being polite about it."

He came round the side and sat on the edge of the table in the room with the one window and the chest under it, and she shut the trade door and turned round and looked at him for about two seconds and then crossed the room and took hold of him.

## What A Clinician Does

"Take your shirt off. You have got four ribs on the left and I have not put a hand on one of them since the fourteenth of May and I am not going to start today, because there is a piece of paper going to Cobb Street this afternoon and I am not standing in a room with a form in it with my hands on anything."

"That is not what I came for."

"I know what you came for," she said. "You came to ask me whether a certificate takes this building. Sit still and I will answer you in four minutes and then I am going to do the thing I set aside."

She counted them along from the angle of the rib with her own fingers, the way she had done in May, and she said the number out loud as she went, because a clinician reads out loud and she had been taught it by a woman who is dead.

"Two. Three. Four. There. Same four. Sharp on the same one, same side, same distance down. It has not moved since the fourteenth of May and it has not moved since before that and it is not going to move this afternoon."

She put her hand away and sat down on the chest and wrote for about ninety seconds in a book with a limp spine, and he read upside down and could see the date and the hour and her name and the words the fourth rib on the left, and then nothing found.

"I am writing that down," she said. "That is the whole of it and it is going into the day book tonight with my name on it and the time on it and it cannot be un-written, and in about nine years a woman in an office is going to read it and is going to find a clinician who examined the thing she had been carrying for four months and wrote down that there was nothing there.

"Which is worse," said Tessa Okonkwo, "than the other version. The other version had a name on it. I did not know what it was, but it had a name, and it was a curiosity, and a curiosity is a thing you are allowed to have. This is a finding. I found nothing, and I am the only person alive who can say whether that means I looked properly."

"You looked properly."

"I looked for eleven minutes with both hands and I said it out loud," she said, "and I have had four ribs on the left since the seventh of April, and on the fourteenth of May I wrote in a book that I did not know what moved in them, and today I have written that nothing did. Both of those are true and they cannot both be on one line of a chart, and the chart has one line."

## What She Is Afraid Of

"The thing about a symptom," said Tessa Okonkwo, "is that it is somebody else's job. You can hand a symptom to somebody and they know what to do with it, and I have been handing this one round this house for four months and about six of the women in this house have taken it and looked at it and handed it on, and it has been the most useful object in this kitchen, and I have been using it.

"There is nothing in there now and I am frightened."

"You are frightened of a clean result."

"I am frightened of what I am," she said. "A clinician who cannot find the thing is a clinician who has been treating a symptom, and a symptom is something you can keep giving away, and what I have been giving away for four months is not a symptom. It is a reason to be in this room."

She got up and put the book in a drawer and shut it.

"You will notice I have not written the word down. I have written that I looked and that there was nothing. The other version goes in the day book at the Vestry tonight, under my name, where two hundred and three people cannot reach it and I can."

## The Answer

"You are going to ask me about the certificate."

"You said four minutes."

"I am going to give you four minutes and I am going to start at the end, because you have been carrying it up and down this building since March and it is the end of the thing and not the middle." She sat down on the table across from him. "Yes. It takes this building. Not on the twenty-seventh.

On the first of next month, when the board gets painted, and probably in March, when somebody who has never heard of me asks a question about a figure and an address and finds that the two of them belong together."

"How."

"Read the correction," said Tessa Okonkwo. "That is what it is for. It is an index made out of returned lines. Not totals — totals are arithmetic, and arithmetic belongs to whoever did it, and the Office does not trust arithmetic, it trusts lines, because a line is a thing a person put a mark against.

"This clinic has never once been entered in anything. Nine years, a quarter of eight every morning, two hundred and three people a morning, and not one returned line in this city has ever come back from this door. That is the only reason this room is safe and it is safe the way a hole in a wall is safe, which is to say it is safe because a great many people have walked past it and not turned their heads.

"The moment it is entered, it is a line. And a line is counted."

## What She Decides

He had come to ask her one question and he had got the answer and then a second thing he had not asked for.

"Have you ever entered this clinic in anything," he said.

"On the thirteenth of last month," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Not this morning. On the back page of a card, in my own hand, in a book that is not the day book and is not the index and is not in the Office and never has been. Nine years of attendance is on that card, and the card is the only piece of paper in this building that says this room exists.

It is a piece of paper that proves I exist to me. That has been enough for nine years of mornings, and it was enough right up until a woman left a line blank on the third of June and a division counted it."

She turned her hands over and looked at them.

"So here is what I have decided this afternoon and I am telling you before I do it, and I am telling you in this room and not in your kitchen, because you are going to want to argue and I do not want to argue in a kitchen.

"I am going to enter this building. Not on a card. On an entry, and it is going into the returned lines, and it is going to be counted, and it is going to put a clinic on the same page as your answer of six, in the same office, under the same address. I have sat here for twenty minutes working out whether that is a reason not to do it, and it is not."

"Why it is not."

"Because your fourth page answers the figure of persons keeping an address at Ferrier Street," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and a clinic is not a person, and I know that, and the Office will know that. And because I have written eleven letters in six years to families whose card stopped working, and not one of them ever had to explain to anybody where a clinic was, because a clinic is not a standing and it is not an office and it is not in the chain.

"And because you answered a form with a number that is one short to keep a woman off a line," she said, "and if I am the only person left in this house with an institution that is not on any list at all, then in four years somebody is going to read a page with a false number on it and a clinic on it, and they are going to be perfectly right about both, and the clinic is going to have been mine."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"Give me a hand with the crate on Thursday. It is two hundred and three people a day and I have been doing it alone for nine years, and I am not going to say thank you, because you will take it as a favour and it is not one."

## After

He went out through the trade door at about two and the market was doing what it does at two on a working afternoon and he stood on the step for a minute and did not go down it.

Then he went back up and knocked and she had the crate already off the wall.

"You have not gone," said Tessa Okonkwo.

"No."

"Then you can carry that end, and I will not talk to you on the way, and afterwards I am going to sit down at about nine in the evening and eat a hot meal in front of another person, and I have not done that since the seventh of April, and you are going to sit at that table while I do it, and you are not going to talk about the fourth page or the certificate or the correction or the board.

"You are going to tell me about your brother," she said. "You have not done that in six months and I am the only person in this house who is going to understand an answer if you give me one, and I am asking you now while there is a crate to carry, because afterwards it will be a conversation and I have not got the day for a conversation."

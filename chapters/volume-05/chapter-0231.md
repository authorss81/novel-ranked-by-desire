# The Empty Room

He went down the sixty-one steps on the twenty-first at eight in the morning to buy bread and came back up with it and stood on the landing for a minute because the wall did not look like a wall any more.

The nail was still there. The paint round it was the colour of the rest of the landing and the paint inside the nine inches by fourteen was paler, and in the daylight from the stair window the edge showed as a line. He put the bread down on the step and touched the middle of it with two fingers. It was cold.

He felt ashamed. It was a small clean feeling and it sat low in him and did not move when he breathed. He had written six on a page on the fourteenth to keep a woman who slept behind a door off a line, and the page was in an index now, and the roll that would have proved him false was in seven pockets, and the shame was not that he had lied. The shame was that the lie had worked for six days.

He wanted, that morning, to get the house through one ordinary day without another paper coming to the table. That was the whole of it. Bread, work, the washroom floor at the Fourth Branch, back before dark.

Sun Meiling was in the kitchen with her coat pocket sewn shut with white thread. She had done it in the night. The seventh row in pencil was inside it.

"You sewed it," he said.

"I did," she said. "If it is going to live in a coat it is going to live shut. A pocket that opens when I sit down is not a place."

Beatrix Halm was at the table with her notebook open and nothing written on the page. She had been a signal operator for eleven years and she could hear a tram three streets off, and she was listening to the stair.

"There is nothing on the stair," he said.

"There is nothing on the stair today," she said. "Yesterday there was nothing on the stair and the day before there was nothing, and I am writing down that there is nothing because morning after morning of nothing is a record too."

Tessa Okonkwo came in with a crate half full of folded sheets and put it down by the door. Nine years of a quarter of eight every morning had taught her to carry weight without showing it.

"You are back early," she said.

"I am early," he said. "The bread line was short."

Ines Duarte came in at half past eight with her bag over her shoulder and her coat on. She looked at the table and at the bread and at the nail through the open door, and she said nothing about any of them. She had said nine sentences the morning before about numbers and none about herself, and she kept to it.

"I am going to Cobb Street," she said. "I will be back at six."

"Is there anything—" he said, and stopped, because the standing instruction in that house was that her business was not asked about, and had not been asked about for eleven days.

"There is nothing you can carry for me," she said. "Go to work."

He went to work. The Fourth Branch washroom on the ground floor had a valve on the fourth floor pipe that had wept since March, and he had reported it twice, and the second report had a date on it and no action under it. He mopped the floor and changed the paper and wiped the tile where the ranks of cleaners signed, and at ten o'clock Lode came in with a bucket and stood beside him for a while without speaking.

Lode had known about the lodged plate since the eighth. Half the landing had known by lunch. Lode had never once used it.

"You have got a look," Lode said.

"I have got a house," he said.

"That is the same thing this month," Lode said, and took his bucket out.

At noon he ate bread on the back step with the plate in his pocket. Plate 8,138. Floor 8,141. Three under. The numbers had not moved in five years and nothing he had done in ten days could move them. What had moved was a weight at the top of Ferrier Street that about forty operators could find, and a figure in his own hand, and a roll cut at the ruled lines.

He had the mechanism whole. Behind a door that stayed open, a woman who had not left a building in eleven years was asleep, because there was nowhere left to write her down except a blank line that had already been counted.

In the afternoon the washroom door opened and a woman from the third floor put her head in and asked whether the second basin on the left was draining, and he said it was draining slow and he would see to it, and she thanked him and went out. It was an ordinary exchange. He held on to it for the rest of the hour because there had been so few ordinary exchanges in June.

He came home at six with wet hands and found the kitchen full and quiet. Petra Ilyin was mending a strap on her bag with a needle. Marguerite Vale was at the table with the thirteenth docket squared to the edge in front of her, the space at the foot empty. Runa Sato was copying figures into a book in her own hand. Nadia Ferrous had her notebook shut.

Agnete Lindqvist was in the doorway at the back with a cup in her hand. She had been behind that door for eleven years. The door stayed open.

"Evening," he said to her.

"Evening," she said. "There was no post."

"No post," he said. "Bread."

He cut the bread and they ate it standing round the table because there were not enough chairs, and nothing with a form number on it was discussed, and Ines came in at half past six and ate a slice standing up and went to the room she shared alone and shut the door without locking it.

After supper Sun Meiling took him out on to the landing and showed him the stitching on her pocket.

"Eleven stitches," she said. "I counted. My mother taught me eleven for a pocket that has to hold."

"Does it hold," he said.

"It holds," she said. "That is not the trouble. The trouble is that I spent eight days making a wall agree with a file so neither of them was a lie, and now there is no wall, and the file is in three offices, and the only true thing left in this house is a piece of pencil in a coat I have sewn shut."

She looked at the pale rectangle.

"I keep thinking somebody will paint it," she said.

"They will not," he said. "Not this week."

"How do you know."

"Because the men who paint walls come on a day and today was not it," he said, and was glad to have one true sentence about paint to give her.

She nodded and went in. He stayed on the landing a minute longer with his thumb in the cold middle of the rectangle, and then he went in and washed the bread knife and put it away.

Nadia Ferrous was at the table with her notebook shut and her pencil beside it. She had kept six notebooks for four years for the six women who kept an address at Ferrier Street, and the seventh row had never had one, and now there was no roll to keep them against.

"Say which list," she said, without looking up. "I am trying to write down where we are and I cannot get it straight."

"Seven women on the roll we cut," he said. "Six with an address at Ferrier Street. Six notebooks. One piece of pencil in a sewn pocket. One woman in the house on no roll since the fourth. One woman on nothing at any time. One woman behind a door on no list at all."

She wrote it down on the back of a page and read it back and nodded.

"That is the first true paragraph in two days," she said. "I am keeping it."

Runa Sato came in late with dust on her coat from the Field division counter at Cordwain, where she worked the returns hatch until half past three. Her hearing was on the first of next month, the same morning as the valuation, and the dead list followed on the fourth, and she had ten days of paper to get through before any of it.

"You are counting again," she said to Nadia.

"Somebody has to," Nadia said.

Runa sat down and put her hands round a cup and looked at him.

"You went to work," she said. "Good. Stay at work. A man with a plate in a chained book until the first of next month is no use to anyone standing in a kitchen waiting for a post that cannot come before the twenty-sixth."

"I know the dates," he said.

"I know you know them," she said. "Five days to the schedule. Six to the certificate. Ten to the valuation. I am saying them because I am the one in this house who says them out loud so they do not live in one head."

At nine he sat at the table and wrote nothing, because there was nothing left in that house that anybody in it controlled enough to write down.

At ten the house settled. The stair light went off on its clock. He lay in his room with his coat over the chair and the head line of the cut roll in the pocket, with his name and the premises and the number on it, and he listened to the building breathe.

He was frightened of the twenty-third. He had not said so to anyone. Wren Kallo had written up what she found in the history of his file and it went to the head of her division on the twenty-third, and when it arrived it would be read by a woman whose whole work was the question of who had held a record that had only been made smaller, and the answer was on his floor.

The fear sat next to the shame and did not move it. He slept after eleven with both of them in the room, and the nail held nothing, and the rectangle stayed pale in the dark, and the dark did not care whether it was pale or not.

# The Seventh Row

He went up to the square on the twenty-ninth because the men had been on that wall on the twenty-fourth and he had not, and for four years he had stood at the bottom of that ladder on every morning they came.

There is one Thursday left in this month and it was five days ago.

The wall was as they had left it. The slot with no tile in it was still no tile in it, and it had been no tile in it since the twenty-sixth of April, and there had been nothing on this week's list either way, and a hole in the wall is not a list.

At the foot of the ladder, on the pavement, there was one of their buckets standing by itself, and the white-bearded one was sitting on the kerb beside it in his own coat, at half past eight in the morning, which is not a day the men work.

"You have stopped asking me," he said.

"I stopped asking you on the twenty-second."

"You stopped asking me on the twenty-second and you came up here five days after I was last on this wall, and I was not here for the men, I am here for the bucket, and the bucket has been at the foot of that ladder since the twenty-fourth and it should not be.

"I am not going to be asked about the bucket," said the white-bearded man. "I have come down here on my own time and I have not told the foreman and if anybody asks me I have come down here on my own time."

"There is going to be a name in that slot." He set the tile down and turned round and looked at the wall. "Not this week. This week's list has got nothing in it and the list is not wrong, the list is the list, and a hole where a person used to be is exactly as much information as a name.

"But there is going to be a name in it, because the slot belongs to a man who was at the certification bench at band seven and the bench does not empty itself, and the day somebody who is owed that slot goes back to work — or is appointed to it, which is a different and worse day — a tile comes down off a rack somewhere and it goes in that hole.

"And when it goes in there, every person on that pavement reads it at about the rate of a hundred and forty thousand people a week read that wall, and there is not one instrument in this city that takes a name off a ceramic tile."

"I am not asking you what it says."

"You are not asking me what it says," said the white-bearded man. "You have been asking me how long the hole has been there for a month, and that was a polite way of asking me the same thing, and I have liked you for it."

He picked the bucket up off the pavement and put it in the crate and shut the lid.

"Go to work," he said. "You are twenty minutes late and you have been twenty minutes late every morning since the first of this month and I have not mentioned it either, and I am not going to now."

He got in at about half past six on the twenty-ninth and there was a form on the kitchen table that he was going to sign in the morning, and it was still not signed, and that was correct, and he had told them on the twenty-eighth that he would do it on the thirtieth at about half past two.

"Thirty-two of the sixty are gone," he said, in the doorway. "Twenty-eight are left. One day of intake. Four o'clock at the counter on the second floor of the Reading and the queue starts about ten past three, because it is the last working day of the month and every broker in this city lodges a return on the last working day of the month."

The room let it go unanswered, which was not unusual on the twenty-ninth.

The lamp was on in the hall. It was the lamp on the landing that mattered and the landing lamp was off, and the light coming across the stair window had stopped about two inches above the seventh row where it had stopped every evening since the thirtieth of April.

He went up at about eleven.

The seventh row had a name in it.

SUN MEILING. And beside it, in the same hand, a date, the twenty-ninth of this month, and under the name, in a slightly smaller hand because she had run out of room above the rule:

I ASKED FOR THIS ROW. I FILLED IT. IT IS MINE BY ASKING.

He read it three times.

It was in pencil. It was in her hand, and he knows the handwriting of every woman in that house and he would have known that one across a room in the dark, and it was in pencil because she had come up with what she had in the pocket of a coat and a pencil is what is in a pocket.

Four feet below the seventh row, in pencil, undated, with no name near it, was a sentence that had been on that wall since the tenth of April and had been read by everybody who has ever gone up those sixty-one steps since.

A PAPER COMES TO ME AND I ANSWER IT IN MY OWN HAND AND I DO NOT ASK ANYBODY ELSE IN THIS HOUSE WHAT THE ANSWER IS.

She had answered a document in her own hand and she had not asked anybody else in that house what the answer was, and she had done it on the twenty-ninth of this month at about ten o'clock at night with a pencil, and she had done it nine hours before the counter shut and a day before he signed.

"Sun."

She was at the kitchen table with the lamp off and her coat on.

"I am going to say this once and then you are going to go downstairs and sleep and we will do the counter in the afternoon."

"Go on."

"I was not going to touch that row," he said. "I want you to hear me say it, because in about eleven years there is going to be a woman writing this down and she is going to say a man took a row off a woman and she will be half right, and I want the half that is not right to be on this landing before she gets here.

"I signed nothing about that row on the twenty-eighth and I was not going to touch it on the thirtieth, and the reason I was not going to touch it is that it is your row and you put it there on the eighteenth and you wanted it noticed that you did it yourself."

"And if I leave it," said Sun Meiling, "then in the morning a woman with a card comes up sixty-one steps and I take it off that wall and hold it in my hands and it says six names and one space, and in the Bureau's intake in Cobb Street there is a sworn declaration signed by the head of that household on the thirtieth of this month that says six members and six names.

"Two documents. One on a wall, one in a file. They disagree by one name.

"And then it is not a preference any more, is it. For a month it was a preference, and I had four hundred words in a queue saying so and a card on that table with my hand at the foot of it.

"From the morning after tomorrow, if that wall says five and the file says six, the woman with the card writes down five. Both numbers go into a district file. And in about six years somebody works out that there is a woman at Ferrier Street who is a member of a certified household and whose roll does not carry her.

"That is how it starts. That is exactly how it started in March of the middle of this year, with a woman whose regularity was in an office because she wrote her own name on a card in four minutes."

She stood up.

"So I filled the row," she said. "In pencil, in my own hand, on the twenty-ninth, at about ten o'clock at night, and I did not ask any of you, and the sentence four feet below the row is nine weeks old and I have been reading it since the tenth of April and I have never once needed it until tonight."

"You did not have to—"

"Do not." She said it hard. "Do not do the thing you did on the twentieth of this month, where you stood on that wall and told the truth about me in front of the room and made it sound like a gift.

"You did not have to. You were going to leave it. And in about nine years that is going to be the best thing anybody did in this house and you were going to be able to say so, and now I have taken it off you, because the roll has to agree with the file or the roll is a lie kept at premises and produced to a stranger on sixty-one steps."

She went past him to the landing.

"And in four days a woman with a card is going to stand in front of that board and read it in the dark, because the light stops two inches above it and has stopped two inches above it every evening since the thirtieth of April, and I have put my name in the two inches of dark on purpose.

"That is the whole of what it costs and I want it written on the wall as well," she said. "Not by you. By me, tonight, with the pencil, where it can be read by anybody who comes up here. Because in eleven years the only person who is going to be believed about this row is me, and I am going to be far too old to remember standing here, and the pencil is not.

"And the thing underneath it is that you did not take it off me. You are going to sign a piece of paper tomorrow and I have filled in a row tonight, and those are two separate things with a day between them, and I have made sure of that, and I have not told anybody which day."

She put her hand flat on the wall four inches below the row, not on it, which is what he does.

"You told me on the seventh of May that a thing done for a door does not get thanked, it gets logged, and there is no book left to log it in.

"There is a book now. It is on the second floor of the Reading and it closes at four this afternoon, and I would like you to take it down there at about half past two and put your name at the bottom of it, and then I would like you to come up these sixty-one steps and tell me it is in."

"It is going to be in."

"I know it is going to be in," said Sun Meiling. "I have known since the twenty-eighth evening. I want to be told."

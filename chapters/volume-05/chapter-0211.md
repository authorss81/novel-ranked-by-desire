# The Monthly Call

The card on the nail in the basement of the Fourth Branch said JUNE. FIRST. EIGHTH. FIFTEENTH. TWENTY-SECOND. TWENTY-NINTH. AND THE FIRST OF NEXT MONTH.

He read it at seven with his coat still on and the kettle going behind him.

Last month's card had ended AND THE SEVENTH OF NEXT MONTH, and he had worked out on the twenty-first, standing on this same floor, that the line was six days out, and he had said so out loud on a landing to six people and had been careful ever since to say it once.

This one was right.

He stood with the kettle going for about four seconds and worked out why. The twenty-ninth is the last Thursday in a month that has thirty days, and the Thursday after the twenty-ninth is the first of the month following, and the card had printed the first of the month following, and last month's card had printed the seventh because that window had never once had the subtraction done at it.

Somebody had done the subtraction this month, and the only difference between the two cards was that there was now one man in this city who had gone and looked.

## The Reading Square

The men were on the wall at nine, eight days after they had last been on it, and he was twenty minutes late to the square and it did not matter, because the men at the bottom of that ladder have never known what time he arrives anywhere.

The bucket was where it belonged this time, in the crate, with the lid shut.

"The first of the month," said the white-bearded man, without being asked. "There are two passes. We come at nine and the monthly figures go up at ten, and we have done the monthly pass before the figures since before either of us was at this wall."

"What does the monthly pass paint."

"Standings." He said it the way a man says a thing that is too obvious to be a subject. "Households with a standing. Number, standing, the date it was certified. The whole column, once a month, and it goes on the board at the side and it is the board the clerks read, not the one the street reads."

Lin Hao looked at the board at the side. It was a long strip of tile and it was about half the length of the wall.

"How many households on there."

"Two hundred and something. It was a hundred and eighty in the spring."

"Once a month."

"Once a month, and it is a number that is findable," said the white-bearded man, getting a brush out of a bucket and not using it. "You did not ask me that. I have been waiting a month for somebody to ask me that and you did not, so I am going to say it and then I am not going to say anything else about it."

The brush went into the bucket and came out again.

"There is a name in the slot," Lin Hao said, "in about six weeks, and you told me on the twenty-ninth and you were right about it, and I have not asked you a single thing since."

"I have noticed," said the white-bearded man.

## Ferrier Street

She came up the sixty-one steps at about eleven and had a docket with her.

"Sixty-one," she said, before anything else. "I counted them, because when a broker enters a security against a building I take the building and not the broker, and the number of steps is in the column."

"Petra Voog—"

"Hanne. Hanne Voog, assessment, nineteen years, and I do the monthly call on securities, and I have been in this building twice before today and both times there was no security against it, and today there is one, and that is the only reason I am on your stairs."

She was about thirty-five and she had a rolled coat over one arm and a docket under the other, and she did not come up past the seventh step, which the household had not asked her to do and which she had decided herself on the way up.

"Marguerite Vale entered an examination against a standing household at this address on the twenty-fourth of last month," said Hanne Voog. "That makes this address a security. It is a monthly call.

"I produce the roll of the persons answerable under the security and I take a copy, and if the roll and the file disagree the security is valued at the lower of the two, and if there is no roll there is no valuation and the entry goes in as unpriced, which is a thing my side of the house will not carry a year running."

Marguerite Vale was in the hall with her coat on.

"Copy the roll," she said.

"It is a pencil board on a landing."

"It is a roll kept at premises and it has no force and you may not value it as if it had," said Marguerite Vale, "so copy it, and then read me what you have, because I have been turning it over since a woman with a docket read my wall this morning and I have got to the part where I do not like it."

Hanne Voog copied six names and a head off a landing in about four minutes, and she copied the seventh row last and she did not ask anybody to hold a lamp, because it was a morning and the stair window at eleven in the morning is as good as any light in this building gets.

She wrote in ink.

Sun Meiling came out onto the landing at about the second name and stood there while the rest of it went in.

"That is mine," she said.

"Yes."

"It is in pencil."

"It is in ink now," said Hanne Voog. "That is the entire difference and it is the only difference, and I have had to sign both sides of it, and if it ever matters, and it will, there are two of me and one of you and the law does not care which of us has more ink."

## The Number

"That is the copy," said Hanne Voog. "It goes in the month book tonight and the month book is read on the fourth and the fourth is the day after the sitting, and I am telling you the reading date because it is on my sheet and I am not going to do this to you twice."

Then she said, "May I say the number."

Marguerite Vale said, "You may not."

"I have to," said Hanne Voog. "It is on the sheet and it is the part you are going to be angry about, and I would rather hand it over on a landing than in a room, because I am about to say something a person has not said to me before in nineteen years and I would like to be able to walk down sixty-one steps afterwards."

Not one of them said anything.

"The examination is secured on the standing of a rank-two broker of the Vant of Brokers," said Hanne Voog. "And it is entered against the standing of the head of that household, which is a man with a plate at 8,138 and no band and a tolerance of three.

"And the value of the book on a monthly call is not what the security is worth. It is what a security can be made to yield. That is the rule and it has been the rule since before the Veil and it is why every broker in this city prices a house low and a woman high."

Not one of them moved on the landing.

"Six names," said Hanne Voog. "One of them is a member of the division that is going to audit the certificate. One of them has an office in this city that pays a district to keep its clock. One of them is a broker who is about to be replaced, and I am not supposed to know that and I have read it in a public return like everybody else.

"And against all six, at the top, a man whose standing is nothing, who is answerable for every one of them, and who has a margin of three."

She put the docket against her arm.

"Six names and a head, valued at the top of a column, and the column does not know any of your names and does not need to. That is what the sixth of June is worth to a book, and it will be worth that again on the fourth of every month until somebody closes the entry, and I have never in nineteen years valued a security that every holder wanted to close."

She went down the sixty-one steps counting them again on the way, because she had said she would.

## After

He came in off the square at half past twelve and Sun Meiling was on the landing with a copy of the copy.

Hanne Voog had left it. That was the arrangement, and it was generous, and Marguerite Vale had negotiated it in about ninety seconds while he was on the square: a copy goes back to the household, and a copy goes in the month book, and the two are compared on the fourth, and the comparison is the valuation.

"She read the wall," said Sun Meiling, "and she wrote it down, and she did not ask me to hold a lamp, and she did not need to, and that is the one thing in this month that has gone the way I said it would and not the way I wanted it to."

He looked at the copy. Her name was at the bottom of it, in ink, in a hand that had never seen her hand.

"That is what the roll is worth now," she said. "That is what it came to. I put a name in pencil in the dark so that the wall and the file would not disagree by one, and I have been sitting on that landing for three days waiting to find out what the agreeing was worth, and now there is a figure for it in a book that a woman is going to read on the fourth."

"Sun—"

"I am not angry with you," said Sun Meiling, which was much worse. "That is the part I would like you to understand and you are not going to understand it tonight. I am not angry with you because you did exactly what I told you to do on the twenty-eighth of May in front of six women, which was to write six.

"I am angry with the fact that I was right. Write six was right. Six on the wall and six in the file and six on a guild's copy with a price against it, and every single part of that was the right answer and it has cost me the only thing I had, which was being a person who could be rubbed out."

She went up.

Marguerite Vale was at the bottom of the stairs with her gloves in her hand and the door shut behind her.

"She priced me," Marguerite Vale said. "Not the household. Me. She priced me at the top of my column and put the whole of the household above me, and that is the first time in thirty years somebody has done that arithmetic in my hearing."

"Was she wrong."

"No," said Marguerite Vale. "She was not wrong and she was not unkind and she did it on a landing because she was frightened of saying it in a room, which I have never once seen a person do and have respected for it before this morning.

"Come up to the rooms at four. Not the Exchange, my rooms, and bring nothing, and do not bring that copy, and I will tell you the one number in that book that is not in hers."

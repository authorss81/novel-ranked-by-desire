# What She Filed

On the sixth of July Ines Duarte went to an office with a door on it in writing with a reason in the way it was done.

It was Thursday and the Thursday crew was at the ranks wall at nine with two ladders and three pots, painting the week off it before the figures went up at ten. The side board stood untouched under its shade with the new schedule on it. The monthly crew had repainted the side board on the first in their own shade. The Thursday pass was a different crew in a different shade and did not touch the side board.

All morning he wanted to ask her which office and which reason. He did not ask. Fear sat in his stomach and love sat directly behind it, and the two had stopped being separable anywhere below the ribs, and he got through the shift by carrying the bucket with both hands.

Seven women on the cut roll ate bread standing round the table before eight. Six of them carried an address at Ferrier Street and six of them carried a notebook, and one coat pocket held a pencil row under twenty-two stitches.

Petra Ilyin tied nothing that morning and was on no roll of any kind. Wren Kallo had gone early with her folder, in no list at any time, the eighth partner. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and had her door open behind her. She had given one true sentence in a pale room on the first and slept behind a shut door once since.

Ines wore her coat with the bag over her shoulder and her hair pinned square. She took the end of the table with both hands on the wood the way she had on the thirtieth and looked at him.

"Be told," she said. "If anything comes to that address with a form number, whoever opens it tells me before anyone discusses it. That stands."

"It stands," he said.

She picked up her bag and went down the sixty-one steps without looking at the nail or the pale rectangle. The house listened to her go.

At ten the audit intake at Cobb Street called her by name to a room with a door on it. The door shut behind her.

The desk held seven papers. The index entry with six on it in the head's own handwriting. The correction of the twelfth with seven on it. The sworn page of the fourteenth with six on it.

The schedule posted on the twenty-sixth with six on it. The certificate dated the twenty-seventh with six on it. The receipt signed on the twenty-eighth. The valuation copy of the first with six names in the broker's own hand.

The division held a true report's worth of paper about her household that she herself had never written.

The officer at the desk set a sheet before her with a form number on it.

"Auditor of record on a certified schedule holding a member's address in the household it certifies," the officer said. "State your review position for the record. In writing. With a reason attached. In the way it is done."

Asking stopped being hers to withhold. Through the thirtieth she had still not asked to be removed and she had not been asked about it. The one removal on the page had taken her off the wrong paper. Now she was asked and the room had a door on it and the question had a form number.

She took the pen. Her hand shook once, then steadied. She wrote in her own hand with the date under it.

She wrote that the index held seven persons of reading age keeping an address at Ferrier Street where the head's sworn answer and the certified schedule said six. She wrote that the seventh returned line had been left blank on the third of last month at ten past seven and counted as a person on the twelfth. She wrote that she was the auditor of record on the schedule and a member of the household on the cut roll, and that she could not certify what she belonged to.

She wrote that the six on the certificate was the head's figure and not the index figure, and that both were on paper with his name at the top of one of them. She wrote the reason attached plainly: a record read correctly in public on the twelfth could not be certified by a woman named in its third line.

It took her eleven minutes. The pen was ordinary. The paper was ordinary. When it was done the division held a report that was true in her own hand for the first time in five weeks.

She blotted it and squared it to the edge of the desk.

"That is my review position," she said. "Filed."

The officer read it twice before she entered it. The second time was slower than the first.

"That is received," the officer said. "You understand what you have done with it."

"I wrote what was on the papers."

"You wrote that the schedule you audit contains a figure the head of the household disputes, and that the index contains a figure the schedule does not." The officer squared the copy before he gave it to her. "A member of a division may not report on a return her own name stands on. There will be an entry saying so, and it will go up with your report, and it will not go up alone."

"Then it goes up," Ines said. "I have spent five weeks being audited by a return I could not read. I would rather be a subject of one than a reader of it."

She took the copy and put it in her bag.

She came home at six and ate a slice standing and went to her room and came out with the bag over her shoulder. The whole house watched because everyone in the room knew what a woman with a bag had already decided.

She stood in the kitchen doorway with nothing in her hands but the straps.

"I filed a report that is true," she said to the room and not to him. "In writing. With a reason attached. In the way it is done. It says the household is seven where the certificate says six and that I cannot certify what I belong to. A division now has to answer for having produced it."

She turned to him.

"I am walking out of a flat I am still in," she said. "My name is on the cut roll with six others. My address is at Ferrier Street with five others. My notebook stays shut in my room with my name on the corner of it. The certificate carries six and I am one of the six. All of that stands. I am still in it on paper. I am not living in it."

He wanted to ask her to stay the way a drowning man wants air. The want filled his mouth and he shut his mouth on it. He felt grief come up through his chest like water through a floor and anger behind it that she had chosen the one day he could not answer.

"Then you are going to your own rooms," he said.

"I am going to my own rooms on Lantern Row," she said. "Two rooms I cannot afford. The only place in this city where a record can be read out loud without being produced to anyone. I will read there. I will not certify here."

She went down the sixty-one steps with the bag over her shoulder. He walked with her to the bottom and did not touch her. Petra stood at the top with her hands behind her back. Runa stood on the stair with her pencil down. Beatrix stood with her good ear to the room. Marguerite sat with her gloves folded.

Tessa stood in the doorway from the market stair. Nadia had her book open and did not write. Sun held her sewn pocket flat under her coat. Wren stood on the step below with ink on her fingers. Agnete stood in the back doorway with her door open behind her.

At the bottom she turned.

"Do not ask me about it," she said. "What is filed is filed. What is left behind is in a division that must answer for having produced it."

She walked toward Lantern Row without turning round. The Thursday crew came down their ladders with their pots and their shade and the wall dried under its paint for another week and the side board kept its number and its schedule in the dark.

He climbed slowly with Petra beside him. The sixty-one steps took longer going up than coming down. Petra counted them under her breath the way she had since the first of last month. At the top the nail held nothing and the pale rectangle held its nine-by-fourteen shape with its cold middle.

Runa met them on the landing with her pencil down and her folder shut. Beatrix stood with her good ear to the stair and her notebook shut. Marguerite sat with her gloves folded and her pen capped. Tessa stood in the doorway from the market stair with her crate at her feet. Nadia had her book open to the back of a page.

Sun held her sewn pocket flat under her coat. Wren stood on the step below with ink on her fingers and her folder shut. Agnete stood in the back doorway with her door open behind her.

He went into the empty room and stood a long time with his hands empty. The bed stood made square. The chair stood straight. The shut notebook lay on the shelf with her name on the corner. The bag was gone. The straps had left two pale lines on the blanket where they had lain.

Petra came to the doorway and stood with her hands behind her back.

"A filed report is a door shut in another building," Petra said quietly. "Eleven years I marked without asking. Three days she sat with a true sentence and then wrote it. That is heavier than marking."

He nodded. The want to say her name had gone up in him twice that day and gone down twice, and the shutting had to hold for as long as she was gone. It would have to hold for longer than that.

Agnete came out of the back room and stood in the empty doorway a while with her cup. The open door behind her and the empty room before her made a line of two open nothings with her between them.

"She gave eleven minutes where I gave one sentence," Agnete said. "Mine sits nowhere. Hers sits in a division. Both were given voluntarily. Nothing in either room compelled them. That is the whole of the discipline and she has learned it without ever standing in my room."

Tessa put her crate down and stood beside Agnete a moment with her hands clean. Two hundred and three a morning stood counted because of lines like the one Ines had filed. The hatch and the report held each other up without touching.

He went up and lay down in his clothes with the head line in the coat pocket, because he had not wanted to take the coat off and could not say why. The house breathed around him with one room empty and its name still on every paper.

He slept after eleven with that empty room in the house, named, waiting for what it would cost.

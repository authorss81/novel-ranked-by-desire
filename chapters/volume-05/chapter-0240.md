# An Office With A Door On It

On the thirtieth he stayed home from the washroom, because Sun Meiling asked him to, and spent the day in the house that now existed on paper.

He had woken wanting to give Agnete one day without anyone at the table talking about what the certificate entitled them to ask. The want was small and he held it all morning. He felt tenderness toward the back room and its open door, and fear for it, and the two lived side by side without moving each other.

At eight Tessa took her entry sheet and went down to the Vestry office to put her clinic into the returned lines. He walked with her as far as the market stair, carrying nothing, because she had asked him on the sixteenth to help with weight and not with paper, and this paper she carried herself. Her hands were steady. Nine years of a quarter of eight had taught them steadiness.

"After today it is counted," she said on the stair. "A hundred and sixty thousand visits a year into somebody's question. I have sat with it since the sixteenth. I am ready."

"You are ready," he said. "Come back for bread."

Petra went with her as far as the market with the bundle under her arm, because Petra was on no roll and had a reason to be on a pavement in the morning. Runa went to Cordwain for the returns hatch. Beatrix rode the yard tram with her notebook. Marguerite stayed at the table with the thirteenth docket, the space at the foot empty, waiting for the first.

Sun sewed a second row of stitches on her pocket and showed him. The white thread stood out against the dark coat. Her fingers were quick. Eleven years of keeping nothing had taught them care with small things.

"Twenty-two," she said. "Eleven was not enough. It gaped."

"It holds now," he said.

"It holds," she said. "A pocket that holds a row holds the house. That is what I tell myself while I sew. Say it back to me."

"It holds the house," he said, and she nodded and bit the thread off.

She laid the coat across her knees and did not put it back on. Six other rows were in six other coats in this building, and two of those coats were on hooks behind doors that stayed open, and one was on the back of a woman who had not gone out of her own gate in eleven years.

"I sew the pocket shut so the row cannot fall out," she said. "I do not sew it shut so that it cannot be seen. If it ever has to come out, it comes out whole. That is the difference and I am the one who knows it."

At ten Ines came into the kitchen with her bag and her coat on and asked for the room. Not the schedule. Not the review. The room. The house gave it. Everyone but him went out to the landing and the stair, leaving the bread and the certificate and the receipt on the table.

Ines took the end of the table with both hands on the wood and looked at him for a long moment. She had read nine sentences about numbers on the twentieth and none about herself, and she kept to it.

"I am going to an office with a door on it," she said. "This afternoon. In writing. With a reason attached. In the way it is done."

He heard her say it in her own words from the ninth of this month, word for word, twenty-one days late and not one word of it changed. She had rehearsed it at that table to an empty room and she had brought it out that morning and put it down flat, the way a woman puts down something she has been carrying up and down stairs for three weeks.

He nodded. He did not ask which office or which reason. Her business was not discussed in that house, and he had kept to it for weeks, and he kept to it now with both hands.

"Be told," she said. "That is the whole of it. If anything comes to that address with a form number, whoever opens it tells me before anyone discusses it. That stands."

"It stands," he said.

She picked up her bag and went out. The door shut without locking behind her. He sat at the table alone with the certificate and understood that she had just told him she was going to do what she had said on the ninth she would do, without saying what it was, and that an institution would apply it to her or not, and that it was not his to decide.

At noon Tessa came back with her copy stamped. Her clinic was in the lines. Nine years of a quarter of eight, two hundred and three a morning, a hatch and a room, counted from the morrow. A returned line was a person as of the twelfth. Her line was a clinic. It would be read as a person anyway, because the office was exact.

"It is done," Tessa said, and sat down suddenly and put her face in her hands for ten seconds, the way Runa had on the division step. Then she stood and went to the hatch.

He had followed her up the market stair because he wanted the ten seconds to be spent on something and not on nothing.

"Nine years of standing in a doorway," she said, "and this morning a woman at a counter took my hours and wrote them into a book because of a line on a form that turned into a person on the twelfth of this month. Not me. Hours. Two hundred and three a morning, and it will be in somebody's question for the rest of the year, and there is no instrument in this city that takes it back out again."

"You said separate was still better."

"Separate was still better than not at all," Tessa said. "I want that on the record, in your voice, at your table, because in about eleven months somebody will ask me why I did it and I will want to remember that I worked it out on the sixteenth instead of in a corridor afterwards."

She went to the hatch and put her crate down where two hundred and three people would pass it in the morning.

At two Agnete came out of the back room and stood in the kitchen doorway with nothing in her hands. The certificate lay on the table. The receipt lay beside it. The copy of Tessa's entry lay beside them. Three papers with the address on them. The house was visible now in a way it had never been.

"There is an office with a door on it in this city," Agnete said quietly, "where a woman can stand and answer in her own discipline, and nothing in the room can compel her beyond it. I have known where it is for eleven years. I have never stood in it."

He looked at her. She was not frightened. She was something beyond frightened, where fright had burned down to steadiness.

"If anyone asks what is at this address," she said, "they will be entitled to ask it from tonight. The certificate is received. I have been behind a door for eleven years. A door that stays open is not a door that hides. It is a door that has not yet been asked to shut."

She went back behind the door and left it open. The door stayed open.

At six the house ate bread standing round the table because there were not enough chairs. Ines came in at half past six and ate a slice and went to her room. Petra squared the bundle for the first. Marguerite capped her pen. Nadia wrote down the day with the hour on it, and which list each paper belonged to, on the back of a page, and read it back.

Beatrix showed him her page before she shut the book. The day in her small level hand. Nothing with a form number. Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. A house that now existed on paper and still ate standing up because there were not enough chairs. He read it and nodded, because a record of ordinary things was the only record left that anyone in the house controlled.

Runa did not show anybody anything, which was how he knew she was thinking about the first.

"Two days," she said, to the bread rather than to the table. "Two days and then a room at the Field division at nine in the morning with a paper in front of me that wants my standing and my instrument and my entitlement, and the standing of this household is yours, and you are a cleaner, and a cleaner's standing does not carry a field operative."

"That is a fact about a household, not about you."

"It is a fact about a household that I have chosen to live in," Runa said, "and there is no instrument I have found in five weeks that lets a woman separate the two, and if one exists I would like it before nine on the first, and if there is not one, I would like somebody to have written down that I looked."

Runa washed the bread knife and put it away. Tessa folded her copy and put it in her coat. Petra tied the bundle string twice. Each small act was exact and complete. The house had learned exactness from the office that held it. It did its own exactness back, in a kitchen, with bread.

At nine he stood on the landing with his thumb in the cold middle of the pale rectangle. The nail held nothing. The paint stayed pale. Below him the city went about its evening with its lamps lit until eleven and its walls exact.

He went in and lay down with the head line and the receipt both in his coat pocket and listened to the building breathe. The schedule was up. The certificate was dated and received. The finding was a division's file. The clinic was entered. The mark would go in on the first at nine, or not, and the plate would come off on the fourth either way, and the hearings were on the first, and Ines had gone to an office with a door on it without saying which.

The house had no roll. The offices had three copies that agreed. The woman the certificate would cost most slept behind an open door on no list at all, and someone, soon, was going to ask her a question she had spent eleven years learning how not to answer, and her whole gift was that a room where she stood could not compel her, and the cost was eleven years indoors, and no instrument in the city could hold her until now.

He slept after eleven with that question in the room, unasked, waiting for its morning.

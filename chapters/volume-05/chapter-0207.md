# Three Grains

Tessa Okonkwo came in at about ten past eight in the morning on the twenty-seventh, which meant she had come off the hatch at a quarter of eight and had done the walk, and it meant there was a smell of the clinic in the stairwell that the rest of that house had learned to breathe through without noticing.

She had been doing the quarter of eight for nine years. Nine years of an hour and a count, because that is the clinician's defence and it is a real one, and in the middle of May a man with a warrant worked out that a quarter of eight every morning in a building with one door is a person, and she refused to name her, and the refusal was correct and it changed nothing at all.

She put her bag down, went to the table, and read the declaration from the foot up, which is the way a clinician reads anything, from the part that cannot be renegotiated toward the part that can.

"That is your figure at the top," she said. "Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight. That is band five and the band above it is six and you are three grains short of the band below your band.

"Read that again with me. You are three grains short of the next band down. That is your entire margin. A standing household is certified at the figure of its head because there is no other figure to certify at, so this household is a band-five household with a tolerance of three, and every one of its six members hangs off that three."

"That is what Doyle told me on the fourth."

"Doyle told you on the nineteenth and I am telling you on the twenty-seventh, and the difference is that Doyle was talking about a permission and I am talking about a body." She put two fingers on the sheet. "Three grains is not a number. Three grains is the width of a plate. And when one member of a household is challenged on her standing, the certification does not fail for that member. It goes into question for all of them.

"In question for how long. There. I can answer that, because I write those letters, because a person whose standing is in question cannot draw on anything the household holds, and I have had to write to eleven families in six years explaining why their card did not work at a hatch in a bad month.

"Fifty-three days, from the fourth of May to a certificate. And for every one of those fifty-three days the certificate does not issue, and the schedule stands, and the sixty days run while it is held, and every day held is a day off the end of the sixty, and the day you signed that sheet on the nineteenth is the day the sixty stopped being a length of time and became a fight."

She sat down, which she did not do.

"Then do not certify six," said Nadia Ferrous. "Certify five and the sixth is not challenged and none of the five is in question over her."

"That is correct," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and I am going to spend the rest of the morning telling you why I would rather certify six and lose the fight than certify five and never be in one."

Beatrix Halm had not come down yet. Sun Meiling was on the landing. Agnete Lindqvist was in the room at the back with the door open, which is where she was in the daytime now, eleven years after a room she had not been outside in.

"Nine years," said Tessa Okonkwo, to the room, and then to Agnete. "Do not let me do that. I am aware of what I have just said out loud in a kitchen."

"I heard it," said Agnete Lindqvist.

"Two hundred and three people come through that hatch on a good morning," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Two hundred and three, and not one of them has a name on anything, and that has been true for nine years and it is the only reason that building has never been in a file. The clinic is unfunded and unentered and a thing that has never been entered anywhere is tolerated, and I have said that sentence out loud in this kitchen twice and both times somebody wrote it down.

"And on the twenty-seventh of June there is going to be a schedule with a head's name at the top of page one and six members on page two, and it is a certified instrument, and it is the first certified instrument in this household's life.

"The moment it exists, somebody is entitled to ask what is at that address. And the answer is a kitchen and six women and a clinic on a floor above a market that two hundred and three people walk through every morning who are not in any of it, because the clinic has never once been entered in anything.

"You want a certificate. So do I. And the cost of the certificate is that the clinic becomes visible the day it exists, and I have not entered it in a piece of paper for nine years and I am not going to do it now, and I would like somebody in this room to understand that those two sentences are both true and that I am saying them at the same time on purpose."

Sun Meiling said, from the stair, "You cannot have it both ways."

"I have never had it both ways," said Tessa Okonkwo. "I have had it one way at a time since the seventh of April and it has been very expensive and I have not once regretted it and I am not going to start on the twenty-seventh."

She stood up and picked up the declaration and turned it round and read her own line.

"There," she said. "Member four. A post at a clinic in the Vestry. No band. No standing entered. The sheet does not record that the clinic has never once been funded, because being unfunded is not a field the sheet has.

"That is a worse line than Sun Meiling's, because Sun Meiling has an instrument and I have a sentence.

"Do you know what Doyle does with a line like that, on a reading, in a room, with a clerk writing it down? He holds the schedule. Not this one, this one's filed. He would have held this one on the fourth of May if the line had been empty and not if it had been a post.

"A schedule with a member whose standing cannot be traced to a document holds. And a held schedule does not issue. And the certificate issues on the twenty-seventh of June on the one you have already filed, and a held schedule sits beside it forever and gets read by the next woman who comes after me."

She put it down.

"Marguerite. You are the bottom of that chain and I have been putting it off for nine minutes because I do not ask a person that at breakfast. Say the answer out loud in front of the table, because the table is going to sign it."

Marguerite Vale took her gloves off.

"In four years the guild replaces me at the annual sitting. The examination passes to whoever holds the book and it cannot be un-entered, and the second line on that sheet says so in the Office's own type.

"As of the twenty-fourth of this month it is in a quarterly return at the Exchange, with my name at the top of the item and the head of that household named in my hand underneath it, and the holder entered as whoever shall be holding this book at the sitting.

"That is the trade and I made it and I priced it on the sixteenth at a window in four minutes and I would price it the same way tomorrow."

"Then you are the load-bearing wall in a house with a three-grain margin," said Tessa Okonkwo, "and you have made yourself so in writing, in public, in a document that a stranger will price in four years."

"Yes."

"I have written eleven letters in six years to families whose card stopped working," said Tessa Okonkwo. "Every one of them explained that a household stands on the highest standing in it, and every one of them was written by a woman holding a document she did not control. You have just saved me the twelfth, and you have saved me writing it to a stranger, and I am not going to be grateful to you and I am going to remember it."

Nadia Ferrous put her hand flat on the table.

"Member five," she said. "A service file, page nine, with a change of shift in it in my own hand, written in ninety seconds at a counter so that it would be my decision and not this house's decision.

"That is a record of where I work. It is not a record of why, and a clerk reading it in nine years is going to see a woman who was posted and not a woman who came, and there is not one hour of work in this city where that difference shows up on a form.

"I have been waiting since the fourteenth of March for a document with my name and my decision in the same place. There is not one. This is not it. And I am not going to pretend to anybody in this kitchen that it is."

"So that is two lines that do not stand up," said Beatrix Halm, coming down at last, "and one that has no instrument at all, and one that has been withdrawn in a room on Lantern Row in a hand I have never seen, and one that is standing in this room arguing with a clinician.

"That is five women and a number. There is a sixth row on that wall with a rule through it and a seventh row with nothing on it, and the seventh row has been empty since the thirtieth of April, and every one of you has been talking about the sixth row for three days."

"So here is the clinician's whole of it, and it is the only thing I know how to say. The certificate does not make this household safe. It makes this household visible. You cannot have a safe house and a certificate, because the second one is a document and the first one is the absence of documents, and this city has been getting better at reading for two years and it does not know how to read a thing that is not there.

"You are going to sign six names on the thirtieth of this month, or five, and I do not know which and I have stopped caring which, and that is the whole change in me between the seventh of May and today and the rest of this room has not noticed it either, including me, until about a minute ago.

"Six keeps the certificate matching the schedule and gets Agnete's question asked by a stranger in a fortnight. Five keeps the certificate clean and puts a sworn head's figure into a file next to the four hundred words she wrote in a queue, and the two documents disagree in public for eleven years.

"That is the trade. There is no third column on this table and there has not been one since the twenty-second of March."

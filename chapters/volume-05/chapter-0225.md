# A Bench Under The Wall

The Thursday pass was at nine and he had walked past the wall at nine every Thursday for five years without stopping, and on the fifteenth of June he stopped at about ten past, because the men had a ladder up against the wall a foot above the standing-household board and were working the tile around where the board is fixed, and the board is painted on the first of the month by a different crew in a different shade and is not theirs.

It took two hours. You cannot repaint a wall around a mounted board eleven feet long without going round it very carefully for two hours, and the crew were doing it with a man on the ground whose whole job was to keep saying the same four words.

"Leave it. Leave it. Leave it. Right, leave it."

He stood and watched for about ten minutes and then went and sat on a bench under the wall, because there was a woman on it.

She had a folder on her knees and her coat folded over the back of the bench behind her, which meant she had been there long enough to have decided she was staying.

## The Counter-Signature

"I countersigned your correction," said Wren Kallo.

He had not planned what he would say and it turned out that did not matter.

"I read it at a quarter to nine," she said. "It comes across my bench at about eight because it alters an indexed schedule and every alteration of an indexed schedule is countersigned before it goes out, and it went out at about ten past nine, and it is in Cobb Street now and the third line of the index has it against your premises."

"You signed a line you did not write."

"I signed a line that was raised by an office against that office's own arithmetic, which is the only kind there is that a person never has to author." She turned the folder round on her knees so the label faced him. "You understand what that means. A correction that goes out against a person carries a requisition number and a name and it can be traced to a man who wanted something. A correction that goes out against an office does not. It has no requisition.

It has no author. It has a finding and a figure and a stamp.

"The only thing on it with a person in it is my name."

She let that stand for about two seconds.

"You cannot unsign it and I cannot hold it. Those are two separate problems and I have had four years of practice and I have solved neither of them."

"How many people know."

"One, since the eighth of June, and I am not going to tell you who, and the reason I am not going to tell you who is not modesty. It is that you would go and find them and there would be two of us on this bench inside a day, and then it would be two, and a thing that is two has a shape, and by the end of the week it would be a thing that half this city holds a copy of."

"You came and found me."

"I came and found you because a woman who cannot keep a thing does not have a choice about it and I am extremely tired of not having a choice about it," said Wren Kallo, "and also because I wanted to look at the wall, and I would like you to understand that both of those were true and that the second one is the one I am going to have to live with."

## The Lists

He looked at the board above her head while she said that.

34-6-118. STANDING HOUSEHOLD. SCHEDULE OF THE FOURTH OF LAST MONTH.

And the name, typed small, at the head line.

"There are three lists in that house," she said, watching him look at it. "Seven women on the roll, and six of them keep a notebook. One woman keeps nothing and is in pencil on a seventh row. One woman is in the house on no roll of any kind and has been since the fourth of June. One woman is the eighth and has never been on anything at any time and does not live there.

And one woman sleeps at the back of it and is on nothing, because there is not one ruled space in this city for her.

"I have read that household out of a file four times in four years and I have got five of the eight and I have never once been given a seventh line for anybody, and on the thirteenth a form came across my bench at about eight in the morning that said the premises answers for seven, and I sat there and worked out that the office had got there by itself, off a line a district officer left."

"You have not asked anybody."

"I have not asked anybody and I am not going to," said Wren Kallo, "because the moment I ask, the answer is a fact about a person, and I have carried facts about people out of rooms before and it goes the way it goes. You know the number it goes as well as I do.

Your woman on the third floor told you the whole of what she felt on the first of the month and she was four days late and a clerk wrote a word on a card about her."

She stopped, and put her hand flat on the folder.

"That was unfair and I am not withdrawing it," she said. "I have four years of it and you have four months of it and I do not get to be proud."

## The Offer

"The board," he said. "You came to look at the board."

"Two things." She held up one finger. "First. I have never seen a standing household's number on that board with a name typed at the head line, and there are two hundred and something lines on it and I have been past here a hundred times, and there is exactly one name on the whole thing and it is a man who cleans washrooms.

Somebody in the Office decided a board that carries a number without a name carries only a number, and that person is right, and I would like it noticed that being right about that is the single most dangerous thing anybody in that building has done in a month, because it is the reason I can find you from a file with one line in it."

"And the second."

"The second is that I am going to come back on the eighteenth," said Wren Kallo. "At night. And I am going to bring this, and I am going to say what I have found in it, and I am not going to do it in that house."

"Whose house."

"Anybody's. Yours has seven women and a notebook and a man at the top of the stairs, and every one of them has been extremely careful for a month, and I am not going to sit in the middle of that and take a document apart in front of them. It is not because they are careful.

It is because I am going to say things about people who are not in the room, and I have watched people get very good at not hearing things, and I do not want to be one of the reasons they had to learn."

She picked the folder up off her knees.

"Night, at the wall, on the eighteenth. Come on your own. Do not bring anybody, because I will not be able to tell you anything I have to tell you if there is a woman on this bench who keeps a notebook, and I would like eleven years of that particular arrangement left where it is."

"Why the eighteenth."

"Because on the sixteenth a page goes into Cobb Street with six on it in the head's own hand," said Wren Kallo, "and on the seventeenth it is indexed, and on the eighteenth there will be a copy in every office that has asked for one, and it will be the first document in this city that says your household is six people and has been checked.

And I want to be standing at a wall when that stops being a thing that can still be argued about, because I have never once seen it happen and I would like to see it before anybody tells me what it means."

She got up and folded her coat and picked up her bag.

"Forty-eight of the sixty gone," she said, without turning round. "Twelve to your certificate. Sixteen to the valuation. And you are going to say those two numbers to yourself every morning for the next twelve days and you are going to get them wrong about twice, and when you get them wrong, that is the day I would think about."

Then she went up the square toward the Reading.

## The Man On The Ground

He sat on the bench for another forty minutes, and then he went over to the ladder.

The man on the ground was about sixty and had been doing it for a long time, and he looked up when he spoke, which very few people at the foot of a ladder ever do.

"How many of those are on the board," said Lin Hao.

"Two hundred and something. Hundred and eighty in the spring."

"How many of them have got a name on them."

"One," said the man. "I asked that myself in April, and there was one, and I have watched it get painted on the first and I have not told anybody I knew the answer before it went up."

"Can a name come off."

"Not with paint." He put the trowel down and wiped his hand on his thigh, which is not a thing you do with lime in your hand and is a thing you do when you want a moment. "Off a wall, no.

You can paint over it, and then it is still there underneath, and there is one woman in this city who can see what was painted over, and she does not work for the Office and she does not work for anybody, and I have seen her work in a yard behind a laundry about two years ago on a whole side of a building.

"The only way it comes off that board is a correction. And a correction on that board leaves a hole, because the book we paint from keeps the history and the wall does not. And there is a hole on that board about a yard long at the low end, and I was not on this crew when it happened, and the men who were still talk about it in the tea hut and not to us."

"Who was it."

"That," said the man, "is the hole, and that is why it is a yard long, and you may go and look at the low end of that board if you have got twenty minutes, and I would go and look at it on my own, because a man who asks about a hole in front of a ladder is a man who has come back tomorrow."

He picked the trowel up and turned back to the ladder.

"You have as long as this section takes," he said. "After that I am on the far end, and you can stand underneath me all afternoon, and I will not see you again."

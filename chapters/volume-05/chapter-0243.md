# What The Book Is Worth

On the third of July Marguerite Vale asked him to come to the Exchange and he went because she asked it plainly and did not dress it as anything else.

He had woken wanting to know what the valuation copy cost beyond the mark. The want followed him down the sixty-one steps and onto the tram. He felt curiosity and fear together and the two steadied each other.

Seven women on the cut roll had left the kitchen in stages before eight. Six kept an address at Ferrier Street and six kept the notebook. The seventh row stayed pencil sewn in a pocket with twenty-two stitches. Petra Ilyin was on no roll of any kind and walked with Marguerite as far as the market.

Wren Kallo was on nothing at any time and was the eighth partner and had gone early to her division. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and her door stood open and she had waved him off with a cup in her hand.

The north desk opened at nine with four people at it and two of them clerks. Kirsten Mahler read back everything that was lodged. The quarterly return stood with the household at Ferrier Street entered against the broker of record. The valuation copy stood beside it with six names in the broker's own hand and the hundred and thirty-third mark at the foot.

Marguerite laid her gloves folded on the counter and set the copy square.

"The assessment side values a security by what it can be made to yield," she said to the clerk rather than to him. "A live account with a full run and a lodged plate behind it yields produce on demand at any valuation. That is what the book is worth this morning. Not the plate. The word."

The clerk wrote the hour under the entry and blotted it. The pen was chained to the desk. The book was chained to the counter. Everything that mattered in that building was chained to something heavier than itself.

He stood beside Marguerite with his hands out of his pockets and his feet planted and understood that the mark at nine on the first had bought twelve months of carrying. The account would not go on the dead list on the fourth. The plate would not come back. The securities would not come off. Beatrix would go into whatever came after the first with the weight still on her hearing.

Marguerite turned to him with her face tired and plain.

"You lodged on the seventh of last month with no right of withdrawal," she said. "I marked on the first with my own hand. Between us we have kept this house live. If you want the dead list on the fourth to free the plate, say so now and I will leave the next docket unmarked. The run breaks. The account goes on the list. The plate comes back."

The offer stood in the air between them with the smell of ink and dust in it. Two officers waited behind them in the queue. The clerk waited with the pen held above the page.

He thought of Beatrix with her notebook shut and her hands folded. He thought of Runa with her pencil down. He thought of Agnete giving one true sentence voluntarily in a pale green room. He thought of Ines eating standing and going to her room without a word.

He wanted the plate back the way a tired man wants a chair. The want was real and he looked at it fully. He felt grief for what keeping it bound would go on costing and anger at having to choose it in a queue with clerks watching.

"No," he said. "Keep it live. Mark the next. I will answer for it."

Marguerite nodded once and capped her pen. The clerk entered his acknowledgment under the valuation with the date and the hour on it. It took eleven seconds. The pen scratched and stopped.

It was done. He had closed the option of letting the account die on the fourth to free the plate and un-encumber Beatrix and take the guild's tail off the fourth page at once. He had chosen the weight with witnesses and in daylight. He could never say afterwards that the book had decided without him.

They rode the tram back standing with the copy between them. The car was full. A guard checked seasons without being asked. Petra met them at the market stair and walked up with them without a word about the book.

He stopped at the hatch on the way up with Marguerite's copy in his hand and set it down out of the way of the morning traffic. Two hundred and three people would pass that counter on the morrow. Tessa's clinic had been counted from the first. Hours and counts stood in the lines where no clinic had stood before. He put his palm flat on the worn wood a moment. A place got smooth by being used truly.

Tessa came down with an empty crate and saw the copy and nodded at it once.

"Then it stays live another year," Tessa said. "My hatch stands counted inside a house that stays bound. I worked it out on the sixteenth. Separate was still better than not at all. Counted is still better than unentered."

"It is counted," he said. "From the first. That stands."

She lifted the empty crate herself and carried it up. He let her because she had asked him on the sixteenth to help with weight and not with paper, and this paper was not hers.

At Ferrier Street Beatrix waited on the landing with her notebook open to a clean page.

"Then it stays bound," she said. It was not a question.

"It stays bound," he said. "I said so at the north desk with the book open. My hearing tail is yours to carry another year. I am sorry for it."

She wrote his answer down in her small level hand and shut the book.

"You looked for an instrument that lets a woman separate a household fact from herself before nine on the first," he said. "There was none. I want that looked for to be written down."

"It is written," Beatrix said. "I looked. That is on the record in my hand."

Runa came in late from Cordwain with ink on her cuff and ate bread with her boots on. Her answer was in. Her hearing had a paper. She sat beside Beatrix and the two of them talked tram talk, seats and seasons and guards.

He sat with them a while with bread in his hand and listened to the talk hold them. Runa's pencil lay down beside her folder. Beatrix's notebook lay shut. Neither reached for either. The evening light laid a thin edge along the table. The certificate and the receipt and the valuation copy lay squared at the far end with space round them.

Tessa came in from the market stair with her hands clean and set her stamped copy beside the three papers without touching them. Four papers with the address on them. Three agreed. One described hours. All were answerable.

"It is a house that exists on paper now," Tessa said quietly. "Nine years my hatch stood unentered because no paper held it. Now it stands counted because one does. I sat with it since the sixteenth. I am ready to be asked about it."

Sun checked her stitches with her thumb and nodded. Twenty-two held the seventh row in her pocket. Nadia wrote the day with the hour on it and read it back. Ines came in at six and ate standing and went to her room. She had not discussed her review and she had not been asked about it.

Wren stopped him on the step after supper.

"Ten days on Hadewig's desk," she said. "Four edits. All removals. It has not been acted on. I count it every night because I cannot stop counting."

"Ten days," he said.

"Ten days," she said, and went in with her folder shut. She had told three people about a thing sworn in a room over four years and watched it travel. This thing she had told to paper and the paper sat. Waiting was a different kind of travel.

He mopped the landing the next morning before work without being asked, working round the nail and the pale rectangle with care. The rectangle stayed nine by fourteen with its cold middle. No crew painted a stair. Sun came out with her coat over her arm and watched him work.

"You keep the wall the office cannot copy," Sun said. "Three papers agree in three offices. The wall agrees with nothing. That is why it holds the house."

He wrung the mop and set it square. The wall dried pale under its unfaded paint. The house breathed around it with its seven pieces and its six addresses and its six notebooks and its one sewn row.

Agnete came out of the back room and stood in the kitchen doorway with nothing in her hands. The certificate lay on the table. The receipt lay beside it. The valuation copy lay beside them. Three papers with the address on them, all agreeing, all answerable.

"If anyone asks what is at this address now," Agnete said quietly, "they will be told six and the answer will be wrong and it will be sworn and receipted and valued. I gave one true sentence in a pale room. It sits nowhere. That is the price of giving it where nothing is written."

She went back behind the door and left it open. The door stayed open.

He went to the landing rail and stood with his hands flat on it a while, looking at the pale shape. Twenty-five days of it since the fourth of last month, and then eight more since the cutting. The shape had outlasted a schedule, a certificate, a receipt, and a valuation. Only the wall the office could not copy had held that long.

Petra came out with nothing in her hands and stood beside him.

"You keep it live another year," Petra said. "I will be at the bottom of the steps for every marking. Ten to nine with the bundle. That is my standing now. On no roll and at the foot of it."

At nine he stood on the landing with his thumb in the cold middle of the pale rectangle. He had signed a receipt on the twenty-eighth with a blank line for a remark and left it blank. He had acknowledged a valuation on the third with no blank line at all. Both were in his own hand. Both closed an option.

He went in and lay down with the head line and the acknowledgment both in his coat pocket. The book was worth a word said four times a year by whoever held it. The word was produce. The book held it. He had told the book to keep holding it.

He slept after eleven with that word in the room, entered, waiting for the fourth to prove what keeping it live had cost Beatrix.

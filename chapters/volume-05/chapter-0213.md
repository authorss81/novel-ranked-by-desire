# The Woman In The Doorway

She was on the bottom step at ten past seven in the evening, which is earlier than the district has any business being on a landing, and she had a card in her hand and the card was the reason and she said so before she came up the first step.

"Six names," said Marit Kolb. "Nil spaces. I have been up them twice to count them and both times I have come up them again at a different hour, because a roll that reads different at eight in the morning and at eight in the evening is a roll that somebody is keeping right, and I would like to know which of the two times is the honest one."

"It is the evening," said Sun Meiling.

"Then that is what the card says, and I have written the hour." Kolb came up eleven steps and stopped at the twelfth, which is where she had stopped on the twenty-second of last month. "Thirty-six of the sixty. Twenty-four left. I print it because it is printed on this side and I am not going to pretend that a form cannot have my habits on it."

She put the card against the rail and looked up the last six steps at a wall she could not quite see the bottom of.

"Who filled the seventh row."

"I did," said Sun Meiling.

"In pencil."

"In pencil, on the twenty-ninth of last month, in my own hand, with the date under my name."

"And it is in the dark and I have had to come close to it." Kolb did not make a note of that. "Under the same heading as the rest of you?"

"It is under the same heading as the rest of me."

"Then it is a member," said Marit Kolb, "and I am entering it as one, and I want you to understand that this is the only reason I am not standing here for eleven minutes asking you why a member of a certified household wrote her own name on a board in pencil instead of on a form."

"You may ask me."

"I may not," said Kolb, "because the answer is not mine to hold. I write down what a roll says. What a person meant by a pencil is a matter for an office with a door on it and a window on it, and this is a landing."

## The Premises

The fourth column of her card was the premises and it had been the fourth column since the third of May, and she asked it the way she always asked it, standing where everybody could hear her.

"How many persons of reading age keep an address at these premises."

The room did not answer.

It was not silence. Sun Meiling had her mouth shut and Runa Sato was at the table and the oven was on and Nadia Ferrous was halfway up the stairs with a plate in each hand, and the room did not answer, and something about the shape of the not-answering was different from a room choosing not to answer, and Marit Kolb turned round slowly and looked at the room at the back where the door was open.

"Ma'am," she said. "I am going to ask you again, and I am going to ask it to the whole room, because if it is the room I have to know it is the room and if it is a person I have to know it is a person."

The room did not answer again.

"That is the fourth time in nineteen years," said Marit Kolb, to the air in front of her, "that a room has not answered me, and I have written on three of the cards that I put the question twice and got nothing, and I have never once written down what it was."

Then she said, "How many."

"Six," said Sun Meiling. "Six women have kept an address at this address since the twenty-second of March and the answer is six and it has never once been wrong."

"That is not what I asked and you know it is not," said Kolb, "but I am going to write six, because six is the answer to the column, and the column is mine, and I have been at this nineteen years and I have never once written into a district card that a room would not tell me what was in it."

She wrote six.

## Agnete

The woman in the back room had been standing in the doorway for the whole of it and had not moved, and Kolb looked at her for about as long as she had looked at the wall.

"You are at this address."

"I sleep at this address," said Agnete Lindqvist. "I do not keep an address here."

"Then I cannot enter you and I want to be exact about why, because I am going to write down that I met you and I do not want the line to lie." Kolb turned the card round and showed her the fourth column, which was empty and which she had just written six in.

"This asks how many persons of reading age keep an address. It does not ask how many are inside. There is a column for the number and there is no second column, and a woman who does not keep an address is not an omission in my book, she is the correct application of it."

"How long have you been inside," said Sun Meiling.

"I have been in this building eleven years," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I have never been outside it, and I would be obliged if you did not write that down, because I have had one document in my life in eleven years and it was not about this."

Kolb had the pen up and then she put it in her pocket, and it took her about two seconds and it was the only two seconds in nineteen years anybody at the Fourth Branch had ever seen Marit Kolb decide not to finish a column.

"I am not going to write it," she said. "I am going to write that the fourth question was put twice and the premises answered six, and I am going to leave the rest of this line blank, and I have never left a line blank in nineteen years and I would like it noticed."

"It is noticed," said Agnete Lindqvist, "and I would like the noticing to stop."

At the top of the stairs Ines Duarte had come down four of them and stopped and had said nothing at all, and she was not going to be asked about it, and nothing in this room was going to be asked of her tonight.

## At The Door

Kolb went down eleven steps and stopped at the twelfth and turned round.

"One thing, and then I am not coming back up them until the twenty-sixth."

"Say it."

"The roll and the sworn answer are now the same statement in two places." She had the card flat against her knee and she was not writing anything and she was not going to. "A roll is nothing. It is a pencil board in a hallway and it has no force and no office has to accept it. But a roll that says exactly what a head's sworn declaration says, on the same day, about the same people — that is not a roll any more. That is a copy.

"And a copy can be produced out of the file without anybody walking up sixty-one steps. Anybody holding your head's declaration in a Bureau building can take six names off page two and say they are the roll, and they will be right, and they will not have been in this house in their lives."

"That is the fourth question," said Sun Meiling, "and it came off the same sheet under the same heading."

"It is the fourth question," said Marit Kolb, "and it is mine, and the other three are the district's."

She put the card inside her coat.

"The schedule goes up on the second floor of the Reading on the twenty-sixth and the certificate is dated the twenty-seventh and I am going to be on this landing at ten past seven on the twenty-sixth, and I am going to come up those last six steps and read that row in the dark like everybody else in this city will read it, because there is nothing else for me to do at ten past seven in the evening and there is a wall."

"You could bring a lamp," said Nadia Ferrous, from the stair.

"I could bring a lamp," said Marit Kolb, "and then I would not be writing down what the light does on that landing, and the light has been doing the same thing to that row since the thirtieth of April and that is the only reason anybody at the district knows about it yet."

She went down the sixty-one steps and counted them on the way up and not on the way down, because on the way up she had been working and on the way down she had been thinking about a woman in a doorway who had told her not to write a number down.

## After

Sun Meiling came down about ten minutes after the door had shut and sat on the bottom stair with the lamp behind her, which put her face in its own shadow and the seventh row in its own light.

"She came up eleven and stopped at the twelfth both times," she said. "Twelve steps off sixty-one. That is her system and I have worked it out from two visits, and I have not told her, and she has not asked whether anybody noticed."

"She asked about the row."

"She asked about the row because the row is a member, and a member is a question she is obliged to enter. She came up eleven steps to ask it. Then she left a line blank for a woman in a doorway instead, and she will go home tonight and think about that line for a long time."

"She did not ask me to hold the lamp."

"She did not ask you to hold the lamp because she did not have to," said Sun Meiling. "Three in the morning in the first week of the month, and a stair window eleven feet off a landing. She could read every word of it and she read it from about four inches away, and she did not put one hand on the wall, and she did not ask anybody anything about me."

She got up and went to the wall and put her hand flat four inches below the seventh row, not on it.

"That is three," she said. "A district officer, a guild, and an office two floors above a washroom. On the twenty-ninth of last month it was one.

"And in eleven years there will be a woman writing this household's record down from one of those three, and she will not have been in any of our mouths, and she will be perfectly right, and she will be wrong about the lamp."

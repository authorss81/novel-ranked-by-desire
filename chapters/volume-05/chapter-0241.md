# The Second Reading Room

He had slept with the receipt and the head line both in his coat pocket and had woken wanting to be at the bottom of the sixty-one steps before Petra needed him to be anywhere else. The want steadied him. He felt dread in his stomach and put bread in his mouth against it and the dread stayed where it was.

Tessa left for the hatch with her copy stamped. Her clinic had gone into the returned lines on the thirtieth and was counted from the morrow. A returned line was a person as of the twelfth of last month. Her line described hours and counts and it would be read as a person anyway.

At nine the house held its breath without agreeing to.

He stood in the chop-house doorway with his hands in his pockets and listened to the hour go by on the market bell. The bell struck nine and the sound went across the roofs. Somewhere above the Exchange a pen moved in a chained book or it did not move. He could not hear it. He stood with his thumb against the head line in his pocket and waited for the sound to finish.

Petra came down at half past nine with her hands empty and her face plain.

"It is in," she said. "In ink. In her own hand. At nine."

He nodded. Relief went through him and left his knees weak. The thirty-year account would not go on the dead list on the fourth. The plate would stay where it had been lodged on the seventh of last month. Every security standing against it stayed where it was on the day it would have come off.

"Then the plate stays," he said.

"It stays," Petra said. "A hundred and thirty-three marks in eleven years with no gaps. The book keeps it. The house keeps the weight."

The side board at the Reading Square was repainted that morning by the monthly crew. The crew came before the figures with their own pots in their own shade and painted the old schedule off the board.

They painted the schedule of the twenty-sixth in its place with the number under it. The pass over the ranks wall was a different crew in a different shade and did not touch the side board. The landing at Ferrier Street kept the bare nail and the nine-by-fourteen rectangle and no crew painted a stair.

Runa came back at noon with her folder thinner and her face set. Her hearing had taken the paper and kept it. She would not say beyond the morning and she was not asked to. Beatrix came back at one with her notebook shut and her hands steady. Her panel had asked the question about the cab and the deaf ear and had written her answer down. She would not say beyond the morning and she was not asked to.

At two a clerk came up the sixty-one steps with a slip for the head and would not give it to anyone else. The slip carried a form number and an hour on it.

It asked for one person of reading age keeping an address at Ferrier Street to attend in the second reading room at Cobb Street at four to answer what was at that address. It named no woman. The certificate lay in the next house on the kitchen table and did not come in. From the signature on the twenty-eighth the household was findable without being looked for.

Agnete stood in the kitchen doorway with nothing in her hands and read the slip over his shoulder.

"That is mine," she said. "Eleven years and it has my shape on it without my name. I will go."

"You do not have to go alone," he said.

"I will not go alone," she said. "You will walk with me to the door. You will not come in. A room where I stand is a room where testimony cannot be compelled. That is my discipline. I will stand in it."

The second reading room at Cobb Street was narrow with one window high up and a table set square under it and two chairs facing across it and a clerk's desk to the side. The walls were washed pale green. The light fell in a shaft and laid a bright edge along the table. No bolt showed on the door. No pegs stood on the wall. No coat hung anywhere.

The clerk at the desk read the form number aloud and set it down.

"Agnete Lindqvist," the clerk said. "Keeping an address at Ferrier Street. Eleven years indoors. The instrument certifies six. The return of the third of last month left a line blank. The index counted seven. The correction of the twelfth raised seven. The sworn page of the fourteenth answered six. Which is true."

Agnete sat with both hands flat on the table and looked at the shaft of light and not at the clerk.

"A room may not take what is not given in it," she said. "That is the first thing in my discipline. Silence is an answer when the room would compel it. Speech is an answer when the woman gives it. I have kept silence eleven years because every room I stood in would have taken it."

The clerk waited with the pen held above the page.

"What is at that address that is not on the certificate," the clerk said. "Answer."

It was the first time in eleven years somebody had asked her a question she could not decline. The certificate entitled the office to ask. The address was on it. She was at it. She sat with her hands flat and her back straight and the light on her wrists.

He waited outside the door with his shoulder against the wall and heard nothing through it for a long time. The corridor smelled of soap and paper. A bell struck the quarter somewhere below. He kept his hands in his pockets around nothing and wanted her to have the room to herself and to have him at the door at once.

Inside Agnete lifted her hands from the table and set them down again a finger's width apart.

"I am at that address," she said. "I keep it. I am on no roll and on no schedule. I was the blank on the third. The officer left the line for me at ten past seven and wrote six in the foot of the column. The office counted seven. Both are true. The line was mine and the figure was his. I have slept behind an open door eleven years because a shut door would have been a testimony I did not give."

"State your name for the record," the clerk said.

"Agnete Lindqvist," she said, and said it clearly into the room, with her hands flat, "keeping an address at Ferrier Street, on no list, by my own keeping, and I give this sentence voluntarily and nothing in this room compelled it."

The clerk wrote it down. The pen scratched once and stopped.

"That is one true sentence," Agnete said. "My discipline knows what it costs to give one. It costs being findable. I have paid eleven years to be unfound. I will pay this to be found."

She stood. The chair scraped. She came to the door and opened it herself and stood in the corridor with him with her face wet and her hands steady.

"I gave it," she said. "One sentence. Voluntarily. Nothing in the room took it."

He walked her down the stairs and out into the daylight with his hand under her elbow and did not speak until they were past the market stair.

At Ferrier Street she went to the back room and stood in the doorway a long time looking at the open door and the bed and the cup on the shelf. Then she turned to him.

"I want you," she said plainly. "I want you tonight in my room with the door shut for the first time in eleven years. I want it because I gave a sentence in a room this afternoon and I am still standing. I am not frightened of being compelled. I am frightened of being left alone with being findable. Stay with me."

"I want you," he said plainly. "I want to stay with you tonight and to shut the door because you asked it shut and not because any paper asked it. I want it awake and willing and with nothing taken."

She nodded. Consent stood between them like a table squared under a window.

They ate bread with the house standing round the table. Petra came down from the mark with the bundle string in her pocket and nothing else in her hands, and was on no roll of any kind. Wren came late with ink on her fingers, and was on nothing, and was the eighth partner, and sat on the step. Agnete stood in the kitchen doorway with her cup and watched the table being used. Ines ate and went to her room. She was not asked about anything.

After eleven the house settled. Agnete took him by the hand into the back room and shut the door herself. The shut door clicked. Eleven years the door had stood open. The click sounded loud in the small room.

The room held a bed made square, a shelf with a cup, a window with a blind half down, a chair with her shawl folded on it. Daylight had gone. A small lamp burned on the shelf. The walls were close and clean. Nothing in it belonged to any office.

She stood with her back to the shut door and looked at it.

"Eleven years open," she said. "Tonight shut because I shut it. That is the difference and I am the one who knows it."

She came to him and put her hands flat on his chest the way she had put them flat on the table that afternoon, a finger's width apart, and held them there.

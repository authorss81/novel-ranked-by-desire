# Named In A Third Line

The finding went in on the twenty-third at ten in the morning, and he was mopping the second floor corridor of the Fourth Branch when it happened and knew nothing about it until the evening.

He had woken wanting to know what Wren Kallo's paper would do once it left her bench, and there was no address to write to and no counter to ask at, because counter-forensics read the history of a record and not the record, and a man could not walk into that room and ask to see the shape of his own edits.

The want sat in him all morning alongside the shame.
He worked the washrooms and changed the paper and wiped the tile, and at ten he was on his knees at the second basin on the left, which drained slow, with his hands in the trap.

At six he came home and found the kitchen full and Wren Kallo gone from it. Beatrix was at the table with her notebook open, writing the morning tram in her small level hand. A season checked four times without being asked. A guard who knew her seat. She wrote it because a record of being looked at was still a record, and hers was the only notebook in the house that kept the outside.

"You saw her," Beatrix said without looking up. "This morning. On the step."

"I saw her," he said. "She sits on a step that is not in any file."

"Good," Beatrix said. "A step is not a room. Let her have it."

Sun Meiling had it out before he had finished wiping the tile, because she had been at it since five and had it in a shape by six, and she said the shape out loud in the corridor while he worked, which was a thing she had started doing and had not told anybody she was doing.

"She sent it," Sun said. "A clerk at Cobb Street saw her go in with a folder at ten and come out without it. It is on the head's desk. From tonight it is a division's file about a man whose record has only ever been edited downward."

Petra Ilyin sat with her bag squared against her feet. She had carried the fourth page to Cobb Street on the sixteenth and it had gone into the index on the seventeenth, and she knew what a counter did with a paper.

"What does the head do with a finding like that," Runa asked.

"She reads it," Petra said. "The head of counter-forensics is a woman called Hadewig Mers. She has been in that division since the Veil broke. She does not hurry.

"A finding about four removals and no addition in five years is a question about who has had access," Petra said. "The first thing she does is pull the index entry that has six on it in his hand. The second is pull the correction with Wren's name at the foot. The third is to ask nobody anything for three days."

"Three days," Sun said. "The schedule goes up in three days."

Marguerite Vale looked up from the thirteenth docket on the table. She held the book that would take the hundred and thirty-third mark on the first of next month at nine in the morning, and Petra would stand at the bottom of the sixty-one steps at ten to nine with a bundle under her arm.

"Then we have three days where it sits," Marguerite said. "A paper that sits is a paper that has not answered anybody yet."

Ines Duarte came in at seven with her coat on and her bag over her arm and listened without sitting down. She had not asked to be removed and had not been asked about it, and the notice of the ninth with her name in the third line was on the table under a salt dish.

"What is on the finding," Ines said to the room. "Exactly."

Sun told her exactly, as Wren had told it on the eighteenth at the wall. In March a figure off a band with a tolerance. On the seventh of April a line out of an instrument column, a whole form struck through for a half form. On the twenty-seventh of April a quantity detached from an account into a place with no name. On the nineteenth of May six lines onto a declaration, every one somebody else.

"And no addition," Ines said.

"No addition in five years," Sun said.

Ines stood for a moment with her hand flat on the table. She was the only person in Ostara who could read a record correctly when the record was her own household's, and she had read nine sentences about numbers the morning before and none about herself.

"Then it is about him," she said. "Not about the household. Keep it about him."

She went to the room she slept in and shut the door without locking it. The kitchen was quiet.

He felt anger then, low and unfamiliar. Not at Wren, who had done her work as she had to. Not at the head who would read it. At the shape itself. Four times something had been taken out of his life and filed away from him, and he had never once been told, and the only person who had seen it was a woman who could not keep a thing and had carried it for four years because there was nothing else to carry.

He wanted to stand in front of the head's desk and ask who had held his file. There was no instrument for asking. A return could have a line struck through, and striking a line was the only question the city asked for free, and there was no line to strike here. There was only a history.

At eight there was a knock on the door at the bottom of the sixty-one steps, and Beatrix went down with her notebook and came back up with a slip.

"District bag," she said. "For the head. No form number."

He opened it at the table with the whole house watching. It was a copy of the correction, countersigned, with Wren Kallo's name at the foot, and a line typed across the top in the Office's type. Produced from the index on the eighteenth. Seven persons where the head's answer said six.

"This is the eighteenth's copy," Sun said. "It was filled on the seventeenth and copied on the eighteenth. That is the date Wren named at the wall."

"Why send it to him," Runa said.

"Because the index is the only complete version," Petra said. "And because from the seventeenth every office that asked has one. Someone wants him to know what they hold."

Nadia wrote it down in her book and then rewrote it, because in that house a sentence that could not say which list a figure came off was a sentence that got torn up and started again. It took her most of a minute and she did it twice.

At nine Agnete Lindqvist came out of the back room with a cup and stood in the doorway. She had slept in the building for eleven years and was on no list of any kind, and the door stayed open.

"You are quiet," she said to him.

"I am thinking about a desk," he said.

"Do not go to it," she said. "A desk that has just got a paper is a room where nothing can reach you yet. Wait until there is a body sitting in front of it."

He nodded. Do not go to a desk that has just got a paper. She had spent eleven years learning the difference between a room that could take testimony and a room that could not, and she was handing him the whole of it from the doorway without ever coming into the room herself.

Before that, at eight, Tessa had taken him aside on the landing with her hands still smelling of the hatch. Nine years of a quarter of eight had taught her to say a hard thing plainly and then stand beside it.

"My entry goes in after the schedule," she said. "Runa is right. If my clinic sits with your six in the same office on the same days, the office reads them together. After, it reads them separate. A clinic is not a person, and I know it, and the office will know it, and separate is still better."

"You decided on the sixteenth," he said.

"I decided and I have not done it," she said. "Waiting is part of doing it. Tell Runa I heard her."

She went back down to the market with her crate. He watched her go with affection that had nothing to do with medicine. She had examined her own rib and found nothing and written it down, and she was frightened, because a clinician who could not find the thing was a clinician who had been treating a symptom, and she had decided to be counted anyway.

Petra stayed late at the table squaring the copy to the edge and squaring it again. She had carried the fourth page to the counter on the sixteenth and had not slept well after it, and the finding going in brought it back.

"The clerk did not read it back," she said to him quietly, while the others washed cups. "He read the figure and put a date against it and pushed it under a grille. I have been an officer for eleven years and I have never been so frightened of a counter. A counter that does not ask is a counter that has already decided what you are."

"You carried it because you are on no roll," he said.

"I carried it because I could be on a pavement in the morning without belonging anywhere," she said. "That is still true. It will be true on the first at ten to nine. Hold to that."

At ten the house settled. He lay in his room with the head line in his coat pocket and listened to the stair light go off. The finding was on a desk now. It would be read by a woman who had been in her division since the Veil broke. In three days a schedule would go up. He slept late with the anger still in him, and it was still there in the morning, low and steady, waiting for a person to stand in front of.

# The Best Room In Ostara

Her rooms were on the third floor above the Exchange and they were the best rooms he had ever stood in, and she had made sure of that on the first of April and she had never once mentioned it since.

There were two chairs. There was a table with nothing on it. There was a window with the shutters at half height and a plate under the sill that he had always assumed was a plant and had once, in his third year, decided was a small brass rule.

"You have come at four," said Marguerite Vale, "which is early, and you have come without the copy, which was the condition, and you have come without asking me a single question in the street, which I would like on the record before I start, because it is the only professional courtesy anybody has paid me this month."

"Sun Meiling says you have a number for me."

"I have a number for you and a number against you and they are the same number, and that is what I have been sitting here since half past one trying to make smaller."

She put her gloves on the table and did not sit down.

"Sit down. I am going to take four minutes and then I am going to stop talking and you are going to have to do the rest of it yourself, and I want you on your feet for the first part, because I am going to say something unkind."

He stood.

"That examination was entered on the twenty-fourth of last month and I entered it because a house is not seizable," said Marguerite Vale. "That is not a figure of speech.

"I have been in thirty years of this guild and I have found the two things in Ostara that no court will put a hand on. One of them is a house and the other one is a household's own good name.

"There is a third one that I had in my hand on the seventeenth of last month and did not buy, because buying it would have cost me four years and a sentence and I would have had to be a person who could afford it.

"On the second of June I found out that I had entered a security, and on the fourth of every month a woman comes up sixty-one steps and reads six names off a pencil board in the morning light and writes them into the month book in ink.

"And I have made six named women into the most liquid thing in a book that has been dead in eleven places since the nineties, and I did it in four minutes at a window, and I am the senior broker of the Vant of Brokers and I did not see the shape of it, and I have been sitting here since half past one being angry about that and not about anything else."

"That is a long time to be angry about."

"It is a long time to be angry about because every time I stop I start looking for the place where I was right, and it took me until about three o'clock to accept that there is no such place in this month." She put two fingers on the table.

"So I am going to give you the number and then I am going to give you the thing underneath the number, and then you may go back down sixty-one steps and be angry at whichever of us you prefer, and I am telling you now that the row you are about to read has your name on it, and you have earned it."

"Say it."

"The book on transfer is worth more with the examination in it than the examination is worth." She said it flatly. "That is not a scandal, that is the definition of the trade. A book is not worth what the securities in it are worth.

"A book is worth what a man with four guilds and one afternoon will pay to be handed it in four years' time, and what he will pay for is the ability to produce six names, one head, and a standing, on demand, at his desk, without a court, without a hearing and without any single person's agreement.

"And that figure is for the book. What a book is worth is what it can be made to yield, and the yield on a security like that one is not a rent and it is not a dividend. It is a word.

"Four times a year a person with a desk says produce, and the broker of record produces, and what comes off that desk is six names and a standing and a date. That is what a woman in Ferrier Street is worth before the sitting happens. Not what she is worth. What she is worth when somebody says the word."

"Then what is she worth when somebody says it."

"Six names at the top of a column and one at the bottom with a number beside it." She said it without any weight at all.

"And I have priced all six of them together and I am not going to price them one at a time for you, because you would ask me which of them is the cheapest, and I would have to answer you, and then you would spend a month doing something about the cheapest one, and it would be the wrong thing, and I would have handed you the instrument for it."

"You said this afternoon you would make me do the rest of it myself."

"So I am going to hand you the thing to be angry about now, and it is not me, and you are not going to be able to leave this room having been angry at me." She put both hands flat on the table. "You are the reason it is priced. Not me. Me I have been arguing with since the seventeenth of last month and I have had thirty years to lose arguments with.

"You put a plate on a piece of paper on the thirtieth, and a woman with a docket copied six names off a wall on the sixth, and now the top of that column is a man who cleans six floors with a margin of three.

"If you held a band, if you were band five with margin on it, this entry would be worth about a third of what it is worth now, and I would not be standing in this room at four o'clock on the second of June.

"Do you understand what I am telling you? In thirty years I have never once had a man with nothing at all make better security than a man with something. I have spent my whole life learning the price of every thing in this city, and you are the best collateral I have ever entered in that book, and it is because you are worth nothing."

"That is a strange thing to say to a man in a room."

"It is the only sort of thing anybody in my profession says to a person in a room," said Marguerite Vale. "The alternative is that we all go home and pretend it is a compliment, and I have watched two senior brokers do that in my time and one of them died still doing it."

"And you are the standing," said Marguerite Vale. "You are the entire collateral. Eight thousand one hundred and thirty-eight grains and a tolerance of three, and there is not one grain of margin in that figure for anything that has ever gone wrong in this city, and it is the only thing in that column that can be produced without anybody having to agree to anything at all."

She sat down.

"So there is the number. Nine years and four months, in my own figure, and I would take eight, and I have had a professional opinion about this since eleven o'clock this morning and I have not shared it with one living soul, and I am giving it to you in a room with the shutters at half height on the third floor because I am still me, and I have not got time to be anything else this month."

He did not say anything.

"You can see it," said Marguerite Vale. "You can see the whole of it, and you can see it faster than any man I have ever priced, and that is why I am saying this to you and not to your head of household, because your head of household is you, and you have known that for a month."

"Say the underneath."

"That is underneath the number."

"Say it anyway."

She looked at him for about three seconds.

"Thirty years," said Marguerite Vale. "There is an account in the ledger on that desk that was entered against the broker of record in the year the Veil broke and it has never been discharged and it has never been written off, and the security standing against it is not a building and not a plate and it is not a firm.

"It is a person. It has been a person for eleven years. That person is entered against this account on the first of every month in a book that this house keeps and that I keep, and I have never in eleven years written a name against the line, and there is a name against it and it is not mine and it is not yours and it is not one of the six."

"Who is it."

"I am not telling you today," said Marguerite Vale. "I will tell you on the fourth, and you are going to hate it, and you are not going to be able to move a thing about it, and I have decided this afternoon that you are not going to be told any earlier than that."

She got up.

"You certified six women and a number on the thirtieth and I entered it on the twenty-fourth and a woman copied it on the sixth.

"And out of those three facts there is now an account in the ledger I keep that has been dead for thirty years, standing up on its own two legs. It is standing up because of you. It will still be standing on the day that woman called Rask buys that ledger, and she will not be reading about six women. She will be reading about six names and a standing and an entry that has come off the dead list by itself."

She picked up her gloves.

"Do not come here on the fourth," said Marguerite Vale. "On the fourth you will be up sixty-one steps and so will I, and there is a woman in a room in this building that neither of us has been in for eleven years, and I have been telling myself since eleven o'clock this morning that what happens in that room is mine and not the guild's and that is not true and I knew it was not true before I finished the sentence."

## After

He did not go down the sixty-one steps for about ten minutes, and then he did.

Sun Meiling was in the kitchen with the copy of the copy under her elbow, and she did not look up, and what she said was:

"Four o'clock in her rooms, and you came back before the lamp came on, and she has told you a number."

"She has."

"Then she is going to take it back on the fourth of every month," said Sun Meiling, "and I am going to be standing in a room when she does it, and I am the only one of the six that copy carries with nothing under my name at all, and a blank in a copy is a question that somebody is going to ask me in four years in an office I have never been in."

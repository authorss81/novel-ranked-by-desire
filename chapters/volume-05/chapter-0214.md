# A Promise With No Maker

She came up the sixty-one steps of the Exchange building at four o'clock and stopped in a corridor with a docket in her hand and did not look at it.

"I am Petra Ilyin," she said. "Guild enforcer, eleven years an officer, and I have been in that room since about eleven this morning. I would like you to come and look at a door."

He looked at the door.

It was a door in a wall on the third floor, and it was eleven feet by seven behind it, and it stood open. The thumb-turn was on the inside and it was turned, because a collecting room's thumb-turn is always on the inside, and it had been open all day, and she had been in it all day, and she had eaten a plate of bread at some point because the crust was on the table beside her.

"You are looking at the open part," said Petra Ilyin. "Look at the thumb-turn. I have had my hand on that for about two hours and I have not turned it, not once, and it would turn if I turned it, I have watched it turn for other people."

She said it the way a field clerk says the time.

"Go on," she said. "Say the thing you are going to say, and then say the one after it, which is the one you actually want to say, and do not bother with the first."

"You cannot leave a room."

"I cannot be the first one out of a room," said Petra Ilyin. "That is the whole of it and it has taken you about nine seconds to get it and I have been getting it wrong for eleven years. I can leave. I can leave eleven times out of twelve.

"On the twelfth my body takes me back to the table and I sit down, and I do not remember doing it, and I have never once been frightened by it, because it does not feel like anything. That is the part I have never once had believed when I have told it to somebody.

"It feels like putting a cup down."

Marguerite Vale was in the corridor with her coat on and a book in her hand with a chain going from the back of it into her coat.

"Petra," said Marguerite Vale.

"Do it now," said Petra Ilyin. "You said on the second and I have been sitting here for two days waiting for you to come up and do it, and I have eaten your bread and I have read your window and I would rather it was done before four o'clock than after."

"Do not do this in a corridor," said Lin Hao.

"Then come in," said Petra Ilyin, "because you are the head of that household and I have three questions for you and I am going to ask them in a room and not on a landing."

## Three Questions

She had a docket open on the bolted table with four ruled lines on it, and she wrote the date and the hour at the top of it before she asked anything, which told him that this was the first half of a form she had filled in a great many times.

"Question one. How many persons of reading age keep an address at those premises."

"Six."

"Write six," she said. "I am not asking you to swear to it. I am asking you to say it out loud because I write what I am told and I do not write what I think."

He said it out loud and she wrote it.

"Question two. Are the persons named in the roll kept at those premises the persons the security is entered against."

"They are the same six."

"Say the second half."

"They are the persons the security is entered against," he said, "and there is a seventh row on that roll with a name in it in pencil, and the seventh row is one of the six, and if that is what you are asking me to be exact about, then no, the roll and the security do not agree exactly, and the difference is a pencil mark and a date and a woman called Sun Meiling who wrote her own name in the dark on purpose."

Petra Ilyin wrote that down. It took her about forty seconds and she read it back twice.

"That is the first true thing anybody has said to me about it," she said, "and I am going to write your name at the foot of it and it is going to be the only name on this docket, and you are going to have a problem in about four hours because a docket with a head's name on it is a document about a household and that is the whole reason your morning went the way it did."

"Question three."

"Question three is the one I cannot answer and I am going to ask it anyway." She put the pen down. "Who holds the account."

Nothing in the corridor moved for about four seconds.

"Me," said Marguerite Vale, from the doorway. "I hold it, I have held it for eleven years, and I swore the oath, and I made a woman of thirty-four swear to me in a room in this building and I have let her sit in it for eleven years and I did not tell her."

Petra Ilyin did not move at all.

"I was twenty-four," she said.

"You were twenty-four and I was the broker of record and you had come up sixty-one steps of a different building with a docket for a debt that could not be paid in coin, and I could not pay it, and I could not write it off, and the guild could not carry an account with nothing against it past a year.

"So I took an officer instead of the money." Marguerite Vale's voice did not change at all, which was worse. "A sworn collector holds until the account is discharged. It is the oldest security in the guild and it is the only one that costs the debtor nothing and the creditor everything, and I have been the creditor for eleven years and I have never once come up here.

"And it is the reason that account is on the dead list at all," she said. "Nineteen years of it were carried on money. Then it was carried eleven years on a woman, and there is no instrument in this guild for putting a woman back where she has been taken out of it."

"You could have come up here," said Petra Ilyin.

"I could have," said Marguerite Vale, "and I did not, and we are not going to spend this afternoon on that, because you have been in a room for eleven years and I have not and one of us is the criminal and it is not going to be settled in a corridor."

"I am going to release it," she said. "An oath of holding is discharged by the holder and I am the holder and it takes one line and I am going to write it in the real book tonight and the pen is chained to it, and I will tell you what it costs and then I will do it."

"Tell me."

"The account is entered against me," said Marguerite Vale. "It has been since the year the Veil broke. It has been in that ledger for thirty years, it has never been discharged and it has never been written off, and the security standing against it was a person and the person was you.

"In about four seconds that security becomes nothing, and an entry carried eleven years on a woman comes off the list at the next valuation, and a woman called Rask buys that ledger and reads a live unsecured account in my own name in my own hand.

"That is the price. I have had it since eleven o'clock on the second and I have not been able to write it, and it is eleven years of you on a chair in a room that costs me eleven years of not being able to say no to a woman I have never met."

Petra Ilyin stood up.

She got as far as the door.

She got three steps. She put her right hand out and it touched the frame of the door on the inside, four inches above the thumb-turn, and it stopped there the way a hand stops on a wall, and she stood in the doorway of a room on the third floor of the Vant with her hand on a doorjamb for about nine seconds while everybody in the corridor watched a woman fail to leave a room that was standing wide open.

"Say it out loud," Lin Hao said. "Whatever the rule is, say it out loud."

"An oath of holding," said Petra Ilyin, with her hand on the frame, "is not a bar. I want that said in a corridor in daylight because I have heard the sentence a hundred times and it is always said as a bar. It binds my body and it binds nothing else.

"It has never once stopped me doing anything at all. I have eaten, I have read, I have answered three questions and written them down. It has never stopped me doing anything except coming out of a room I am the last one in.

"And I am not the last one in here today," she said, "which is why it is not holding, and I would like somebody to understand that I have never once known that before and I have just worked it out standing in a doorway."

Marguerite Vale came in and took the chained book out from under her arm and put it on the bolted table and opened it and wrote one line in the left-hand column, and it took her about eleven seconds, and she did not stop to check it, and Lin Hao understood that she had written it out in full somewhere in the last thirty years and was copying it.

Petra Ilyin came back into the room.

"Eleven years," she said. "And that is the last of it. I am discharged. I have never been discharged from anything in my life and I have been an officer since I was twenty-two and I have never had a discharge and I have never wanted one until about four o'clock this afternoon."

"What do you want," he said.

"I want that written down somewhere by somebody who is not me," said Petra Ilyin. "And then I want to be finished. And it is not him, and I would like that said too, because everybody in this building has been looking at the door since the fourth of May."

She sat down on the edge of the bolted table.

"I have got a room I can leave," she said. "I am going to find out what that is like for about an hour. You may stay."

## The Hour

She did not close the door. She said she was not going to close it and she did not, and when he came into the room properly she was sitting with her back to a door whose thumb-turn she had not been able to turn a minute earlier and her boots were already off, folded one on top of the other, because she was a person who took her boots off when she meant to stay.

"Now," said Petra Ilyin. "I am going to do this the way I do everything, which is going to sound like something it is not."

"You are going to tell me what things cost."

"I am going to tell you what things are, in the only vocabulary I have got, and then I am going to stop using it, and if I start again you are to put your hand over my mouth, and I will not mind, because that is the first instruction I have given anybody in eleven years and I want it on the record that I gave it to you and not to him."

She looked at Marguerite Vale in the doorway. "Go and stand somewhere else. Not because of anything. Because a woman in a room with a chain on her is a woman being held and I have been held for eleven years and I would like one hour of it not happening."

Marguerite Vale went out and shut the corridor door, not the room door.

Petra Ilyin took the coat off the peg and laid it along the bolted table and then folded it in three and put it on the peg again, because she was not finished with it, and came back with nothing in her hands.

"Right," she said. "You are band five with a tolerance of three and six women are standing on you, and a woman on the fourth floor of this building has a debt with my name on it that is about to go live.

"And before the end of next month you are going to be standing in a room on the second floor of the Reading with a certificate on a desk and six names on page two of it. And you have not slept properly since the nineteenth of last month.

"That is the inventory. That is what I do. I take stock and I say it out loud so that the thing is in the air and cannot be got back into a drawer, and I have never once had to take stock of a man before, so forgive me if I am slow."

"Take as long as you like."

She came at him from the side and got a hand behind his head, the way you take hold of a thing you have decided the price of, and then she stopped and put her other hand flat against the front of his shirt and left it there.

"Stop," she said.

He stopped.

"I am not going to be collected tonight," said Petra Ilyin. "I have spent eleven years being the instrument that takes a thing off a person and I am telling you, before anything, in my own voice, that I am not here because you owe anything, and I am not here because Marguerite Vale has put me in a room and the door is shut, and I am not here because I have nothing else to do with a body until the middle of next week.

"I am here because the room is open and I have never been in one with a man before and I would like to find out whether I can stand being wanted, because for eleven years the answer has been no and it has not been a sad answer, it has been an arithmetic."

"Say yes and stop talking," he said.

She said, "Yes," and pulled his shirt out of his trousers with one hand and then took her own hair up off her shoulders and held it, which was the only concession to the room she made all afternoon.

Afterwards she lay along the bolted table with her boots on the floor and one arm over her eyes and counted nothing, because she had no reason to count, and when he asked her what she was thinking about she said, "Eleven years," and then, after a while, "The peg."

"The coat."

"The coat is on that peg and it has been on that peg since the day I came up here, and I could not take it with me, because you cannot leave a room in a coat you may need again in four hours, and so for eleven years I have been coming back to a room in the same coat.

"And in about ten minutes I am going to walk out of this room in nothing but a shirt and I am going to keep walking, and I want to tell you that I have never once done that, and that the whole of what I am frightened of is not you and is not the account."

## The Break

She got up at about six and put her boots on and did not look at the peg.

Then she stopped, and went back, and took the coat off the peg, and hung it up again, and stood looking at it for about two seconds, and did not touch it.

"That stays," she said.

"You will want it tomorrow."

"I have wanted it every morning for eleven years," said Petra Ilyin, "and it has never once been for the weather."

She walked to the door and out of it and down the corridor and did not stop, and she did not touch the frame, and at the top of the sixty-one steps she said a name out loud. It was the first time in four hours she had said one and the corridor heard it.

"Lin Hao."

"Yes."

"That is the first time in eleven years I have said a man's name in a doorway and left through it," she said. "I do not know what it means. I am going to go and find out and I am not going to pretend that I am going to sleep."

# The Quarterly Return

The Vant's quarterly return went in on the twenty-fourth of this month, six days before she had to, because Marguerite Vale had decided on the fourteenth of March that she would never leave a filing to the last day of a month and had not gone back on it in nineteen years.

She went at nine with the book under her arm and did not tell anybody she was going, because she did not want the room with her, and because she had priced the morning before she left the house and had put a figure on it and knew what the figure was for.

The Exchange opens to the public at nine and the north desk is where a broker lodges what she has, and the desk reads it back to her while she is standing there, and there are four people at it and two of them are clerks.

A return is due on the last working day of a month and every broker in this city lodges one on the last working day and not one of them lodges one early, so a woman who lodges six days early has that desk to herself.

The clerk was a woman of about thirty called Kirsten Mahler and she had been at the north desk for two years and she read everything back.

"Right," said Mahler, turning the second leaf. "Page three. Securities standing against the book's entitlements, as at the date of lodgement."

"There are two."

"There is one. Stand in front of me while I say it back to you, because if it is wrong I have typed it and it is in the book before either of us knows it.

"Item. A standing household, premises in Ferrier Street. Members six. Entered against the broker of record in this return, and not against the account of the head of it, and I want to be sure I have got that the right way round."

"That is the right way round."

"That is an unusual way round."

"It is the only lawful way round," said Marguerite Vale. "The examination is entered against me. The head of that household is band five and holds nothing that can be entered, and where an examination is not entered against a standing it is not an examination, it is a letter."

Mahler typed it and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"And the head has a name."

"The head has a name," said Marguerite Vale, "and I am going to say it once and I am not going to be dramatic about it, because it is going into a public book and it is the reason the line underneath says what it says."

Mahler had the pen out.

"Lin Hao," said Marguerite Vale. "No band. No standing. Head of the household by an assumption signed at a counter on the nineteenth of this month, and the schedule of record is the certification schedule of the fourth, and it is in the Reading under a tray on the second floor, and you may go and read it if you want it, and I would advise you not to, because it is a four-page form and it does not repay the walk."

Mahler wrote it.

"And that is the first time in nine years a man with no band at all has had his name entered in that book, and I entered it, and I put it there on the afternoon of the seventh of this month at a window, and the reason it is in there now instead of then is that I have taken four days to decide whether I could live in a house with it in there.

"I can. That is the answer to the only question anybody in that house is going to ask me about it, so let us get it said and go back to the column head."

"Value," she said.

And this was the moment, and Marguerite Vale had known it was the moment since the sixteenth of this month standing at a window on the second floor, and she had not known on the sixteenth what she would say.

"Read the column head again."

"Value to the book." Mahler's finger was on it. "That is not what it says. It says value to the book, on transfer. Which is a different number from value to the book, held."

"Then put down whatever your column takes and I will correct you if it is wrong."

The clerk typed for a while and then read it back, and the clerk's voice did the thing that clerk's voices do and went flat at the end of the second line.

"Open-ended," said Mahler. "Annual examination, renewable, no fixed term, and the line underneath says that on the death, resignation or replacement of the broker of record the examination passes to the holder of this book."

"That is the standard second line and it is the one that frightens me."

"Then I cannot leave it off."

"You cannot leave it off and you cannot enter a holder," said Marguerite Vale, "because the holder of that book on the annual sitting is a woman who does not exist yet, or a man who does not exist yet, and I have been trying for about four years to think of a way to write the word whoever and I would like you to type it and I would like it to be typed badly."

The north desk on the twenty-fourth was quiet. Two clerks and a man filing a lien and a woman reading back.

"It will not take whoever," said Mahler.

"Then take the sentence."

Mahler typed for about a minute and a half and turned the leaf round.

"THE HOLDER OF THIS BOOK ON THE ANNUAL SITTING, WHOEVER THAT IS. There."

"That is exactly what I said and it took you ninety seconds and I could not have done it in nineteen years," said Marguerite Vale.

"Your receipt," said Mahler. "Lodged the twenty-fourth, read back at the north desk, initialled by me."

And then she said, "May I ask one thing."

"You may ask me one thing and I am not obliged and I will answer it about a third of the time."

"The value to the book. Held. Against transfer." The clerk looked at the leaf and not at her. "That is not a value anybody in this building is going to be asked for. That column gets used once every eight or nine years and it is used when a book changes hands, and when it is used it is used by the person buying."

"Then put down what you would put down if somebody asked you tomorrow."

"I would put down a rank," said Mahler. "A rank two bought an open-ended security out of her own entitlement, three days after she bought it out of her own entitlement, and lodged it in her own book. An open-ended security with no named holder has one buyer and it is whoever takes the book next, and it is worth about what a rank two is worth to a person who has never had one.

"That is the number and I am not going to say it out loud to you because I am a clerk and you are a broker and you will do the sum yourself before you are out of the building."

Marguerite Vale did the sum before she was out of the building.

She did it on the step outside with the receipt in her hand and the book under her arm, standing in a street with trams on it, and she did it out loud, because she has a habit of about thirty years, and because the step outside the Exchange is the one place in this city where a woman can stand and be counted up without a single person in the queue objecting.

"A rank two book is worth a rank two to whoever inherits it, plus what the book holds. That is the whole of how a guild prices a succession and there is not one line in the rule book about anything else.

"I have put an open-ended security into my own book with no holder named on it. Whoever takes the book on the annual sitting inherits the standing of the highest member of a household of six women in Ferrier Street, and the examination that goes with it, and the liability of being named on it.

"That makes my book worth more to her and worth less to me, by about the difference between a rank two and nothing, and I have known the sign of that number since the seventeenth of this month and I have been carrying it about for three days telling myself that the difference was small.

"The difference is not small. The difference is four years."

She stood on the step for a while longer.

And the thing that was new — the thing she had priced correctly on the sixteenth and had not allowed herself to look at since, because she was a woman who looked at things — was not the number.

It was that in eleven years there would be a woman at that desk reading a lodgement receipt and a book and a line, and the line would say that a person she had never met holds the examination of a household she had never entered, and the woman at the desk would have no way at all of knowing what it was worth to the woman who had written it, and would price it anyway, because that is what a desk does.

She put the receipt in her coat.

She had a copy of the indemnity in that coat already, dated the seventeenth, and she put the receipt beside it, and the two pieces of paper were the same shape and the same weight and about four months apart in her life.

She came up the sixty-one steps at about one in the afternoon and put both on the kitchen table and told the room what she had done in about ninety seconds, in the flat voice of a woman who is not going to be thanked and does not want to be.

"Half of this city could not tell you what a Vant is," said Beatrix Halm. "And a quarter of it could not tell you what a security is."

"The Exchange reads everything," said Marguerite Vale. "The Exchange does not have to understand it. And neither does a desk at the north window, and it will be read every quarter for as long as there is a household at that address, and one day a woman will be sitting there who has never heard of any of you and she will read my handwriting and price it against my successor.

"That is not a risk. It is a line item. It is the first line item in nine years that I have entered in that book against myself and I entered it in my own hand and initialled it myself and I would do it again on the twenty-fourth of next month."

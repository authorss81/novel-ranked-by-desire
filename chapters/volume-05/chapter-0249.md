# The Report That Is True

On the ninth of July he went to Lantern Row at seven in the evening because Ines Duarte had left word with Petra that she would read to him if he came alone.

He had woken wanting to hear her voice in a room without a form number on it. The want followed him through the washrooms all day and would not be set down. He felt grief and hunger together and under them a steadiness he had not felt since the third.

Nothing had been said at Ferrier Street about where she slept, and this was the household's doing and not hers. Seven women on the cut roll had carried their pieces out in seven pockets all week, and every one of them had carried the same seven names and the one thing they did not have, which was a woman at the table who could read what any of them had done.

Six of them had a door number and six of them had a notebook, and the seventh thing in the house was a pencil row under twenty-two stitches. Petra Ilyin, who was on no roll of any kind, had brought the word to him that morning.

Wren Kallo, the eighth partner and in no list at any time, had gone that morning to ask Hadewig Mers what a division does with a file held sixteen days. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and stood in her doorway waving him off with dry eyes and the door wide.

Lantern Row ran two floors above a laundry, eight feet wide, with a stair that smelled of soap and heat. Her two rooms stood at the top. A washstand took one wall and a single bed ran along the other under a window that gave onto the street with the shutter open on its hinge.

A chair sat in the window with her coat over the back of it and a low stool beside it. A worktable stood against the partition with a cup, a folded cloth and nothing else on it.

No lamp burned. Her bag stood on the floor by the stool with the straps squared under it. The copy of the true report lay on the worktable squared to the edge with her name at the top of it and the date under her name, and the cup stood clear of it.

She stood with her back to the open window and her hair down and her coat off and looked at him a long moment.

"You came alone," she said.

"You asked alone," he said.

"I did, and I want to be clear that I asked it because I want you in my own rooms and not because any paper wants you here. My rooms are not your household. My name is on your roll and my address is at Ferrier Street and my notebook is shut on a shelf in an empty room there. All of that stands. I am still in it on paper. Tonight you are in mine."

He took the coat off the chair and hung it on the hook behind the door where there was only the one hook. The room had one chair and he sat on the stool, which put him lower than her and gave her the window.

He told her about the day to fill the room with ordinary things before the night took it. The washrooms floor by floor. The boiler chased on the fourth floor. The side board in the monthly shade with the schedule of the twenty-sixth. The Thursday shade fresh beside it from the sixth.

The hatch running two hundred and three through the morning. The dead list quiet on the fourth. The finding sixteen days on Hadewig's desk that morning when Wren went to ask. He laid each fact down plain and square the way clerks squared frames.

She listened with her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging loose between them. Four years she had held records in her head until her own memory paid for the retention. Tonight she held nothing but his voice.

"You keep the ordinary record," she said. "Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. Beatrix taught you that. It is the only record left that anyone in your house controls. Keep it."

"I keep it," he said.

She told him what the division had said to her in the room with the door on it, which he had not asked her about and she was telling him because she had chosen to.

"They have written that I may not report on a return my own name stands on," she said. "It goes up with the report. It means that in about a month I can be put at a desk where I am the subject instead of the reader, and I will not be able to refuse it, because refusing is what people do who are still on a roll."

"How long can they hold you off the work?" he said.

"Not long. A fortnight is the ordinary answer to a question like that, and I have not got a fortnight." She looked at her own hands. "I cannot pay for two rooms above a laundry out of what they will allow me to answer. That is the whole of it, and I am telling you because you will hear it from the market first."

"Then you have this," he said, "and you will not pay for it with anything you have to answer for."

"You will put that in a room somewhere." She almost smiled. "You will put it in a room and it will be true and it will cost you a figure."

He stood and came to her. The street noise came up through the open shutter and went away again.

"The division holds my report three days," she said. "Eleven minutes in my own hand with the date under it. It says the index holds seven where the certificate says six and that I cannot certify what I belong to. That is filed and it is not tonight."

She put one hand flat against the partition and looked at it, and then took it off again, and then put it back.

"Tonight is what I left behind in a flat I am still in," she said. "A bed made square. A chair set straight. A notebook shut with my name on the corner of it."

She looked past him at the open shutter and the street going along under it.

"A woman who can read any record in this city, five weeks being read by one of them," she said. "I want one night where I am not an auditor of record and you are not a head. I want you in my bed in my own rooms with the shutter open and the street going past, because I want the world to keep going while it happens."

"I want you," he said plainly. "I want you tonight in your rooms on your terms, awake and willing and with nothing taken. I want to be read by you and not produced to anyone."

She nodded and did not put her hand on him yet. She took a cloth off the worktable and wiped the one cup dry, and set it down, and looked at the place she had set it.

"You asked me once what a room can take off a person," she said. "Tonight I am the room. Nothing gets taken out of you tonight that you do not hand over. That is the whole of what I can promise and I am promising it before anything starts."

She went to the window and pulled the blind down three inches and left the shutter open, and turned the key in the door, and put the key on the sill where he could see it.

Nothing was taken from either of them. Everything was handed over.

She drew him up off the stool and kissed him standing in the draught from the street. Her mouth was soft and sure. Her hands left his face and went to his shirt and undid it button by button down the front. She had read nine sentences about numbers on the twentieth of last month and none about herself. Tonight she read him with her hands and every sentence was about herself.

He pulled his shirt over his head and dropped it on the floor where there was room for it. She opened her own blouse and let it go and stepped out of her skirt. She was slender and pale with a narrow waist, her breasts small and high, dark hair neat at her thighs. A faint line marked her left side where she had once held a record too long. She stood in front of the open shutter without putting her hands anywhere.

"That is all of me," she said, "and you are not certifying it."

He looked. He set his hands at her waist and brought her in against him. Her skin was warm from the window. She made a sound low in her throat and pushed into him.

They went down onto the bed under the window. She pulled the blind down the rest of the way and left the shutter open, and the street came through in bars. The bed smelled of soap and of her. The rooms were loud with the laundry below and the street beyond.

She parted her thighs and took him in her own hand and seated him herself, unhurried, with her eyes on his face the whole way in.

"Go on, then," she said. "I want to feel all of it and I want to be the one who says when."

He pushed in. Her breath went out of her in a short sound and she took him deeper, both hands at his back, heels finding him. He worked into her with long strokes and she worked back, matching him and then outlasting him. The bed knocked the partition. Somebody in the laundry below knocked a pot.

She talked to him throughout in plain words.

"Deeper. There. Do not stop. I am slick for you and glad of it. Four years I owned being in no book. Three days I own being in two at once. Tonight I own being glad in my own rooms and I am not going to apologise for the grammar of it."

He quickened. Sweat came off his chest and ran down onto her. She got a hand into his hair and pulled his mouth to hers, open and wet and unhurried, and her legs locked at the ankle behind his back.

She came first with a cry she put into his shoulder, all through, her inside clamping and releasing round him. He stayed buried and did not move through it, holding her at the hips and letting it take its own time. Then she pushed him over herself and stayed with him while it went, one hand flat on his sternum and her eyes open the whole way through it, watching him.

They lay a long time with the bars moving across the ceiling.

She put her hand flat on his chest over his heart and left it there.

"My report says the household is seven where the certificate says six," she said. "My body says I am yours where no paper says it. Both are true. The first sits in a division that must answer. The second sits in my rooms where no division can produce it. That aftermath is mine and I will inhabit it."

He held her while the light went off the window. The laundry below shut its doors. The street quieted to one cart.

"I am still in your flat on paper," she said. "I am not coming back to sleep in it. I will sign for the household when signing is what it needs and not before. I will read to you here when reading is what you need. That is my standing and it is not on any roll."

He nodded with his cheek against her hair. He had wanted rank nine to open a file since the first month he had this address. Tonight he wanted the woman in his arms to stay in her own rooms and to let him visit them. The second want did not cancel the first. It priced it.

"One thing more," she said, and sat up to get it before the room got cold. "Read me nothing while I am in this room. Nothing with a number on it, nothing with a date under it, nothing you have brought from a landing. I have read all of it and I cannot put any of it down again."

"That is easy."

"It is not easy for you and we both know the price of it." She pulled the blind up off her knees. "Paper is how you hold a house. Paper is the only thing that has ever made this household stand up in a room. If you read to me in here then I am a room that holds papers, and I have been one of those my whole life and I have had four years out of one."

"Then I will read to you on the landing and not in here," he said. "The landing is mine and it is not held by anything. That is what I have got."

She put her hand out and he shook on it, in the dark, in two rooms above a laundry, with the street still going past outside.

She told him about the three days alone in the rooms before the night took that too. The key turned at night from the inside. The shutter opened and shut on its hinge. Reading done aloud to an empty room to hear whether her own voice still held. Bread eaten standing at the worktable. All of it borne alone and now laid down in front of another person.

He told her about the house without her in return. Bread standing round three papers. An empty room swept and swept again. A rail oiled. A hatch carried. All of it done without her and kept for her.

She got up and put her clothes on and gave him the floor of the room to dress on, and he understood the arrangement without being told it.

She unturned the key and opened the door herself and stood aside for him. The corridor smelled of soap and heat. He went down the stair with her eyes on his back.

He stopped at the stair foot and looked back up once. She stood in her doorway with the street behind her and her hair down and one hand on the frame. The true copy lay squared on the worktable behind her with her bag beside it. Three days filed. One night given. Both true. Both hers.

"I will read to you here when reading is what you need," she called down. "Go up and keep the house that is still yours on paper. I keep my rooms. You keep the landing with its nail and its rectangle."

He walked home past the Board with the paint still readable under the lamps and the walls exact. The ranks wall stood dark above its Thursday paint. The side board kept its number and its schedule in the dark.

He climbed the sixty-one steps without counting them and put his hand flat on the pale rectangle on the landing, which was cold in the middle in the way it had been cold every night since the fourth of last month, and then he went in.

At Ferrier Street the house breathed around an empty room with its name still on every paper. Seven pieces sat in seven pockets. Six addresses stayed. Six notebooks stayed. One sewn row stayed. One woman on no roll stayed. One woman on nothing stayed. One woman on no list stayed behind an open door. One woman stayed in her own rooms on Lantern Row with a true document behind her and a door she had opened herself.

He went in and lay down and could not sleep for a while, and got up again, and took the paper off the table at the far end of the kitchen where the three of them lay squared, and put it in the drawer under the bread, and shut the drawer, and stood in the dark kitchen with his hand still on it.

Three papers that agreed with each other and not with the index. A household he had promised a woman he would not read them to.

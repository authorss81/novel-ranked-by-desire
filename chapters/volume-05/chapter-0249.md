# The Report That Is True

On the ninth of July he went to Lantern Row at seven in the evening because Ines Duarte had left word with Petra that she would read to him if he came alone.

He had woken wanting to hear her voice in a room without a form number on it. The want followed him through the washrooms all day and would not be set down. He felt grief and hunger together and under them a steadiness he had not felt since the third.

Seven women on the cut roll kept their pieces in seven pockets. Six kept an address at Ferrier Street and six kept the notebook, one shut in an empty room at Ferrier Street. The seventh row stayed pencil sewn in a pocket with twenty-two stitches. Petra Ilyin was on no roll of any kind and had brought the word that morning.

Wren Kallo was on nothing at any time and was the eighth partner and had gone to Hadewig's desk that morning to ask what a division does with a file held fifteen days. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and her door stood open and she had waved him off with dry eyes.

Lantern Row ran two floors above a laundry, eight feet wide, with a stair that smelled of soap and heat. Her two rooms stood at the top with a window over the street and a table set under it and two chairs facing across it and a bed made square behind a curtain. The walls were washed pale yellow. The evening light fell through the window and laid a warm edge along the table.

No lamp burned yet. No papers lay on the table. The copy of the true report lay squared on the side desk with her bag beside it.

She stood with her back to the window with her hair down and her coat off and looked at him a long moment.

"You came alone," she said.

"You asked alone," he said.

"I did, and I want to be clear that I asked it because I want you in my own rooms and not because any paper wants you here. My rooms are not your household. My name is on your roll and my address is at Ferrier Street and my notebook is shut in an empty room there. All of that stands. I am still in it on paper. Tonight you are in mine."

He sat down in the chair she pointed to. The chair was plain wood with a rush seat. The table between them held nothing but a cup of water and a folded cloth.

He told her about the day to fill the room with ordinary things before the night took it. The washrooms floor by floor. The boiler chased on the fourth floor. The side board in the monthly shade with the schedule of the twenty-sixth. The Thursday shade fresh beside it from the sixth.

The hatch running two hundred and three through the morning. The dead list quiet on the fourth. The finding fifteen days on Hadewig's desk that morning when Wren went to ask. He laid each fact down plain and square the way clerks squared frames.

She listened with both hands flat on the table and her eyes on his face. Four years she had held records in her head until her own memory paid for the retention. Tonight she held only his voice.

"You keep the ordinary record," she said. "Bread. A hatch carried. A wall looked at. Beatrix taught you that. It is the only record left that anyone in your house controls. Keep it."

"I keep it," he said.

She stood and came round the table. The evening light laid its warm edge across her shoulders.

"The division holds my report three days," she said. "Eleven minutes in my own hand with the date under it. It says the index holds seven where the certificate says six and that I cannot certify what I belong to. It is true. A division that produced both must answer for having produced them. That is filed and it is not tonight."

She came round the table and stood before him.

"Tonight is what I left behind in a flat I am still in," she said. "A bed made square. A chair set straight. A notebook shut. A woman who can read any record in this city and has spent five weeks being read by one. I want one night where I am not an auditor of record and you are not a head. I want you in my bed in my own rooms with the door shut because I shut it."

"I want you," he said plainly. "I want you tonight in your rooms with the door shut at your asking. I want it waking and willing with nothing taken. I want to be read by you and not produced to anyone."

She nodded. Consent stood between them like a table cleared of paper.

She shut the door herself and turned the key herself. The key sounded loud in the small rooms. Three days she had lived behind it alone. Tonight the shut door was hers to open from the inside.

She came to him and put both hands on his face the way she had once put both hands on a table end, and held him there and looked at him.

"Speak my name," she said.

"Ines Duarte," he said. "Keeping an address at Ferrier Street and living in your own rooms on Lantern Row. On the cut roll with six others. Off it in body since the sixth. Still in it on paper."

"Say your own," she said.

"Lin Hao," he said. "Head of that house. Findable without being looked for since the twenty-eighth. Answerable for six where the index says seven."

"Then we stand named," she said, "in my rooms, by our own mouths, and no clerk is writing it down."

She drew him up out of the chair and kissed him standing in the warm edge of the window light. Her mouth was soft and sure. Her hands left his face and went to his shirt and undid it button by button down the front. She had read nine sentences about numbers on the twentieth of last month and none about herself. Tonight she read him with her hands and every sentence was about herself.

He drew his shirt over his head and set it on the rush seat. She undid her own blouse herself and let it fall and stepped out of her skirt. She was slender and pale with a narrow waist, her breasts small and high, dark hair neat at her thighs. A faint line marked her left side where she had once held a record too long. She faced him bare and did not cover herself.

"Look at me," she said. "Three days alone with a true copy on a side desk. Look and do not certify."

He looked. He set his hands at her waist and brought her close. Her skin was warm from the window light. She made a sound low in her throat and pressed herself to him.

They stretched out on the square bed behind the curtain and she drew the curtain shut herself. The bed smelled of soap and of her. The evening light came through the curtain weave and laid lines across them. The rooms were quiet except for the laundry below and the street beyond.

She parted her thighs and settled him between them. She took him in her own hand, steady and unhurried, and seated him at her wet entrance.

"Now," she said. "Slow. I want to feel you come into me and to say yes to it. I have said no to every room in this city for three days by saying yes on paper. Tonight I say yes in my own bed."

He sank into her slowly. She was hot and tight and wet around him. She caught her breath and gripped his shoulders, rising to meet him. He worked in her with long deep strokes, feeling her clasp and release and clasp. The bed creaked under them. The curtain swayed on its rail.

She spoke to him throughout in plain words.

"More," she said. "There. Do not stop. I am slick for you and glad of it. Four years I owned being in no book. Three days I own being in two at once. Tonight I own being glad in my own rooms."

He quickened. Sweat gathered on his chest and dropped onto her breasts. She caught his face in her hands and kissed him through it, open-mouthed and hungry. Her legs locked around his back. Her heels pressed into him.

She peaked first with an open cry, shaking all through, her inner muscles throbbing round him. He stayed buried deep and motionless through it, feeling each throb, steadying her by the hips.

Then he let go inside her with a groan, pushing deep and staying there while pleasure rolled through him. She held his face through it and looked into his eyes and did not look away.

After they lay joined and cooling, the shut door beyond the curtain and the true copy squared on its desk past that.

She put her hand flat on his chest over his heart.

"My report says the household is seven where the certificate says six," she said. "My body says I am yours where no paper says it. Both are true. The first sits in a division that must answer. The second sits in my rooms where no division can produce it. That aftermath is mine and I will inhabit it."

He held her while the light went off the window. The laundry below shut its doors. The street quieted.

"I am still in your flat on paper," she said. "I am not coming back to sleep in it. I will sign for the household when signing is what it needs and not before. I will read to you here when reading is what you need. That is my standing and it is not on any roll."

He nodded with his cheek against her hair. He had wanted rank nine to open a file since the first chapter of his life in this city. Tonight he wanted the woman in his arms to stay in her own rooms and to let him visit them. The second want did not cancel the first. It priced it.

They dressed slowly in the darkening rooms. She unturned the key herself and opened the door herself. The corridor smelled of soap and heat. He went down the stair alone with her eyes on his back.

He stopped at the stair foot and looked back up once. She stood in her doorway with the light behind her and her hair down and her hand flat on the doorframe. The true copy lay squared on the side desk behind her with her bag beside it. Three days filed. One night given. Both true. Both hers.

"I will read to you here when reading is what you need," she called down softly. "Go up and keep the house that is still yours on paper. I keep my rooms. You keep the landing with its nail and its rectangle."

She refilled the cup of water after they sat down and set it square between them. The cloth stayed folded beside it. Small squares steadied the table. The side desk held the true copy with her bag beside it. The table held nothing that could be produced.

He drank half the water and set the cup down square. The water was cool. The evening light moved a finger width along the table while they sat. Time passed in light rather than in paper. That was the whole of the rest they took.

She told him about the three days alone before the night took that too. The key turned at night from the inside. The curtain drawn on the bed. The copy squared on the side desk. Bread eaten standing. Reading done aloud in empty rooms. Eleven minutes filed. All of it borne alone and now laid down.

He told her about the house without her in return. Bread standing round three papers. An empty room swept and kept. A landing swept round its nail. A rail oiled. A hatch carried. All of it done without her and kept for her. Both accounts stood without being entered.

She smoothed the folded cloth square while he watched. The cloth had no mark on it. Unmarked cloth could hold a cup without being read. That was enough work for one evening.

He watched her hands smooth the cloth and loved their steadiness. Four years they had held records till memory paid. Tonight they held cloth. The holding was the same and the cost was not.

She drew the curtain across the bed rail slow with her own hand. The rings moved quiet. The weave threw lines across the pillows. A drawn curtain in her own rooms was a door shut inside a door shut. Both were hers.

He helped her straighten the pillows without being asked. The pillows smelled of soap and of her. Straight pillows held the coming night square. Small squares steadied them both.

She pinned her hair up before the lamp was lit and then let it down again. Pinned hair was for divisions. Loose hair was for her own rooms. Tonight the rooms decided.

She lit the small lamp on the side desk herself after the curtain was drawn. The flame took and steadied. Light fell warm across the true copy without reading it. Paper and light lay together without touching.

He watched her hands cup the flame and loved their care. Eleven years they had steadied records. Tonight they steadied light. The steadiness was the same and the room was hers.

She set the lamp low herself when the rooms warmed. The flame sank steady. Shadows settled. The curtain weave threw its lines darker. Warm rooms held them without asking.

She banked the lamp herself before sleep and the rooms dimmed true. The true copy slept squared on its desk. The table slept cleared. Both kept till morning.

He breathed her air after the lamp was banked and found it sweet with soap and heat. The laundry below had shut its doors. The street had quieted. Sweet air held them till sleep.

She touched his face once after the light went low. Her fingers were warm. He turned into them and slept.

He walked home past the Board with the lamps lit until eleven and the walls exact. The ranks wall stood dark above its Thursday paint. The side board kept its number and its schedule in the dark. He climbed the sixty-one steps without counting and stood on the landing with his thumb in the cold middle of the pale rectangle.

At Ferrier Street the house breathed around an empty room with its name still on every paper. Seven pieces sat in seven pockets. Six addresses stayed. Six notebooks stayed. One sewn row stayed. One woman on no roll stayed. One woman on nothing stayed. One woman on no list stayed behind an open door. One woman stayed in her own rooms on Lantern Row with a true document behind her and a shut door she opened herself.

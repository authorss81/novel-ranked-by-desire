# A Quarter Of Eight

On the fifth of July Tessa Okonkwo worked the hatch from a quarter of eight as she had for nine years and he carried her crate down for her because she asked him to help with weight and not with paper.

He had woken wanting one day in the house with no new paper on the table. The want was small and he held it all morning. He felt tenderness toward the back room and its open door and grief for the days coming and the two lived side by side.

Seven women on the cut roll ate bread in stages before seven. Six kept an address at Ferrier Street and six kept the notebook. The seventh row stayed pencil sewn in a pocket with twenty-two stitches. Petra Ilyin was on no roll of any kind and squared the bundle string twice out of habit.

Wren Kallo was on nothing at any time and was the eighth partner and had gone early. Agnete Lindqvist was on no list at all and her door stood open and she had slept behind it open since the first.

The hatch ran two hundred and three people through the morning that ran to two. He set the crate where they would pass it and put his palm flat on the counter worn smooth by nine years of hands. A place got smooth by being used truly.

"You have carried enough crates," Tessa said. "Go up. Work."

He worked the Fourth Branch washrooms floor by floor. At noon he ate bread on the back step with nothing new in his pockets but the head line and the receipt. The step was warm from the sun. The yard clattered. For ten minutes no paper moved.

A woman from the fourth floor asked whether the hot water on the second landing had been off on purpose overnight, and he said it had not been off, it had been cold, and he would chase the boiler, and she said she was only reporting it and went down. Lode nodded to him on the stairs and the nod said everything the landing knew and would never put in a book.

He mopped and wiped and changed the paper. The work steadied him the way a table squared to an edge steadied a room. At three a clerk from the Reading came through with a docket frame and newssheet and hung a notice about hours beside the washroom door. The clerk squared the frame, stepped back, went on. Hands quick and plain.

He came home at six and found the house mending what could be mended before the week turned.

Beatrix oiled her tram gloves with care and folded them square. The gloves smelled of oil and work. Eleven burned months sat in a stove somewhere behind her record and she did not speak of them. She folded the gloves and set them by her notebook.

Runa cleaned her boots with a rag and set them square under the table. Ink still marked her cuff in a thin line. Her folder stood shut against the wall. Her hearing had a paper from the morning of the first. The paper wanted her standing and her instrument and her entitlement. The standing of the household was his, and he was a cleaner, and a cleaner's standing did not carry a field operative. That fact sat between them without being said.

Marguerite capped her pen and uncapped it and capped it again. The valuation copy lay squared with the dead list copy beside it. One said the account lived. The other said nothing about it. Between them they held the plate where it had been lodged on the seventh of last month.

Sun bit a loose thread off her pocket and showed him the twenty-two stitches holding. The white thread stood out against the dark coat. Eleven years of keeping nothing had taught her fingers care with small things.

"Twenty-two," she said. "Eleven was not enough. It gaped. Now it holds."

"It holds," he said.

Nadia wrote the day with the hour on it. She wrote which list each paper belonged to on the back of a page. Seven women on the cut roll. Six addresses at Ferrier Street. Six notebooks. One sewn row. One woman on no roll. One woman on nothing. One woman on no list. She read it back without turning the book round.

Ines came in at half past six and ate standing and went to her room. She had not discussed her review and she had not been asked about it. Her bag stood packed by the door of her room with the straps squared. She had said on the thirtieth that she was going to an office with a door on it in writing with a reason in the way it was done without saying which.

He passed her door on the way to the landing and heard her moving inside, quiet and exact. A drawer opened and shut. Paper squared to an edge. Straps pulled tight and buckled. The sounds were small and complete. The house had learned exactness from the office that held it. She did her own exactness back, in a room, with a bag.

Petra watched the bag the way she had watched hands for eleven years and said nothing about it. Runa looked once and looked away. Beatrix kept her good ear turned aside out of respect. Marguerite kept her pen capped. Tessa kept her hands folded. Sun kept her thumb on her stitches. Nadia wrote nothing about the bag, because some things stood best unwritten until the morning they moved.

He wanted to knock and to put his hand flat on the door and to say her name through it. The want filled his throat and he swallowed it. He felt love for her steadiness and fear for what the morrow would file and grief that he could stand in neither place for her.

Wren stopped him on the stair after supper with ink on her fingers and her folder shut.

Wren stopped him on the stair after supper with ink on her fingers and her folder shut.

"Twelve days," she said. "On Hadewig's desk twelve days. Four edits and every one a removal. It has not been acted on. I am the person who wrote it and I am the last person anyone will go to. I wanted you to hear the count."

"Twelve days," he said.

She leaned her shoulder against the stair wall a moment. The wall was cool through her sleeve. She had sat on a kerb in the Reading Square on the eighteenth with a folder flat on her knees and asked for four hours with nothing altered. Tonight she asked for nothing but the count to be heard.

"Tomorrow the Thursday crew comes," she said. "Two ladders and three pots at nine. I will be at my bench before they are on the wall. A division's file sits and a wall gets painted. Both move without anyone deciding."

Agnete stayed in the doorway longer than usual with her cup, watching the table with its three papers squared to the edge. The certificate. The receipt. The valuation copy. Three papers that agreed with each other and not with the index that held him.

He went to her with bread.

"Eat," he said.

She ate. The door stood open behind her. The evening light laid a bright edge along the table the way the high window had in the second reading room.

"Tomorrow the wall crew comes again," she said. "Not the monthly crew. The other one. I have watched them from my window eleven years. They paint the week off and leave the side board standing."

He nodded. The pass over the ranks wall and the board repaint were different crews in different shades. The landing kept the bare nail and the nine-by-fourteen rectangle.

At nine he stood on the landing with his thumb in the cold middle of the pale rectangle. The paint stayed pale. Below him the city went about its evening with its lamps lit until eleven.

He carried Beatrix’s gloves up to the landing after supper because she had left them on the table. The gloves smelled of oil and work. She thanked him and folded them square by her notebook. Small acts held the house the way stitches held a pocket.

Runa cleaned the bread knife after the meal and put it away with care. The blade caught the stair light a moment and went dark in the drawer. Exactness answered exactness. The office filed remarks and moved nothing. The house washed a knife and put it away.

Marguerite sat late with the dead list copy and the valuation copy side by side. One said the account lived. The other said nothing about it. Between them they held the plate. She capped her pen and left both squared to the edge.

Tessa folded her stamped copy into her coat and patted it flat. Two hundred and three a morning stood counted from the first. Nine years of a quarter of eight stood in the lines. She had worked it out on the sixteenth. She held to it.

Petra stood at the stair foot a while with her hands behind her back, looking up the sixty-one steps. Ten to nine on the first she had stood at the bottom with the bundle. Half past nine she had come down empty. Both stood in her body the way eleven years of markings did.

He checked the bundle string on the kitchen table before bed out of habit. Petra had tied it twice on the first and loosened it after. The string kept the shape of the tying. He smoothed it flat with his thumb and left it square.

Agnete thanked him for the smoothed string in the morning without saying why. A room where she stood could not compel testimony. A table with a squared string let her give it. Small squares held her the way they held Beatrix.

He set the bundle string square before sleep and left it for the morrow. The morrow was the sixth with its Thursday crew and its filed report. The string would hold till then.

He went in and lay down with the head line in his coat pocket and listened to the building breathe. The mark was in. The list had stayed quiet. The clinic was counted. The finding sat twelve days. The bag stood packed by a door that stayed open.

He slept after eleven with that bag in the room, packed, waiting for the morning to say which office had asked for it.

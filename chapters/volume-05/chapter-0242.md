# The First Valuation

On the second of July he went to work at five and mopped the fourth floor while the city read the new board without him.

He had woken wanting to see the side board himself before the figures drew a crowd. The want went unmet because the tram ran late and the washroom rota ran early. He felt disappointment sharp in his chest and carried it down the corridor with the bucket.

Lode met him on the stairs with a nod that said everything the landing knew.

"Mark went in," Lode said.

"It went in," he said. "At nine. In her own hand."

"Then the fourth is quiet for you," Lode said. "A live account does not go on the list. The securities stay where they are."

"They stay," he said.

He worked the washrooms floor by floor and let the morning go past in soap and paper. At ten a woman from the second landing asked whether the side board had changed and he said it carried the schedule of the twenty-sixth now and the number under it. She thanked him and went down. The exchange cost him nothing and steadied him.

At noon he ate on the back step with the receipt copy in his pocket. The step was warm. For ten minutes no paper moved anywhere in his life. He held the ten minutes with gratitude and both hands.

The valuation of the first stood entered in the real book with the hundred and thirty-third mark in ink in the broker's own hand at nine. The thirty-year account stayed live and secured. The plate at 8,138 stayed where it had been lodged on the seventh of last month. The floor stayed at 8,141 with a tolerance of three.

He came home at six and found the house counting what the first had settled.

The women on the cut roll had eaten bread standing round the table before he left. Six of them had tied a bundle string that night out of eleven years of habit, six of them carried a notebook and a door number, and one coat pocket held a pencil row sewn shut with twenty-two stitches.

Petra Ilyin had gone down the steps at ten to nine and come back at half past nine with the bundle emptied. Wren Kallo had come in late with ink on her fingers and said nothing about it. Agnete Lindqvist had slept for the first time in eleven years behind a shut door and had opened it herself in the morning.

Runa sat at the table with her hearing folder shut and her pencil down. Her morning at the Field division had taken the paper and kept it. She would not say beyond the morning and she was not asked to.

Beatrix sat beside her with her notebook shut and her hands folded. Her panel had put the same question to her it had been putting to her since the spring, the one about the cab and the ear that hears nothing, and had written her answer down. She would not say beyond the morning and she was not asked to.

Marguerite sat with the valuation copy squared to the edge of the table. The mark stood at the foot in her own hand, in ink, at nine, with a hundred and thirty-two behind it and not one of those years empty. The run held.

"It is worth what it secures," Marguerite said to the table rather than to him. "Six names in my own hand on a guild sheet. The assessment side will carry it a year running now that it is priced. That is what yesterday bought."

Petra stood at the end with her hands behind her back and looked at the copy without touching it.

"Eleven years I marked dockets on the second of the month and never asked who produced them," Petra said. "Yesterday I watched a hand mark at nine that I did ask about. That is the difference and I am the one who carries it."

Tessa came in from the hatch with her crate empty and her hands clean. Her clinic had gone into the returned lines on the thirtieth and was counted from the first. Two hundred and three a morning into somebody's question. Nine years of her standing at that counter before anybody wrote anything about it stood in the lines now.

"It is counted," Tessa said, and sat down suddenly. "From today. Hours and counts. A hatch and a room. I worked it out on the sixteenth and I am glad I did it before the valuation and not after."

Sun checked her stitches with her thumb and nodded. Twenty-two stitches held the seventh row in her pocket. Six other pieces sat in six other pockets in the building. The landing kept the bare nail and the nine-by-fourteen rectangle. The side board kept the new schedule.

Nadia wrote the day with the hour on it on the back of a page. She did not turn the notebook round and put it face up. That motion was done with in this house. She wrote and read back without it.

Ines came in at half past six and ate a slice standing and went to her room. She had not discussed her review and she had not been asked about it. The house kept to it with both hands.

He stopped at the Reading Square on the way home with Petra to look at the repainted side board with his own eyes. The monthly crew had come before the figures with their own pots in their own shade. The schedule of the twenty-sixth stood under the number with the paint still dark at the edges. The ranks wall beside it carried the week in the other shade. Two crews and two shades and neither touched the other's work.

A clerk from the Reading came out with a book, wrote the hour under the frame, went in. A woman read the number aloud to a child and moved on. Petra watched the hands and not the paper. She had marked a hundred and thirty-two dockets on the second of the month for eleven years. She knew what a hand did when it was not answering for anything.

"You wanted the board to argue for you," Petra said on the tram back. "It will not. A board persuades nobody. It only makes the question easy to ask. Yesterday an old man asked whether the number was a street number. Tomorrow a clerk will ask what is at the address. The second question has a form number on it."

He nodded. He had answered the first question truthfully and it had cost him nothing. The second had taken Agnete into a pale green room. Both had come off the same board.

At Ferrier Street Beatrix waited on the landing with her notebook open to a clean page. She wrote the board's new date in her small level hand and read it back to him. Her panel had asked its question that morning and written her answer down. Her hands were steady. Her good ear had caught every word.

"My hearing took the paper and kept it," Beatrix said. "It will not say beyond the morning. That is how a hearing holds a woman. It takes the morning and keeps it."

Runa came in late from Cordwain with ink on her cuff and her folder shut. Her morning at the Field division had taken her paper with a minute to spare before the counter closed. She ate bread with her boots on and talked tram talk with Beatrix, seats and seasons and guards. The talk held them both the way a rail holds standing passengers.

Wren stopped him on the stair after supper with a folder under her arm.

"Hadewig Mers has had my finding nine days," she said quietly. "Since the twenty-third at ten. Four edits and every one a removal. It has not been acted on. A division's file sits and waits. I wanted you to hear the count from me."

"Nine days," he said.

"Nine days," she said. "I will not be the person who asks what a division does with it. I am the person who wrote it."

Agnete stayed in the kitchen doorway longer than usual with her cup, watching the valuation copy on the table beside the certificate and the receipt. Three papers that agreed. The shut door of the night before stood open behind her in the day.

He went to her with bread after supper.

"Eat," he said.

She ate standing in the doorway with the door open behind her. The open door in daylight was a different thing from the shut door at night. Both were hers.

"Thank you," she said, "for waiting outside a door yesterday and for shutting one at night because I asked it. A room where I stand cannot compel testimony. A room where you stand lets me give it. That is the whole of my discipline and you have learned it."

He nodded. He had wanted to be the man at the bottom of the steps when the visible thing came asking. Yesterday he had been the man outside a door. The want had moved and he moved with it.

He carried Tessa's empty crate back to the hatch after supper because the evening run left it at the stair foot. The hatch counter stood bare with its wood showing the smoothness of nine years. He set the crate square under it. Two hundred and three people would lift from it in the morning. The work took four minutes and settled him.

Sun met him at the stair foot with her coat over her arm and her pocket turned out to show the stitching. Twenty-two white stitches stood against the dark cloth. Eleven had gaped. Twenty-two held.

"A pocket that holds a row holds the house," Sun said. "That is what I tell myself while I sew. Eleven years of keeping nothing taught me care with small things. Say it back to me."

"It holds the house," he said, and she nodded and turned the pocket back.

Petra squared the bundle string on the kitchen table out of habit and then loosened it. The bundle had gone up at ten to nine on the first and come down empty at half past nine. The string kept the shape of the tying. She smoothed it flat with her thumb.

"Eleven years a hand can do anything if it never asks," Petra said to the room. "One morning it asks and watches. That morning weighs more than the eleven years."

At nine he put his hand flat against the pale rectangle on the landing and then took it off again to feel the cold it had kept all day. Nine by fourteen, the nail above it holding nothing. Below him the city went about its evening and the walls stood exact.

He went in and lay down with the coat still on because there were two documents in it and he had stopped being able to separate himself from what he was carrying. The mark was in. The board was repainted. The hearings had taken their papers. The clinic was counted. The finding sat nine days on a desk. The woman who could price any of it had eaten standing and gone to her room without a word about her own paper.

He slept after eleven with that silence in the room, unasked, waiting for its week.

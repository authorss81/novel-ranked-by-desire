# The Fourth Question

On the morning of the twenty-third the envelope was still on a pallet in a room on a floor above the Reading, where nine people worked and every one of them worked faster than any of it could be read.

It had not been opened. The name of the household at Ferrier Street was written on the outside of it in a district hand, and it was going two floors above the room where Ines Duarte had eaten lunch at a table for two years without once sitting opposite anybody.

The clerk was Marit Kolb and she came at ten past seven that evening with her satchel and her card and the same coat, and Sun Meiling opened the door of the ground-floor flat before anybody had said anything and went up ahead of her.

"You have three days' warning," said Kolb on the bottom step, without being asked, "and I was told to say that I have three days' warning, and I would like it understood that I am saying it because I was told to and not because I think it is good of me."

She did not come up for that. She came up when she was told to, which was different, and Sun Meiling went up with her and stood at the top of the sixty-one steps with her hand on the rail.

The board was bare except for the head's line and the seven rows and the rule through the sixth of them.

Kolb read it from the top down and did not touch it and did not ask anybody to explain it, the same as the woman from the tenth, and she had a card out and a pencil and she stood on the landing in a coat she had not taken off, which is the whole of what distinguishes a person who does this properly from a person who has done it badly, and it was forty-five degrees and she did it anyway.

"One," she said, and wrote. "The number of names upon the roll and the number of spaces upon it."

"Six names and two spaces," said Sun Meiling.

Kolb wrote it down. "Two. Whether the roll carries the name of a head at the top of it, and whether the name is in a hand other than the office's."

"It does, and it is in his own hand, and it is dated the twelfth of this month."

"Three. The number of persons of reading age who keep an address at those premises."

That is the question that had been on a form since the twenty-second of March, and the answer to it had been six since a clerk wrote it in a queue, and the answer to it had been certified on the fourth of this month in a room with four pages on a table.

The six of them looked at one another on a landing and not one of them said a word, and the not saying took four full minutes, and in four minutes a woman with a card works out a great deal.

Kolb waited. She was not a woman who filled a silence. She stood on a landing with her card down at her side and waited, and the light came across the stair window and stopped about two inches above the seventh row, and the seventh row was empty and had been empty since the thirtieth of April, and it was the only thing in that room anybody was looking at.

"You are entered to ask it," said Nadia Ferrous, from the kitchen door.

"I am."

"Then ask it in that form. You have not asked it in that form. You have asked it in a form where the only answer that finishes the card is a number, and the number is not the answer, and the answer is who."

Kolb looked at her.

"I have not written anything," she said.

"You have written the date and you have written my name as the person asked," said Sun Meiling. "Both of those are on there. If the card comes back and it has got a question that was never asked, it comes back to you and not to me, and that is not what I am protecting."

Kolb turned the card round and looked at it for about four seconds.

Then she wrote one line and turned it back so the room could see it.

Three. Is any person keeping an address at these premises an officer of an office of this city. Yes or no, and if yes, name and office and standing.

The kitchen was extremely quiet.

"The six of us," said Beatrix Halm, from the stairs, "and one of us has to say it, and it is not going to be said by her, and we are not going to do it for her either."

Ines Duarte took the card off Kolb.

She did not ask. She did not say she was going to. She put her hand out flat and Kolb handed it over, and then Ines read the line on it with her head slightly on one side, which is the thing she does to a document, and then she wrote on it under the question, in a small even hand that four people in that room had seen on a landing and two of them had seen on four hundred words in a queue.

Yes. One. Ines Duarte. Rank two. Auditor.

She did not put the office's name on it, because a card with an office's name on it is a card about an office, and she wrote her name and her rank and the word that says what she is, and then she gave it back.

It took her four seconds to write. It has taken her four years to arrange everything else in a working life so that she would never have to, because the one advantage a woman who can read any record in this city has is that she is almost never a subject of one, and she has built a career out of that and it has cost her nothing at all, and it is the only thing she owns that cannot be priced.

Kolb looked at what she had written for about two seconds and then wrote one more word under it, which was the date, and put the card in the satchel.

"There," she said. "That is the third one and it is answered and it is on the card and I did not have to ask it twice."

"That was not the fourth question," said Runa Sato.

"That was the third question," said Kolb. "There is a fourth and it is not mine. It came off the same sheet under the same heading, and it is the one they sent me up sixty-one steps for, and it is this."

She read it off the card, because she had it written out, and it was a question about whether any person keeping an address at those premises held any standing, office or entitlement in a body corporate, guild, exchange or office of this city, and whether that person would produce the instrument evidencing it, and whether the instrument could be produced at that counter on the day.

"Yes," said Marguerite Vale.

She did not come up for that either. She was on the second stair with her gloves on.

"I hold rank two of the Vant of Brokers. It is an entitlement of this city and it is held under a guild of thirty years and it is not mine, it is the book's, and it is mine while I hold the book.

"The instrument is a quarterly return lodged at the Exchange and there are two of them in the year and the next one is in about three weeks. You may have the copy when it is lodged and not one hour before."

Kolb wrote it down in the ordinary way, at the ordinary speed, and did not ask her any of the follow-up questions that a person who has just been told a rank two by a broker would normally be required to ask.

"You are not going to ask her for it," said Sun Meiling.

"I am not going to ask her for it today," said Kolb, "and I want you to understand that those are not the same and that I will be at this door in three weeks and she will have a week less to find it."

"You are a district officer," said Nadia Ferrous, from the kitchen door, and she had not moved off it in about ten minutes. "What is your name."

"Marit Kolb."

"You have written six lines on that card in eleven minutes and you have not once said a wrong thing, and I have been counting, and I am going to say one thing to you and then I am going to leave you to your work.

"That woman wrote her own name on your card about ninety seconds ago and it is the first entry of its kind in that building in a very long time, and she did it because the question was hers to answer and not yours to force, and she has spent four years making sure that no office in this city can put her on a piece of paper without her putting herself on it.

"That is not me telling her not to do it again. That is me telling you what you are carrying down sixty-one steps, because you did not ask and I would have asked."

Kolb looked at her.

"Then I am carrying it down," she said, "and I did not know, and I have been doing this for nineteen years."

She went down the sixty-one steps and at the bottom of them she stopped and did not turn round.

"You read that card better than I did," she said, to the landing rather than to any of them. "I have been holding cards on landings for nineteen years and I have never once had that happen to me."

Ines Duarte stood where she had been standing with her hands in the pockets of a coat she had not taken off, and looked at the seventh row.

"Six names and two spaces," she said.

"Six names and two spaces," said Sun Meiling.

She went the rest of the way down with the card in the satchel and out into a street with trams on it.

The card had an hour on it and a name and a figure for names and a figure for spaces, and it was going into a building on a floor above the Reading, and from there into a division two floors below the room where the woman who had answered the third question ate her lunch every day at one o'clock.

The division that had raised those premises on the twenty-first had not told her. There is a rule that says an officer is not told about her own division's index sheets, and not one person in that building was going to break it, and Ines Duarte stood on a landing and looked at an empty row and did not know any of it yet.
